Memorial Union 

INCE the establishment of "Old 

Administration" on the crest of college hill in the eighties, no building erected on the campus has had so profound an effect on the life of the College as the Memorial Union. A product of sentiment and ideals, like that first College building,-the evidence of a purpose to do something tangible for the College as a token of faith in its destinythe Memorial Union is all that its name implies, a memorial and a center of campus unity.

It is first of all a memorial, a monument erected to the soldier dead of the College. It is also a campus union, housing the various undergraduate activity groups, the alumni association, the student publications, the graduate manager, the student loan officè, etc. It provides social and recreational facilities for students, alumni and faculty. It has complete facilities for serving meals-restaurant, tea rooms, special dining rooms and banquet halls. In general, it serves as a home-center for campus life. 

The building, costing approximately $750,000 with equipment and furnishings, was begun in May, 1926, and completed in October, 1928. It was made possible through contributions made by students, alumni, faculty and friends of the College.

This project, a student union building, first proposed by the student body, was selected as the most fitting type of memorial to the College men whose lives were sacrificed in the World War and 

Spanish-American War; since through its service it becomes a highly practical monument. 

Three stories and a mezzanine floor mount above the ground dimensions of 

102 by 286 feet. The front entrance is surmounted by an ornamental terra cotta dome. The frame is of reinforced concrete and steel with outside walls of Oregon-made, red, tapestry brick, trimmed with cast stone. The roof where exposed to the elements is of copper, as are all flashings, gutters, downspouts and Kalamein doors. The building is entirely fireproof, class-A construction throughout.

Executive control of the Union is vested in two boards. The Board of Governors has charge of finances and construction; it consists of four alumni, two student members and one "regent of the College to be appointed by the Board of Regents." The Board of Directors has charge of building activities; it consists of four student members, one faculty, one alumni member, and graduate manager. 

A general manager is appointed by the Board of Governors. He is responsible for all financial affairs of the Memorial Union, for the orderly conduct of the building and for its employees. The administration of the Union is responsible to the Board of Higher Education for the proper conduct of its affairs.

This booklet is published with a view to giving a brief summary of the history, purpose and use of the Memorial Union. 

It is in the nature of a report to the thousands of donors, all over the Pacific 

Northwest, and indeed throughout the world, whose personal contributions made [5]

possible this magnificent building. It aims to tell the story chiefly through pictures, the report of public ceremonies, and the addresses of persons who have given closest attention to the progress of the movement. The booklet gives abundant evidence of the faithful stewardship of those commissioned to carry out this splendid project. It shows that already, after only two years of occupancy, the building is fulfilling its high mission. It is doing, in short, what President Kerr predicted it would do when he addressed the student body through the Beaver in the spring of 1928. Following is the closing paragraph of that address: "Thus, through the agency of this beautiful building, will all the currents of life and tradition, of honor and faith, on this campus be blended into a powerful force. for upbuilding and ennobling the ideals of the institution. Students, alumni, fac-. ulty-all will be fused into a finer and firmer unity for fostering a splendid tradition of manhood and womanhood on this campus. All will have a clearer conception of the objectives of the institution. in serving the State of Oregon." Plantings now beautify the terrace and approaches.

The Reason for the Memorial Union 

President W. J. Kerr's Message to Alumni

DDRESSING the alumni of the College when the general purposes and plans of the Memorial Union project were fully matured, in January, 1925, Dr. 

W. J. Kerr, President of the College, put in circular form certain facts about the Need for a College Community Center, the Advantages of a Student Union Building, and the opportunity thus offered to alumni and friends of the College to show their loyalty to their alma mater in a tangible and impressive way. Following are some of the paragraphs of that message. 

Need for College Community Center 

There comes a time in the life of every large educational institution when its departments and activities become so diverse and specialized that it needs the unifying force of a great social center to retain its solidarity. The simplicity of organization inherent in its life as a small college community, under pioneering conditions, cannot persist in a modern state institution equipped to serve the complex interests of today. It needs a unifying and democratizing center. 

For this reason the O. 

S. C. student body, several years ago, in conference with representative alumni and faculty leaders, determined on a stual to be erected on the College campus in honor of our soldier dead. At that time the impulse to establish a worthy memorial for O. S. C. he roes of the World War and the Spanish-American War, became a ruling passion on the campus.

It resulted in definite student-body action, endorsed by the Administrative Council and the Board of Regents, whereby the students voted to tax themselves, at each term registration, a sum of three dollars, or nine dollars a year, throughout their college course.

Thus was begun the Memorial Union, dedicated to the ideals of our soldier dead, and to the character-building of dent union building as the ideal type of memori- their thousands of successors on the campus.

Advantages of Student Union Building 

Aside from the satisfaction to be derived from fulfilling a high duty in establishing a memorial to our soldier dead, what are the advantages of a student union building? The following are some of the benefits that will undoubtedly come to the College as an institution, to the student body, and to the alumni, as a result of the student union building on the campus.

They provide not only the opportunity but the compelling incentive to express our conviction in the ideals of the institution by building, on our own initiative and through our own sacrifice and service, this Memorial Union. [ 7 ]

"The scene from this point is one of symmetry and grandeur.

" [ 8 ]

For O. S. C. As An Institution 

1. Hold up a perpetual ideal of service and sacrifice, as exemplified by the lives of those who died for their country. 

2. Develop a great Beaver spirit of loyalty to the entire institution and its broader interests. 

3. Develop a center, adequately equipped, for the accomplishment of large undertakings that concern the entire college community -students, alumni, faculty, and friends. 

4. Serve as a social center for the student body, thus encouraging a democratic, alert, and purposeful ideal of student fellowship. 

5. Give opportunity for a more intimate, yet democratic, direction and regulation of student social interests. 

6. Provide a place of entertainment for alumni and institutional guests, where they may gain a natural view of student life and activities.

For the Student Body 

1. Provide a center for democratic fellowship among all students of the College-men and women, rich and poor, engineers and farmers, journalists and musicians, athletes and debaters, freshmen and seniors. 

2. Socialize the student under a dignified and wholesome environment, thus rounding out and developing his personality. 

3. Foster recognized student organizations by giving them a suitable place for their business and social undertakings. 

4. Through responsibility for the management of the Union, make the student more efficient in community service. 

5. Bring the student into congenial contact with members of the faculty and with visiting alumni, lecturers, musicians, and artists. 

6. Establish under one roof such essential conveniences as a social hall, cafeteria, bookstore, postoffice, reading room, conference rooms, and student organizations. 

For the Alumni 

1. Provide offices for the General Alumni

Association with adequate facilities for editing the alumni monthly and for safeguarding the records and property of the 

Association and making them available for fullest use. 

2. Provide a campus home for the returning alumnus, with dining-rooms, committee rooms, reading and writing rooms, and all the conveniences of a campus club. 

3. Intensify the bond of Beaver Brotherhood by embodying in an enduring structure the traditions of loyalty and sacrifice that have animated the O. S. C. men and women of other days. 

4. Give the alumnus a chance to keep in closer touch with the changing life of the campus and to make his imprint upon it. 

5. Furnish a congenial center for renewing associations with former teachers on the faculty and for making contacts with contemporary members of the College staff. 

6. Afford occasion for expressing his appreciation and gratitude to his alma mater, in a concrete way, by helping to establish a greater center of college life and a lofty memorial of the sacrifices of his fellow students and alumni. 

A summer session group of 1929. [9]

O. S. C.'s Outstanding Heroes 

What college has a more secure glory than O. S. C. in the service of its men in the late war -faculty, alumni, and students? Two thousand men were enlisted in the various arms of national service. More than sixty of them "paid the last full measure of devotion." The names of these men sanctify to us forever some of the most significant engagements of the war. And among the living, whom we still have with us in the work of perfecting the country that they and their dead comrades helped to save-what a brilliant galaxy! From the faculty, "The 

Rock of the Marne" and others who won distinguished honors. Among the students-the most distinguished soldier of the state of Washington, and the most distinguished soldier of the state of Oregon, her representative in the Living Hall of Fame. These are but instances of the heroic achievements that shed luster upon the fame of the College that we love. 

The First Call to O. S. C. Will Be Nobly Met 

Never before has an opportunity been offered all the alumni and friends of the College to contribute to the erection of a great building on the campus. In 1887, Benton county citizens, desirous of helping to fix for all time the location of the State Agricultural College at Corvallis, raised $25,000, a very large sum in those days, for the erection of the first building on the campus, the present Administration building. 

But since then no general appeal for gifts has ever been made. Hence to the alumni and former students comes for the first time the opportunity to express, in tangible terms, their loyalty and gratitude to their alma mater. The need for the Union building on the campus is press- ing. It cannot be built from institutional funds, even if funds were available. As a memorial to our heroic dead it is a challenge to our patriotism and our comradeship. It calls every one of us to a united and magnificent effort to uphold the dignity and grandeur of O. S. C. Shall we do it? For O. S. C. we will!

The Dean of Men's Message to Students 

From the Beaver, 1927-28 HE Memorial Union building will be a union indeed in affording a common meeting ground for the many and varied elements of the big O. 

S. C. family. Today, if a student committee desires a meeting which represents various divisions of the campus, whether it be in the interest of athletics, social life, or a professional fraternity, the first question raised is, "Where shall we meet?" Tomorrow the Union building will be the answer. 

Today our faculty counts well into the hundreds and with the constant institutional growth the number will increase. The size of the institution means a natural separation of the faculty units, both in location and interest. Today there is no club house where the different groups may meet for common committee studies. There is no eating place where the faculty members may come for their meals with the resultant talk fest. There is no common meeting place for games and recreation; neither are there facilities for entertaining visitors or student friends. Tomorrow the Union will provide all these facilities commonly afforded by a faculty club house. 

The third member of the O. S. C. family, the alumni, will likewise realize that a long-felt need has been satisfied. From the nature of the case, the memorial feature will attract the alumni. There they will meet their friends of the past, whether they be fellow alumni or faculty, and there they will meet the present student body to live over the past experiences and talk over the problems and hopes of a greater O. S. C.

And now by our own efforts we are about to realize our ambition. How great will be the convenience and joy of all the members of this big O. S. C. family when this hope in the

Memorial Union building has matured, and as a whole family we can plan and play together.

The Meaning of the Memorial Union By Edwin T. Reed 

Address before the student body, February 29, 1928, on the occasion of a "drive" for contributions by undergraduate students who were not on the campus when the main 

OF 

drives rolled up a fund of over half a million dollars. 

HAVE heard it said that in its zeal for making use of the new building being finished alongside of us here, the campus has forgotten the primary purpose for which it was erected-a memorial to the men who served and died in war. 

If so, this is not greatly to be wondered at. None of you college students were born when the 

Spanish-American War was fought. Most of you underclassmen freshmen and sophomores were only three to five years old when the World 

War broke out. When the United States got into the war, you were only six to eight years old, and even when the Armistice was signed and all conditions of actual warfare had ceased, you were still scarcely more than children-too young to appreciate the full burden of your loss even in the death of a father or an older brother. 

So it is no wonder that to you the memorial feature is not supremely vital. To us, however, who witnessed the war and who shared in the initial campaigns to provide the funds for this great building, the memorial idea was a vivid and compelling issue. 

But why a memorial? If you go to Harvard the first thing they will show you is the John

Harvard statue at the point of the triangle between Kirkland and Cambridge streets.* But if you are like me, your eyes, when you saw it. first, would dwell but a moment on the beautiful bronze figure of the founder of Harvard College in the foreground, because of the overshadowing grandeur, the massive Gothic beauty and the aspiring tower of Memorial Hall just back of it. And as they took you through the deep arched doorway of that first college memorial ever built in 

America, into the tall rotunda, where, with the sunlight sifting through tall mullioned windows, you read the tablets dedicated to the gallant sons of Harvard who died in their country's wars, you would have a sense of reverence, of exaltation, of the timelessness of heroic deeds, as I did when 

I first beheld it, that would fill your heart with a deep and abiding realization of the influence of a college memorial building. For Memorial Hall, built at Harvard in 1874, has been the forerunner of many other great college memorials throughout the country. 

Scores of memorials have been built since then, especially since the close of the World War, some as stadiums, some as auditoriums, some as campaniles, some as student union buildings, many involving expenditures of a million dollars or more, and all gifts of loyal friends who wanted to show by their own self-denial some measure of appreciation of the sacrifices of the men who died in battle or in the hazards of war. Cornell has such a memorial, Columbia *Recently moved to the "yard" in front of University Hall. [ 14 ]

has such a memorial, Michigan, Michigan State 

College, Missouri, Ohio, Illinois, Indiana, Purdue, Iowa, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Kansas, Texas, California. A great wave of loyalty and devotion has swept over these institutions and has piled up memorial funds that are rearing splendid monuments to immortalize their heroes of the war.

There are two kinds of immortality-the immortality of monuments-of things to look at and recall, and the immortality of use-ideas and ideals incorporated into our every-day lives as the vital forces of society. Our Memorial Union building will combine both these immortalities. It will stand on this campus as an enduring monument to the memory of the men who flung their bodies, a living sacrifice, upon the altar of their country in time of war. 

But it will not be a mere symbol-a lifeless mass of stone standing for an ideal but utterly devoid of utility. It will conform to the best standards of the International

War Memorial Associ ation, which decreed that all memorials erected in the European war zone must be of use to the living as well as commemorative of the dead. It will provide a shrine, therefore, a hearthstone, broad as the life of O. S. C., for the perpetuation of those fires of duty, honor, aspiration, and service that blend in the flame of Beaver spirit. It will be a magnificent Memorial proclaiming to future generations that we have kept faith with those that died.

But can an idea be immortalized in brick and stone? What to us is the civilization of ancient 

Egypt except the Pyramids and the Sphinx?

All the grandeur that was Greece is epitomized for you and me in the splendid symmetry of the Parthenon. The imperial genius of ancient 

Rome for organization, for political power, for popular glory-is perpetuated in the masonry of the Forum and the Coliseum. All the beauty of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance-their longing for religion and cultureis crystalized forever in their great cathedrals, like Rheims, St. Peter's and St. Paul's. Why a 

Lincoln memorial overlooking the Potomac, majestic in its marble isolation, or a Grant's 

Tomb overlooking the Hudson, if not a memorial to our own war heroes on this campus? 

But you say, we had no Alan Seeger, like Harvard. No, we had no singer who wrote such lines as did Alan Seeger, looking out from his front line trenches at the Marne. ...

Under the little crosses where they rise The soldier rests. 

True, we had no fullthroated singer like that. 

But neither did any other college. He was unique. 

But we had our Norval 

Carnie, author of the junior class play of the class of 1917, a young man of undoubted literary talent, a mere lad, yet full of a gentle and abounding originality, who would undoubtedly have been heard from in the years to come. Life was as sweet to Norval Carnie as it was to Alan Seeger. 

He was as dear to his family and his friends as 

Seeger was. But, like 

Seeger, he had a "Rendezvous With Death," and like him he did "not fail that rendezvous."

We did not have a Quentin Roosevelt, as did 

Harvard, hurtling like a thunderbolt through the illimitable ether, and falling, as all the world knows, in an air battle behind the enemy's lines. But we had our Mark Middlekauffone among many gallant O. S. C. air men-performing as dangerous a mission as Roosevelttesting war planes for service and giving his life with as blithe a spirit, while piloting a new plane to the front for battle.

We had over two thousand men enlisted in actual war service-students, alumni, and faculty-most of whom went over seas and got into action, and seventy-one of whom lost their lives in service, many of them on the field of battle, performing prodigies of valor. For it is a fact readily demonstrated from official [ 15 ]

records that our memorial building commemorates a contribution to the World War as glorious in its heroism as that of any college in America-as efficient, as positive, and as immediate to the winning of the victory.

Any of you who have studied the war know, beyond a doubt, that after the first great German drive in the spring of 1918, every time the enemy hordes were checked in their colossal drives, every time they were turned back, every time they were attacked and decisively beaten, American troops were in the Allied action, or in parallel actions of their own, and among these American troops were your friends and mine of O. S. C.

The first American victory of the war was at Cantigny. The battle, comparatively speaking, was a small engagement, but it was massive in its consequences. In the midst of surrounding gloom and defeat, it was an uncompromising and overwhelming victory over the foe.

Except for the twelve French tanks, it was virtually an all-American victory. As a demonstration of what green American troops could do it was a revelation to the Allies and to the world, and O. S. C. was in it. It seems only yesterday that Lloyd I. Grubbe, Co. D.,

First Engineers, a student at the College, sat in my office, telling of the deadly sacrifices necessary to win that victory. Thirty men were killed near him in a quarry by a single bomb and five machine gun bullets penetrated his kit pack as he lay wounded face downward in a shallow trench. But he came out victor in the first American offensive of the war. 

On May 27 the Germans started their second big offensive of the year. Bursting by strategic surprise through a weak part of the Allied line, they swept down between Soissons and Rheims, and in a few days had completely filled with their invasion the great triangle, Soissons, Rheims, Chateau-Thierry. 

Then turning within that triangle, they faced westward, and plunged in four days forty miles toward Paris. Nothing seemed able to stop them until at last they struck the Second Division of American troops, among which were at least a score of O. S. C. men. In that Second 

Division also were two brigades of Marines who played a glorious part. Thrown across the road which runs from Chateau-Thierry to Paris, they countered the Boche short, held him, and in successive days of brilliant fighting wrested from him the villages of Bouresches and Veaux, and the immortal Bois de Belleau.

Among those heroic marines were Capt. Ralph 

Wilcox, who went over the top five times during the war, winning the D. S. C. and Croix de 

Guerre, and who reports that on the evening of June 6 only five of the thirty officers of the marines came out of the deadly but triumphant struggle for Belleau Wood. In the long and grilling campaigns of the Marne and the Meuse-Argonne, hundreds of Oregon State men, in some of the most confusing and desperate engagements of the war, gave their utmost to help turn the tide of battle and bring the conflict to a close. Several of these lost their lives in these engagements and others were grievously injured. Many performed amazing exploits and were awarded highest honors by the United States government and the allied nations for heroic service. A very few examples, taken from different campaigns in which Oregon State men fought, will indicate the brilliance and self-sacrifice of these achievements.

The first is that of Colonel U. G. McAlexan- [ 17 ]

der, former Commandant of Cadets at the College, who, as commander of the 38th U. S. Infantry, between July 15 and July 18, 1918, in the second great battle of the Marne, presented such immovable resistance to the enemy as to break the German advance on Paris, though the force and fury of the assault were so deadly that it swept past him on both flanks. 

The plan of the enemy was to force a crossing of the Marne in the Jaulgonne region, force the Surmelin Valley, and strike for Paris past

Chateau-Thierry. The 38th regiment held the key position at the mouth of the Surmelin river. It was supposed to be adequately supported. 

Before the end of the first day's fighting, however, the Allies on their right had given way, the left was unsupported. All day long on July 

15, according to authentic accounts, Colonel "Mac's" regiment fought on its flanks as well as its front. For three days the German high command hurled at them all the forces they could muster in their desperation to open the gateway. They did not even take the first line of Colonel "Mac's" defense. Unable to consolidate their lines through his regiment, the Germans were obliged to abandon this last and most desperate of their drives on Paris. "The history of the world," said Hertling, the German Chancellor, shortly before his death, "was played out in those three days between July 15 and July 18, 1918."

Among others whom he saved from the battle field, desperately wounded, was Lieutenant

At Miller's Hill, where Wight also rendered heroic service to the wounded, he was assisted by Wallace W. Smith, '20, who was cited for conspicuous bravery in action, helping to carry off the wounded from the field, though himself twice wounded.

He was behind the enemies' lines as a spy a half dozen times, including a visit to Metz for information, with a companion of the intelligence service, just before the Armistice.

The College in the World War 

(Compiled from a report to the President of the College in the Biennial Report of 1918-1920) 

HE War involved the Oregon State 

Agricultural College, like many others of the land-grant colleges offering military training, to such an extent as to be a dominant interest even before the declaration of war and long after the signing of the Armistice. Volunteers among students and alumni joined the Allied forces before the United States definitely entered the War. Over two thousand students, alumni, and faculty members were enlisted in United States war service; while over two thousand men, in addition, secured training for military service at the College in the S. A. T. C. and other military and naval training units. Eight ceremonial occasions, emphasizing the importance of events in which the College contributed toward the winning of the War, were held on the campus, attracting thousands of citizens. 

The first was the convocation of April 27, 1917, in honor of the 204 men, all advanced students, who left for the first training camp of the 

Officers' Reserve Corps; the second and third were the dedication exercises of the first and second service flags, April 21, 1918, and June 3, 1918; the fourth was the inauguration of the 

S. A. T. C. on October 1, 1918; the fifth was the 

Armistice Day celebration, November 11; the sixth, the exercises in honor of the demobilization of the S. A. T. C., held on December 11, the demobilization officially occuring on December 21; and the seventh the exercises of Commencement week, 1919, when the Gold Star

Memorial Service was held in the Men's Gymnasium on June 9. Ten years later, June 1, 1929, the Memorial Union, completed and occupied, was formally dedicated to the memory of the Oregon State men and women who lost their lives as a result of military services in the

Memorial Union One of West's Finest Structures By Irven Ellsworth Stevenson 

'HE Memorial Union, "dedicated to the service and inspiration of the living and to the memory of our immortal dead." Thus denominated, there is being erected on the campus of Oregon State College at Corvallis, one of the finest structures of its kind to be found on the whole 

Pacific Coast. 

Set in artfully terraced lawns, surrounded by wide porches reached by winding walks, crowned with a majestic dome and glorified by a touch of the Parthenon in the colonnade-bordered loggias, the Memorial Union will be a picture of classic magnificence in design and a monument to good building in its choice of materials. 

Founded on massive piles, driven deep into the campus, rises this practical monument to the heroic dead. With ground dimensions of 

100x300 it will stand three stories high, with a frame of reinforced concrete and steel, walls of dense, Oregon-made red tapestry brick, trim of cast stone and a dome of ornamental terracotta. The roof, where it is exposed to the elements, will be of everlasting copper, as will also be all flashings, gutters, downspouts and Kalamein doors.

Approaching the structure from the greensward of the campus knoll, a series of light *Reprinted from Pacific Builder and Engineer, July 

7, 1928. gray steps of Nelson Island granite lead to the broad platform of the main entrance, which is flanked on either side by wide porches supporting buttresses and column bases, all of the same material. The granite is plain hammered finish in harmony with the classical style selected by the architects for this memorial.

Characteristic of the beauty and majesty of the entire structure, is the fine bronze entrance motif of the main entrance. Nearly 28 feet high and more than 12 feet wide, this detail is of solid bronze, carefully cast or wrought in appropriate design. There are four doors, a pair in the center with a single door at each side. Flanking the center pair and separating them. from the single doors, will stand two rectangular fluted bronze columns, surmounted by bronze statues of the beaver, the animal emblem of Oregon. Above the doors of solid, or extruded, bronze is a wide ornamental spandrel which extends the full width of the entrance. Upon the exterior of this will be a freestanding bronze cresting, simple yet decorative, and on the inside will be a bronze panel, appropriate for an inscription.

Above the doors, extending to the top of the arched entrance, will be windows with a bronze frame and bronze muntins.

Inside this entrance one stands in the main vestibule facing a grand staircase twenty feet [ 24 ]

wide leading up to a transverse concourse which extends almost the full length of the building. 

On each side of this majestic staircase an eightfoot stairway leads down to the ground floor. 

The entire scene from this point is one of symmetry and grandeur. Roman travertine is used for floor, walls, stair treads and risers. 

Bordering the grand staircase on each side is a massive parapet, two feet wide, rising in a series of steps, each three and one-half feet long and surmounted with solid marble carved consoles, 14 inches high, each weighing 1200 pounds. 

Travertine Used Extensively

A 9 foot 4 inch travertine wainscot skirts the vestibule, while the main entrance door has a border of the same material. A solid marble trim and cornice follows each side of the main stairway down to and entering the ground floor. 

Exemplary of the care taken to maintain the symmetry of the classic design is the perfect alignment of joints in the marble floor and walls of the vestibule. Tiles and ashler must be cut to special sizes, so that the transverse and longitudinal joints will line up with the stair parapets and risers. The grandeur of marble is carried throughout the building by a jet black Belgian marble base in the main rooms and corridors with 

Italian black and gold marble hearths for the fireplaces, marble window stools on the ground floor, and Napoleon gray marble partitions in the lavatories.

The air of simple, restful richness and luxury predominating the interior is to be further enhanced by the woodwork. All doors and trim including counters and panels, will be of figured red gum. Oak floors are specified on the first and second floors.

The offices on the ground floor will be finished in Oregon fir and hemlock. The main lounge on the first floor, a room of magnificent spaciousness, will have a deep wainscot of figured red gum panels, as will also the boys' and girls' smaller lounges at each end, the women faculty's room, men faculty's room and one large office suite. Counters will have beautifully finished gumwood tops with flush panel fronts.

All doors, classic in design, will be 1 inches thick with six panels each, and will be fitted with unit locks and hardware of solid cast bronze, waxed finish. The walls, above the wood and marble wainscot, will be finished in soft toned caenstone plaster. The ceilings will be done in ornamental plaster, preserving the classical spirit in their symmetry of design. 

The building will be beautifully lighted by day and night. Sunlight will enter through plate glass. 

More than 6,000 square feet of plate glass will admit the light of day and afford an undistorted vision of the beauties of the campus that surrounds the building. 

Artificial light will be shed throughout the building at night by fixtures that, by night or day, will be subject for admiration. The fixtures in themselves are eloquent of the careful regard to design and workmanship characteristic of Memorial Union. In the main entrance vestibule, a massive chandelier four feet in diameter and of classical design will be a feature. The concourse on the first floor will be lighted from a concealed source. The main lounge, which is to be finished in the style of the Spanish rennaissance, will have fixtures in keeping with that style. Two great chandeliers with branching arms will shed a soft glow of light from powerful bulbs concealed in hand-made Spanish globes of colored glass. All will be beautifully made, their craftsmanship denoting permanency and dignity in keeping with the practical-monument idea of this beautiful building. [ 25 ]

President Kerr's Introduction N introducing the Dedicatory Pro gram, President Kerr spoke as follows:

The idea of a Student Union building originated about ten years ago with members of "Gauntlet and Visor," a sophomore student society. Warren Daigh and A. G.

Schille, members of the group, urged the society to launch a movement for voluntary contributions as a nucleus to provide a building large enough for student-body social functions, but at the same time so planned as to provide facilities for various functions desired by smaller groups on the campus.

During the latter part of 1919 Mr. Daigh had occasion to visit Ann Arbor, where he became acquainted with the Student Union building of the University of Michigan, and its advantages to the student body. Upon his return he and 

Mr. Schille conducted an extensive investigation of similar buildings at other colleges and universities throughout the country. 

Immediately following the Armistice enthusiasm began to develop on the campus for establishing a war memorial to those who lost their lives in military service. Early in the school year 1920-21 sentiment among the students was crystalizing in favor of uniting the memorial idea with the student union building. 

Later the idea of a memorial to the soldiers of the World War was expanded to include also those who had participated in the Spanish- American War. The student body under the leadership of Roy S. Keene, President, had now become officially enlisted in the project. 

After careful study by a special committee on ways and means, the student body recommended the establishment of a student Memorial Union building fee of $3 a term, or $9 a year. This recommendation was approved by the President of the College, and on October 7, 1921, was ratified by the Board of Regents. 

On June 2, 1923, the Alumni Association adopted a resolution approving the plans of the student body and pledging the support of the alumni. 

A corporation was formed on January 10, 1924, and on June 7 of the same year a Board of Governors was appointed, composed of representatives of the Board of Regents, the Alumni Association, the faculty, and the student body.

On July 9, 1924, plans for raising money were adopted, and on January 20, 1925, a campaign for funds was started on the campus, with an initial subscription by students and faculty of nearly $100,000.

Following this, a vigorous campaign was started among alumni and with the combined efforts of students, alumni, faculty, and friends, subscriptions and money were received aggregating $684,737.49.

After consultation with A. D. Taylor, Landscape Architect, of Cleveland, Ohio, the Board of Governors recommended that the building be located on the present site, and on January 

6, 1926, this recommendation was approved by the Board of Regents. 

Architect Lee A. Thomas, Portland, 1907 graduate of the College, and Marbury Somervell, of Los Angeles, California, were employed to prepare plans and specifications. 

The site was dedicated March 21, 1926; ground was broken in June, 1927; and the contract for construction was awarded to Parker & Banfield, of Portland, on July 19, 1927. The building was formally opened for use at Homecoming, November 17, 1928, and was accepted by the Board of Governors as completed on 

November 25, 1928. 

And today, June 1, 1929, the building, completed and equipped, overflowing with the life of the entire college community, students, alumni, faculty and friends-is being formally dedicated in memory of the heroic dead who gave their lives in their country's wars. On this auspicious occasion the College, joining with the many donors of this beautiful building, has the honor of paying deserved recognition to all its sons and daughters, who, under the stars and stripes, gave to their country and the world, in time of bitter need, the fullest measure of their strength in faithful service, heroic deeds, and noble sacrifices. I can well understand why Pericles commended the Athenian public funeral in memory of soldier dead, but doubted the wisdom of any speech, declaring that where men's deeds have been great, they can be honored in deed only. Words, even were I able to summon the most eloquent, are empty in face of the record graven on these walls. Oratory cannot adorn the supreme sacrifice. 

It is not for the dead, but for the living that we need hours of remembrance and commemoration. Those whom we honor today are indeed far above and beyond "our poor power to add or detract." It is for our own discipline that we need to come together to find courage in the memory of their great deeds, to draw strength from the recollection of their weighty sacrifices, to seek inspiration in the rehearsal of their high hopes and endeavors.

In this spirit let us look back on the campus of the Oregon State Agricultural College in the spring of 1918. Many volunteers among students and alumni had joined the allied forces before the United States definitely entered the war, and the first call for volunteers threatened to start a stampede to enlist. Then came a proclamation from the Secretary of War urging college men, especially those receiving military training in land grant colleges, to continue their studies until called upon by the War Department.

Obedient to that counsel, the men of the Oregon State Agricultural College took up the harder part. Although many were burning with eagerness to enlist at once, although their zeal to serve made apparent inaction almost intolerable, nevertheless they remained steadily at their work, and with such patience as their youth and patriotic fervor would permit, they waited quietly for the call of the government. This was during an hour of crisis when men's emotions sometimes overrode their sense of justice, when words were warm rather than carefully weighed. To realize how hard must have been the task of holding to their daily routine in the midst of a vast upheaval, we must remember that this irksome duty was carried out in face of unjust insinuations, of slurs which cut to the quick the proud and impatient spirit of youth. No small part of the honor which surrounds the war record of the State Agricultural

College belongs to those young men who, despite the powerful temptation to cast aside restraint, could yet realize that there are times when, "They also serve who only stand and wait." 

Toward the end of April came the call that had been so eagerly awaited, and it was like a signal of release. Five minutes after the telegram from the War Department was received the Senior Class of one department had signed up in its entirety. From then on the lists were crowded. In less than a week, the College assembled in convocation to bid farewell and 

Godspeed to the more than two hundred advanced students who were departing for offi- [ 37 ]

cers' training camps, and who went out with the President's parting message, "Be true to yourselves, be true to your parents, be true to your College, your country, and your God."

In an incredibly short time the College was enrolled for war service in every department. 

Nearly two thousand students, alumni, and faculty members enlisted in the service of their country. Their record is a proud part of the magnificent chapter which is the war history of Oregon.

I have borrowed here the words of your College Editor, "After the first great German drive in the spring of 1918, every time the enemy was checked and thrown back in his prodigious drives, every time he was attacked and decisively beaten, American troops were in the allied actions, or in parallel actions of their own, and among these American troops were men of O. 

S. C. O. S. C. men were in the first American victory at Cantigny, at least a score took part in the decisive fighting at Chateau-Thierry, a number were among the marines who shared the bloody but brilliant encounter at Belleau Wood; more than forty were in the Battle of 

St. Mihiel; and hundreds took part in the long and gruelling campaign of the Meuse-Argonne. 

These are but instances of the gallant and effective service of the men of the college." 

That same gallant and effective service was carried on at home as well as overseas. Every activity essential to the successful prosecution of the war was loyally and ably supported by 

The Grand Concourse. 

The doors were formally opened at Homecoming, 1928. alumni, students and faculty members. Special mention is due the more than two thousand members of the Students' Army Training Corps who carried on their military service in addition to their academic training. From Major 

General to Private and on through the ranks of civilian service, from names outstanding in their distinction to those who served without personal recognition, the men and women of the College acquitted themselves proudly in the service of their country.

They displayed the spirit which in the Spanish American War made the famous Second 

Oregon Volunteers the pride of the Philippine service, the spirit that won for Oregon in the late war the name of "The Volunteer State," the same proud spirit which has always characterized Oregon's response in times of national emergency. They brought honor to themselves, their state and their college. 

When the Armistice was signed they again bridged the vast gap between war and peace as quickly and effectively as when they took up arms. Perhaps it was the harder task to gather up the uneventful thread of every day life after their spectacular interlude; however, as unobtrusively as they had once awaited the call of duty they returned to their particular fields of civilian service.

But some there were who did not return. 

Death mustered out more than seventy men of the College from the gallant fellowship of arms. [ 38 ]

The south side of the Main Lounge. 

They gave their lives as a part of the price of victory.

Life is always precious. Men cling to it with an amazing tenacity in face of circumstances which appear intolerable. But how immeasurably dear is life to the young college man in the prime of his youth with all the enthusiasm, the fellowship, the joy and promise which belong to that great day, and within his grasp the opportunities for realizing and developing his possibilities for experience and service. The offering up of this gift by the men of the College who died in the World War and in the Spanish 

American War must never be forgotten.

The beautiful building which we dedicate today is evidence that the memory of their sacrifice is a living reality. You have heard that the 

Memorial Union Building was conceived in the minds of students of the College; it was designed by an Alumnus of the College; it was made possible by the work, the faith, the generosity and the sacrifice of students, faculty, alumni and friends of the College; it is dedicated not only to the memory of the immortal dead of the College but to the service and inspiration of the living.

Since its completion it has been the warm and active heart of the campus, from which go out the streams that unify this great college in all its departments, and which feed that intangible but none the less real force, the College spirit. Its beauty will be a constant source of inspiration and its utility an encouragement and stimulus to the fine functioning of the College. Here student-body activities have quarters which enable them to work efficiently, to keep in close touch with one another, and to know the uses of cooperation. Here fellowship and recreation can go forward in surroundings that by their character exercise a salutary influence. Here students and faculty have a common meeting place in which to achieve closer understanding and friendship. Here the comradeship of the town and campus can be promoted to their mutual benefit. Here alumni can come together to renew old friendships and to recapture the priceless spirit of college days. 

I can conceive of no more fitting memorial to those who served to "the last full measure of devotion," than this structure which daily rededicates itself to service.

The faith, the idealism and the endeavor of which this building is a creation and a symbol are evidence that the men who served and died, and the men who served and lived, did not do so in vain. They are not martyrs of a closed episode but heroes of a cause that was once theirs and ours and is still ours. This building is not only a symbol of reverence and grief for those who died, it is a symbol of adherence to the ideals for which they gave their lives. 

If we honor their memory here today, and tomorrow deprecate the cause in which they found their glory and their death, then we are guilty of betrayal not only of the dead but of the living. They marched forth inspired by the 

The Main Lounge from the Concourse. 

[ 39 ]

ideals of honor, liberty, democracy and human civilization. Those great words were then full of meaning and reality to them and to us. What are they to us now-vital truth, or vain illusion?

When these young men went out, "to make the world safe for democracy" were they responding to an oratorical platitude, were they pursuing a hopeless vision? There are those who would tell us so. But it seems to me a very shallow cynicism which cannot see that democracy is vastly nearer complete realization today than it was before the War. 

Where are the absolute rulers on the face of the earth today? Where are the monarchs with complete autocratic power? Even such strongholds of absolute monarchy as Turkey are struggling toward the approximation of representative government. Where strong men have arisen and have taken advantage of the confusion that followed the war to acquire great personal power, the forms of democracy have been retained and will recover their vitality. 

We now realize, it is true, that the war to make the world safe for democracy cannot be finally won on the battle field. It must be won in the school room, at the polls, through the press, and in the whole wide fields of constructive citizenship. Nevertheless, those who carried the battle cry of America into the World

War gave democracy an impetus which has extended to the remotest corners of the earth, and they have left us a challenge to strengthen and purify our own democracy to the end that those who follow our lead may have a worthy pattern.

These young men who died in the "war to end war" were not pursuing a visionary dream.

The League of Nations, the World Court, the 

Briand-Kellogg Peace Pact, the Locarno Trea ty, the various international agreements toward disarmament all bear witness that their faith was not sterile but fruitful.

More reassuring even than these structures designed to offer insurance against recurring wars is the general will to peace in the hearts and minds of men and women. On every hand they are speaking and thinking and writing of peace. When war veterans gather together they talk not of war, but of peace. The utterances of high officers in our forces of national defense reflect the ideal of preparedness for peace, not preparedness for war. Statesmen, publicists, educators, right-thinking men everywhere are planning and working for peace. 

During this week the Chief Executive of the United States called on the people of the nation to "clothe faith and idealism with action" in the interests of peace, and the Commander-in- 

Chief of the American Expeditionary forces, speaking in the land where he served so ably, reminded Americans of their solemn duty "to find a way whereby nations may settle their differences other than by force." 

The cry "give peace in our time" is heard on every side. It reflects not the isolated enthusiasm of individual men, which has always existed, but rather a unified coordinated effort, without the stress of crisis or the stimulus of catastrophe to spur it on, moving steadily, hopefully, and inevitably to an attainable goal. 

Education, commerce, science and invention, statesmanship-these are only a few of the forces which are now carrying on the war to end war. The sacrifice of the brave men whom we honor today has thus set in motion forces which will carry their cause to victory. The torch thrown from the dying hands of those whose names are recorded upon these walls has been caught and will be carried proudly forward by those who live and work and learn within these walls.

Let us dedicate this living memorial to the living memory of the immortal dead by rededicating ourselves to their cause, which is also our cause, having not only on our lips but in our hearts the earnest prayer that our footsteps may be guided in the paths of peace. PPPPPT! [ 40 ]

The Uses of the Memorial Union 'HE use of the Memorial Union becomes greater and more representative of the entire College community as the months go by. A member of the College faculty, not an alumnus of the institution, but holding degrees from two great Middle Western universities, to each of which he has made contributions as an alumnus, sent in the last of his installments on a generous pledge to the Union recently with the following comment: "I feel impelled to express my appreciation of the increasing value of the Union to the campus and the College. Observing the uses to which the building is put from week to week around the year, not only on such occasions as homecoming and commencement when large numbers of visitors are on the campus, but through the ordinary round of daily student events, one wonders repeatedly how the College ever got along without such a campus center as the Union affords. As a matter of fact, of course, the Union makes possible the development of a campus spirit that would be impossible without such a center. The value of the building as a unifying agency will increase in future years."

Suppose We Had Lost the War?

By E. C. Allworth 

(Armistice Day address over the Radio, KOAC, November 11, 1929)

T's hard to believe that it was eleven years ago today the World War ended. Armistice day has a different meaning to nearly every one, in keeping with his experience. Those of us who were at the front remember well the day, and recall exactly what we were doing eleven years ago at this exact time. 

You who were at home, had your own peculiar emotions, as fathers, mothers or friends of soldiers. So the immediate mental effect of the signing of the Armistice on each of us depended upon our individual situations at that moment, and our recollections of it are colored in the same way. 

I have read and heard of the great celebrations and demonstrations staged throughout this country; of the hilarity and rejoicing that lasted throughout the day and night. After the long, strained concentration of effort on the part of the people supporting the armies and worrying about their loved ones, there surely was much occasion to rejoice. 

When I turn my thoughts back to the hour of 11 o'clock on November 11, 1918, I find that

I cannot stop there, but must keep tracing the days back to October 11, a month earlier, when my outfit entered the Meuse-Argonne battle. 

The last 30 days of the War, indeed, seemed as one long day, since there was no breaking away from that constant drive against the enemy that proved to be the last. The things that flit across my mind in retrospect may give you an insight into the Armistice Day feeling of a man at the front: 

The morning of October eleventh, when we took our positions, after marching all night, began a period of tremendous and harrowing activity. I see it all as in a troubled dream-a phantasmagoria of tumult and upheaval. Nothing is clear in its entirety; but the lightning flashes of tragic events that burst from hour to hour throughout those closing days of the war will never be effaced from my memory. They are as vivid today as they were eleven years agothe terrific shell fire we took all day-the boy in the sixth squad blown to smithereens by a direct shell hit-the wild look on the faces of the boys from the other companies as they filtered back from their all-day struggle to capture the woods in front of us. That Italian boy in the second squad calling: "Captain, now look what they've done to me," when it seemed no voice could be left in a body blown so full of holes; the lack of stretcher bearers within reach; darkness and a nervous, watchful night; daylight in a heavy fog; ordered back to reorganize for a new attack. 

We go over the top the next morning, across a field alive with bursting shells and crackling machine gun bullets; we gain the woods full of smarting high explosive powder; the Germans are driven back from the edge by this time; our dead of the day before are lying scattered among the trees; the boy I am talking to crumples with a bullet through his head.

We push on till night-dig in, determined to hold the ground is too rocky. An Austrian fieldpiece shoots pointblank at us all night; we [ 54 ]

"Dedicated to the service and inspiration of the living, and to the memory of our immortal dead.

" [ 55 ]

have only a handful of men left. Morning comes at last. We are organized to continue the attack. The German machine guns glisten opposite us. Six men pile up like dead flies, trying to cross the line of fire of one machine gun; can't make any headway with so few men.

Dig in again. Rain fills up the holes and we lie in water; night again, the fourth with no sleep and little food.

The other brigade relieves us and carries on. 

We gather up remnants of our outfit, get some new men and in turn relieve them. We hunt for a place to cross the Meuse River. Our Second 

Battalion tries it November fourth but is badly shot up. We get orders to cross-are worn out when we reach the bluffs over the river but scramble down to the bottom and wade through marshes, waist deep-cold, like it is here now.

All is quiet. We steal across the engineers footbridge and reach the canal on the other side of the river; Germans, suspicious, and firing constantly, puncture pontoons across canal, and the bridge sinks, leaving part of our outfit on the German side alone. A soldier tries to return and sinks into the canal loaded with equipment; screams for help, bringing deluge of machine gun bullets; darkness begins to break; bullets start to fly. They fly faster until all is an inferno. Germans running along skyline to reinforce those in front of us; the deadening fire increases; Germans start running back along the skyline but many do not get away; plenty of prisoners; one of the new men, with a deep wound in his leg, wondering if he should go back. The boy from New Jersey with a bullet through his head-he had just heard of a new baby boy at home. The new officer only three weeks out of New York sticks his head up to look around and gets his. And so it went on until the momentous eleventh of November.

Through mud and water, worn with strain and lack of sleep, tired from physical exertion, weak from dysentery, praying for relief but pushing on and on-mud deeper. The 10th we marched all day through thick mud nearly to the knees. Brush too thick to get out of beaten path. Men fell exhausted, stuck, thrown aside to come on when they could. Early in the night two men slipped into the brush and put bullets through their hands, not alone taking the wound but courtmartial and disgrace for a chance at relief from the strain.

Later that night we came up to the new German line of defense eleven miles back behind the Meuse. We rested until nine the morning of the eleventh. Then prepared to attack the 

Prussian Machine Gun Battalion that had been left to stop us. That ceaseless movement toward the enemy's line was under way again. 

A runner came up from regimental headquarters with news of the Armistice. A few spoken commands stopped the advance. There were no bugles; a man or two yelled; then all was quiet again, for we must be on our guard since we had heard many rumors before. We could not relax entirely, for an armistice is only a temporary cessation of hostilities and we had no details about the agreement signed on the eleventh. No news of the outside world had reached us, moreover, so knew nothing of the general conditions. We were much like the safety and his opponent in a football game when he lets a punt strike the ground. They both follow the ball along ready to grab the other fellow, if he starts anything. Instead of seeking rest, we had to move the night of the eleventh to put ourselves in a better position to renew the attack, should the negotiations fail of permanent peace. Thank God they did not fail! 

How thankful we are for this wonderful period of peace. As far as we personally are concerned it was well worth the struggle, but we cannot speak so blithely for those we left on the other side. They paid the price for our peace of today. Did you ever contemplate what our situation today would be if we had lost the 

War? Your imagination is as good as mine, but

I am convinced our beautiful peace would be of quite a different nature.

Great strides are being made today by the foremost statesmen of the world toward permanent peace, but such a move must proceed with caution and deliberation lest we find ourselves "out on a limb" as we were twelve years ago, our only way to safety the sacrifice of those thousands of our youth.

I can truly say that more than any other group of citizens the men of the American Legion desire permanent peace. We hope that the progress now being made toward that end by the nations of the world will reach the pinnacle of everlasting permanence. But until such a time arrives we do not propose to forget our old buddies. Only an honorable peace, carrying with it the freedom and liberty for which our forefathers contended, will be acceptable to America, and such a peace must be fought for, if all other methods fail.
