January 18, 1959 Subject: Conflicts between Elected Attorneys General and Elected Pistrict Attorneys 

On December 17, 1958, Representative Sumner Z. Kaplan of brookline, Clerk of the Committee on the Judiciary, wrote asking the Legislative Research Bureau for information as to "certain problems that might possibly arise where a conflict develops in the handling of a case between an independently elected attorney general and an independently elected district attorney". 

Historical Background 

The question posed is interesting both from the historical and the legal point of view. The first attorney general of England, appointed in the year 1472 or thereabouts, was proclaimed to be the chief legal officer and legal adviser to the Crown in all civil and criminal matters. During the period of American colonization, the office of attorney general was brought to these shores by the Crown charters, which were the governing laws of the colonies. 

With the steady growth of the colonies, both in population and area, the attorneys general could not individually handle all the Crown's legal business. 

Accordingly, they began to appoint assistants in the more thriving and populous localities. 

After the American Revolution, the states continued the same practices and procedures. Usually, the governors appointed the attorneys general, and the latter appointed local prosecutors. However, this procedure was modified as new state constitutions and statutes were adopted. 

Today, the attorneys general are elected in 42 states. The few exceptions occur in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Wyoming, where those officials are 925201

Local prosecutors (district attorneys) are elected in 44 states. In 

Connecticut they are appointed by judges of the superior courts, and in New Jersey by the governor with the consent of the Senate. In Rhode Island and Delaware, local prosecutors are Assistant Attorneys General appointed by the Attorneys General 

Areas of Conflict 

The transformation from earlier appointment of attorneys general by the Crown, to (a) attorneys general elected by the people with prosecutors appointed by them, and (b) district attorneys elected by the people, has not been accomplished without strain. Problems have arisen and will continue to arise, all stemming from the inevitable clash of authority between the long established common law powers of attorneys general and the newer constitutional and statutory powers of district attorneys. Apart from several minor points of difference, four important areas of conflict arise which are discussed below.

This power to supervise could be an element of strong control in the hands of the attorney general. However, such has not turned out to be the case. 

Courts invariably have interpreted and construed the power to supervise very cautiously and strictly. For all practical purposes, attorneys general have encountered considerable difficulty in utilizing provisions authorizing supervision.

Conclusions in the United States.

Ancient common law powers of attorneys general exist to a strong degree However, they are not nearly so all-inclusive as in colonial days. They cannot be compared, for instance, to the authority of the attorney general of the United States over federal prosecutors throughout the country. 

In general, the courts have ruled that the common law powers of an attorney general are still applicable today, except where modified or restricted by constitution or statute.

In this connection, it should be pointed out that it is difficult for an attorney general to obtain judicial sanction of his attempts to exercise common law prerogatives. He must convince the court that the common law power does not conflict with constitutional or statutory law. Such attempts are usually bitterly contested by district attorneys who are normally powerful political figures and have the sympathy and backing of many who believe that criminal prosecution is the responsibility of local officials.
