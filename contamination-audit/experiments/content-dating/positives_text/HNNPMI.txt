CHAPTER I 

Tel-el-Dar D-R-R-R-B. 

Across the vast expanse of trackless sands, silver now under the light of the moon, there re-echoed the short, sharp thump of an Arab signal drum. 

Remote, mysterious, the strongest of desert music, it seemed to speak of something barbaric, compelling, age-long as the sands themselves. 

Then from the Hotel Mirage, a blaze of light on the edge of the desert, there broke forth a wild riot of modern jazz, to the tune of which the cosmopolitan crowd thronging the oasis town of Tel-el-Dar, the new fashion centre of North Africa, danced, flirted and gossiped the glamorous night away. 

Invalids in search of health, old people in search of sunshine, young people in search of pleasure; girls and widows in search of husbands, grass-widows and bored wives in search of adventure, effete young men of the lounge lizard type in search of rich

women, and rich women of the exotic type in search of the imaginary sheiks of romantic fiction; Eastern potentates in gorgeous dresses and turbans, French

Officers from the Garrison in their mess uniforms, civilians in evening dress, and women in Parisian gowns, all vied in a moving kaleidoscope of colour past the wide open, windows leading on to the verandah, beyond which stretched the desertalmost as light as day under the African moon. "I am dancing with tears in my eyes, Because the girl in my arms isn't you," the leader of the orchestra was singing in a sweet fruity tenor.

D-r-r-r-b went the drum. 

A tall dark man standing in a doorway gave an expressive gesture. "Listen to that. It's typical. We are on the borderland out here where civilization and barbarism meet, the thin line dividing East and West. Yet how many of these fools know?"

Then the somewhat bored expression which marred the clear-cut beauty of his olive-skinned face changed to one of interest, as from the far end of the ballroom a girl came walking slowly, smoking a minute cigarette. A girl who moved with a light, swinging movement from the hips that was a miracle of grace, and whose figure, under the lines of her daringly tight rose and silver gown, was slender as that of a boy, yet perfectly formed. A girl whose

dark hair was cut in the severest of Eton crops, but from which little curls escaped at the neck and temples; whose brown eyes were artificially darkened, and her mouth a sudden splash of crimson against the ivory of her skin. "Who is she?" Lady Belhurst, a red-faced portly dowager, asked the question. Accompanied by 

Winifred, Daisy and Maude, three uninspiring daughters, whom she exploited each year in every town where men abounded, she had just arrived in the Oasis, having tracked Gustave de Fontenay, so ill-natured folks said, from Algiers to Biskra, and from Biskra to Tel-el-Dar, on the strength of his once having danced twice with Maude, her youngest hope, who persisted in a romantic and totally unrequited attachment to him. 

He did not reply, and it was Mrs. Vane Smith who answered the question. "The new girl gigolette, professional dancer for the season here," she murmured. 

Mrs. Vane Smith was a small spare woman, who resembled nothing so much as a thin-skinned lemon which has been squeezed and left out to dry under too many tropical suns. She was of the type which while professing democracy, secretly worships a title, and though she hated the Dowager like poison, felt it worth while to keep in with her. "I shall speak to the Management about it," boomed Lady Belhurst after a moment's silence. "About what, dear?"

Mrs. Vane Smith leaned back in her chair, fanning herself and watching the Dowager's enraged face from out of half-closed eyes. There had been dreams in that quarter too, she knew, regarding

Indignation getting the better of self-control, Lady Belhurst lost her temper. "I shall certainly complain to the Management,"

she averred, and with an angry sweep of her voluminous garments, she rose and sailed away. Meanwhile Gustave de Fontenay had reached the girl who was the object of their interest, and pushing his way through the crowd of men surrounding her, bowed, with the arrogant grace that was characteristic of him, as he murmured easily: "Your dancing and mine are, I think, a sufficient introduction, Mademoiselle. May I have the honour?" In his eyes was that almost insolent look of admiration which for many women held an irresistible attraction. But of these, Naomi Morrison was not one. She had heard of this man and of his successes with women, and was determined that he should be taught a lesson. "Pardon me, but it is not sufficient." 

Her eyes expressing haughty surprise, her brows arched delicately, the girl opened a giant peacock's feather fan and waved it to and fro. 

Gustave de Fontenay's breath hissed sharply, while the crimson blood swept his face. Opposition was new to him, and insult a thing his proud spirit could ill brook. Partly French and partly it was said of Arab blood, he was, besides being a multimillionaire, a great power in North Africa, where so influential was he in Eastern affairs that he went by the name of "The Eyes of the Desert," and the Embassies had been told to watch him well. So far, however, he had shown no signs of wishing to

embroil himself in Eastern politics, and his influence with the tribes appeared exemplary. Educated at 

Eton and Oxford, most of his later youth had been spent in Paris and on the Continent, and to-day, in North Africa at thirty-five, he was nothing if not an interesting personality, though a somewhat mysterious one. The finest rider and fencer that side of the East, he was also the best shot, while as a dancer he was without equal, and his tall, graceful figure the admiration of every ballroom that he entered. Dancing was to him both a recreation and a hobby, and to his magnificent villa of El Touran on the edge of the desert, came the world's most beautiful dancers, there to perform at the lavish entertainments he gave, about which many wild tales were told, or to help charm away some of the incurable boredom and soulweariness with which most of his leisure hours were cursed. "Your reason?" he demanded after a moment's silence. "I see no necessity to give you one."

Her cool insolence matched his, and the little sparkling devils in his red-brown eyes changed to a glint of real anger. Hitherto, despite his reputation, women in his life had meant but little to Gustave de Fontenay, though partly on account of his wealth, and partly because of a certain dare-devil, somewhat brutal charm which he possessed, they had flung themselves at him, until his whole attitude

towards them told of a chase that had always been too easy.

He clicked his heels together now, as he bowed military fashion, saying with delicate irony: "Your pardon, Mademoiselle, I understood that you were the professional dancer here, paid to dance with such of the visitors as should demand your services." 

His tone was like a whip and like a whip her answer flung back at him. "Even as a paid professional, I reserve my right to refuse to dance with an Oriental."

Even before the words were spoken Naomi regretted them, and she felt her own face whiten, while the glance of de Fontenay was like a scimitar drawn under an African sun. 

How rude she had been, and yet the man's assurance had maddened her. She had seen him watching her only that morning when, surrounded by her usual crowd of admiring men, she had arrived back from her morning ride in the desert, and something in his eyes had annoyed her then, making her conscious of the boyish riding-costume which revealed so plainly the long lines of her slim figure. 

But her refusal to dance with him was an unfair one, and she knew it. Even if Arab blood did run in his veins, de Fontenay was a gentleman and an aristocrat to his finger-tips, and by every law of good breeding she had been wrong to insult him.

But before she could speak again to utter the

half-formed words of apology which rose to her lips, he had turned on his heel. "So be it, Mademoiselle, I shall remember." The words in their quaint un-English phrasing were flung back at her as he walked away, and she sank back upon a sofa, bitterly ashamed. 

What would he do? Complain to the Management and get her dismissed? That would be easy, for it was no secret that it was de Fontenay's money which had made this Oasis town the latest fashion and health resort of central Europe, but something told Naomi that if ever de Fontenay should stoop to revenge himself for the insult she had flung at him, it would be of a nature far less trivial and more far-reaching than that.

CHAPTER II 

Ayesha the Dancer

NAOMI MORRISON's story was a curious one. Her mother had died in her infancy, and as soon as she left school, she had gone out to India to keep home for her father, who was in command of a crack cavalry regiment. And there, the acknowledged belle of every station where they were quartered, she had ridden, danced and laughed her life away, a veritable little queen. Then had come the smash. The father she adored had been killed playing polo, and when his affairs came to be settled, it was discovered that during the last years of his life, he had been speculating wildly, so that his only child was thrown upon the world penniless, excepting for a tiny pension, quite insufficient to live upon. 

Thus, from the gay, glamorous life of a military station, Naomi came home to England to face the bitter struggle for existence, among the huge army of working girls in the great Metropolis. And there she had all but starved. Relations, the few she possessed, either avoided her or turned the cold shoulder as soon as they realized her position, 16

while friends soon forgot or else never knew of the terrible straits she was placed in, until one day, she met by chance one more kindly, who obtained for her the position of instructress in a well-known dancing academy. There she was noticed by a man interested in a big syndicate of hotels, who, impressed by her striking appearance and her beautiful dancing, had offered her the post of professional dancer for the season at Tel-el-Dar. 

It had been a wonderful opportunity which Naomi had gratefully accepted, and she had proved an undoubted success. The new girl "gigolette" became the rage, and the slight air of hauteur which was in such flagrant contradiction to her somewhat daring appearance, only helped to add to her attractions.

From the first moment she set foot in the town of Tel-el-Dar, men had admired, women had envied, and one man had loved her. Loved her from the first moment he had seen her, to his own utter astonishment, and to the dismay of all the chinwaggers and gossip-mongers of the Oasis.

Alan Carstairs, who held a high official position there, attached to one of the Legations, who was a member of an ultra-snobbish old-fashioned family, and would one day inherit a title. Alan Carstairs, who was the soul of convention, and was supposed to be indifferent to women-in love with a gigolette! 

A quiet, grave-faced man, his face turned a slow

crimson under its skin tanned by many Eastern suns as his eyes rested for a moment upon the girl who at the farther end of the ballroom stood receiving, with brilliant boredom the assiduous court paid to her. He knew that already this love of his was on every lip, a subject of conversation for teatables, and betting at the clubs, and he wished irritably that people would mind their own business, even while he wondered like Lady Belhurst a little nervously, what would Lady Alicia say! The old lady would, he knew, be quite capable of cutting him off without a cent, and yet for the first time in his life, in which until now, work and some sport had sufficed him, he felt his hitherto somewhat unemotional nature stirred to its depths. It seemed as if a faint, hot breeze from the desert wafted in like a warning whisper, telling him that here at last had come experience, romance, pain, and joy perhaps unspeakable, into his quiet life from the depths of a girl's dark laughing eyes.

For a moment his keen gaze, with the somewhat tired lines underneath which lent such character to his face, rested upon Naomi thoughtfully, and then he crossed the ballroom and joined her. 

She hailed him playfully. "Look at my new dance."

She took a few steps as she spoke, while the men clapped and some of the women looked down their noses. Then, seeing the vexed expression on his face, she said mischievously:

"What's the matter?" "I wish you wouldn't," he said a trifle aggrievedly, for he had all the conventional Englishman's dislike of anything savouring of the ostentatious among his women-folk, and during the performance of that dance, which he regarded as an exhibition in a public place, he had been haunted by a visionary marchpast of his entire family, led by the Lady Alicia, who with arched brows and upraised hands regarded that scanty gown, that provocative face, painted mouth, and little insolent cigarette, and summarized them all up in those two fatal words, "bad form." "Wouldn't what?" She linked her arm in his as, the cynosure of all eyes, they walked away. "Wouldn't make yourself so conspicuous. And, Naomi, why will you 'make up' quite so much?

It may be becoming, but a man doesn't like it with -well, his own women-folk." "But I'm not of your own 'women-folk.'” Her tone hid a note of bitterness. "You forget I am just what they call a girl 'gigolette.' I must be conspicuous, I must make up, I must be modern: it is part of my stock in trade." "You seem to forget," he said a trifle stiffly, "that you are also engaged to me."

Naomi was silent for a moment. She had promised to marry Alan Carstairs, partly perhaps from astonishment, and partly because she really liked him. He was so essentially English, and so quiet and well-bred, and her two years of being buffeted

by the world had taught her to value the security of the position he had offered her. 

Besides, her whispered engagement had been a great triumph for her in the Oasis, and Naomi would have been more than human if she had not felt a little thrill of elation at getting even with some of the women who had snubbed her. But tonight a little chill of fear touched her like a cold hand. Was gratified vanity and ambition, even when allied to genuine liking and respect, sufficient to make a happy marriage? Would she ever "get on" with a man who, for all his excellent qualities, was so prone to bow to the conventions, and to wonder, "what would people think?" And his family? Already she was a little tired of hearing about his family, which in her own mind she dubbed flippantly as "good old British stodge." "Don't let us quarrel," she said at length. “I have just had quite a scene with Captain de Fontenay. I'm afraid I was awfully rude to him." 

Carstairs looked grave as she told him what had occurred. "Were you quite wise?" he asked. "Why not? A few rebuffs will not hurt de Fontenay. Women have spoilt him dreadfully." "All the same, I wish you had not offended him," 

Carstairs said. "These Easterns are a strange race, and he has great power out here." "What could he do?" Naomi asked carelessly.

They had wandered into the garden, where the scent of the flowers was like incense and from

somewhere near by the faint sweet notes of a guitar sounded. Out across the far horizon line, the drum was beating. "One never knows," Carstairs replied. "Things are unsettled out here, and at any moment trouble might arise with the tribes. If it did, much might depend upon him." "Yes, there is something about him very strong, very powerful," Naomi answered thoughtfully. "Do you hear that drum out there? It reminds me of him. It is like the desert which calls and calls." "Nonsense," he said practically. "The call of the desert only exists in the minds of lady novelists." "Oh no, it doesn't." The girl's face was very grave. "I feel it strongly sometimes, especially when the drums beat, and I know one day I shall answer it and go."

And even as she spoke, there re-echoed the sound of that drum again. Imperative, insistent, compelling, it drowned the love song of the guitar, and even the noisy jazz from the band, filling the world like a giant heart-beat. 

The next moment came a sudden blare of music from the ballroom, the floor cleared as if by magic, the lights lowered, and the guests assembled around the walls. Guitars throbbed, stringed instruments wailed, as by some subtle device the lights changed from jade to peacock, orange to primrose and sapphire, and lastly to a rosy hue supposed to

represent the dawn. Then enveloped in a veil of cloudy blue through which her kohl-darkened eyes shone mysteriously, a girl came, her slight body swaying to the barbaric music as a sapling under a storm against her own native hills.

Her dance was a triumph of artistry, as unconscious as it was inborn. A child of sorrow, it was the story of her life expressed in movement, a message of tragedy in every gesture and sobbing breath, until at last with a clash of cymbals that wild hill music stopped, and she stood panting slightly, her meek head bent to the storm of applause which burst forth, her little hands outstretched as if in mute pleading that she might be allowed to depart. 

She had not long to wait. The lights flared once more, the band struck up again, and the ballroom began to fill again, while amid the shadows, the little dancer with her great pathetic eyes, her swaying arms with their symbolic slave bangles, and her movements like a breeze upon the mountains, sat forgotten by the jazzing, glittering crowd.

A Turk in a red fez came over to where she sat, and spoke to her quickly and sternly. "Come, your dance is over. We will depart."

She was about to follow wearily with the same beaten submission characteristic of her every movement, and her race, when de Fontenay, who had been watching her dance from a doorway, crossed over to them. "Another dancer ?" he asked carelessly.

De Fontenay nodded curtly and swinging through the doors leading into the ballroom, went on his confident careless way, and the loud, broken sobbing of the little dancer followed him, like a note of ill omen which he heeded not.

She must be allowed to leave to-morrow." "Indeed." He removed the cigar from his lips. "May I ask by what right you speak?" "By the right of common humanity." "And if I do not choose to recognize such a right?"

She drew herself up to her full height, and faced him, and the clash of their wills was like bared steel. "Then I shall ask the authorities to step in." 

He laughed. "My dear little lady, I am afraid you do not realize the position. At El Touran I

come under no law but desert law, and there this 

Turkish gentleman and charge I shall consider under my protection. In other words, I can keep the girl for as long as I like."

The little dancer heard, and burst once more into a storm of sobbing. "My lover will die," she wailed in her pitiful piping voice. "Already he is ill, and they will surely kill him. And I have danced to save him, danced until I can dance no more."

A second later, in a stream of passionate words, the whole tragic story was poured out. 

The attack of the Turks on the Armenians, the massacre of the girl's family, her capture, and the horror of the long forced march of the prisoners through the mountains, where some fell out and were killed by their brutal captors, others died by the way, and many went mad. 

Finally, of her exemption from death, or the worse fate of a Turkish harem, when it was found that she could dance, since when she had travelled across Europe with her master, from city to city, continent to continent, dancing for the miserable pittance which he gave her as a protection to himself against any law to which she might appeal, though she was too crushed and broken, a child in a strange land, to have done so, even had she known of one. 

Out of this, through God knows what sacrifice, toil and agony, she had saved sufficient money for her passage home, her release, and her lover's ran-

som, only to meet with this blow to all her hopes in the hour of their fruition.

Naomi met the inscrutable eyes of de Fontenay as the pitiful story died at last to silence. "You hear?" she said accusingly. "Is it true, as people say, that you have no heart?" 

His mouth shone like a streak of red in his pale face as he answered bitterly: "A soul, perhaps, but no heart, Mademoiselle; remember I am an Oriental." 

She shrank back at his words, feeling her insult to him of an hour ago already flung back at her. 

But for it, she might have saved this girl now, might have touched something finer and nobler in his nature, but between them that insult must ever stand now, an insuperable barrier.

Acting upon a sudden impulse she went up to him and said gently, "I apologize for being rude this evening; it was unpardonable. Will you dance with me now? A waltz is just beginning.' 99 

For a moment he gazed at her, in his eyes that remote mysterious look which she was beginning to know, then he said briefly: "Very well." 

The orchestra was wailing out a dreamy melody as they entered the ballroom, and a moment later, with his arms scarcely touching, yet perfectly enfolding her, Naomi felt herself swept away as if upon clouds of light. 

Never had she dreamed that a dance could be so divine as they moved together in perfect harmony

of motion, and the music sang of tropical nights made for love when the breeze passed sighing through the palm-trees. Of passionate nights full of joy and happiness unspeakable, of nights such as are known only to true lovers, when two hearts beat as one and joy is complete. She felt herself swooning, almost falling, it was his arms that held her now; felt herself taken from the crowded ballroom to a deserted ante-room; felt herself caught in strong arms, fierce kisses on her lips; felt herself swept by a passion wild and untamed as the desert winds. "As the sands are blown from the far, far South, Even so am I, when swept by thy desire."

CHAPTER XXV 

“El mak tub mak tub” (What is written, is written)

THE sun had not long risen the following morning when Naomi looked out towards the desert, whence de Fontenay all those weeks ago had come to her, whither now he had gone. The desert had taken him once again, would it ever give him back? 

Even as amid the storm of battle, his father had ridden out of the far South, so into the battle his son had gone, back to it again. At the call of a woman had his father come, a woman had sent forth his father's son. The fatalism of it all seemed inevitable. 

Dry-eyed, she had watched him ride out, knowing that the time had come for her promise to the old sheik to be fulfilled, but now as her gaze searched that undulating waste of sand, shimmering in the heat, a sudden mist veiled it for a moment from her sight. Oh, if she could only know that he still lived, and that he thought of her. 

She had waited all night for news of either him or Carstairs, had waited in breathless suspense with

Lady Alicia, and now with a tinge of her old reck- 249

less spirit of adventure, she was half thinking of going in search of him.

Then, suddenly, sentinel upon the far horizon, silhouetted against the azure sky, she saw him seated upon his horse. He might only have been a wandering tribesman, for it was too far away to distinguish more than the outline of his form, but she would have known him amongst a thousand men, and bent her head low in a voiceless prayer of gratitude.

There were two roads at the point where she sat her horse. One leading into the desert, the other back to Tel-el-Dar, and for a moment, she was tempted to forget her promise, and flinging all but her love to the winds, to ride out to him, while a little Sahara breeze murmured across the sands, and it seemed to her in that moment, fraught with intensity, as if, motionless out there, he was waiting for her to choose. 

Then suddenly, she saw his scimitar flash in the sunlight as he held it high above his head in greeting, and she knew that he had signalled to her in Arabic, the words to a loved woman, when on the eve of battle: "My sword is at thy feet.” 

A moment only she hesitated, then she stood up high in her stirrups, and in answer flung her arms wide in a gesture of utter surrender and farewell, while her voice broke over the words, customary of the country, in its native tongue :

"Go with God."

And it seemed to her as if that little Sahara breeze took them up and bore them to him on swift wings, and that he understood, for he bowed his head and lowered his arm. The next moment, the desert hid him from sight.

Her horse's hoofs made no sound upon the soft sand as she turned back upon the road leading to Tel-el-Dar, and it was noon when she reached the oasis and the cry was rising from mosque and minaret. "Allah, il Allah." ("There is no God but Allah.") 

And ere she entered the gates, she turned her face towards the desert once more, bowing in prayer as an Eastern woman. 

Whatever came, she knew that her heart would always lie out there, linked for ever with that of the man who had loved her with a wild, lawless love that had gone nigh to plunging North Africa into war for her sake. That in her dreams she would often wander once again in the house on the edge of the desert wherein that other European woman had lived all those years ago, and whose life story was told in those words written at the foot of her picture, which de Fontenay had shown her. "Paradise lies at the feet of the Mothers." 

Naomi turned and bowed her head towards the desert once more.

"Allah, il Allah. There is no God but Allah," she whispered. "Go with God."
