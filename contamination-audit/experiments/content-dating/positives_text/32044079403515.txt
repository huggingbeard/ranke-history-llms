JOHN WOOLMAN 

1720-1920 

Two Hundred Years Ago 

"The child was born beside the Delaware Who in the power a holy purpose lends Guided his people unto nobler ends 

And left them worthier of the name of Friends.' 

-Whittier's Pennsylvania Pilgrim. 

And the "child" not only thus guided "his people," the Religious Society of Friends, but helped also to focus the attention of other thoughtful men at home and abroad on the crime of negro slavery. Nor is this all. 

From his early manhood his life was an awakening cry against oppression in various forms. Indeed, so high did he rank the obligation to be just to the laborer that he made it a prime duty of every Christian: "To labor for a perfect redemption from the spirit of oppression is the great business of the whole family of Christ Jesus in the world."

These were the words of that pioneer in the cause of social justice, John Woolman. 

The "child born beside the Delaware," or, more precisely, beside Rancocas Creek, 3

which flows into the Delaware River, was a country boy living on his father's "plantation" of two hundred acres in Burlington 

County, New Jersey. All around lay rich and level lands peopled by other Friends, industrious farmers with their negro slaves.

In his Journal, started in his thirty-sixth year, his outward life is introduced mainly to give a setting to his religious experience and reflections. But we know that he had worthy Christian parents, John and Elizabeth

Woolman, who early taught him to read and sent him to school. In his happy rural home there were many brothers and sisters-thirteen in all-mostly with Bible names, John coming fourth, the eldest boy. On First-days. (Sundays), they had a little family Bible school, the children reading aloud, turn about. way,

He tells us how early he began to long for that purity which so strongly marked his writings and actions: "As I went from school one day I remember, while my companions went to play by the 

I went forward out of sight and, sitting down, I 

read the 22nd Chapter of Revelations: 'He showed me a pure river of water of life, clear as crystal, proceeding out of the throne of God and of the Lamb,' etc., and in reading it my mind was drawn to seek after that pure habitation, which I then believed God had prepared for his servants. 

The place

where I sat and the sweetness that attended my mind remain fresh in my memory." 

Another incident of his childhood, which shows his wrong-doing, but also that "the child is father of the man," he narrates with great simplicity and humility: "Once going to a neighbor's house, I saw on the way a robin sitting on her nest, and as I came near she went off, but having young ones, flew about and with many cries expressed her concern for them. I stood and threw stones at her till, one striking her, she fell down dead. At first I was pleased with the exploit, but after a few minutes was seized with horror for having in a sportive way killed an innocent creature while she was careful for her young. 

I beheld her lying dead and thought those young ones for which she was careful must now perish for want of their dam to nourish them; and after some painful considerations on the subject I climbed up the tree, took all the young birds and killed them, supposing that better than to leave them to pine away and die miserably; and believed in this case that Scripture proverb was fulfilled, 'The tender mercies of the wicked are cruel.' I then went on my errand, but for some hours could think of little else but the cruelties I had committed, and was much troubled." This was he who in later days refused to ride behind overtaxed stage horses,

who pitied the oxen lashed to make them draw their loads, and chickens suffering in the long sea voyages.

During his youth there were some lapses in his zeal for holiness, for he says at that time his "chief study was to exceed in vanity and promote mirth." He was evidently fond of society and had an active imagination. It took time for him to gain a proper sense of life's true purpose and to proportion aright its various interests. Happily that Voice he so early heard, the Love that would not let him go, gave him an uneasy conscience for misused time and talents. It gave him, too, the controlling conviction recorded in his Journal that "True religion consisted in an inward life wherein the heart doth love and reverence God, the Creator, and learns to exercise true justice and goodness not only toward all men, but also toward the brute creatures.' 99 While he says enough to show his Christian belief, he deals little with doctrines and preferred to judge of religion by its fruits. "I found no narrowness respecting sects and opinions, but believed that sincere, uprighthearted people in every society who truly love God were accepted of him."

But to return to the life experience of this young man. God's love had arrested him in his wanderings and he evidently felt that he

had reached a turning-point in his career. 

From this crisis onward he bent his heart and soul and mind to the working out of his mission, probably not fully conscious of the distinct ends to which he should attain, but as "ever in his Great Taskmaster's eye."

All this time he was still on the "plantation," working for his parents by day and "improving" himself in winter evenings. But there were six other boys at home, and so before he was twenty-one he turned his feet to the neighboring village of Mt. Holly, there to sell goods and keep books for one who was a shopkeeper, a tailor and a baker all in one. 

John soon learned the tailor trade and set up in that business for himself, adding a little shop to increase his gains.

According to his modest estimate he was successful; "the way to a large business appeared open," he writes, and it may here be said of him that he always had enough of this world's goods, although on principle he lived frugally. 

John Woolman was not as other men, however, and we wonder whether any of us in this money-getting age can profit by his example soon to be cited; can realize all that it would mean to do as he did. After fifteen years of trading he found the increase of his cares became a burden. "I believed the Truth did not require me to engage much

Then I lessened my in cumbering affairs. outward business, and as I had opportunity told my customers of my intentions that they might consider what shop to turn to, and in a while I wholly laid down merchandize and followed my trade as a tailor by myself, having no apprentice. I also had a nursery of apple trees in which I employed some of my time in hoeing, grafting, trimming and inoculating." It was his thought, that "if the leadings of the Spirit were more attended to, more people would be engaged in the sweet employment of husbandry, where labor is agreeable and healthful."

He had, however, and continued to have a variety of occupations besides his tailoring and farming. He could make shoes; back of his shop was the meeting-house, where he sometimes taught school; he was a surveyor and a conveyancer; and he wrote wills for the people of the neighborhood. 

But these things were far from engaging all his attention. When about twenty-two years of age he appeared as a witness for 

Christ, a gospel minister, who he said should be as "a trumpet through which the Lord speaks to His people." In following this calling, journeys were later taken by him to the Southern or to the Northern Colonies.

The Indians about Wyoming Valley were visited, although at that time some of these

tribes were on the war path and traveling was dangerous thereaway. But they did him. no harm. In 1749 he married “a well-inclined damsel," Sarah Ellis. A little daughter, Mary, cheered their peaceful household, and for a time a son was with them, but not for long.

All these days, however, he had been pondering the sad and perplexing problem of the oppression of the poor. At this time, i. e., about the middle of the 18th century, while some advanced spirits had branded slavery as anti-Christian, and therefore to be kept clear of, the great mass of people were not so minded. All through the texture of society the roots of slavery had wound their way. 

Climatic conditions made slaves less numerous in the North than in the South, but in all sections they were held by planters and merchants, Friends and non-Friends; as late as 

1800 there were over 12,000 slaves in New Jersey.

In 1742 something decisive happened in that Mt. Holly store where Woolman had so lately entered as an employee. Let him speak for himself: "My employer having a negro woman, sold her and desired me to write a bill of sale, the man being waiting who bought her.

When he visited Virginia or the Carolinas on a religious mission, the harm of the system to both slaves and slave-owners was often surging through his mind, and he had to speak in public or in private according to his vision of the Truth. He noted the lack of care for the negro's mental, moral, social and physical state. And yet, he said, "These are the people by whose labor the other inhabitants are in great measure supported, and many of them in the luxuries of life. These are the people who have made no agreement to serve us and who have not forfeited their liberty that we know of. These are the souls for whom Christ died and for our conduct 11

toward them we must answer before Him who is no respecter of persons."

He felt uneasy with accepting hospitality from those who held slaves, and left money to pay for their services; a delicate matter, it would seem, "but few" (slave-holders), he says, “manifested any resentment at the offer." Truly some heavenly Power softened their hearts as they listened to his gentle speech, appealing to their understanding and conscience.

In the North as well, and amongst his own sect, there was work, hard and close, to be done. But Friends yielded in time. 

In 1776-note the date Philadelphia

Yearly Meeting declared its independence from the system. Other Yearly Meetings took the same course and before the close of John Woolman's century, though not before the close of his life, slavery amongst Friends in the United States was no more. 

The waves of Woolman's anti-slavery influence are traced by Whittier to later agitation in the United States, and to successful work in the French and British possessions in the West Indies, to such work in Russia, and, in fact, "wherever a step in the direction of emancipation was taken in this country or in Europe."

But Woolman's sympathy with the oppressed did not stop with the negro slave. 12

Any one who to secure things necessary had to work beyond his strength and became a mere drudge excited his pity. 

Of modern Socialism he knew nothing. 

Nor did he seek a universal leveling system. 

For says he, "By the agreement and contract of our predecessors and by our own doings some enjoy a much greater share of this world than others; and, while these possessions are faithfully improved for the good of the whole, it agrees with equity." He did seek, however, to know that his hands were free from unlawful gain. "Have none of my fellowcreatures an equitable right to any part of what is called mine?" was one of the searching questions he would have each one put to himself. Again he counsels, "It is good for those who live in fulness to cultivate tenderness of heart and to improve every opportunity of being acquainted with the hardships and fatigues of those who labor for their living."

He was fearful that great possessions might include luxuries. That word "luxury" meant to him grave evil. It spelled pride and vanity, selfishness, injustice, tyranny, discord. For said he, "Every degree of luxury of what kind soever hath some connection with unnecessary labor," consequently with hardships. In these luxuries and the love of wealth he beheld also the cause of war. 13

"This is like a chain in which the end of one link encloseth the end of another." First comes the desire of wealth, then the resulting effort, the acquiring of riches; next the defense of these dear possessions; power comes with wealth; corrupt bargains and proceedings are supported; then strife. "So the seeds of war swell and sprout, and grow and become strong." This agrees with the findings of the modern examiner who tells us, "The seeds of war are in our social order." 

During these years John Woolman, while warmly sympathetic toward the poor and humble, had also his associates among the cultured men of the day. John Smith, of 

Burlington, was an intimate friend. Smith married Hannah, a daughter of James Logan, the highly educated secretary of Wm. Penn, and influential for fifty years in Pennsylvania politics. Then there were the Pembertons and Rebecca Jones, of Philadelphia, on his list of friends. With Anthony Benezet, of French descent, a school teacher and an anti-slavery worker, he must have had much in common. These intimacies, besides the internal evidence afforded by the Journal, certainly show that the charge of illiteracy sometimes made regarding Woolman cannot be sustained.

Tributes of literary men to the high char- 14

acter of his writings are not wanting. We recall Charles Lamb's dictum, "Get the writings of John Woolman by heart"; Henry

Crabbe Robinson enthusiastically calls the 

Journal "a perfect gem," "he writes in a style of the most exquisite purity and grace"; 

Wm. Ellery Channing said it was "the sweetest and purest autobiography in the language," while President Eliot, of Harvard, placed it as one of the indispensables on his five-foot bookshelf. Learned or unlearned, Woolman has at least ministered to the tastes of the literati. 

In 1771 his only child, Mary, married 

John Comfort, and her father built for her the brick home now standing at 99 Branch Street, Mt. Holly. It is restored to its original state and is open to guests as the John Woolman Memorial. A visit there may answer the same purpose in warming one's piety as the visit proposed by Dr. Johnson to the ruins of Iona. 

But Woolman was aging though yet in middle life. His tender, sensitive heart bore the burdens and griefs of others; he truly exercised himself to keep a conscience void of offense toward God and toward men; his bodily frame was weak, and he refused outward relief and comfort within easy reach lest he should lose the simplicity and selfdenial that mark the Christian. 15

Weak though he was, he responded to a call of the Spirit for religious service in England, Northern England more particularly.

Accordingly, after making his outward arrangements as one who was not to return, in the summer of 1772 he took passage in the Mary and Elizabeth, bound for London. He believed the simple furnishings of the stuffy steerage were best for him. There he came in contact with the seamen, grieved for their profanity and pitied them for their hardships. "I often," he says, "feel a tenderness of heart for these poor lads, and at times look at them as though they were my children according to the flesh." "I have looked at the present dispensation (i. e., his quarters in the steerage), as a kindness from the 

Great Father of mankind, who in this my floating pilgrimage is in some degree bringing me to feel what many thousands of my fellow-creatures often suffer in a greater degree." His mind reverted to the responsibility of those who seek gain at the expense of such, that they should be "delivered from the entangling expenses of a curious, delicate and luxurious life, and promote the sea-faring life no further than that Spirit which leads. into all truth attends us in our proceedings."

Friends did not at first perceive his true worth, but later forgave some singularities and acknowledged him as "serviceable in the church" and "a man endued with large natural capacity." He traveled through 

Central and Western England, attending meetings, noting the worldliness of the rich and the bread and water diet of the poor; the post boys so exposed in their stage rides on the long winter nights that some were frozen to death. Woolman walked. 

At York he was stricken with smallpox.

Though kindly cared for at the home of Thomas Priestman, he sank under the weight of disease and on the 7th of Tenth Month (October), 1772, his weary but beautiful life closed. Only fifty-two years had been completed, but how much they contained! 

A few days before he died he uttered this remarkable prayer, so in keeping with his character, so descriptive of the deep struggle of his life: "O Lord my God, the amazing horrors of darkness were gathered around me and covered me all over, and I saw no way to go forth. I felt the misery of my fellow-creatures separated from the Divine harmony, and it was heavier than I could bear. I was crushed down under it. I lifted up my hand, I stretched out my arm, but there was none to help me. I looked round about and was amazed. In the depths of 18

misery, O Lord, I remembered that thou art omnipotent, that I had called thee Father.

I felt that I loved thee, and I was made quiet in thy will. I waited for deliverance from thee, and thou hadst pity upon me, when no man could help me. I saw that meekness under suffering was showed to us in the most affecting example of thy Son, and that thou wast teaching me to follow him, and I said, 'Thy will, O Father, be done.' 999 

What message has John Woolman to the world today? Though the negro slavery that vexed his soul is, in the form that he knew it, and in civilized countries a dead and buried issue, oppression is still much alive.

We look out on a torn and troubled industrial world. Employer and employee stand opposed in bitterness of spirit; strikes, lockouts, monopolies call our attention to the fact that the rule is, each class for itself. The law of competition makes things more highly valued than people. The development of the individual is lost in the automatic performance of repeated action, hour after hour, day upon day. Unearned profits piling up, supply so many luxuries that the rich lose sight of the real condition of the poor. interest. The day when "all men's good shall be each man's rule" seems far off. 

And yet we are striving to reach it. By what path shall we arrive at the reign of peace in the industrial world? 

Shall we try force? Force may for the moment overcome, it may crush; but the untamed spirit returns to life and is again potent. Shall we try mere morality? Wanting a higher sanction and support morality lacks endurance. Shall we try economics? When the crucial test appears again we yield and our cause fails by default. Shall we try legislation? Laws are dead things unless backed by human hearts and wills.

More and more as we read the pages of 

John Woolman's Journal and Essays we become convinced that we have found there the solution of the great problem. The sign by which he conquered was the sign of the Cross. "From age to age, throughout all ages, Divine Love is that alone in which dominion has been, is and will be rightly conducted." 

He speaks with authority.

Though his method of work has been presented in the foregoing narrative, let us summarize. Take the matter of slavery: first, he visualized the sufferings of the slaves and put his soul in their souls' stead; then he arrived at a profound conviction that slavery was wrong; this was followed by an equally 21

profound conviction that if, knowing the truth, he failed to declare it, or if he received any profit from the vicious system he was sinning against God and wronging his own soul. There must be no turning aside on his part from perfect purity.

His aim was not simply to relieve the poor man by personal assistance, but to arouse such compunction in the mind of the oppressor that he would apply the remedy himself.

Surely this is fundamental work. In his own heart tenderness and compassion for the offender prevailed. He set about the task of proving the wrong with no feeling of superiority, with no threats, taunts or reproaches. "My inclination," said he, "is to persuade and entreat and simply give hints of my way of thinking." The later Abolitionists aroused the ire of the slave-holder. Woolman left the spirit of Truth to do its own work. Would that a hundred John Woolmans had a century ago been let loose in the South to "sensitize the consciences" of her high-spirited people; let such reformers come on now, the times are ripe for further work. 22 22
