FOREWORD By HERBERT E. HAWKES Dean, Columbia College 

It is not only interesting but exceedingly important that the results in terms of human accomplishment of a gen erous benefaction like the Pulitzer Scholarship Fund should be evaluated. All of us who have been intimately concerned with the Pulitzer Scholars and their work have realized in a somewhat subjective manner that the stipends perform a service of outstanding significance. 

Now, for the first time, Mr. Schwartz has made a careful study of at least some of the results of Mr. Pulitzer's generosity. In this study it is obvious that our confidence in the Pulitzer Scholars has not been misplaced. 

It is requested that these findings not be made public until or after December 8, 1939.

FIFTY YEARS OF THE PULITZER SCHOLARSHIPS 

... the enduring monument of Joseph Pulitzer consists of the men whose lives he influenced by his generous philanthropy."-WILLIAM E. GRADY 

TODAY, a half century after Joseph Pulitzer established the scholarships bearing his name, it is difficult to appreciate how revolutionary that action appeared to his contemporaries. We now regard generous financial aid to brilliant, but needy, students as a commonplace and are not unduly surprised when colleges and individuals follow the path first blazed by this journalistic genius. 

In 1889, however, things were viewed far differently. 

This is not meant to suggest that scholarships were unknown at that time. They existed, of course, and in fact City College had already been founded to give free higher education in this city. Mr. Pulitzer's startling proposal was that poor boys should receive funds adequate to support them with food, clothing and shelter while attending college, thus relieving them of the necessity of earning their own livelihood while receiving collegiate training. Without such subsidy, Mr. Pulitzer knew, many talented students could not even take advantage of the free tuition at the College of the City of New York. It was the need of this group that his generosity was designed to meet. 

To accomplish this end, he proposed to select twelve graduates of the public grammar schools each yearthere were no public high schools at that time in New York City - and pay each a stipend of $250 annually for the five years of the course at City College. The selections were to be made after impartial, competitive examinations, and the boys chosen were to be the most promising, the most deserving and the most needy. 

The great merit of such a plan is obvious immediately, but in 1889 it aroused quite a good deal of controversy. Mr. Pulitzer and his friends agreed that it was an experiment for the success of which they were hopeful, but critics of the idea were loud in their denunciations. One newspaper asserted that the winners would have their moral fiber weakened and would become dissatisfied with their station in life, an argument which it seemed to regard as crushing the plan. Senator Chauncey 

Depew predicted that the recipients of the award would become paupers and dependents. But disregarding these and similar attacks, Mr. Pulitzer put his project into execution.

This article is an attempt to evaluate the results of 3

this experiment, fifty years after its inauguration. The conclusions and data which follow are based on a survey made by the writer with financial aid from the 

Pulitzer Free Scholarship Committee and Columbia 

University. In addition, he had constant aid and advice from Frank H. Bowles, Secretary of the Committee, and 

Professor Frederick E. Croxton.

It seems fitting to begin with a presentation of Joseph Pulitzer's objectives in establishing the scholarship. 

Basing his remarks upon his own experiences in struggling from obscurity and poverty to fame and riches, he wrote the following in June, 1889 to Superintendent of 

Schools John Jasper: 

"I believe in self made men. But it is not the aim of this plan to help people for ordinary money making purposes 

There are nobler objectives in life. And my hope is not that these scholarships will make better butchers, bakers, brokers and bank cashiers, but that they will help to make teachers, scholars, physicians, authors, journalists, judges, lawyers, and statesmen. They certainly ought to increase, not diminish, the number of those who, under our free institutions, rise from the humblest to the highest positions." 

With this as its goal, the plan began operation and from 1889 to 1892 inclusive, thirty-nine boys were sent to City College. This proved unsatisfactory, however, since it was soon found that the subfreshman year at that school, the prelude to the regular four-year course, provided a very inadequate preparation for the transition from the elementary school work to that on the college level.

The opportunity for a change came in 1893 when Columbia University began soliciting a $2,000,000 fund to erect buildings on its new site at Morningside Heights. In consideration of a gift of $100,000 from 

Mr. Pulitzer, the University agreed to accept ten winners each year and give them free tuition for the seven or eight years required to finish the complete courses. 

at Horace Mann School and Columbia College. Dur ing this extended period the scholarship's founder agreed to provide the $250 annual stipend to the boys selected.

By 1901 public high schools had been opened in Manhattan and the Bronx, and accordingly, the terms of the scholarship were modified to meet its original conception. The competition was thrown open to graduates of the public high schools who, upon winning it, would receive the stipend for the four years of their college education and, if they chose to attend Columbia, free tuition. They had, however, the option of attending any other college and paying their own tuition there.

In 1907 the scholarship scheme received its present form when eligibility was extended to graduates of public high schools in all five boroughs of New York City.

After Mr. Pulitzer's death in 1911 it was found that his will provided for the establishment of a $250,000 fund from the income of which the stipends and the other expenses of the scholarships were to be met. 

In concluding this historical summary it should be noted that for the first thirteen years of its operation, the scholarships were being awarded mainly to boys thirteen and fourteen years of age who were just emerging from the elementary grades. That so many of them did very well in adult life is a tribute to the discernment of the committee which selected the winners, using the criteria set down by the donor. This committee included representatives of the Board of Education, Columbia 

University and Mr. Pulitzer.

The author and those associated with him feel that one of the most significant conclusions to be drawn from this study is the fact that sheer intelligence can be recognized early in a youth's development, and that its possessor stands an excellent chance of triumphing over whatever obstacles may be in his road. This study is, thus, a record of what a group carefully chosen can achieve.

Since their inception, the scholarships have been won by 551 boys. Of these 400 were successful in graduating from college before June 1939; 103 lost or resigned the award before graduating; and 48, those attending college now or who graduated in June, 1939, are not included in this study. Of those in the first group, 319 graduated from Columbia, and the others from a group of thirteen colleges throughout the country. 

Of the 503 winners studied, 42 were found to be dead. Biographical questionnaires were sent to as many of the rest as could be located with the aid of the Columbia and other alumni offices, the telephone directories and various miscellaneous sources. A total of 274 answers was received. A majority of the remaining 187 could not be found and only about 40 of those receiving the questionnaire failed to return it. Most of those whose addresses are unknown belong to the group which did not graduate. Of the 366 former scholars who did graduate and are alive, 268 or about 73% did reply thus giving a relatively complete – although possibly not representative - picture of the entire group. 

The results given below should and will be interpreted with several considerations in mind. Since the data and conclusions are based mainly upon informa- 5

tion furnished by the men themselves, the question of veracity must come up. Wherever it was possible to do so, an independent check was made upon the statements given and they were invariably found to be correct. It is the writer's belief that while there may have been some inaccuracies in the original material, which might modify some of the details presented, they are few and do not vitiate the main conclusions of the study. 

In the second place it must be remembered that the individuals in this group began life as members of the lowest economic class. Although equipped with intelligence and ambition they had little prospect of forging ahead. Their successes, financially and otherwise, are of the Horatio Alger type, except that these are records of real life. Most of those studied would have been unable to attend college at all except for Mr. Pulitzer's aid, and even with it most of them had to work to augment their family incomes while attending college. As a matter of fact, the Committee's records show that many of those who resigned or lost the scholarship did so because of the necessity of working very hard at outside jobs with the consequent disruption of their studies. 

Perhaps the most obvious-although sometimes the most fallacious-criterion of "success" is that of financial standing. Joseph Pulitzer said specifically that he did not intend to train men for money-making purposes alone; but the extent to which these men have been able to provide their families with economic security and the decencies of life must certainly be considered in evaluating their record in adult life.

It may be objected that these figures are not representative of the entire group, since it is quite possible that those who did not answer or could not be located are not as well off as those who did reply. This is a valid point. Nevertheless, the data presented above demonstrate that under the economic system which has existed in this country during the past half century, it has been possible for many who have ability and ambition as their principal assets to work themselves up the economic ladder if aided as these men were. 

It would be enlightening if we could compare our figures with data on the income distribution of all college men, but reliable statistics of this type are lacking, and those published as such are usually based upon extremely inadequate and unrepresentative samples. 

More light is shed on the meaning of Table I if we know the situations of those in the lower brackets there. 

Thus several of these are young doctors who began their practice sometime in 1938; others graduated in that year and held positions for the period following their exit from school; one is a shellshocked veteran who ekes out his subsistence on a farm, and another is in a sanitarium afflicted with an incurable disease. In general, therefore it seems fair to say that those in these lower brackets promise either not to remain there long or are there for reasons beyond their control. Only one of those who returned a questionnaire stated that he had been unemployed in 1938 although desirous of obtaining a position.

Both the average and the median incomes here are close to the $2,000 mark, far below that of the entire group, but probably higher than that for all college graduates during this parlous period. 

As might be expected, most of the former scholars have entered the learned professions. Thus, among those answering, there were fifty-two engineers, thirtysix lawyers, thirty-one college professors or instructors and an equal number of doctors, and twenty-seven engaged in teaching below the college level; most of these latter were in administrative positions. Sixteen are engaged in business while another twelve are doing chemical or physical research. Nine journalists are following Pulitizer's profession and there are seven advertising men. Accounting, economics research, and architecture have several representatives each as has the actuarial profession.

The remaining number have wandered far afield vocationally. They include a book publisher, priest, machinist, fireman, purser on a ship, farmer, and the head of the largest refugee aid organization in the 

United States as well as others.

To the extent that these men have contributed to the well being of this nation in greater measure than they would have been capable of doing if they had not received Joseph Pulitzer's aid and gone through college, this group represents a distinct gain to the United States.

While it would be making an invidious and unjustified comparison to select a few names and say they were the best who have held the Pulitzer Scholarship, we can give several representative outstanding alumni of this award. Among these are Chester W. Cuthell, attorney for New York City in the present transit unification proceedings; George Z. Medalie, former United

States Attorney for the Eastern District of New York and the man who gave Thomas E. Dewey his first chance to obtain public attention in office; Simeon 

Strunsky, an outstanding journalist who occupies an important position on the New York Times, and others. 

Among the deceased are such individuals as Edward Sapir, formerly Sterling Professor of Anthropology at 

Yale University; Fred F. French, a leader in the New

York real estate field; and Felix Fuld, one of the chief fighters for good government and civil service reform in New York City.

Columbia University's faculty has Professors Stephen 

P. Burke, Frederick W. Hehre, Clay Ray Murray, Edwin B. Matzke, and Meyer Schapiro, Dr. Ian F. Fraser and Mr. Andrew Chiappe among its Pulitzer Alumni.

New York City's school system has many former

Pulitzer Scholars in its top administrative offices. 

Among these are Associate Superintendents of Schools 

William E. Grady and John E. Wade; Assistant Superintendents David H. Moskowitz and Oswald Schlockow; and Leonard Covello, Principal of Benjamin

Franklin High School. Mr. Moskowitz, appropriately enough, now aids in the selection of Pulitzer Scholars. 

Mr. Leon Goldrich, now deceased, who founded and headed the Bureau of Child Guidance of the Board of Education was also a member of this group. Others are elementary school principals, departmental chairmen, or hold other positions of responsibility in the pedagogical world.

While attending college, the members of this group did very well. For instance, 110 belong to Phi Beta

Kappa and 54 to Sigma Xi. There is almost no scholastic honor at Columbia that has not been won by at least one of them. Other awards they have received include three National Research Fellowships in the physical sciences, two Social Science Research Council awards, and two Guggenheim Fellowships. One was successful in obtaining a Pulitzer Traveling Fellowship from the School of Journalism, thus benefiting twice from the generosity of the same man.

In extra curricular activities we find that this group has provided two editors-in-chief and four business managers for Spectator. Many of its number have won silver and gold King's Crowns. Only a few, however, have starred athletically. Among these, crew men seem to predominate although there have been several football, basketball, and baseball players too. One former scholar who attended Amherst won six major letters for his proficiency in sport.

Graduate work has attracted many of those we are studying. Not only are there the many professional men listed before, but there are fifty-seven holders of Master's degrees and thirty-five of Doctor's. As graduate students, many of these have held scholarships and fellowships to assist them. 

The Pulitzer Scholars who answered have written ninety-one books which have been published, and literally hundreds of articles in various scientific technical and professional journals. Dr. DeWitt Stetten holds. the record, having ninety-one articles to his credit in various medical periodicals.

The backgrounds of the winners provide interesting evidence of the democratic spirit which has dominated the selection. Of those replying, ninety-five, or more than one-third, listed themselves as Jews, eighty-

two as Protestants, and thirty-five as Catholics. Ten more called themselves Christians without specifying any denomination, while forty-three either declined to give their religion or said they had none. Two are ethical culturists and one is a Christian Scientist. 

About 70% were born in the United States, but most of them are first generation Americans since 80% of their parents were born abroad. Almost every country in Europe has descendants in this group. One boy of Chinese parentage and at least one Negro have also won the scholarship. 

An overwhelming percentage, 80%, have married and most of these have had stable marital careers since only seven divorces were reported, or only about 4% of the number married. This last number included one individual who divorced his wife and then remarried her.

As a group, those reporting have not reproduced themselves and their mates, since they have had only 

299 children or almost 1.5 per family. There is a very high correlation between income level and number of children and, in fact, it was only the upper income group which averaged more than two children per family. Another factor in producing this result, no doubt, was that the older men replying tended to have the larger incomes. While most of the married couples had two or three children, one had five and about twenty were childless.

Non-college girls have been as successful in attracting Pulitzer Scholars as their more academic sisters for only 50% of those studied married girls who had attended or graduated from college. Hunter College's alumnae were the most numerous among the wives, twelve of them having been students there, while an other eight were from Maxwell Training School for 

Teachers. New York University and Barnard had six representatives in this group while Mount Holyoke, Vassar and Smith had three or four each. The wives were selected from many colleges, however, and many of them had done graduate work too, apparently, for they were listed as having Ph. D.'s or as being doctors or lawyers.

Practically all children of college age or over reported by this group attend or attended a university. Columbia leads Harvard by a slight margin in being the most popular college for the offspring, but together they have only about 15%. Universities all over the country have children of Pulitzer Scholars in their student or alumni bodies.

It seems appropriate to note here that the editor of 10

The Columbian for 1937-38 was the son of one of those replying, while the son of another was admitted to 

Sigma Xi at Columbia this year.

There were no cases of fathers and sons who had both won Pulitzer Scholarships. One reason for this, probably, is that most of those having sons of college age were in the upper income brackets so that their children were ineligible to compete.

Several pairs of brothers have won the scholarship, but the outstanding record in this respect is held by the 

O'Neill family, three members of which have won the award during the past decade. 

The fears voiced in 1889 that the scholarship winners would become lazy because of the aid are strikingly refuted by the fact that 80% of those surveyed had worked to some extent while in college in order to augment their incomes. In most cases this consisted of summer jobs or tutoring during the school year, but several reported having had full time positions because of their status as sole bread winners in large families. 

Most of these men show a better than average sense of civic responsibility since most of them voted in each of the last five elections. Few, however, have participated actively in politics or belong to political organizations. 

A few exceptions are noteworthy, though. George

L. Donellan, now Justice in the Court of General Sessions, was a Tammany District Leader in 1906, and was the youngest man ever to hold such a post until that time. George Z. Medalie has been high in Republican councils for many years. Henry Epstein served as Solicitor General of New York State for seven years, and two of New York's assistant corporation counsels, Nicholas Bucci and Samuel Silverman, also belong to this group.

Among outstanding individual feats might be mentioned that of one winner who successfully prosecuted Jack ("Legs") Diamond, notorious racketeer and gangster. Another has designed and supervised the construction of several of the largest hydro-electric plants in the country. Still another discovered a property of the blood which has been found useful in diagnosis and prognosis. This recital of individual achievements could go on on for many pages but these are representative. Mention should also be made of the large number who reported that they had served overseas with the 

A.E.F. during the first World War. 

But what, it may be asked, of the negative aspect?

Surely some of these men have done things of a discreditable nature. Their childhood backgrounds might seem 11

conducive towards that sort of conduct. To the best of this investigator's knowledge, however, there are only two instances of former Pulitzer Scholars becoming seriously involved with the law.

Several years ago one of them was indicted in connection with the failure of one of the largest banks in this city, but his conviction was reversed upon appeal to the higher courts. Another was indicted this past summer for conspiracy to evade the New York City sales tax laws, but his case had not come to trial at this writing. This is not to say that these have been the only cases of this type; they are merely the only ones of which we have knowledge, as with giant su Although all lived in New York City when they won the scholarship, the members of this group have scattered throughout the country and even to some foreign lands. Most still reside in the Metropolitan area, but California has a good many too. Of the five abroad, one is an industrial engineer in Brussels, another heads a sugar firm in Cuba, a third manages the Manchukuoan branch of an American bank, and two are engineers in Arabia.

In broad outline, the story of the Pulitzer Scholars and some of the results of that experiment that began in 1889 stand above. More than one million dollars has been expended on this group, and society has received an incalculable return in lives saved and achievements made possible. Society's debt to Joseph Pulitzer is exceeded only by the obligation owed him by those, who, like the writer, have had the course of their lives altered by his generosity.

It seems a valid conclusion that this experiment has been successful, and highly so. The task, however, is far from finished. There are still thousands of boys and girls throughout the land who find financial difficulties in the way of developing their abilities to serve their fellows and themselves. America must fully utilize its human resources as well as its natural ones if it is to remain strong and move forward. Joseph Pulitzer was a pioneer in pointing the way. The record above is the chronicle of what has been done in following that path, and, hopefully, a forecast. 17 12
