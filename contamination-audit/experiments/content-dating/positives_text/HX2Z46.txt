Fine Mency FOREWORD BY THE PASTOR REV. A. H. COOKE 

On Sunday afternoon, January 10, 1943, The Congregational 

Church of Patchogue, N. Y., celebrated the 150th Anniversary of its founding on January 14, 1793. A feature of the celebration was a history of the church, written by Dr. Frank Overton, Clerk of the church since 1926. The historical facts were derived from two principal sources: 

1. Loose records and memoranda of agreements and business transactions from the early days of the church. 

2. The bound volume of the business meetings of the church beginning in 1845.

The business papers of the church during the first ninety years of its existence were retained by three deacons and clerks named Isaac Overton-father, son, and grandson-the last of whom died in 1881. The papers were placed in a private box which was seldom opened until its contents were examined by the clerk, Dr. Frank Overton, who was grandnephew of the third Deacon Isaac Overton. The papers are now of special interest because they contain lists of subscribers for the erection of the church buildings, and records of settlements of disputes between its members.

The earliest records of the business meetings of the church are contained in a book of 370 pages, which was begun by the pastor, James H. 

Thomas, in 1845. Mr. Thomas divided religion into two branches: 

1. DOCTRINAL, or theological beliefs and creeds. 

2. EXPERIMENTAL, or the exemplification of the doctrines in the private life of each member. 

The modern church expresses these two principles in the simple statement, "The fatherhood of God, and the brotherhood of Man." 

The work of the Patchogue Congregational Church is creative and constructive, and follows the lines expressed by Bishop Trent, who wrote: "God's work on the first day of creation was the light of day; on the sixth, the light of reason; and his work ever since has been the illumination of the Spirit."

The spirit of the Congregational Church of Patchogue is expressed by Jesus Christ, in Matthew 5, 16: "Let your light so shine before men that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father who is in heaven." 

Arthur H. Cooke, Pastor.

The Patchogue Congregation al Church has always been noted for its liberality in its doctrinal principles, and its independence in the management of its internal affairs. It now (1943) has a membership of about 510 persons; but a large group of non-members contribute their time and money to the support of its religious, educational, and social activities.

The Congregational church of Patchogue is the natural grandchild of the parent church in Setauket, through the daughter church in Southaven. During colonial days and the early years of the organized nation, these three churches served the people who lived on the three hundred square miles that now compose the township of Brookhaven. The Township now has a populatiion of 30000 people, who are served by 30 churches of 7 denominations. Amidst this multiplicity and diversity of form and methods, any person can find a congenial church group with whom he can work happily and efficiently.

THE GRANDPARENT CHURCH IN SETAUKET The reputation of the Patch ogue Church as an efficient working organization is an inheritance from the first settlers of Brookhaven Town in Setauket, dating from the year 1655. 

Their objectives were: First, to wrest a comfortable living from the soil, the forest, and the adjoining waters. Second, to cooperate together for mutual helpfulness. In these two objectives the church was the civic center in every respect and the pastor was almost the only educated leader in the community. He cultivated a farm, and raised cattle and sheep. He taught the parish children their A.B.C.s, and was the wise counselor of the people in their private affairs, as in their public activities. 2

The church building was the meeting-place in which the people came together once a week, not only to hear a discourse by the pastor, but also for informal discussion of their relations to one another, both in this life and in the life to come. The church was the working center of all phases of the social, the civic, the educational, and the religious life of all the people; and in these modern days it still remains their major source of inspiration in both private life and in community affairs. 

The early settlers found 

Brookhaven to be full of natural resources in its land, its ponds and streams, and its bays; but the head of each family had to toil long and hard to build his own modest house for shelter, and to make his own crude furniture and implements. But the millwrights constructed sawmills and gristmills, and thereby enabled the people to obtain comfortable houses and nourishing food. They erected fulling mills which performed the hard process of washing, bleaching, and carding their wool, and then delivered it in rolls which the housewives spun and wove into warm cloth during the long winter evenings.

The millwrights also constructed mills to grind the seeds of the flax plants of the farmers, and to press out the useful oil called linseed (linen seed) oil.

The mills relieved the people of a great part of depressing toil in producing the bare necessities of life, and were a great factor in enabling the people to keep a sabbath day of rest and devotion.

The pastor's right-hand helper was the blacksmith, on whom the people depended for making and repairing the iron utensils which they used in their daily life. He was necessarily a man of judgment, and his shop was the forum in which the men folk discussed the Sunday sermon and the current news, and exchanged ideas regarding public events and civic affairs. Longfellow's charming poem, "The Village Black smith," is a true picture of the pastor's first assistant throughout the colonial days of Brookhaven Town.

INDIAN RELATIONS 

On April 14, 1655, the early settlers of Setauket made an agreement with the local Indians regarding the ownership of the land and its products, based on two fundamental principals:- 

1. The natural resources of the land and water should belong to all the people, both Indians and whites. 

2. Whatever was the direct product of human effort or skill, belonged to the individual or group that produced it.

By the original agreement, or "Purchase," with the Indians the Colonists acquired four principal items of essential value:- 

1. Full ownership of a restricted tract of land bordering on the Harbor, to be used as home sites and cultivated farms. 

2. The right to pasture cattle beyond the limits of the cultivated fields. 

3. The right to cut timber growing in the natural forest. 

4. An agreement for mutual aid and for protection against enemies and wrong-doers. This fourth item was of particular interest to the Indians, because about a half century previously, they had been the victims of a murderous raid by

Pequot warriors from the mainland across Long Island Sound.

But the agreement was of equal value to the settlers; and their relations with their Indian neighbors were always peaceful and cooperative.

Evidence of the permanent confidence between the settlers and the Indians, is the fact that deeds of land sales often contained the provision that Indians reserved their rights to gather reeds and flags from the marshes, and to dig ground nuts on the low lands that were acquired by the white people.

These rights may still be exercised by any person who can prove a direct descent from an

The social and civic life of the early settlers of Setauket centered in the church, as it did in the other early settlements on Long Island and in New England; but the pastor's influence arose from the fact that he was an official "Social 

Worker" during week days.

The "Meeting-House" was the first public building to be constructed, and for many years it was the only one. It was extremely crude, like the first church building in Patchogue.

It was used for religious services on Sundays, and for public assemblies and Town Meetings on week days. It was the school house in which the pastor taught the children their three "R's"--reading, 'riting, and 'rithmatic-on week days, and the Westminster Catechism on Sundays. It was the civic center of the community in every sense of the word.

For his services the pastor received an annual salary of twenty or thirty pounds (about $75) and the use of as much land as he could cultivate. If the pastor was concerned only with preaching and doctrinal discussions, he seldom lasted more than a year or two; but if he lived as one of the people, cultivated the church land, and raised cattle, he stayed with the parish for a lifetime; and when he died he left a substantial estate; and citizens today are descendants of early pastors of the churches in Setauket and Southaven, for example, the families of Phillips, Brewster, and Rose.

The settlement in Setauket grew steadely. In about half a century the people had spread eastward along the North Shore to Mount Sinai, Millers Place, and Wading River, and had built up a hamlet wherever a "Landing" was available on the shores of Long Island Sound.

The people in these new centers of population on the North 

Shore maintained their direct connection with the Setauket settlement and its church until they were able to support their own churches.

The people also spread down the valley of the East Connetquot River, and established homes in a large area in the southeastern section of the . township, for it offered three special advantages to settlers:- 

1. Its coves and estuaries of shallow water teemed with fish and shell fish, and provided an easy waterway to New York City. 

2. Its extensive areas of low land and salt marsh were unfailing sources of excellent grass for summer pasturage and winter hay. 

3. Numerous streams flowing south into the Great South Bay provided excellent sites for water power,-four on the East Connetquot River, four on the streams of Moriches, and four on streams of Patchogue.

For these three reasons the settlements in the southeastern sections of Brookhaven Town ship prospered, and by the year 1720 their population was almost equal to that of the Setauket section. In about the year 

1730 the people organized their own church and erected a modest "Meeting House."

The early records refer to the southeastern section of the township as "Brookhaven in the South;" and later they called it "South Brookhaven;" and then they shortened the name to "Southaven." In 1757 the town meeting of Brookhaven 

Township voted that "Southaven" should be the official name of the parish in the southeastern section of the Township.

The church building of the Southaven Parish was located, as it is now, on the west bank of the East Connetquot River, at the only ford, or "Going 

Over," that existed between the mouth of the river at Fireplace, and Yaphank eight miles to the north. The first public bridge across the river was erected in 1862, a few rods south of the private mill dam. From this bridge all the east and west roads in the southeastern section of the township still radiate to the eight settlements in the neighborhood,-Fire Place, Middle Island, Yaphank, St. 

George's Manor, the "Three 

Moriches," and Manorville. In this respect the Southaven

Church was like the typical parish churches in Virginia, each of which stands alone in the center of a ring of surrounding plantations.

The proprietors of the Manor of St. George, adjoining 

Southaven on the east, were not only staunch supporters of the church, but were outstanding leaders of the Revolution, including General John Smith, General Nathaniel Woodhull,

President of the First Contin ental Congress; and William

Floyd, one of the signers of the 

Declaration of Independence. 

In Moriches, to the east, lived Col. Josiah Smith, a civic and military leader. Probably no other parish in the country produced so many outstanding civic leaders as that of the 

Southaven church.

Church services were held in the Southaven Parish with uncertain regularity before 1740; but in or about that year, a "Meeting House" was erected on the site of the present building. 

ABNER REEVE, Pastor 

The first regular pastor to occupy the church was probably Abner Reeve, a native of 

Southold, L. I. 

While he was pastor at Southaven, one of his sons, named Tappan, was born. This son was the founder and conductor of the Law School in Litchfield, Connecticut, which for many years was the most famous Law

School in the colonies. The building in which the school was held is still preserved as a historic shrine.

DAVID ROSE 

In 1765 David Rose of Connecticut began a long pastoral service of thirty-five years. He lived in the church manse which was located on the South Country Road immediately northeast of the crossing of the Long 

Island Railroad. Like his predecessor, he was a successful farmer, and the ear mark of his cattle is recorded in the 

Town Clerk's Office. 

Pastor Rose was also educated as a physician and practiced his profession with skill. He was also interested in local education. He was a soldier during the Revolution and spent some time in a British prison in New York. "Priest" Rose, as he was affectionately called, was extremely popular and was considered to be the first citizen of the community. The new church building measuring 36 by 40 feet was erected in 1820 just east of the first one. It still stands and has been converted into a store, but the original plan of its interior and the method of its construction may still be traced in the attic. This building was considered to be so beautiful and satisfactory, that in 1823 its builder, George Curtis, was engaged to build a church exactly like it for the Presbyterian Parish of Smithtown.

The Smithtown Church is still in use, and is carefully preserved in its original form, except that a steeple and a Sunday School wing have been added. A gallery runs around the end and two sides of the main assembly room. The overhead ceiling between the side galleries is arched from side to side, and a high pulpit leads the eyes of the worshippers upward as if to the starry arch of the heavens. The room is beautifully adapted to inspire the worshippers with reverence.

The second church building in Patchogue was used by both the Congregationalists and the 

Methodists as the first building had been used. Agreement as to the use of the building was a simple problem so long as the only activity of each organization was a preaching service held once a week. But with the development of the mid-week meetings, the Sunday Schools, the Missionary Societies, and other branches of church work, there was an increasing need that the control and use of all of the physical property of the Congregational church be vested in a board of trustees. This was accomplished in accordance with a State Law, "To provide for the incorporation of religious societies," passed April 15, 1813.

The Articles of Incorpora tion of the Patchogue Church are recorded with the Clerk of 

Suffolk County on June 4, 1828 in Liber A, Certificates of Incorporation, Page 44.

In 1843 the Congregational 

Church was nearly wrecked by a schism resulting from the preaching of two pastors of the local Church-Rev. B. Matthais (1839-43) and Rev. S. P. 

Gammage (1843-1844), who upheld the Millerite doctrine, propounded by William Miller (1781-1849), of Pittsfield, Massachusetts, that the end of the world was at hand. The Millerite preachers were excluded from the pulpits of the local churches, but their followers met in barns where the village hoodlums made high sport of them, with the usual result of fanning the flames of the new belief and drawing about fiftyeight members away from the

Congregational Church. As the 

"Ascension Day" approached, the Saints gave away their property and bought "Ascension robes"; but nothing happened, except that the town 

Overseer of the Poor had to feed about two dozen starving 

Millerite families throughout the winter.

The church minutes contain the records of three members who regretted their support of the Millerites, and who were restored to membership in the

Congregational Church. 

The end of the Millerite movement came at the close of the first half century of the life of the Patchogue Congregation al Church, and was followed by the pastorate of the Rev. James 

H. Thomas, who served the 223

church from May 1, 1844 to

April 1, 1849. 

Mr. Thomas was an excellent pastor. He clearly outlined the practical fields of church work, and organized its activities with skill and good judgment. He recognized the natural division of religious experience into two branches:- 

1. Doctrinal, or the beliefs and creeds which are expressed by the

Pastor in his Sunday sermons. 

2. Experimental, or the exemplification of the doctrines in the daily lives of the parishioners. "Applied religion" is the modern term for this branch of church work.

The records show that candidates for membership were examined regarding their proficiency in both doctrinal and experimental religion. On January 27, 1849, one member on trial for his doctrinal beliefs, testified that he still adhered to the doctrine of the final annihilation of the wicked, or "believed that he believed it."

Two months later he was formally excommunicated. But there was an increasing emphasis on the exemplification of the Christian virtues in the daily lives of the church members, according to the standards set by St. Paul in Galatians 5: 22-23, "The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, long suffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance." 

Mr. Thomas introduced system and order into the work of the church by starting a permanent record book of 370 pages, eight by thirteen inches in size. This book contains the minutes of the business meetings of the church for eighty years, from January 

18, 1845 up to January 14, 1925.

The first three pages contain an outline of the church in which is the following item, dated April 30, 1845:- "Since the organization of this church there have been received into her communion 438 members. There are now (April 

30, 1845) 204 members. There are about 115 or 120 families connected with the congregation." 

An alphabetical list of the members who joined between 

1793 and 1845 is recorded on pages 367-370. The same minute book, pages 292-366, contains lists of members who were added to the church rolls from 1845 to 1895. 24

In June 1887 the church issued a manual of 26 pages in which the roll of members was printed in two parts. Part one consists of former members who had moved away or died, 545 in number.

Part two contains the roll of members, 240 in number, who were living on June 1, 1887.

Therefore a total of 875 members had joined the church during the first 94 years of its life. 

During the first half century of the life of the Patchogue

Congregational Church there was a gradual evolution and expansion of its activities. On 

Sundays, the noon recess, with its social lunches between the two long preaching services, was a great value in forming acquaintances and promoting the informal exchange of ideas regarding experimental religion. Social prayer-meetings had been held on week days in the four natural sections of the parish, but pastor Thomas organized them as regular branches of church work. In 1845 he appointed a committee, called a "Conference committee” in each of the four sections of the parish. The duties of each committee were to conduct local prayer meetings and promote other branches of church work, including the investigation of charges against members. The plan of the third building followed the standard design of the leading religious organizations of the period. It was rectangular in shape, with a tall spire or steeple on the front, and high, narrow side windows which nearly reached to the eaves of the roof. 

The front end was partitioned off across its entire width, and contained the main entry way below, and the choir and organ loft above. The remainder of the interior was occupied by the auditorium, which was used for the Sunday morning services and for concerts and lectures on educational and civic matters during the week. 

The building also had a basement which was used as a Sunday School and prayer meeting room and as an assembly hall on social occasions. It also had facilities for serving light refreshments.

Pastor Thomas would have been delighted with the practice of experimental religion by the parishioners living in East

The accompanying map which is an enlargement of a small section of a wall map of Suffolk County published in 1858, shows the location of the houses of the 37 home owners who lived in that section of the parish. In addition a a dozen families lived in tenement houses that are not shown on the map.

Union Street was a community of over 200 persons living along the two-mile stretch of the South Country Road be tween Swan Creek and Bellport. Over ninety per cent of the people were Congregationalists. 

A variety of living conditions made the people co-operative and social. On the north a forest of pines and oak extended up to the middle of the Island.

Fertile farms bordered the main highway; and on the south was the Great South Bay and the 

Atlantic Ocean. A citizen was therefore classed as a hunter or woodcutter or farmer, bayman or sea captain, and frequently he could qualify in all these lines, but always he had instincts of friendliness with his neighbors. 32

The building, or chapel, as it was called, was at once erected, and during the Civil War the Stars and Stripes were displayed at all services.

Religious services were held in the chapel twice a week. On 

Sunday afternoons a service of worship was led by the Pastor or a deacon who read a sermon by a popular preacher. This was followed by a Sunday

School. 

On Wednesday evening a prayer-meeting was conducted by one of the deacons. A pleasing feature of all these services was the singing, led by Capt.

Charles Homan, and later by 

Deacon Charles M. Hedges. 

A monthly sociable was established during the late eighties and was kept up for more than twenty years. The people met in the homes of the parishioners and brought the materials for a simple supper, which always included baked beans. 34

The chapel was in active operation for over a half century when the need for it gradually declined, for two reasons:- 

1. The decrease in the number of persons who were interested in its welfare as the older supporters passed away and the younger generation moved to the city. 

2. The automobile enabled the people to attend the home church in Patchogue.

The chapel building was therefore sold, and moved to a site on the Middle Island Road and converted into a dwelling house. In 1938 the net proceeds of the sale, amounting to $997.- 

91 were given to the parent church in Patchogue to be used in reducing its building indebtedness.

Accessory expenses brought the total cost of the new church to about $50,000.00. The subscriptions obtained before the 35

church building was erected amounted to about $20,000.00. 

The fourth building of the

Congregational Church was designed to serve the whole man in his three natures:- spiritual, intellectual and social.

The spiritual life of the church is centered in the sanctuary, or main auditorium, which is designed to inspire the worshipper with a feeling of reverence. Its high ceiling is composed of twenty-four arches which direct the eyes of the worshippers heavenward. The side walls are illuminated with windows of stained glass in memory of devoted workers of past years. At the front of the sanctuary are the altar, the pipe organ and choir loft; and facing them are semi-circles of cushioned pews for five hundred worshippers. 

The intellectual services of the church are centered in the chapel, in which prayer-meetings are held and the Sunday School is conducted and study groups assembled.

The chapel has a seating capacity of two hundred per- sons, and becomes a part of the sanctuary when large sliding doors between the two rooms are opened.

Social and recreational facili ties are provided in the basement floor of the chapel, where kitchen and dining facilities are available for serving refreshments to one hundred persons.

This social room is in use on nearly every evening of the week; and the fraternal and social spirit developed in it is a great factor in promoting an interest in the religious and educational work of the church and a fraternal spirit among its members and supporters. Some phase of church activity is exemplified on nearly every evening of the week, and justifies the growing reputation of the 

Congregational organization as a "Church at Work."

Three Isaac Overtons-father, son and grandson, were outstanding leaders in the Congregational

Church and in civic life for over three quarters of a century.

The first Isaac Overton (17441799) lived in Bayport. He was a blacksmith, and purchased the site for the first church in 1793. The second Isaac (1765-1839) was also a blacksmith. He lived in Bayport, and later in East Patchogue, and was deacon and clerk of the church. His brother, Stephen, was pastor of the Congregational 

Church in Chester, N. J. for more than a quarter of a century. The grandson, Isaac, (1797-1880) was a farmer in East Patchogue. He was clerk of the church, and served as deacon from 1847 until his death in 1880. The tablet was donated by his son, Isaac Lewis

Overton. 

Deacon David Wickham Case (1807-1893) lived on East Main 

Street, Patchogue, at the corner of Case Avenue. He was a tanner and shoemaker, as his father was before him. He was elected deacon in 1868, and served until May 21, 1888, when his son, Edward H.

Case, was elected deacon.

Church has been the center for training an outstanding group of religious leaders in the Home and

7. Edward Percy Case 1. Stephen Overton 1772-1830 

The first member of the Patchogue Congregational Church to become a professional church worker was Stephen Overton, son of Isaac Overton, who bought the site for the first church building in 1791, and brother of the second Isaac, who was deacon in the church until his death in 1838, aged 73 years. 

Stephen Overton was born in 

Coram, L. I. in 1772 and died in 

Chester, New Jersey in 1830. He was a leader in church work from his early youth. He became pastor of the Congregational Church in 

Chester, New Jersey soon after 

1800, and remained in that position until his death. He is buried in the churchyard at Chester, near the grave of Rev. S. P. Hughson, who served the Congregational 

Church at Patchogue during the years 1874-1878. Mr. Hughson often spoke of the fame of Stephen Overton as a preacher and as pastor to his father's family. 

2. Anjinettte (Tooker) Ford 

1824-1882 

Anjinette (Tooker) Ford was the daughter of Deacon James 

Tooker, who lived in West Patchogue near the second building of the church. She was born on June 

23, 1824 and died in Elk Point, Dakota on March 21, 1882. She joined the Patchogue Congregational Church when she was twelve years old. She married Rev. George 

Ford in 1846 and both were consecrated as foreign missionaries. 

They at once went to India and spent ten years as missionaries. 

Her health failing, they returned to America, and Mr. Ford served parishes in Massachusetts and in

Dakota. Their son became a successful minister of the Gospel. 

3. Charles Hallock Beale 1854-1939 

Charles Hallock Beale, one of the leading Congregational pastors of the United States, was the 

Ichild of the Patchogue Congregational Church. His grandfather, "Esquire" William C. Beale, lived on East Main Street nearly opposite the present Congregational Church building. He was active in Educational lines and was overseer of the public schools of Brookhaven Town. Charles' father, David 

Brainerd Beale, moved to East 

Patchogue when his sons, Charles and William, were born and were brought up under Congregational influences. Charles joined the Congregational Church in 1866, when he was twelve years old. He became one of the leading Congregational pastors in the United States and had parishes in Boston and the Middle West; but always he was loyal to his relatives in 39

Patchogue, and to the church of his fathers. 

4. William Beale 

William Beale, the younger brother of Charles H. Beale, became a pastor, serving the Methodist 

Conference. He married a Bellport girl, and was successful as a minister of the Gospel. 

5. Daniel Hawkins Overton 

1863-1920 

To the older members of the

Patchogue Congregational Church 

"Dannie" Overton was an outstanding example of the Christian virtues. He was born in West Yaphank in 1863, and was "Cousin" to all the numerous Overtons in its vicinity. He came to Patchogue to work in Wiggins' Store, next store to the second church building. In 1888 he graduated from 

Lafayette College, the most popular man in his class. He attended the Union Theological Seminary, and held a growing pastorate in 

Brooklyn. But his heart was in rural work and he became pastor of the Presbyterian Church of 

Islip, and then he died of a sudden illness on August 22, 1920. 

6. Rev. Charles G. Burd 

Charles G. Burd is the grandson of Austin Roe, former proprietor of Roe's Hotel, Patchogue; and who in turn, was grandson of the 

Austin Roe who was Washington's trusted scout in New York City during the Revolutionary War. 

Mr. Burd was born in Patchogue on February 10, 1875, and joined the Congregational Church on 

February 6, 1887. He graduated from Amherst College in 1898, and from Union Theological Seminary in 1901. He was excellent in athletics, especially baseball. He was associate pastor of the First 

Congregational Church of Newark, N. J. for eleven years. 

He was pastor of the Verona Congregational Church for fourteen years, and is now the pastor emeritus. 

Mr. Burd's sister, Clara M. Burd, was a noted artist. She painted the large picture of the church building on North Ocean Avenue, which contains portraits of members leaving the church after the 

Sunday morning service. 

7. Dr. Edward Percy Case Dr. Edward Percy Case is the son of Deacon Edward Case, and the grandson of Deacon David W. 

Case, both officers of the Patchogue Congregational Church. He was born on July 4, 1883. He graduated from Lafayette College and from the Medical School of the University of Michigan. He was ordained as a medical missionary on September 5, 1911 by a council assembled in the Patch ogue Congregational Church. He went at once to Erzerum, Asia

Minor where he served as Medical missionary until he, and all other "Foreigners," were expelled in 

1917 during the World War.

The Sunday School 

Very little information is available concerning the origin of Sunday Schools in Patchogue. Religious instruction of the children seems to have been attempted during the years of the late forties, by both the 

Congregationalists and the 

Methodists. During the early fifties a Sunday School was held in the district school-house that stood about where the railroad depot is now located, and another was held in the schoolhouse that stood on East Main street, just east of Medford avenue. These were sponsored and supported by John S. Havens, George F. Carman, and Deacon 

Elisha Ackerly, and the teachers were volunteer workers from both the Congregationallist and the Methodist churches.

After the new church buildings were erected in the center of the village, Sunday School was regularly conducted in each church on Sunday afternoons.
