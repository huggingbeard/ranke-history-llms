Preface 

I am very much honoured by the invitation to introduce Madame Markowa's book. For those of us who are in any way acquainted with the tragic history of Poland, it is an experience of rare beauty and consolation to read her story of Saint Hedwig. Here was a Saint of the twelfth century who speaks to the suffering Poles of today with the same exquisite compassion and the same assured hope as she spoke to the men and women of her own time. Perhaps we listen to her all the more gratefully because she had been herself of German blood. We are entranced by her fragile, feminine humanity, and by the supernatural strength with which she transcended it. 

Hedwig was a woman, a wife, a mother. She was also a sovereign; yet her saintliness did not diminish these natural vocations. She was only the more adorable, as she expended upon all her people the charity she received from God. 

Madame Markowa shows her to us as a weak, empty vessel, filled to overbrimming by grace. Her pages open like a mediaeval chronicle, illustrated with all the art of miniature. A pageant of pictures passes before us, each remaining delicately and elaborately graven upon the mind. We are made to feel the windswept, unkind spaces of the Baltic plain; the hurrying to and fro of [7]

of insatiable armies; the rough feudal politics; the boarhunt in the forest, and the fir-trees bending with the snow; the wolves howling and the sledges slipping through the night; the turreted castles, and the convents springing up under the inspiration of Saint Hedwig's sanctity; the white-habited Cistercians at their prayers.

Madame Markowa recreates all this without any taint of false mediaevalism. She describes no golden age peace and piety. It was, on the contrary, an age of violence and pride; and Hedwig appears in lonely protest against the wickedness of her own world. As she grows older, she becomes the more solitary in her saintliness. 

She will heal the beggar at her gate, and give life to the felon condemned to die. But her sons are at mortal odds with each other, and her husband, Henry the

Bearded, incurs the anathema of the Church. All her temporal felicities are stripped from her, one by one. 

She is left naked of everything but the virtues of faith, hope, and charity. These weapons sustain her. 

Madame Markowa does not soften the severities of her story and it is painful reading, at times, even to Catholic ears. Sanctity is difficult to achieve in ourselves, and it is not always comfortable to behold in others. Madame Markowa refuses to spare us the fearful mortifications and the more painful privations, undertaken by Saint Hedwig. She shows her to us separated, in the end, even from her own husband. But she justifies this asceticism not only in itself, but in its fruit. The pains of Saint Hedwig are like the thorns on the stem of the rose. They spring from the same seed as the flower which ravishes the senses. We cannot have the [8]

one without the other. Perhaps we are all in need of violence, and Saint Helwig is a reminder that the Kingdom of Heaven must be taken by storm, that there are some devils which go not out except by prayer and fasting, and that we dare not place any limit to the need for the expiation of sin.

This is, in brief, a very remarkable story. We do not question its essential truth, but we should read it as a poem-and as a prayer-rather than as a psychological or a historical essay. Madame Markowa's literary method, sustained throughout in the historic present, is itself poetic in selection and emphasis. It gives a continual prop for meditation. It is a very considerable achievement to have built up, with such brevity, but also in such detail, the portrait of a woman, whose life is a diagram of sanctity and to have made us breathe the atmosphere and taste the conflicts of a period so remote from our own.

ROBERT SPEAIGHT

THE GLOWING LILY

The Glowing Lily 

1174 The child was born in the castle they call the "Lily 

Gate," an ancestral castle; her father is Lord of Andechs,

Count of Merano, Margrave of Istria and of the Tirol. 

Her mother, Agnes of Dedo, descended from the Carolingian kings, has already brought five children into the world.

The baptism of the newborn child will take place in the chapel of the castle, which is dedicated to the 

Blessed Virgin, St. Nicholas and St. Catherine. She is to receive the name of Hedwig. 

Above the tower of the castle floats the banner of what is, probably, the most renowned branch of the family in the Empire. On one side the banner carries the proud emblem of two sable lions on a field of gold, and on the other a white band crosses the red ground, binding in a sheaf the symbols of dignity strength, purity, and love.

Two lakes wash the foot of the castle of Andechs, from which the snow-covered Alps can be seen, piled up against the horizon. From today, the songs of the nurses will float gently above the cradle of the little countess. [13]

Their songs will be an echo of those that, at the court of the margrave, the wandering minstrels send vibrating from the strings of their lyres. 

The ringing armour of the knights, journeying by thousands toward their vision, toward Jerusalem. 

The cathedrals rising in grey stone, with manycoloured windows, built by the toil of the multitudes. 

This year, 1174, they sing of the saint of Clairvaux, whose quiet strength can overcome the weapons of war. 

These songs will mingle with family memories. . . .

No one in the country but has heard how under the walls of a Lombard town, Berchtold, the kinsman of the infant countess, struck off the head of an arrogant colossus who, mounted on a magnificent charger, had presumed to join issue with him. And the kinsman of the little Hedwig earned a word of praise from the 

Emperor Frederick Barbarossa himself. 

There are other songs too. They tell of princes who, laying aside their swords, their breastplates, their helmets, and their mailed gauntlets, put on habits of rough frieze, and with head uncovered and barefoot, tramp the new highways where convents spring up and burning sermons are preached. 

All these songs together lull to sleep the little margravine, who is in radiant health and suckled at her mother's breast. 1179 Everything here belongs to the little Countess: parents, brothers and sisters, courtiers, knights, retainers. [14]

the towns and villages often spoken of at the margrave's court; the castles where one day follows another in security. Here everything is hers: these avenues in the park where her tiny, childish feet patter, in little satin boots - these avenues whose shady depths beckon —and this sun which mellows the castle, and the stars at the bottom of the well in the courtyard. 

Little Hedwig grows up amid the lavishness of the Bavarian court, surrounded by the power of majesty. 

Seated on the little stool at her mother's feet, she hears from the lips of the Duchess words which delight and fascinate her, and contrast so strongly with everything around her: her mother's words are all of sacrifice, humility, poverty. The little margravine drinks in those words and tears run down her round pink cheeks her mother bends and lays her hand on the child's hair, deftly arranged under the spun gold caul, sprinkled with pearls. The folds of the mother's long gown mingle with those of her daughter's, and together, mother and daughter, they form a group, an entity. 

Thus listening and watching, little Hedwig comes to know the world around her and to know the human heart which beats in the breast of this child of five years. This heart is like an instrument touched by the 

Great Artist alone. 1185 A rambling Benedictine convent, high-walled like a great fortress, stands in the Bavarian countryside above the River Main, at Kissingen. Several gates, cut in the [15]

thick wall, lead into the convent from various directions. 

The exterior of the convent chapel is built protruding and overhanging and the upper window is in the form of a rose. Two towers point to the sky. On one side are grouped the outhouses. The main gate has an iron knocker within reach of the traveller's hand, and when it is struck, the echo of the knocking flies far and wide. 

In this convent of Kissingen, far from the world, the daughters of kings and princes are nurtured for the world. It is five years since Hedwig came. Today she has reached the age of eleven years. When, for the first time she appeared here, in her long, richly embroidered gown, and as she sprang from the litter on to her little feet, the abbess of the convent fixed a searching look upon her. Immediately little Hedwig felt herself to be at home. 

The days, the months, and the years went by. 

Now the little Countess Hedwig can thread a pearl, a ruby or a turquoise with deft fingers on to a gold strand (precious stones lose their mysterious power on contact with an unworthy hand); the woven threads form an embroidered scene which gradually takes form under the needle. The countess can handle, too, a fine pointed reed, dipped in gold powder, or deep crimson, lapis lazuli or purple. . 

Thanks to the work of unknown hands in the convents, the store of illuminated books grows ceaselessly. In the music lessons given by a Benedictine monk, Hedwig's little hand wakes the strings of the harp or the cithera. She possesses, too, the art of writing. 

Her forward steps in knowledge are happy ones: [16]

her learning is a vocation and feeds on an inner strength. Her qualities as a pupil do not escape the keen gaze of Mother Petroussa, one of the nuns, to whom especially the heart of the little countess cleaves. 

Sometimes Mother Petroussa lays her hand on the child's head: "Countess, are you happy among us? Do you not sometimes pine, far from your mother, the 

Duchess?" Hedwig looks at the nun and sighs. “Yes, yes. I love the castle of Andechs and I am longing for it. I can remember the drawbridge and the horses' hooves ringing on it. Today still, I often feel myself walking at my mother's side, holding her hand." The eyes of the little countess are veiled in tears and seek the crucifix which, like a bird in its flight, hangs from the white wall. "Countess Hedwig dreams perhaps of wearing the black habit of our order," says Mother Petroussa that evening to the Mother Abbess. 

That same moment, the countess takes from an open coffer a new robe, and running to her friend, of the same age as herself, begs her to wear the robe, to dim its finery.

Let it be less lovely when Hedwig puts it on! 1186 The driver is high in his seat, and, at his side is the Prince's servant, both in coloured liveries. The carriage, whose white paint is set off with gold, glitters in the autumn sun, as, surrounded by an escort of riders, it skirts the river Main. Near Würzburg, [17]

a princely castle set on a hill looks forth from its narrow windows on the coach driving by, on the horsemen, and listens to the gentle creak of the harness. Some words, louder-sounding, are wafted on the soft air and hang above the river, whose waters flow regularly on. 

In the vineyards through which the company is passing, workers are kneeling at their task among the abundant fruit. They recognise the coach, straighten themselves, remove their broad-brimmed hats.

The sovereign, Berchtold III, leans from the carriage window, in which his head is framed: the rays from the setting sun cast their red glow upon it.

His hand is raised in friendly salutation. "Greetings, vineyards!" "Greetings, harvesters!"

The carriage skirts Würzburg, then leaves it behind. 

At Berchtold's side is seated the guest he is taking with him. They are sitting very close, almost touching. 

A gold tray rests on their knees. On this tray is spread a square of dried and stiffened skin with a chessboard pattern upon it; the chessmen, carved in ebony and ivory, incrusted with gold and mother of pearl, stand upright despite the continual swaying of the coach. 

They send the light glittering back, as one or other of them is lifted and poised in a thoughtful hand. 

Far from the battlefields of the Crusade in which they once both took part, the two friends take their ease and relieve the monotony of their journey in a game of chess. "I play my queen.' "Then I move my king back." [18]

"Too late." "Checkmate."

On the hand of Berchtold, holding the black velour bonnet he has taken from his head, glistens a signet ring of great price. Boleslav the Tall, white-haired before his time, wears, on the hair which reaches down to his shoulders, a square skull-cap, colour of amaranth, adorned with a band of precious stones. A cloak flows from his shoulders and covers his tunic; its clasp bears his arms graven upon it.

These two lords represent two kingdoms, two powers, two interests.

Through the open window of the carriage, a breeze laden with the fragrance of the sun-lit fields fans their faces. "A good while ago now, in 1175, I brought over the Cistercians of Pforte-on-the-Saale. I gave them the monastery newly built at Lübeck." 

Berchtold listens sympathetically. Has his friend some request to make of him? 

For some while their game has been suspended. 

They are nearing Kissingen. 

Berchtold's hand through the carriage window seems to indicate, with an expressive motion, the countryside through which the coach is passing: "the country is fruitful, civilised; its people are happy." 

Yes, the Duke of Tirol and Istria knows of Henry,

Boleslav's young heir, and of the valour and the heart of gold of this prince of eighteen years. No need to speak to him of that. And he also knows his daughter 

Hedwig, despite her remoteness from him. [19]

"The daughter resembles her mother," he murmurs.

And in this exchange of diplomatic words, in this atmosphere of friendship, destiny is forged. 1186 Hedwig and her father are alone with one another in a high-windowed room of the convent at Kissingen. 

Berchtold finds it hard to recognise in this girl of twelve the child he had entrusted to the Abbess, a few years earlier. How she has grown, and how beautiful she has become! The duke draws himself up, in a movement of pride, joy, and knightly feeling. 

Hedwig stands before her father, her eyes cast down; her lips tremble faintly. 

Has the Duke divined her desire? Has Mother 

Petroussa summoned him here? 

Duke Berchtold motions his daughter to a highbacked chair; he himself stands facing her. Hedwig waits, without daring to question. She feels sure that the Duke knows her desires and her projects. The father silently studies his child. There is about her something that he himself cannot define. Perhaps it is the strength that flows from her, and against which he must summon all his authority as father and as sovereign.

On Hedwig's dress there are no precious stones. 

Her fingers are free of rings. Her countenance is instinct with peace.

At least, Berchtold begins to speak. In brief, concise words he specifies the object of his visit. [20]

His words empty her heart of every dream like gold emptied from a purse.

The little countess remains with her eyes cast down; only the pallor of her face shows that she is listening and understands; her lips have ceased to tremble. 

Suddenly the father is looking into his daughter's eyes; such eyes, he has never before seen.

No, it is not a future nun that he has before him, but a newly arisen sovereign. In one swift moment there comes home to her the change in her vocation, and she accepts it at once like a voice from heaven. 

Hedwig is ready; ready for her new road.

And although dressed as before, small in stature, she seems to Berchtold taller and clad in brilliance. "The young Prince Henry is waiting at the castle of Andechs. We are returning there at once." 

Hedwig bows over her father's hand.

At that moment the door opens, and on the threshold appears Boleslav the Tall, the Silesian prince, father of the young Henry. 1186 The marriage is celebrated in the castle chapel, where, twelve years before, the little Hedwig was carried to her baptism. 

The chapel has a long, high nave, which, with the transept, forms a cross. Above the spot where the two arms of the cross meet, a single tower rises from the roof. Through the windows the light steals in, and [21]

falls on the altar, blending the many colours of the stained glass. Above the altar, standing like a stone sarcophagus covered with a white woven pall, is raised a great cross, incrusted with five rubies in token of 

Christ's wounds. 

They light the oil lamp, hanging from a long chain before the altar, and likewise all the spiral candles of yellow wax, flowering in the silver sconces. The bishop, his face toward Hedwig and Henry, keeps his eyelids half closed, as the tall mitre is placed upon his head. In his chasuble, whose full folds hide an alb of the same colour so that only the hem is seen, the bishop extends one white-gloved hand toward his crook and the other toward the betrothed. On each side of them stretches a row of kinsmen and princely guests. 

In her close-fitting robe with its silvered train, Hedwig, wearing scarcely any ornament save on her loose curls a diadem incrusted with pearls, raises and holds with the forefinger of her left hand, the edge of her robe, in token of submission. . . . Her right hand reposes on the right hand of Henry, the Silesian duke. 

The hand of the young knight closes slowly over Hedwig's. At that moment, the bishop returns his crook to his assistant, takes their two hands, and with his other makes the sign of the cross upon them. 

The young Henry's countenance is solemn, and at his slightest movement, his knightly costume gives forth gleams and sparkles of fire. Hedwig's eyes, full of admiration, turn slowly on Henry, and thence to the bishop; then rising higher, above his head, to the hanging crucifix. [22]

In spite of her twelve years, the Silesian princess realises the change that had come in her life, and faces it. Amid the general excitement aroused by the solemn pomp of her marriage, she retains her tact, and also a certain reserve. Her whole mind is given, not to the things she is leaving, but to the things that await her.

The numerous guests who have come from Silesia with Boleslav the Tall and Henry, and the continuous banquets held in the great halls or out in the open, on the grassy lawns of the park, give to the court an unwonted animation.

For many evenings the castle is ablaze with light and resounds with singing.

The moon sails above it, as it might above an ark cut off from the world.

Within the castle, servants girded with coloured sashes glide over the flagstones. They move among the guests seated at the long tables; the delicately chiselled silver goblets they fill with wine, and the wooden ones with beer.

The arts of the kitchen are not without importance here.

On the table in front of each guest and on a cloth ornamented with arabesques, stands a deep vessel, from which rises the steam of successive dishes. Baskets are at hand with sweetmeats and fruits.

In addition to the foreign guests, the whole Bavarian court is there at the festivities with all the dignitaries [23]

and chief men of the country, the knights, the chaplain and Berchtold's chief steward, in whose governance are the kitchens, the minstrels, and almost the whole court, including the Fool.

The ladies in the suite of the young princess, who will accompany her into far-off Silesia, are bustling about their task of packing away the trousseau in many chests.

Their voices resound through the castle, from the chambers of the nobles to the outlying wings. 

The little princess sits at the banquet, motionless, her eyes never leaving the prince.

The little princess cannot hide her emotion and blushes whenever he rises to speak.

When at last the castle sinks to sleep, the young husband and wife go down the broad steps, open the great door, and plunge into the leafy avenues, and are caught up by the gigantic song of the trees. 

The stages of their journey are long, for they often halt at the hospitable courts and in the strongholds of lesser princes. 

The travellers are royally welcomed. 

Is it not the occasion for new festivities?

Are not the guests the reigning prince of Silesia and his son? 

The princess and her court are feted, too.

An unknown world, like a virgin forest, draws one on into its depths.

The eyes and heart of Hedwig are opening to new [24]

horizons. As the road lengthens and the frontiers are crossed, the face of nature alters. Until now, it had seemed to Hedwig that it had only one face, that of her native countryside. . . . To her surprised gaze, great tracts of earth, roughly ploughed or lying fallow, unfold themselves; with them appear wretched strawthatched cottages.

The inhabitants, clad in poor, sack-like garments come out on to their thresholds, and shielding their eyes with their hands, look to see who is passing by. 

For the first time Hedwig is face to face with poverty, with sickness and suffering, the world which, for a child brought up in a castle and in a convent did not exist. 

The earth which she had thought the same everywhere, the unchangeable, begins to bring her some bewildering surprises. 

At the halts, as the horses drink, Henry comes toward his wife's carriage, while she puts out her charming child's head at the open window. 

Henry is anxious: is his wife travelling in comfort? 1187 Almost a year has passed since the arrival at court of the young Hedwig of Andechs. The little princess is with child; a time has come in which the soul feels new stirrings. In her new state, amid the strange atmosphere of an alien tongue and foreign ways of life, moving among the pitfalls of the court, and surrounded by her husband's love, Hedwig does not lose hold on her own helm. [25]

During that time, with a gentle strength above her age, Hedwig avoids her husband's presence. Henry, quite under his wife's spell, submits to her wish. 

In her night of pain, as the women move through her room upon tip-toe, and from the corners flutter frightened whisperings, Hedwig awaits in prayer the sign of a new life. 

That life burns faintly awhile, then after very few days, flickers out.

The infant is covered up, and enveloped, stiff and white, in its winding sheet.

Still under the shadow of this loss, and only dimly realising it, Hedwig shows herself at her husband's side. Her child's face, pure as a tear, is marked by a shadow. That shadow has made of her a grown woman.

In Henry's eyes, his subjects read the admiration he has for his wife-his wife whom he finds on her knees at night when he wakes from his sleep.

In the room a small oil-lamp sheds a flickering light. 

The great carved bed is surmounted by a canopy. At its head, Hedwig has hung an ivory crucifix. 

The prince, in a half-dream of happiness, watches his wife.

The court of Silesia, restless like our own times and rent by conflicting passions, possesses great sinners and perhaps will bring forth great saints.

Since she arrived, the little princess has moved in the very midst of the court, but has let herself be drawn neither one way nor the other. This she opposes reso- [26]

lutely, with the charm of a child. She at once has found her place at court. As if she were deaf and dumb, she avoids the tangle of intrigues and quarrels; above all, the disputes between her father-in-law, Boleslav the Tall, and his sons of two different marriages: Jaroslav, and her husband Henry. It is the state of the country that attracts her interest, and every detail of the daily happenings finds her absorbed. She senses egotism, exploitation, anarchy.

The young princess talks much with her husband of her adopted country, plying him with questions, as if it were some sick person dear to her, who must be cared for; and the thought scarce comes to her that in the avenues of the park the roses are breathing forth their sweetness, and that the happiness of marriage resembles their fragrance.

The court leads a nomadic existence, travelling from one residence to the other, for the sovereign is himself the supreme justiciar, indispensable in turn in each of his domains.

He travels with his family, with the dignitaries of the court, many in number and diverse in merit, with the clergy, the knights, the physician, and the Fool.

Boleslav the Tall always takes with him the young 

Prince Henry, and Henry does not part from his wife.

At banquets, Boleslav seats his son at his side, and Hedwig takes her place on a sign from Henry. 

In the hall, with its vaulted stone roof, long tables and benches are set. The table services, the many [27]

bright candles, the apparel of the guests, all tell clearly of affluence (for many taxes and much tribute flow into the Treasury without counting the revenue from the broad lands and the gold mines, which are worked in that country with exemplary efficiency). A feigned cordiality between sovereign and vassals, often veiling threats or divisions, obscures the political state of the country, far indeed from unity. 

During the banquet, the cups are raised: to your good health. Speeches flow in plenty and the warp and woof of court intrigues and family hatreds are ravelled and unravelled.

But Prince Henry looks out on the world with confidence. His head is high and he looks around him with sparkling eyes. His happiness is at his side. "My dove!" 

Nothing escapes Hedwig's penetrating gaze.

Sometimes she assists her father-in-law and husband in the dispensing of justice. She sees, shadowed by superstition and traces of paganism, and seemingly hewn in wood, the faces of those who are judged with despatch and a light conscience. The severest penalties and fines are exacted for the slightest offences.

Ignorant as yet of the language, she is already beginning to enter the poor cottages, with diffidence at first; and there she finds black bread as the sole food, sullen features, and sombre looks that receive her with mistrust. But the children clutch her dress, and she lifts the smallest in her arms. "Who is it that calls for help in the night?" [28]

"Is it the earth weeping?" At the sumptuously decked tables, at the banquets, there arrives at last, by way of the troubadours, the echo from the far away world.

The songs announce defeat: the sovereign of Egypt and Syria has stormed Jerusalem. 

Late in the night, after the meal, Hedwig remains alone with her husband.

The great bed falls away into the half shadow of the great chamber.

Hedwig takes her veil from her head, and it slips to the floor from her hand, motionless at her side. She kneels at once, and with a look of entreaty, without speaking, she indicates to her husband the place beside her.

With a sudden, violent movement Henry casts himself on his knees, for the first time since his marriage, at the side of Hedwig, whose head is bowed low. 

The prince clearly repeats the prayer, word by word after his wife, and to both of them it seems that on this evening their marriage is consummated a second time. 1188 

Princess Hedwig's second child, the little Sophie, after scarcely more than a few weeks of life, is laid to rest in a church in Lübeck. For the second time Hedwig looks down into an empty cradle, and her hand resting on it, rocks it mechanically. Despite this new grief, it is [29]

manifest to all who surround them that the happiness of husband and wife does not diminish, but rather grows from one day to the other. 

Henry seeks his wife's company and is radiant when she stays at his side.

Wroclaw, the capital, is the most frequent residence of the young couple. The principal square is enclosed by wooden houses, painted in colours; and in front of every house, with its sloping roof, a balcony stands out from the first floor. The river Odra washes the massive bases of the city walls like those of an immense fortress. 

From the palace windows, the princess looks out each day upon the river, the city, and the unfinished cathedral, whose construction was begun the year before. In the morning when she opens her eyes, the sight of the church in building, amid timber and stone, conjures up to her the vision of the Church Eternal, "beautiful as the full moon, terrible as an army in battle array. 99 

And ever there rises within the young princess the conviction of the Church's immortal strength, of her unshakable holiness, despite the human sin that, so long as things divine are entrusted to human hands, will lurk in her bosom.

Hedwig, in spite of her youth, knows from instinct what others know from experience.

Better than the vagabonds, the unprincipled, the idlers, the mockers, who swarm in the land, she knows the failings of human kind—but her knowledge comes through her pity.

Heretical sects are growing, but new saints are rising up. [30]

Since the day Hedwig became Princess of Silesia, the still but unmistakable voices within her, directing her every step, mingle with the voices from without that her place at court obliges her to hear; she must overlook nothing which concerns her fellow beings. 

The voices from without bring news from the whole world, and from every tiny corner of the country; 

Hedwig has to be a master of orchestration not to let them stifle those other hidden voices which seem so weak and are yet so strong!

It is as though the young princess bears naturally within herself the design of all the events in the world; with particular tenderness in her heart she turns her mind to the country that her vocation has made so near and dear to her, and her love for it flows from her desire to do her duty to it. It seems that little by little she herself is becoming the country's valiant chancellor. 

Alone with her husband she questions him at propitious moments about the business of the state and thus becomes the councillor and the inspiration of the prince, although he himself is as yet but the collaborator of his father in the government.

This year the Papal Legate has arrived to regulate the most pressing of the Church's concerns, but the sovereign princes, following the example of other lands, refuse to allow the Church to be independent of the State. [31]

This situation has dragged on since the time of 

Boleslav the Bold, of whom the Chronicle of 1079 relates the following:

The conflict between the King and the Bishop Stanislas Szczepanowski proceeds. The king will recognise no limits to the royal power. His highhanded encroachment on the rights of the Church bring down upon him its condemnation. In his violence the King suspects the Bishop of being at the head of a political plot, lately hatched. His irrevocable decree goes forth against the Bishop: tear him asunder!

At Skalka, whence the neighboring city of Cracow can be seen, a service is in progress in the little Church; the 

Bishop is singing a Mass.

Although it is the order of the King in the madness of his rage, no one is willing to carry it out: neither the knights who stand there motionless, nor even a hired assassin, for a wage of gold.

Here, King Boleslav draws his sword. Vainly they bar his path. Holding his sword above his head, he forces his way into the chapel. Before the first cry of fear makes the vaults ring, the terrible deed has been done.

Hedwig re-reads that page in the chronicle, and the pain of it wrings her heart. She steals a glance at her husband. She divines in the mystery of his eyes that he, too, aspires to unlimited power. 

With what haughty mien his gaze ranges before him; how imperiously his hand clasps the hilt of his sword! 

And as yet, he is but heir to the throne. * * The days flow by at the court of Wroclaw and by God's grace the third child of the royal couple is advancing in vigorous health. Once again it is a girl, Gertrude. [32]

But the fight is not over. Death has not effaced the anathema, and the Pope recalls it by the voice of the Archbishop of Gniezno, who is still waiting for a sign of repentance, if not from the dead prince, at least from his son.

Without this gesture, the Duke will not be buried in Christian ground.

Hedwig turns her pleading toward the new sovereign, who nods his head in sign of assent. Then she weeps for joy, as if for the last time and forever she had won the soul of her husband. From the top of the terrace, the long steep river bank can be seen, stretched at the foot of the castle of Krosno, which is on a hill, and far away in the silence of the valley lies the spacious countryside. 

Three years before, Henry the Bearded died here alone, without the presence of his wife. Today Hedwig is once again in this residence where she has stayed so often in the past, when the Prince brought her there for State Assemblies or for his hunting.

Hedwig is here again today, but how the circumstances have changed.

She is a widow; her daughter Gertrude and her [106]

daughter-in-law, Anne, are with her.

Their only support, her son Henry the Pious, has entered the lists with an enemy whose terrible fame is spreading through the whole world. 

Hedwig has foreseen this moment with the same precision as if she had already lived through it, this moment when she would be obliged to take refuge in some fortress.

The clouds are gathering for a long time, for years; for the past few months they have been quite near, and more and more threatening. 

THE TATAR INVASION 

Do they come from the fastnesses of Asia or from the depths of Hell? 

Wherever they pass they spread death and terror.

Their inroads are more terrible than the overflowing of rivers, than fires. . . .

Towns are ablaze, lit at the four points of the compass by the murderous hands of the Mongols. 

Princes die cruel deaths, crushed, suffocated between two planks. 

China taken and the Volga crossed, the Scourge bears down on Europe. 

Five thousand horsemen are galloping forward on nimble little horses

The warriors bring in their train a countless mass of women, children, slaves, and cattle. 

Is it an unruly mob? [107]

No, it is a disciplined army, obeying its leader like a god, on the least sign from him.

Their arrows easily pierce the thickest armour. Their fighting tactics consist of indescribable agility and cleverness in anticipating the movements of heavily equipped knights. 

The very sight of them strikes chill terror. 

What do they look like?

Short, thickset, flat-nosed, metal-eyed, with black glossy hair, they are like the garments they wear, cut from skins.

Their savagery and cruelty spread everywhere like a hurricane.

Hedwig listens, outwardly calm but enduring hours of torture.

The voice of the Pope calls for supplication to God through penance.

The Tatars reach Hungary and her king is cut to pieces.

Poland, Silesia are invaded. Hedwig considers the possibilities: "My son has thirty thousand men-at-arms behind him. "Yet Sandomir and Cracow are but ruins after their passing. "The nuns at Trebnitz have returned to their families in an attempt to save their lives."

Hedwig gives her only remaining possession on earth; her beloved son; she sends him straight into the jaws of the monster.

For hours on end she beseeches the Creator to deign to have mercy on this head which she has pressed to her heart when he was leaving. [108]

From the first moment of the hasty preparations for the battle, the Duchess bears a cruel certainty in her heart.

At night she is awakened by terrifying dreams, and being unable to escape them, she sends for her ladyin-waiting. Demunda: "You must know, Demunda, that I have lost my son.

My only son has flown from me like a swift bird and 

I shall never again see him on this earth."

Hedwig, pallid-faced, passes through the rooms of the castle, and the smile of pure goodness plays on her countenance.

During this time her son Henry musters his army, divides it into five Divisions, thus displaying that deep military experience and that prudence in contingent circumstances in which he resembles his father. 

He takes the head of one of these Divisions in person. 

He realises clearly that his chances of victory over these wild savages, these cruel pagans, are as nothing before the numerical superiority of the enemy, his strategy in fighting, the terror that he spreads around him. 

In spite of that, Henry the Pious keeps a stout heart and good hope, into which heavenly stars are woven.

Hedwig hears the yells of the invader, the groans of the wounded, the death-rattle of the dying, as if she were present at the battle. "Is it my son who is drawing his last sigh, his last breath?"

The place of the encounter is three days' away from the castle where Hedwig is living, and on the third day of the war a messenger appears before the duchess, her [109]

daughter and her daughter-in-law, with blood flowing from his imperfectly dressed wounds. 

It is Prince John of Janovic, friend of her son and his companion at arms in the battle of Lignica. "In a meadow newly flowered, a desperate encounter is taking place. . . . The Tatars, seeing their luck turning against them, bring up-no one knows from where - a machine made of tubes of copper which, until then, they have kept hidden . . . 

This machine, like a serpent of monstrous length, begins to rain forth death-dealing stones and suffocating fire. 

Stones and fire hit blindly.

The frightened horses rear with the shock, throwing the knights weighed down by their armour and crushing them under their weight.

Banners held high, swords raised, the din of helmets which clash together, the weird shouts of the Tatars, a crowd clenched into a knot, a spasm, a grasp of death.. It is impossible to estimate the number of knights killed. 

The young Prince Henry, seeing his friends mown down around him, throws himself relentlessly into the fray, determined to bar the way to the hideous opponent with his own body.

In vain has he been advised to escape. He fights with steadily growing valour, charges through the enemy, thrusting in all directions. 

A Tatar runs his horse through: he takes another which is offered to him. [110]

His rich armour flashes against the dull ground of the Tatar ranks. grey back They hurl themselves on him, as on to some dazzling light they would like to put out.

A silence of death spreads over the meadow. 

Henry's naked sword is held high above his head, measuring the thrust it is going to give. 

The slit eyes of a Tatar under their vizor examine closely the grooves of the breastplate. By surprise and in the back the blade is plunged: Henry the Pious rolls helpless from his horse."

Duchess Hedwig is standing erect. She stands, her arms supporting her daughter-in-law who has fainted. "The last drop of gall. 

The enemy swoop on the fallen Duke, and after a few minutes there appears, very high above the crowd, amid the shouts of the barbarians, the princely head fixed on a lance.

Those around distinctly hear Hedwig murmur: "Since God wishes it thus, we must accept.' 

And she continues, as if she were alone: "Lord, I thank Thee for having given me a son, who, while he lived, loved and respected me and never made me suffer. "Naturally I should have liked to keep him alive, and yet I am proud that he has given his blood for Thee, O my God, and that Thou hast called him to 

Thy eternal glory. Lord, I commit him to Thy mercy."

Is not the whole life of Hedwig contained in these words? [111]

For years, always, death opens the gate of heaven for Hedwig, if only for an instant. 

And the more often it is opened for her, the more she yearns for it. "Yet a few steps," she tells herself. "Yet another convent to build on the spot where her son fell."

Once more Hedwig undertakes new projects, digs the foundations, chooses the materials, once more.. 

In the reconstructed capital, Princess Anne is acting as Regent and Hedwig settles permanently at Trebnitz. 

All that her husband and her son built with so much care and during so many years slips from her hands and crumbles away

She seems to have built her own life on a tottering foundation, on shifting sands In the seclusion of her cell, in the stillness of the night, the last battle is engaged. Evil, which rises before her like a bright mirage, has not sufficient attraction to make her fall, but it is strong enough to disturb her peace. The world which Hedwig fought within herself during her long life, is trying now to draw her to itself. 

During the daytime, the princess is like a corpse enclosed in a coffin, whose soul has been carried away by the angels.

In spite of that, in spite of her voluntary retirement, nothing escapes her. "Is there a disobedient religious to correct?" "Is there another alms to send?"

Hedwig also asks for the Last Sacraments. [112]

Yet she is quite well.

They try to make her abandon this idea. 

Useless effort. Hedwig knows what she wants. 

She demands the last help. 

She has no more strength for the struggle against herself. 

From the moment she receives Extreme Unction, a violent fever, inexplicable to all, lays Hedwig low. 

Her face burns with so hectic a flush that it radiates on those around her.

Through the open window of her cell, a tree-top can be seen with countless red and gold leaves. And each leaf separately quivers, sighs, breathes, lives. "Is there someone else in the room?" "Hedwig's lips are moving and she is speaking softly to some unseen person."

Now the fever has abated. Her body is cold, her eyes are lucid. Her head is surrounded with a dazzling light, which is finer by far than the ducal crown she wore in times gone by.

Hedwig begs those about her to kneel down. "Do they not see the procession of angels which enters?"

Softly, with a smile of delight, Hedwig closes her lids. "Who would have thought it?"

In the final moment, careful hands had placed between the stiffening fingers of Hedwig, crossed on her breast, a little ivory object, a church carved with great art, the Church her Love. [113]
