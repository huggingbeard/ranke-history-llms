The doorman at the exclusive apartment house opened the plain mahogany door with his pass-key and ushered Audrey Merivale into a hall without windows, so that an electric light burned perpetually. 

From there she went into a den-office-library sort of room that was well furnished in an unimaginative sort of way. "Thank you, I will wait."

Audrey put her brief case and portable typewriter down on the corner of the desk and pulled off her gloves, while the man hovered curiously, taking the opportunity of sidelong glances at the slim figure as he thought: "Not over twenty and a beauty."

When he left her alone she looked around with a feeling of distaste. There was the smell of stale cigar smoke in the room, that was paneled with bookshelves broken only by the fireplace and the folding doors leading into the bedroom.

Against this background Audrey Merivale, head stenographer in an agency that supplied secretaries at a minute's notice, stood out in all the fresh, well-bred youth that was so essentially a part of her fascination. 

Her hair was just a sleek band of gold showing beneath the brim of her hat, and her face was alive with vitality and candor, the kind of expression that took life on a square deal. Her figure was supple, yet slim and curved in her tailor-made suit, showing a hand-worked silk blouse. The gray pearl earrings in her ears almost matched her eyes, that were wide, with arched brows.

Earning a living was not an amusing occupation, but Audrey had accomplished the task quite successfully since her parents had been drowned in a storm off the Jersey coast. It had been a shock to find that there was nothing left for her when the affairs had been cleared up, but now she had forgotten the dim past when money apparently grew at the bank and her father always talked happily of his pictures that never sold.

But there were some jobs she disliked, and this was one; she hated being sent to stray men, young or old, to take down a few letters and to answer their bantering, feeble attempts at flirting with her. And she knew that she had to be frank, indifferent, so creating a barrier they could not pass. It was only when she had regained the street after one of these jobs that she could let her feelings relax and the smile return to her gray eyes.

And it was this capacity for being efficient as well

as beautiful, and aloof, that had made her salary leap up appreciably and her work to be demanded above all others. "Gosh, I sometimes wish I had a turned-up nose and a skin like an elephant," she thought as she got out her machine and prepared for Leonard Peters's arrival. 

The room depressed her; it confirmed all that she had feared from the moment the doorman had ushered her in. Then she went over to a small table on the right of the window and at the back of the large desk set at right angles to the wall. The curtains were too heavy for the room and she wondered if she could move the table that was just the correct height for her typewriter, or pull back the drapery so that the light would fall on her machine.

She stood considering for a moment, shadowed in the gloom of the hangings when she heard a faint noise and the window, already open a little at the bottom, was raised quietly. Audrey stood petrified with fear as she waited, for over the window sill came a leg, and the next moment a man slipped into the room. 

He was young, handsome, fair, with the figure and movements of an athlete. He glanced around the room, and Audrey shrank back, the curtains shielding her from his eyes that were already dazed by coming from the light into the dim room.
