INTRODUCTION. 

This report is concerned with that part of the millinery manufacturing industry which is carried on in the New York Metropolitan Area. It is over this area (embracing the New 

York City and New Jersey markets) • that the Millinery Stabilization Commission has jurisdiction. About two-thirds of this country's millinery manufacturing is carried on in this region. 

Of the 1033 concerns which the Census Bureau reported in the industry in 1935, 665 were located in New York State. These 

New York firms employ not less than 12,022 wage earners and do an annual business of $54, 389,089. The Census reports for the industry for the year 1935 indicate that 103,601,184 trimmed hats were manufactured in that year in the United States and that their value (in terms of manufacturer's selling price) was $84,172,349.

The corresponding totals for New York State are 61,328,928 hats produced with a value of $52,102,513. It follows that New York

State (nearly all of whose millinery manufacturing is done in 

New York City) produced in 1935, 59% of the country's millinery by physical volume and 62% of it by value. The average wholesale value per hat was 81 cents for the United States as a whole and 

85 cents for New York. 3

The figure given here for New York State, and other Census figures cited for New York State for 1935 have been specially reported to the Commission by the Bureau of the Census which generously took the trouble to make a special tabulation of the data for us. The Bureau does not regularly publish these particular figures separately by states. It is safe to say that nearly all of the 665 firms reported for New York State are located in New York City. 2 These e are the Census figures for New York State for 1935. 

The Bureau of the Census, whose returns exclude firms whose annual sales are below $5000, reports 22,770 workers in the industry in 1935. It is commonly stated in the industry that there are about 850 firms in the Metropolitan area, employing about 20,000. These guesses may be too high. Not one of the 272 firm-reports underlying the present analysis reported a 1935 volume of less than $5000.

Fragmentary reports are on file for 155 more firms. Thus a total of 427 firms have submitted more or less complete statements. The present report is based upon the 272 substantially complete reports, and particularly upon the reports for the 199 firms quite fully covered for both 1935 and 1936.

The absence of unit-volume data is serious since it makes it impossible to ascertain whether, e.g., increased dollar volume reflects increased physical volume or merely higher commodity prices. 

There is little doubt that the firms whose reports are analyzed herein are, on the average, more efficient and more successful businesses than the general run of the non-reporting firms in the industry. Certainly the reporting firms keep better books than the non-reporting firms. There are, of course, exceptions. 

Bookkeeping methods in the industry are, in general, slipshod. 

Accounts, too often, are on the cuff or under the hat. Even in the case of the reporting establishments, firm-to-firm variations in practice precipitate difficulties in making comparison of results. Thus, what one concern charges to selling expense, another debits to salaries. Where selling expense or salaries are not separately shown, one or the other, or both, may be concealed under "administrative expense". Since most of the reporting firms are very small partnerships or individual proprietorships, paying practically no salaries except such as are paid to the proprietors or firm-members, it follows that the personnel of the groups receiving profits and salaries, respectively, are substantially identical. There is, therefore, a somewhat similar shifting of bookkeeping entries between "profits" and "salaries". So, some firms which turn up in the red, are seen to have done quite well in the matter of salary drawings, and, on examination, to have been more successful, financially, than certain other firms that were definitely in the black on the face of the audit. In summarizing these several sorts of data two varieties of averages have been utilized: the arithmetic mean (or the ordinary garden variety of average) and the median. These two averages are alike in that they both are devices for discovering, and measuring as accurately as possible, whatever central tendencies there may be in the figures studied. The orginary average is better for some purposes; the median is more reliable for other purposes.

Thus, when we examine a long series of percentages of change the median seems to be a more accurate measure of whatever central tendency there may be in the series. 

The ordinary averages have been calculated by aggregating the dollar amounts of the several items for all the firms in each price-group and dividing by the number of firms reporting the respective items. The ordinary averages for all of the firms, irrespective of price-range, similarly have been found by adding the dollar amounts and dividing by the total number of firms. These

These estimates, because of variations in bookkeeping practice as to what employees are included under "direct labor" and because of similar variations in the data on number of employees, are doubtless subject to a considerable margin of error. 

The results however show such a close correspondence with the averages derived from the Census reports (see Table 26), that we feel warranted in presenting them. The percentage of change derived from the average-earnings estimates are of course much more accurate. since the number of firm members in most cases has remained the same throughout the two years covered the percentage of change in the amounts charged (in the profit and loss account! to salaries becomes also the percentage of change in average (or per capita) yearly salaries. -7-

Average profit-and-loss-account ratios have been found by aggregating the gross-dollar-volume amounts for the firms the average is to cover, aggregating the dollar amounts of the profit-and-loss-account item (e.g. amounts paid for materials) and then dividing the first aggregate into the second. 

The medians have been found by arranging the firm-items desired to be averaged in a continuous series beginning with the largest item and ending with the smallest (in the case of profits or profit-ratios, ending with the grestest loss, or loss ratio) or vice versa and then spotting the middle item, i.e., the median. 

Thus, when profits (or losses) are so handled the median or profits figure for 1935 turns out to be -$144 or a loss of $144 for the median (or average) firm; when profit (or loss) ratios are so handled, the median for 1936 appears as -0.2 percent, and when changes in dollar volume for the 199 firms are so treated the median turns out to be 5.9% indicating that, on the average, dollar sales volume increased 5.9 percent from 1935 to 1936. -8-

II. 

THE STATE OF THE INDUSTRY. 

1. 

Amounts of Income, Expense and Profit Items and Changes Therein.

We now can turn from methods to results. 

It is appropriate, at the outset, to report what information is at hand concerning the extent of business mortality in the industry. The frequency with which millinery manufacturing establishments go out of business is, perhaps, the most striking index to the condition of the industry. Examination of the 

Commission's file of firms registered with it reveals that of 564 registered firms in business at the beginning of 1936, 146 or 26% are no longer in business. In addition, 5 of the 164 firms moved out of town. The mortality percentage reported for the New 

York market for 1934 (in the First Annual Report of the Code Authority, p. 9) was 20%. It is significant that the business mortality of the firms from which the Commission has received reports of their business operations is very much lower than that for the mine-run of firms registered. As compared with the general mortality percentage of 26% above reported, the 213 firms which reported their 1935 operations to the Commission was only 7%. It strikingly appears that concerns which keep adequate records of their operations have vastly better prospects of business survival. 

The 199 firms which are intensively analyzed here had a dollar sales volume in 1935 of $19,703,533. end in 1936 a volume of $21,313,296. While all of the firms whose reports are here analyzed have reported their gross dollar volume of business, none of them has supplied data on the physical, or unit, volume of production.

This lack is an unfortunate one because, as pointed out above, it makes it impossible to determine to what extent, if at all, the 8 percent increase in dollar volume of business reflects enhancement in factory prices, and to what extent, if any, it reflects increase in the number of hats manufactured. 

What little information is available on the physical volume of production in the industry during the period here covered is to be found in the reports of the Bureau of the Census on the census of manufacturers for 1935. Again unfortunately, the census figures on this subject are not broken down by states. The

III. 

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE OPERATIONS OF SUCCESSFUL AND UNSUCCESSFUL FIRMS. 

It is evident that the condition of the industry, as Calvin Coolidge was said to have said about the condition of the country in the later Hoover years - is not good. A small - a very smallproportion of the firms are operating successfully. The bulk of the trade, indubitably, is skating along just over the edge on the red-ink side. Moreover, there are, additionally, about as many which are deep in the red as there are concerns safely in the black. 

The circumstances contributing to this deplorable business situation are numerous and complex. To a considerable degree, no doubt, they are unfathomable. Certainly no pretense is made here to fathom them or exhaustively explore them. The present aim is a less ambitious 

It is to examine the limited and none too accurate records that we have at hand with an eye to the discovery, if possible, of some of the operating conditions and practices that seem to spell out business success and some of the conditions and practices that seem to spell out failure. one.

The data we use are the same as those analyzed in the attempt made in the previous section to throw some light on the condition of the industry at present and in the near past, namely, the profit and loss accounts of our 272 reporting firms and, particularly, of the 199 identical firms reporting for both 1935 and 1936. The method used is that of segregating the concerns which have experienced marked success, lining them up on one side for some scrutiny of their habits and similarly herding off to the other side the firms which, on the record, are clearly demonstrated business failures and giving them the same sort of once over. It was not - and is not now expected that such an exploratory stratagem could possibly do more than provide the basis for intelligent hunches, which in turn could be the basis for a few common-sense suggestions that might help the industry to inch along hereafter in the general direction of what millinery men wistfully call "the black", instead of creeping lower into "the red". It is realized that often the untoward results that in these pages we blithely charge to syndicate selling, or to failure to sell "net" (or, at least, at 7/10 E.O.M.) may in many instances be due to bad luck, or chance, or "Acts of God. It is not forgotten, either, that relations among operating factors or conditions that appear to be causal may in fact not be so at all.

The first task, obviously, must be that of fixing upon a dependable criterion, or measure, of business success, using that phrase (as practical men must, and as, surely, is appropriate in an investigation like the present one) in the sense of pecuniary success. At the outset it was thought that perhaps something more than the rate of profit would have to be considered. It already has been pointed out that in this industry both profits (if any) and salaries accrue to almost exactly the same group of men the proprietors or firm members. Since almost all of the firms are either partnerships or proprietorships practically all profits go to the active managers. Also practically none other than active managers draws salary. Additionally, not a few cases were noted 

-39-

in which, with profits near, or beyond, the "break-even" line, generous sums were drawn in salaries. Also, other instances were noted wherein salaries were low but profits adequate or even ample. 

It was thought, therefore, that there might be an inverse relationship between salaries and profits and that, in addition to, or possibly instead of the rate of net profits, use should be made of the ratio of "profits-plus-salaries" to gross sales. A simple test of this theory was made by constructing a "scatter diagram" 

(Fig. F). On the diagram each dot represents one of the 184 

firms which reported salaries as well as profits. The position of each dot indicates, for the firm it represents, both the 1936 profits ratio and the 1936 salary ratio-to-gross. The vertical distance of the dot above the bottom line is proportionate to the profit percentage on the scale set out along the left-hand side of the chart; it is proportionate, in other words, to the percentage of net profit to gross.

The horizontal distance of the same dot from the line at the left side is proportionate to the percentage of salary to gross. 

A direct (or positive) relationship (or correlation) between profits and salaries would be indicated by a clustering of the dots along a line drawn diagonally from the lower left-hand corner of the chart to its upper right-hand corner. An inverse (or negative) correlation would be shown in the measure that the dots tended to cluster along a line drawn diagonally from the lower right-hand to the upper left-hand corner. A grouping of this latter type would be formed, then, if our hypothesis that salaries tend to pinch-hit for profits (or profits for salaries) were in accord with conditions in the industry. 

The dots in the diagram show no appreciable tendency toward either pattern. The conclusion seems inescapable that there is, after all, no appreciable correlation, either positive or negative, between salary and net profit ratios. The profits-plus-salary ratio criterion, therefore, is out of the window. 

It cannot be denied, however, that salaries are that salaries are a factor entering into the degree of profitableness of some firms. Reference to the "scatter" in Fig. F will show that 5 firms paid themselves salaries in amounts which figured between 20 and 25 per cent of their respective dollar volumes. There can be little doubt that these five concerns are better off pecuniarily, despite the fact that they all were "in the red", than the group of 15 firms which made profits ranging up to 5 per cent, but whose salary rates were well below 5 per cent. For this reason we have not failed to include some appraisal of the salary item as an ancillary factor hearing upon profitableness of operation. But in one sense salaries - and this seems peculiarly true in the millinery industry - are simply one cost item- the cost of managerial and supervisory service. The simple rates of profit serve adequately, without modification, when the question is: what was the profit condition in a given year in this firm and in that firm and what practices were followed, what cost conditions existed, in this firm and in that firm when they were in this profit position? 

But if there is need to ask a different sort of question altogether: what change took place in the rate of profits in this firm or that firm between 1935 and 1936 and what concomitant changes took place in, say, indirect expense ratios?-a different criterion may be appropriate. At first blush it may be thought that the percentage of change in the rate of profit would serve. But consideration of this device reveals its inadequacy. When the profit rate is a profit-in-the-red rate one year and a profit-in-the-black rate the next year percentages cave in. A firm which has a profit ratio of 

0.1 per cent in 1935, and, doing slightly better in 1936, shows a rate of 0.2 would have to be ticketed with an increase in rate of profit of 100 per cent !

Considerations of this nature have led to the use for our second and only other measure of business success the "points of difference" between the profit ratios for any two (or more) periods. 

The data of Table 26 are assembled on the basis solely of straight rates of net profit. The method of assembling the data is as follows, using for example the block of data for 1935 at the left of the table: the tabulation cards for the 199 identical firms (one card to a firm) are arranged in the order of declining magnitudes of the 1935 ratios of net profits to gross, so that the firm-card bearing the highest ratio of all is at the top of the pile and the card bearing the very lowest ratio at the bottom. The highest 10 per cent of the cards (in this case 21 of them) are segregated; also the lowest 10 per cent (another group of 21). The 21 rates of discount, 21 materials-cost ratios, labor-cost ratios etc., on the top 21 cards are listed and their respective averages found. These averages (both medians and means) are listed in column one as the characteristic ratios for the "best" (1. e. most successful) ten per cent of the 199 firms.

Similarly, averages for the several cost items are made for the "worst" firms and listed in the third column. For convenience, the arithmetic averages for all of the 199 concerns combined are set down in the middle, as representative of the typical or run-ofmine establishment. The evidence about syndicates already set out in Table 25, above, showing that the least successful price-group of firms concentrated their sales much more heavily through syndicates than any of the other groups, is partially contradicted by the 1935 data of Table 26 and wholly contradicted by the 1936 figures of that table which indicate that the "best" firms deal more largely with syndicates. It is, of course, to the manufacturer's interest to distribute his goods though channels which will yield him the greatest profits. From this point of view it is of course desirable to know as definitely as possible what is the most financially advantageous method of distribution for the manufacturer to follow. 

The returns from the 199 identical millinery manufacturing concerns are by no means conclusive in this respect. IV. CONCLUSIONS. 

The preceding comparison of the operating conditions existing in, and the business practices followed by, the markedly successful and the markedly unsuccessful firms, respectively, suggests some obvious answers to the question: What shall the millinery manufacturer do to be saved? As to some aspects of their business record the lessons brought home are unmistakable; as to some they are inconclusive. 

Consider first the two points in respect to which the lessons are inconclusive: namely, the important matters of the discount rate and the distributive channel used. It is not clearly demonstrated that the more successful firms are less prone to sell through syndicates. Neither is the reverse of this proposition proved. Fig I indicates that, insofar as the 183 firms reporting selling channels may be considered a representative sample, there is no noticeable correlation between the profitability of the manufacturers' business operations in 1936 and the extent of their dealings with the syndicates. The data of Table 26 for 1936 indicate that both the "best" and the "worst" firms dealt less generally with syndicates than did the general run of firms and that the most successful firms dealt with them more than the unsuccessful ones. The data for 1935 in the same table also indicate that both the ten per cent "best" and the ten per cent "worst" firms used the syndicate method of selling less extensively than the general run of firms. Aside from this, the data for 

1935 are flatly contradictory, the data based on ordinary averages showing a more general reliance upon syndicates by the "best" than by the "worst" concerns and the data based upon medians showing precisely the reverse. The figures in Table 27 show that in the ten per cent of the firms which showed the greatest improvement from 1935 to 1936 in their profit positions sold smaller proportions of their goods through syndicates than did the ten per cent of the firms which suffered the greatest increases in losses. Here the result is the same whichever type of average is used. And, as before, the data in Table 27 indicate that both the firms which improved most and those which declined most resorted less extensively to syndicate selling than the general run of firms. 

Finally, it has been shown (in Table 25) that, when the distribution-channel percentages are broken down by price-range group, the price group (Group I) which showed the least successful results (both in 1935 and 1936) was the group which sold more extensively through syndicates than did any of the other price groups.

The other factor in respect to which the data are inconclusive is that of discounts on gross sales. We have seen (Fig. T) that when the points marking the relationships between 1936 rates of discount and of profit are plotted they show no appreciable correlation, either positive or negative. Moreover, when the 10 per cent of the 

199 firms with the highest rates of profit in 1936 and the 10 per cent of them which incurred the heaviest losses are separately segregated and their operating characteristics compared, we have found (Table 26) that discounts were somewhat higher in the most successful and somewhat lower in the least successful firms. 

-49-

The corresponding comparison for 1935 is inconclusive, pointing to discounts higher in the successful than in the unsuccessful firms where the ordinary average is used and to discounts lower in successful than in the unsuccessful firms where the median (average) is used. The data on discounts in Table 27 indicated that, along with the general increase in discount rates from 1935 to 1936, there was a somewhat greater increase in discounts in the 10% of the firms with the highest rates of profit and in the 10% of the firms with the greatest rates of loss, but that the rate of increase in the discount rate was somewhat higher in the "best" than it was in the "worst" firms. When, however, the 10 per cent of the 199 firms which increased their profit positions most between 1935 and 1936 are compared with the 10 per cent of them which suffered the greatest increase in losses in that period, it appears that, while the discount rate in the general run of firms increased about 1 per cent, on the average, it increased 3 per cent among the firms whose losses declined most, at the same time that it declined about 1/2 of one per cent among the 10 per cent of the firms whose profits increased most. 

Evidently, then, neither the allowance of high discounts nor the heavy concentration of sales through syndicates is necessarily fatal to success. Some millinery manufacturers, it seems, are making fair profits, either in spite of, or because of, their policy on discounts and their practice in respect to syndicate selling. If two firms have exactly the same cost and expense ratios (apart from discounts) and the same sales volume, it would seem that that one will do best which operates with the lower discount rate. Whether the same conclusion can be drawn with respect to syndicate selling it is impossible to say. Finally it must be emphasized once more that all our figures respecting discounts are seriously vitiated by the fact that many firms achieve a technical avoidance of discounts by selling at proportionately lower "net" prices.

When we turn, however, to other aspects of the businesses examined, we find such clear contrasts between the conditions and characteristics marking the strikingly successful firms on the one hand and those marking the strikingly unsuccessful ones on the other hand that the lessons implied in the two sets of experiences seem fairly obvious. It appears from the data of Table 26 that in both years the firms that enjoyed the highest rates of net profit had much higher gross profits per dollar of gross sales than did the firms which incurred the heaviest net rates of loss. For the "best" firms gross profits were 28 cents on the dollar for 1935 and 30 cents for 1936. For the "worst" firms gross profits were 

23 cents on the dollar in each year. (For the two hundred firms gross profits were 25 cents on the dollar in 1935 and 24 cents in 1936). This superiority of the "best" firms in respect to gross profits evidently was the result of lower ratios to gross for both materials and labor. The "worst" firms spent for materials 41.3 cents on the dollar in 1935 and 48.3 cents on the dollar in 1936, while the "best" firms spent for materials only 40.9 cents on the dollar in 1935 and only 40.2 cents in 1936. For direct labor the "worst" firms spent 31 cents on the dollar in 1935 and 28 cents -50-

in 1936, while the "best" concerns spent only 25 cents in 1935 and 

23 cents in 1936.

This does not mean that the "best" firms profited at the expense of labor or that either the rates paid to labor or the earnings of the workers were lower in the more successful firms. 

The evidence, on whole, indicates on the contrary, as will be set out more in detail below, that although in 1935 the workers in the "best" firms had lower average yearly earnings than did those in the worst firms, in 1936 the workers in the "best" firms had appreciably higher annual earnings than did those in the worst firms. It will be noted also that the changes in labor earnings from 1935 to 1936 were more favorable for workers in the "best than for those in the "worst" firms and that the "best" firms appear to employ more workers than the "worst" ones. It would seem, therefore, that the advantage of the "best" firms in respect to unit labor costs was built upon better management and utilization of the labor power of their workers, rather than upon the cutting of wage rates or the restriction of employment. 

Where the comparison is made (as it is in Table 27) in terms of change of conditions rather than conditions existing in a given period, the evidence respecting the rate of gross profit and its component rates confirms the implications of Table 26. It appears that while the rate of gross profit in the firms whose 1936 rates of net profit were lowest had declined 16 per cent, the corresponding rate had increased 5 per cent, on the average, in the firms whose 1936 rates of net profit were highest. The number of cents on the dollar paid for materials had increased 2.5 per cent in the "best" and 6.7 per cent in the "worst" firms. The number of cents on the dollar paid for direct labor decreased 3.7 per cent in the "best" while it increased 2.8 per cent in the "worst" firms. 

Similar contrasts appear in the figures on indirect costs. 

Here, indeed, we note the most striking contrasts of all. It appears from the data of Table 26 and the charts supplementing it, that while the "best" firms, on the average, had indirect costs of 

18 cents on the dollar in 1935 and 19 cents in 1936, the "worst" firms staggered under over-head burdens of 32 cents on the dollar in 1935 and 35 cents in 1936. Examination of the five classified items of overhead indicates that the superiority of the "best" firms was chiefly due to their lower cost ratios for administrative expense and shipping costs. Although in 1936 all of the four items shown were lower for the "best" than for the "worst" firms, in 1935 the "best" firms incurred slightly higher unit costs for selling and for the purchase of blocks and dies than did the "worst" firms. 

The figures (in Table 27) reflecting comparative changes in overhead costs in the "best" and "worst carry a similar message.

The firms whose rates of net profit were highest in 1936 had achieved (since the previous year) a reduction of 9 per cent in the number of cents in each sales dollar spent for overhead, while those whose profits in 1936 were least (1.e. net losses greatest) had -51-

suffered an increase of 2 per cent on the average, in the number of cents per sales dollar laid out for overhead. The firms which were in the "best" position in 1936 had managed to reduce unit costs of administration 13 per cent, while in the firms in the "worst" positions in that year such costs increased 13 per cent. Similarly unit costs of selling had declined 18 per cent in the best and 5 per cent in the worst firms. Unit costs of blocks and dies had declined 17 per cent in the best, while they increased 16 per cent in the worst, firms. Unit costs of shipping had declined 5 per cent in the best, while they increased 9 per cent in worst, firms. Unit costs of salaries of firm members had increased 13 per cent in the best, while they increased 23 per cent in the worst, firms. 

Average annual labor earnings in the "best" firms were about $889 in 1935 and $771 in 1936, while in the "worst" concerns they were $950 in 1935 and $670 in 1936. Such earnings in the firms with the highest rates of profit in 1936 had increased 2 per cent since the year before while in the "worst" firms they had declined, on the average, 18 per cent. As might be expected the average yearly salaries of firm members in both years were uniformly higher in the "best" than in the "worst" firms. The change in average salaries, however, seems to have been less favorable in the "best" than in the "worst" firms. In the firms which were in the worst profit position in 1936 average salaries had declined 3 per cent from the previous year while in the best firms they had declined 10 per cent. 

In Table 27, and the graphs supplementing it, are some significant comparisons between the "best" and the "worst" firms relating, not to ratios-to-gross, but to the changes in the absolute dollar amounts expended. While the two hundred firms, on the average, disbursed 12 per cent more in discounts in 1936 than in 1935, the "best" firms expended 14 per cent more and the "worst" ones one per cent less - a "fact" which would be more disconcerting if we were unaware of the frequent sales on a "net" basis and of their distorting effect upon the figures. The firms which were in the "best" profit position in 1936 had increased the amount spent for materials 8 per cent since the previous year, in contrast with the concerns in the "worst" profit (loss) positions that year, which had increased their disbursements for materials 20 per cent, while for the whole 200 firms such disbursements had increased 13 per cent. 

Similarly, the concerns which were in the best profit position in 1936 had increased the amount spent since 1935 for direct labor by 4 per cent, while the firms in the worst position that year had reduced such payments 6 per cent since 1935. 

The data in Tables 26 and 27 on dollar volume for the "best" and "worst" firms are not less significant than the figures on other operating items that have been reviewed. The "best" firms definitely have had much more volume than the "worst" firms. In 1935 the average volume for the "best" firms (using the ordinary average) was about $92,000, in contrast with $50,000 for the worst firms, while the whole two hundred firms averaged $100,000. Differences of the same sort are evident for 1935. The contrast is no less striking with respect to changes in volume. The firms which in 1936 had the highest rates of profit had experienced an increase in volume over -52-

the previous year of 8 per cent, while the firms which were on the lowest loss levels in 1936 had suffered declines of 19 per cent in dollar volume since 1935.

The long-run low profits situation in the industry doubtless is due to many causes. Excessive discounts and, particularly, materials costs are part of the answer. Overhead, selling and administrative costs are another part. Managerial inefficiency and inefficiency of labor play their roles. Although wages are high in this industry compared to many other trades, they are certainly not too high. Moreover it is not workers earnings or even weekly wage rates that are relevant here; it is labor cost per unit of output, which conceivably may be cut down by better management and better and more skillful application of his labor by the worker without cutting wage rates.

The figures which have been passed in review seem to indicate that the bad condition of the industry cannot be charged primarily to syndicate selling or to undue yielding by the manufacturer in the matter of discounts. True, other factors being the same, the manufacturer will certainly be more successful if he keeps discounts down than if he does not. He will also fare better if he successfully resists the buying pressure of syndicates (or, for that matter, of any other buying group) than if he does not so resist. 

But, by and large, it seems to be true that the chief enemy of the millinery manufacturer is himself. More precisely the chief obstacle to profitable results is poor management, insufficient attention to such overhead items as administrative and selling costs, lack of sustained effort in the direction of economy on overhead, utilization of labor, the buying and use of raw materials. 

It was not merely luck that enabled ten per cent of the two hundred firms to make around ten per cent net profit both in 1935 and 1936. 

A large part of the answer to the question: How did they do it? is embalmed in the comparative figures which have been here reviewed. 

Finally, it will have been noticed that the anlysis which has been made of the contrasting business experiences of the "best" and the "worst" in millinery manufacturing strikingly confirms the theory upon which the manufacturers' association and the union acted in embarking upon the campaign of stabilization which it is the function of the Millinery Stabilization Commission (their joint creation) to promote. Labor does stand to gain by improvement of the profit position of the firms to which it sells its services. 

Increased employment, maintained high rates of wages, sustained and increased labor earnings are not to be squeezed successfully out of establishments that are struggling head over ears in the red.

Management does stand to gain by full cooperation with a labor organization wise enough to recognize the mutual gains to be won by a joint campaign for the elimination of the bad practices that explain business failure. The millinery manufacturers of the New 

York New Jersey Metropolitan area have demonstrated their practical wisdom in committing themselves to such cooperation.

APPENDIX IV THE SUPPLEMENTARY AGREEMENT. 

A close study of the Millinery Industry since the National Recovery Act was declared unconstitutional reveals the fact that the Millinery Industry is now in a state of chaos due to the lack of planning and to the breaking down of the trade practice provisions in its former code, so that neither the employer nor theirworkers can have any assurance of security either in the obtaining of business or in the obtaining of employment. A coordinating agency should be set up with the cooperation of both employers and workers which should supply the necessary information concerning the management and the conduct of the Industry and should devote itself to the consideration of plans and policies for the rehabilitation of the Industry so that the present aimless and planless tendencies of the Industry may be curbed and the evils from which it is suffering checked. 

The consumers are an important factor in the successful execution of any plans that may be decided upon. Any proposal for the meeting of the need referred to above must take into account the possibility of enlisting the cooperation of consumers in the execution of the plans agreed upon. 

In view of the foregoing, it is mutually agreed by the parties hereto that they will jointly cooperate in establishing for the benefit and protection of the Millinery Industry of New York City, a MILLINERY STABILIZATION COMMISSION, which is hereby created. The said COMMISSION shall consist of three members, to be mutually agreed upon, but none of whom shall be a member of the Industry or the Union. 

The purpose of the said COMMISSION shall be to formulate fair trade practices and to govern the relationship of the members of the Industry towards each other and toward other factors in the Industry; to obtain from all members of the Industry in New York City reports as to operating costs in all factories, to survey the Millinery Industry, to study its evils and formulate and execute plans for its rehabilitation, to receive complaints of violations, to provide hearings thereon and to adjust such complaints. 

The COMMISSION shall adopt such measures as will, in the opinion of its members, insure compliance by members of the Industry, with the rules, regulations and provisions which the COMMISSION may promulgate for the effective enforcement of the powers and duties conferred upon it by these provisions. 

The COMMISSION shall have the right to investigate any or all books and records of the manufacturers for the purpose of ascertaining whether the rules and regulations and fair trade practice provisions formulated by the COMMISSION are being observed. Information obtained as a result of such investigations shall be held strictly confidential, except for compliance purposes. -72-

In order to further effectuate the provisions of this agreement and the rules and regulations of the MILLINERY 

STABILIZATION COMMISSION it is further mutually agreed that all millinery manufactured or distributed by the members of the Industry under contractual relationship with the Union shall bear a "Consumers" Protective Label", to be adopted and approved by the COMMISSION. 

It is further agreed that the control and distribution of the said Label shall be vested in the said COMMISSION. The 

COMMISSION shall make a charge to the members of Industry for such Labels, the charge not to exceed the cost of printing the same and the necessary expenses of administration incurred by the COMMISSION in discharging the duties and functions conferred by these provisions. The COMMISSION shall adopt suitable rules and regulations for the distribution and use of such labels, and such administrative measures as will make the use of the Label an effective means of enforcing the fair trade practices which the COMMISSION shall formulate. 

The Union agrees that in any contracts it may enter into with independent manufacturers, it will be provided that such independent manufacturers shall be required to subscribe to the supervision of the said MILLINERY STABILIZATION COMMISSION and to the use of the "Consumers" Protective Label".

The Union hereby agrees to submit to the COMMISSION a list of all independent shops with whom it has contractual relations, and to notify the COMMISSION of any changes or additions thereto as they occur. The Association agrees to submit to the COMMISSION a list of its members and to notify the COMMISS- ION of all changes or additions thereto as they occur. 

Either party to the collective Agreement shall have the right to terminate this Supplementary Agreement by serving notice in writing at least ninety (90) days in advance of its intention to effect such termination or sooner by mutual consent of both parties.

This memorandum shall not be deemed to be any modification, alteration or amendment of or to the collective agreement dated the 1st day of January, 1936, by and between

EASTERN WOMEN'S HEADWEAR ASSOCIATION, INC., and the CAP & MILLINERY DEPARTMENT OF THE UNITED HATTERS, CAP & MILLINERY WORKERS INTERNATIONAL UNION and the JOINT BOARD OF MILLINERY WORKERS UNION, LOCALS 24 and 42 of the said CAP & MILLINERY 

DEPARTMENT.

Nor shall it be deemed to be intended nor contem plated that the MILLINERY STABILIZATION COMMISSION or any other agency thereof, or created thereby, shall in any way be empowered or authorized to alter, amend, clarify or do any other matter or thing with respect to the collective agreement dated the 31st of January, 1936, as aforesaid. Nor shall it be contemplated nor understood that this agreement and the collective agreement dated the 31st day of January, 1936, as aforesaid, shall in any way be interrelated or interdependent, excepting in the -73-
