TO MY PARENTS

FOREWORD 

THE title, The Prospects of Philosophy, was chosen for the present work, because it is intended to furnish the reader a bird's-eye view of the immense field of philosophy and to indicate briefly some of the tasks which await the philosophers of the future. It is an attempt to gauge the expectations of philosophy and divine its probable orientation, as far as this is possible from a general examination of its present status. 

It is hoped that this book may aid in dispelling some erroneous notions concerning philosophy and its relation to the special sciences. It endeavors to emphasize the desirability of a closer cooperation between scientists and philosophers. It is not intended to be a textbook or an introduction to philosophy. It is written for those who know something of philosophy and its history. 

It has been composed primarily for students who may be thinking of devoting some time to a more intensive study of philosophy, but who are undecided in which direction to turn and know not what specialty to select.

It is designed to awaken interest and arouse the impulse to deeper investigation of some special portion of the vast area of philosophy. In order to excite curiosity and encourage further attention the mysterious veil concealing the various problems of philosophy has only partially been lifted.

The PROSPECTS OF PHILOSOPHY CHAPTER ONE PRELIMINARY OBSERVATIONS 

IN RECENT years the study of philosophy has been taking on a new significance, and it appears destined to exert an extensive influence and a wider control over human affairs. Generally speaking, the centers from which philosophy radiates are the higher seats of learning, the colleges and universities. Despite certain trends in the universities themselves distinctly inimical to philosophy, it continues to be taught, and we have every reason to believe that it will continue to hold its place in the curriculum of the schools. Within the precincts of the universities there are found conflicting views regarding the supposed exclusiveness of the range of philosophy and its title to a field on which no other science may encroach. Some regard philosophy as a venerable relic of the past. They aver that such a universal science has quite outlived its usefulness. However, even those who do not share that opinion often ask the question, what has the future in store for philosophy? Has it any promising future at all? These are legitimate questions which can probably be answered by those who are bet- 1

ter acquainted with philosophy, those who have devoted their lives to the study of philosophy, the so-called professional philosophers. It will escape no one, however, that their answers must be more or less problematic. 

The history of philosophy is often regarded as an agglomeration of discarded theories, of premature generalizations that have been thrown overboard. Both sides of the path of the history of philosophy are marked by lateral moraines consisting of doctrines no longer tenable, but bearing unmistakable testimony to the efforts of thinkers of bygone days. Thus the history of philosophy may be likened to a museum filled with objects that have lost their actuality and their utility; they have but an historical value. This is true to a certain extent, but this criticism is not applicable to philosophic thinking alone, as distinguished from scientific thinking. 

As one example from the history of science one may cite the old phlogiston theory designed to explain combustion. That theory was given a coup de grâce and was definitely disposed of by the well known discoveries of Lavoisier and Priestley. The hypothesis of the all-pervading ether of the physicists of our own day may be cited as an example of something which cannot exist in reality, if for no other reason than that it has been endowed with the mutually exclusive properties of limitless elasticity and extreme rigidity. It is not surprising that many thinkers should have rejected the doctrine, holding it to be a provisional supposition no longer needed. The histories of science and philosophy plainly evince that both scientific thinking and philosophical

3 thinking have been similar in the past, since both have been groping in the dark. On the other hand, the history of both scientific and philosophical thought may be regarded as a series of attempts, more or less successful, to find the truth. The so called failures then appear as stepping stones, as necessary conditions of progress and advancement. It is fortunate that in the past there were found resolute men who did not hesitate to propose various hypotheses as serious attempts to give an adequate account of reality. Bertrand Russell is right when he contends that philosophers should be bold in advancing hypotheses. It is to be hoped that the future will bring thinkers who will not shrink from expressing their views for fear of hostile criticism. At all events, criticism will test their doctrines and clarify their concepts.

The past of philosophy may be compared to a broad avenue lined on both sides with abandoned doctrines, but also with monumental achievements of which mankind may justly be proud. A gaze down that avenue discloses an imposing vista of the accomplishments of the greatest mathematicians, scientists, and philosophers the world has ever known. It should be borne in mind that philosophers are obliged to keep in close contact with the sciences. They must not be regarded as a group of impractical dreamers. Some of the most celebrated mathematicians, to mention only Descartes and Leibnitz, are included among the philosophers of highest rank. 

Many of the supreme scientific thinkers were also noted philosophers, and philosophers also have manifested their

competence in the sciences. Whatever the future may hold in store for philosophy, whatever may be its vicissitudes, all will agree that the labors of philosophers must continue because the task of philosophy has not been completed. An immeasurably great work lies before it; ever widening horizons greet its eye.

Even without a systematic and careful survey the future of philosophy looks rosy and promising. As a view of the past reveals a broad avenue of solid gains and permanent acquisitions so the vista of the future discloses the dim outlines of baffling problems inviting the attention of earnest thinkers who are not content with past explanations. No doubt the unsolved problems of philosophy will continue to attract the attention of thinkers of succeeding generations, of budding philosophers fired by a sacred enthusiasm. One may conjecture that philosophy, particularly in this country, will very probably consist of restatements, reëxaminations, and revaluations of the thought of the past. However, it will readily be acknowledged that it may not exclusively consist of such refurbishings of outworn teachings. In view of the remarkable conquests of the special sciences in recent years, it is becoming more and more apparent that some synthesis will be required. It is not improbable that many partial syntheses will make their appearance before a more embracing and universal unification of all knowledge will be brought about in order to satisfy the unifying tendencies of the mind of man. While it remains true that a system of philosophy cannot be built by employing the method of mass pro-

duction, nevertheless numerous partial contributions of individual thinkers may be skillfully adjusted and made to fit into a grand edifice by some greater and more universal mind in the future, which may succeed in creating a more satisfactory philosophic construction, a harmonious whole or totality. Distinctly philosophic thinking is primarily to be ascribed to individual men, not groups of men, yet the efforts of such individuals are appreciably promoted by favorable conditions and circumstances, and stimulating surroundings, or a proper milieu. They stand, so to speak, on the shoulders of their predecessors, and they profit by the labors of those who have gone before them. The philosophy of any particular period or epoch is an outgrowth of earlier efforts and previous attempts at philosophic thinking, an efflorescence of some rich culture or civilization, the veritable fine fleur of an age of accomplishments and triumphs, like the Golden Age in ancient Greece, or the thirteenth century in Christian Europe. The great idealistic systems of modern Germany and the more recent widely known scientific constructions are to be regarded as the characteristic product of their time. 

History has proved that the progress of philosophy cannot be dissociated from economic, political, cultural and religious conditions. Frequently a period of increased philosophical activity follows upon a period of political decline. Great catastrophes and national calamities often arouse and awake men from their lethargy, make them seek the causes of their misfortunes, force them to evaluate the past, and oblige them to speculate on and prepare for the future.

CHAPTER TWO  

SINCE THE future of philosophy cannot be divined without a knowledge of the past, it becomes necessary to give it some attention. A depreciatory attitude toward the past of philosophy has manifested itself in recent years. Some feel that philosophy, at any rate as it is being taught in the schools today, is confined too exclusively to the history of philosophy. Others think that too many philosophical dissertations are devoted to historical topics. There seems to be some truth in this view; perhaps too much stress has been placed on the philosophy of the past. However, it is not surprising that many theses of candidates for the doctor's degree have been concerned with the elucidation of some more or less obscure point in the teaching of either past or contemporary thinkers. It should be remarked that such a dissertation is but the beginning, or should be the beginning, of further studies in the field of philosophy. 

It is usually easier to examine critically the doctrines of others than to start out with the independent investigation of some difficult philosophical problem. Moreover, the writing of an historical dissertation provides an excellent training and a desirable preparation for future productive work in this fertile field. Besides, so many 6

doctrines appear new, or are made to appear so novel, that they can scarcely be recognized as rehashings of ancient theories. It is, of course, useful and even necessary to be able to detect such old doctrines decked out in their new garb. Hence it appears that a study of the past becomes an indispensable preparation for any prospective philosopher.

There is, of course, always the danger that too many workers of some particular historical period may devote too much time to a minute examination of the past. 

Many ascribe the sterility of some periods of Byzantine philosophy to undue preoccupation with the philosophy of the ancient Greeks. It would follow from this that it is not advisable to fix one's gaze on the past too restrictedly since that may bring about a relative infertility and sterility. Yet it must be remembered that perhaps no one was better acquainted with the past of Greek philosophy than Aristotle, and that he is one of our most reliable sources of information on early Greek thought. His historical studies did not warp his mind or render it sterile, rather he was prompted to correct the mistakes of his predecessors and beget his own individual system of philosophy. The present is unquestionably conditioned by the past, and the past and present of philosophy permit one to make inferences regarding the probable course it will take in the future, provided the future be not too remote. After all, even modern philosophy which consciously strove to detach itself from a despised and disappointing past, did not like Athene spring suddenly from the head of Zeus, but

gradually evolved from the thought of the mediaeval period, as recent works of Gilson, Koyré, and Krakowski on Descartes and Locke have made quite plain. Cicero's saying, historia magistra vitae, is equally applicable to the history of philosophy as well as to political and economic history. It is possible to divine the future to a limited extent from a careful study of the past and the present.

Some think that the field of philosophy will be almost entirely divided up among the special sciences. It is well known that some sciences, now quite independent, formerly were regarded as branches of philosophy. Even among the Greeks the sciences of astronomy and mathematics were detached from philosophy, as in modern times psychology and sociology have become separate and distinct from philosophy. In this respect those separated sciences may be compared to icebergs which have broken off an advancing glacier and fallen into the sea upon which they float away from the main glacial structure. But although the area reserved for philosophy has become more restricted, and granting that in the future it will be more circumscribed, there will still remain an irreducible minimum, in which there will always be something to explore. The province of philosophy may be compared to a snow-covered peak of a lofty mountain. No matter from what point one may begin the ascent one will eventually reach the region of perpetual snow and ice. Similarly, no matter from whatever science or field of research one may start, if one pursue the research far enough, one will finally

reach the realm of philosophy. It is evident that, as the boundary between philosophy and the special sciences is not rigidly fixed and determined, so also the distinction between scientists and philosophers is not sharply marked. Gradually scientists wander into the field of philosophy, and scientists must not neglect the results obtained by the various special sciences. Scientists become philosophers and philosophers must be scientists, at least to a limited extent. 

Some have taken the position that the history of philosophy has been sufficiently studied and explored, and hence its further study is scarcely worth while. However, worth-while books are still written on the past, even on the history of Greek philosophy. Many ancient 

Greek philosophical works have been lost; very infrequently fragments are discovered in our day. Occasionally such a fragment of a writing on philosophy of the ancients turns up in the form of a papyrus. Kenyon's discovery of a papyrus containing the lost Constitution of Athens of Aristotle is a noted instance. By good fortune the ruins of Herculaneum may yield up some lost works of the past. The chances of finding works no longer extant in the ruins of Herculaneum are indeed promising. On the other hand, one can scarcely expect any notable finds in Pompeii which seems to have been rather a place of entertainment and recreation, a city of villas of prosperous Romans. Werner Jaeger has proved that it is still possible to write a superior book on ancient philosophy, even on Aristotle, although thousands of books have been written on the life and teach-

ing of the founder of the Lyceum. It cannot be maintained that the last word has been written on some of the perplexing problems of ancient thought. 

As far as mediaeval thought is concerned, one has every reason to believe that it still offers exceptional opportunities for research, since so many sources of mediaeval thought still remain unpublished and unknown, hidden in the recesses of various European libraries, some of which are comparatively obscure. The growth and development of mediaeval thought will better be understood and appreciated after the preliminary labor of editing and publishing the sources will have been completed. This applies not only to Western 

European thought but to mediaeval Arabian thought as well. It cannot be said that there exists an adequate history of Byzantine philosophy in any language today. 

The known sources, of course, are at the disposal of anyone who would devote himself to this sort of work. 

Much remains to be learned of mediaeval Arabian philosophy despite the splendid labors of such scholars as 

Max Horten, Carra de Vaux, and Asín y Palacios. The recent publication of the texts of the Arabian philosophers under the direction of Maurice Bouyges should awaken a new interest in this field and should furnish the occasion for a more intensive cultivation and a more exact and accurate presentation of the doctrines of the better known Arabian thinkers. It will also make possible a better appreciation of their influence on Western thought and on mediaeval Jewish philosophy, which owing to the writings of Ibn Gabirol and Moses Mai-

monides exerted a profound influence on the greatest of the Scholastics. The publication of the extensive additions to Brockelmann's History of Arabian Literature should facilitate and encourage research in the field of Arabian philosophy. Besides a more careful search and examination of the books of many Mohammedan mosques and especially the madrasas attached to them may still yield lost or forgotten works. This might occur anywhere from the Maghrib to India. One should bear in mind that Chinese philosophy and particularly the thought of ancient India are indispensable to those who would delve into the past of China and Hindustan in order to study the culture and civilization of those great peoples. One need but refer to the recent histories of Indian philosophy by Radhakrishnan and Dasgupta as worthy specimens of such work. The finest thought of the past has always exerted a certain fascination, and we have every reason to hope that this fascination will be the occasion of future brilliant works in this field of the science of history. 

The sources of the history of philosophy since the Renaissance are almost all well known, and the records of modern philosophy are so well catalogued and filed that the chance discovery of lost or unknown sources scarcely exists. The sole exception might be private letters, either unknown or unpublished. Thus it is well known that many letters of Leibnitz still remain unpublished. However, the new edition of Leibnitz's works now in course of publication in Germany should bring all these to light. It is obvious, of course, that modern

philosophical works are scattered far and wide as soon as they are printed and published, hence copies are found in many libraries. On the other hand, copies of ancient and mediaeval works are not so numerous. 

One may remark here that the systematic critique of the numerous currents of modern thought is a task which still remains to be done; this is particularly true of the more recent movements. However, we are too close to much of this thought to view it in the proper perspective. 

One may readily conjecture that much that looms as important today will eventually be relegated to its proper place and position in the history of philosophy. 

We possess rather adequate lexicons and dictionaries of philosophy in English, French, and German, namely, those of Baldwin, Lalande, and Eisler. However, we do not have an exhaustive and complete dictionary of Arabic philosophical terms. The closest approach to such a work is the glossary of several thousand philosophical terms which Max Horten has appended to his book on the Theology of Islam according to Razi and Tusi. One must add here the recent Avicenna Lexicon of Goichon. It is generally agreed that Ast's Lexicon on Plato and Bonitz's admirable Index to Aristotle cannot take the place of a suitable dictionary of Greek philosophy, although it is apparent that the new edition. of the Greek dictionary of Lidell and Scott will help to a certain extent. In former days bibliographies were inadequate, while today almost complete bibliographies are at the disposal of writers on philosophical subjects. 

Published lists of dissertations and theses make it pos-

sible to become acquainted with what is being done in the field of philosophy in other parts of the world. 

Gilchrist's list supplies such information about this country. Numerous periodicals now appearing in many countries are especially helpful in furnishing information concerning every philosophical trend or current of thought. In this country the bibliography sponsored by the American Philosophical Association, which is to be a continuation of Rand's bibliography, is eagerly awaited.

More aids and advantages are today placed at the service of those studying the history of thought than ever before. And yet, despite all these aids, some noted contributions to the history of philosophy have too long remained unknown and unappreciated. As an example one may mention here the writings of Ephrem Longpré on the philosophy of Duns Scotus, which, published in an obscure periodical, long remained unknown, even when they were gathered together and published in the form of a book. This work on Duns Scotus was startling. Longpré showed that current opinions concerning Duns Scotus were founded on spurious works or on writings of doubtful authenticity. From his investigations it now appears that the supposed great discrepancies between the teaching of Duns Scotus and Thomas Aquinas do not exist. Scotus was not as much of a sceptic as many supposed. The widely trumpeted Kant of the Middle Ages seems to be a myth. At any rate some commonly accepted views have been subjected to considerable modification. Strangely enough too many

textbooks on the history of philosophy, published since the appearance of the work of Longpré, took no cognizance of his studies and contributions and repeated the same old stories found in the textbooks of former days. This has been found true both of non-Catholic as well as Catholic authors of textbooks. Fortunately such carelessness and undue reliance on textbooks of former days is becoming less frequent. From what has just been said the value and importance of exhaustive bibliographical compilations and ready reference works on all philosophical literature become quite apparent. 

This may also serve as a caution particularly to students who would study or write on the philosophy of the past. 

It is obvious that textbooks cannot aim at completeness; however, they are assumed to supply accurate and reliable information. No doubt standards of scholarship are being forced higher and more exacting demands are being placed on prospective authors, all of which should promote and enhance the prestige of philosophy. This should result in the training of a new generation of workers in the field of the history of philosophy, in which painstaking scholarship will always be esteemed and appreciated.

One need not emphasize here the disadvantages, if not the dangers, of national separation and isolation, which hang over those who would devote themselves to the study of the history of philosophy. Philosophy should not be divided into strictly national areas, separated from one another by unscalable walls. The workers in one country should know and should be able to

study at their leisure what is being done in other countries. It is to be expected that the new periodical, Bibliographie de Philosophie, published in Paris, will make international cooperation between philosophers more feasible and render regrettable omissions and oversights impossible in the future. Anything tending to prevent or hinder international meetings and personal contacts of scientists and philosophers of whatever race or creed should be promptly and definitely eliminated.

While it remains true that present conditions do not warrant a healthy optimism, yet the way is still open to truly international collaboration which should guarantee further progress in acquiring an accurate and detailed knowledge of the philosophy of the past.

CHAPTER THREE PHILOSOPHY TODAY 

IT MAY be asserted that the day of the armchair philosopher is gone forever. It is no longer permissible for the philosopher leisurely to contemplate the world without availing himself of the data furnished by the sciences. 

In the early days of philosophy there scarcely existed any aids to the senses. Today, however, after the invention of the marvelous instruments which have, so to speak, extended and intensified the power of the senses, the philosopher must avail himself of the help of the microscope, the telescope, the spectroscope, the camera, and all the ingenious apparatus of the laboratory, not only to study inorganic nature, but also the organic world, nay, man himself. The philosopher of the twentieth century must not confine himself to the study of books containing the description of data discovered by others. He, himself, should have spent at least several years in a laboratory becoming acquainted with the technique of scientific research, in order to be able to appreciate, evaluate, and eventually to make use of the results obtained by others. Moreover, it is generally acknowledged that careful work in a laboratory is an invaluable preparation for the difficult task of interpretation and explanation of facts. It prevents one 16

17 from making too hasty generalizations from too few facts, and from prematurely advancing theories and thus exposing philosophy to the taunts and gibes of those who would laugh at the Jack of all trades and the master of none, namely, the philosopher.

Some will argue that philosophy existed before the sciences, that it has its proper and exclusive methods, and consequently one need not know or study the special sciences in order to philosophize in an orderly manner. It is true that one may philosophize and draw conclusions from the data furnished by sense experience without having recourse to the special sciences. One might begin at the point at which Thales and his followers began, as one might return to olive oil lamps and travel on horseback. However, there would be no excuse for such a procedure today. Why should one decline to profit by the stupendous advance of the sciences since the birth of philosophy in ancient times? Every philosopher should take cognizance of the progress of the sciences in his own day. Unfortunately, the neglect of the science of one's own day is a reproach which justly has been levelled at some philosophers of the past. 

It is also true that some philosophical writings of the present betray an insufficient acquaintance with the advances of scientific thinking. It becomes apparent that an entirely new burden has been placed on the shoulders of would-be philosophers, for even restatements of the tenets of the past are scarcely of any use, unless these teachings are explained in the light of recent scientific discoveries. It is obvious, of course, that no one can

master all the sciences of our time, and that such an extensive knowledge is not required of anyone. However, without mastering all, one may put oneself in the position to appreciate and understand the significance of new discoveries. The day of the universal genius has passed away never to return. There is some reason for calling Leibnitz the last universal genius. At that time it was still possible personally to explore the whole realm of the knowable, but given the usual length of human life of three score years and ten this would no longer be possible even for a transcendent genius.

In the past, it is true, philosophy has very largely been the product of some definite individual. Thoughts, hypotheses, theories, doctrines, have ever been ascribed to some particular thinker who, consequently, was either praised or criticized. Mass production has no place in philosophy, at least it had no place in the past. And yet, it seems that this will have to be modified to some extent in the future. Since it is no longer possible for one man to be sufficiently acquainted with all the sciences, it becomes apparent that the day of far greater cooperation between scientists and philosophers is dawning, and the necessity of a far more exact articulation and unification of the entire field of knowledge is forcing its attention on all thinkers. However, no one will deny that what one man cannot do unaided, many men can successfully accomplish. In the future it probably will be necessary for many to band together to assure the progress of both scientific and philosophical thought. Today scientific discoveries and the results of scientific research

One need but mention Mendel's discoveries of the laws of heredity, which long remained unknown and forgotten, until they were brought to light again by De 

Vries, Correns, and Tschermak, and particularly made known to the English speaking world by Bateson. From this we learn that the publications of no country may be neglected, no language may be ignored, lest something really worth-while remain unknown among the learned and thereby retard the progress of both science and philosophy. There can be no doubt that Mendel's work was not only of the highest importance in biology but also in philosophy for it furnished a new insight into the mysterious factors of heredity and organic evolution. Owing to the numerous publications of learned societies and academies, as well as a multitude of periodical publications, there are less chances of overlooking an important discovery than in former days. It must be observed, however, that it is no longer sufficient to confine one's attention to the productions appearing in the dominant languages alone. It is becoming increasingly useful, if not necessary, for the philosopher to possess a reading knowledge of some other language, especially if important papers in some particular science are appearing in such an idiom for very special reasons. 

International exchanges of books, papers, proceedings and periodical publications are becoming more common and widespread. One may observe that the philosophers have already held their ninth International Congress.

Such international gatherings are truly advantageous especially for students of recent and contemporary philosophy. any

There seems to be one danger threatening the parallel development of philosophy and the sciences, and that is their undue separation and isolation. First of all, it should be noted that it is not normal for thinker to keep his philosophy and his science in water-tight compartments in his mind. The usual course for a true thinker is frequently to cross the imaginary boundary between philosophy and the sciences without even consciously and deliberately adverting to it. A scientist will not concern himself about trespassing on the sacred domain of philosophy, neither will the philosopher refrain from delving into the vast deposits of facts provided by the scientists for the purpose of bolstering and supporting his speculations. The question, Is he a scientist or a philosopher should not be taken too seriously, for frequently the same person is both a scientist and a philosopher. A scientist should never fear to be called a philosopher by anyone; that should rather be regarded as highly complimentary and a tribute to the extent to which he has followed the promptings of curiosity to delve into even the most abstruse and often hopelessly impractical questions, which may nevertheless possess a highly theoretical value. It is hardly possible, especially today, to pass on to the consideration of the ultimate reasons of things without first having served an apprenticeship in some of the sciences, and having studied things much closer to hand. Today one

21 must be a scientist before one may hope to attain any signal success in the province of philosophy. One usually approaches the portals of philosophy after one has pursued some particular science to its very limits. 

Thus some have ventured into philosophy following the avenues of mathematics, psychology, physics, astronomy, medicine, sociology, politics, or even history, particularly the history of civilization. We have many instances of such approaches to philosophy in recent times. 

Theology, based on revelation, as distinguished from natural theology or theodicy, often offers the occasion of a careful examination of the tenets of the various schools of philosophy. When theologians wander into the field of philosophy they may not neglect the natural sciences, else their philosophical speculations may be too arbitrary and not close enough to reality. Theoretically there should be no conflict between revelation and reason, theology and philosophy. The conflicts which have occurred in practice, however, were due to misinterpretations of revealed texts or the acceptance of provisional hypotheses or unproved theories as demonstrated truths.

The history of Christianity reveals numerous cases of those who misinterpreted Scripture and consequently were declared heretics. Some incompetent theologians have ventured into philosophy and have helped to discredit in the eyes of the uncritical, those eminent theologians who have attained success and distinction in the field of philosophy. Hence theologians have been erroneously looked upon by many as enemies of science and philosophy.

This brings one to the consideration of bias and prejudice shown by philosophers, as well as their sympathies and antipathies, and their various pet theories. It is undeniable that philosophers do sometimes betray certain propensities, likes and dislikes, which they share with all other human beings. Besides they share the opinions of the general public of their day, and even the racial prejudices of their own fellow citizens. One expects a philosopher to be more balanced in his judgments than others, and to exert a constant control over his feelings and thoughts. Such a constant control and independence of the winds of doctrine of the hour should be a characteristic of the philosophical life. Philosophers are not entirely immune from the influences of propaganda, but perhaps they are more free of them than any other category of men. Often, of course, they deem it more prudent not to manifest their thoughts indiscriminately, especially if they might be misinterpreted. Discretion is often the better part of valour. Plato attempted to introduce social and political reforms in Sicily, but in Athens he prudently refrained from all political activity and service of the state, except military service in his youth. Socrates did teach in season and out of season, conscious that he was performing his duty, with utter disregard of the possible unpleasant consequences of his activity. Fichte by his famous Reden an die deutsche 

Nation roused his countrymen to opposition to Napoleon. Many are shocked by Aristotle's attitude toward slavery; they thing that he should have condemned the institution of slavery without any reservations. Others

PHILOSOPHY TODAY 

23 are astonished at Plato's leniency toward some Greek customs. But it must be remembered that both these great thinkers were men of their own time and environment. Seneca excused himself for not always acting like a philosopher; he did not hesitate to confess the weakness of human nature and the difficulty of living up to the Stoic ideals which he professed. The lessons of the past would indicate that the philosopher of today cannot hope to escape all narrowness of outlook, various prejudices, and the prevailing attitudes of his day, yet in our time it is possible for a philosopher to be more universal and cosmopolitan than ever before. His opportunities for knowing the world of the past as well as that of the present are much greater, for he is less hampered by the limits of space and time. It is easier for him to travel, to learn foreign languages, to become acquainted with the doctrines of the present day by meeting the authors of those teachings, or their followers and disciples. There is consequently less excuse today for national, racial and also religious prejudices, for catering to more or less ephemeral vogues and theories enjoying a temporary success. Never before have scientists and philosophers interested in contemporary thought been so favored with advanced modes of transportation, which make it possible for them to attend meetings and congresses, which tend to break down all narrowness and insularity.

Thinking men of today are not expected to retire permanently from the world and live in a cask or barrel like Diogenes. Occasional seclusion for the purpose of

collecting and arranging one's thoughts is to be recommended. In summing up this chapter one may assert that the thinker of today should profit by the advantages and opportunities offered at present which certainly would have been welcomed by the philosophers of by-gone ages; he should not be content to remain always in one place and only read books. Foibles and eccentricities which nowise contribute to human thinking should be studiously avoided. Above all, a true philosopher may never be a poseur, he should never write or teach with his tongue in his cheek, or seek to amuse the crowds. One may ask, has that ever been done? Alas, it has. On the other hand sincerity is always respected, even though it may be attended by a mild form of eccentricity. Finally, it is generally conceded, that a philosopher's life should harmonize with his doctrines.

CHAPTER FOUR  

ARISTOTLE began his Metaphysics with the observation that all men naturally desire to know. No one will deny that man is naturally inquisitive and being rational he is led to treat of knowledge scientifically and philosophically. At all events, after man has satisfied his curiosity to a certain extent, he becomes curious about knowledge itself. The validity of knowledge, its extent, the different kinds of knowledge, the distinction between the true and the false or erroneous, and the difference between certitude and probability, all become the object of knowledge itself. The agreement between the representation in the mind and that which is represented, or the object, is called the truth. Science seeks facts which it interprets and upon which it founds its generalizations. However, all truth is not factual truth, or the conformity of things to reason. From real truth formal or logical truth is distinguished, which consists in the harmony or agreement of reasoning with universally admitted logical principles. Thus in mathematics truth is a matter of agreement or consistency with certain axioms or postulates, which are assumed to be true, hence they may also be called assumptions.

The Greeks, the creators of the science of mathe- 25

matics at first included it in philosophy, but subsequently it was detached from philosophy. While mathematics is no longer a part of philosophy its principles and fundamental assumptions do fall within the province of philosophy, hence the necessity for a philosophy of mathematics. Today mathematical philosophy is engaging considerable attention. It has been found necessary to reëxamine and probe the validity of the fundamental assumptions of mathematics and pronounce judgment on its alleged certitude. Leibnitz agreed with the Scholastics who held that there would be nothing in the intellect unless its contents came through the senses, but he added significantly, "except the intellect itself," thereby indicating the subjective element of knowledge. Without admitting with Leibnitz innate ideas, it is now generally acknowledged that there are two elements to be considered when dealing with scientific knowledge, as distinguished from knowledge which may not be admitted as scientific, namely, the data derived from sense experience and also some principles and assumptions. In order to put an end to all disagreements among philosophers Descartes intended to make philosophy as certain as mathematics, by employing his geometrical method. No one in the entire history of thought applied the geometrical method to philosophy as thoroughly and consistently as Spinoza. His attempt produced only negative results, for he plainly showed that by employing the deductive and analytic method alone one cannot construct a satisfactory system of philosophy. Such a system fails to reckon with

too many facts supplied by sense experience; it cannot satisfy the demands of the human mind for an adequate explanation of reality. In order to be entirely consistent with some assumptions and a priori postulates one may be obliged to disregard too many facts of the physical order. On the other hand the inductive and synthetic method alone is also inadequate. One can furnish no better example of this than the honest and sincere attempts of Francis Bacon to discover a method of studying nature which would permit its exploitation for the benefit of mankind. Bacon's method did not lead to any discovery; it was really unworkable for it demanded an impossible accumulation of positive and negative instances of occurrences of phenomena. Bacon failed to realize the preponderant rôle of the creative imagination of the scientist in forming provisional hypotheses frequently based on comparatively few facts, which subsequent experiments might destroy, modify or verify. 

The science of mathematics is unquestionably necessary for the philosopher of today, yet no one would any longer attempt what Descartes and Spinoza tried, namely, to provide philosophy with a certitude equal to that of mathematics. It has been found that geometry does not possess the degree of certitude ascribed to it by Descartes, and that no philosopher or scientist can possibly follow Bacon to the letter. To repeat, today every science which purports to explain extramental reality consists of both assumptions and facts. Of course, pure mathematics is not directly concerned with the facts revealed by the senses and studied by scientists and

philosophers in laboratories and observatories. No one tries to explain facts without advancing some provisional hypotheses which appear plausible, that is more or less probable. One single fact may ruin a provisional hypothesis or diminish its probability. This brings one to the realization that frequently enough one may not be able to find certitude. Lacking that, one will have to be satisfied with greater or lesser probability. 

Constructions of arguments and syllogisms alone will not always lead to truth. On the other hand an accumulation of data or facts must be made the basis of logical and consistent generalizations. The schools and laboratories are filled with fact-finders, but some do not know what use to make of them after they have found them. 

The scientific view of the world today is largely mathematical, and as much as possible also a logical view. 

Without mathematics it is impossible to gain an adequate insight into the functioning of the physical cosmos. Astronomy itself is largely mathematics, and the same may be said of physics. It must be acknowledged that in these cases we have applied mathematics, as distinguished from pure mathematics. Pure mathematics need not concern itself with the real world, but on the other hand applied mathematics is conditioned by the objective world of facts. It has become more and more evident that there are limits to the application of mathematics to reality. Mathematics cannot be applied to the physical cosmos with perfect exactitude, and it appears that all reality is not reducible to quantity and magnitude. Nevertheless, it remains true that the science of

mathematics is one of the superior products of the genius of man. The language of mathematics, to use that expression, is the most exact and precise in existence. Mathematical symbols are superior to words and express thought with a precision and clarity unsurpassed by any other method of expression of thought. Mathematics, by developing its marvelous aggregate of conventional symbols has become the most adequate and truly international method of expression. Certainly in many respects the language of mathematics is far more exact than that of philosophy. It should be remembered that mathematics is untrammelled by real objects, whereas philosophy is not purely formal; it should try to explain all reality whether corporeal or incorporeal or even divine, hence philosophy is less free than mathematics because it is always conditioned by objective reality with which it may take no liberties. This applies also to the other real sciences.

If we compare the language and terminology of philosophy with that of mathematics, we must acknowledge that it is less precise and subject to greater variations in different countries of the world, hence less generally accepted by philosophers. This is partly due to the fact that philosophy was developed in parallel lines by peoples using different tongues. However, the development of philosophical terminology is a notable achievement, and one should not forget that numerous philosophical terms have been accepted and incorporated into the common speech of educated men. Although philosophical language has become quite conventional, and it 1224

is comparatively easy to translate philosophical works from one language into another, if the translations are limited to those languages that have created a philosophical terminology; nevertheless one may not deny that too many philosophers have taken unwarranted liberties with a terminology that has become conventional and given words an arbitrary meaning. In so many cases it has become necessary, or at least advantageous to compile special glossaries, lexicons and dictionaries to the works of certain philosophers. This is not necessary in mathematics. This is due, however, to some extent to the nature of the subject itself. It is obvious that when men think thoughts never thought before, they must take terms already in use and qualify their meaning, or coin new terms and create various neologisms. In time many of these are generally accepted or else they are soon abandoned and eventually have nothing but a museum value. The arbitrary use of terminology is a charge which may not be levelled at philosophers alone, as an examination of the history of the special sciences abundantly shows. At all events, the very perplexities of terminology of the special sciences are a testimony to their progress and advancement.

Mathematicians need never sacrifice consistency, in fact they may not. However, scientists as well as philosophers are at times driven into a cul-de-sac from which there is no escape except by abandoning strict consistency, especially when reality itself does not allow itself to be pigeon-holed at will. To be strictly logical is an

admirable ideal not always attainable, at least during certain stages of the development of the sciences or philosophy. It is deplorable, however, that some thinkers have neglected logical, which becomes tantamount to mathematical, procedure, to such an extent that their thought loses much of its value. It is often repeated that life laughs at logic, but neither philosophy nor the sciences may do so, even though occasionally they may find themselves in an impasse. There are times when it would seem entirely logical to admit certain consequences flowing from previously admitted doctrines or explanations, or more or less plausible assumptions, yet reality itself prevents it; too many facts, or often one fact alone, do not allow it. In that case it is obvious that a well ascertained fact may not be denied, but some previous assumptions may have to be modified or perhaps even discarded.

As an illustration of what has been referred to above one may mention that as a mathematician Descartes was strictly logical and consequential, but not as a philosopher. Spinoza's philosophy is more consistent than that of Descartes. Hegel started out with some preconceived notions which he adhered to consistently, but in our day many will agree that he departed far from reality. One of the reasons why the Germans themselves turned away from their celebrated idealistic philosophers was because they found among them too much uncontrolled speculation. Both scientific thought and philosophical speculation must be conditioned and determined by the objective world which we have not

fashioned, and which we may not remake. But we do fashion our assumptions, preconceived notions, postulates, provisional hypotheses, or even startling intuitions and products of the creative imagination, whatever we may call them. It may be stated that in general the more a philosophical synthesis has respect for reality, the more it is eventually esteemed and appreciated, despite the fact that it may be rather hard to harmonize some doctrines and reconcile them with some of the thinker's original assumptions. Some thinkers are loath to discard some of their favorite preconceptions, to which they obstinately adhere in the face of numerous serious objections. Of course, the safest procedure is to seek some other hypothesis, as is commonly done both by careful scientists and reputable philosophers. This is the deeper meaning of the pragmatism of William James and John Dewey, which demands that theories be validated and corroborated. 

There are some fundamental principles which no one may deny, and which are implicitly admitted by philosophers, mathematicians, scientists, and all people possessing ordinary common sense, because they are evident. An example is Aristotle's principle of contradiction which need not and cannot be proved. Some propositions are admitted because they have been demonstrated, and still others are accepted because they are probable, that is they are regarded as the most satisfactory or plausible for the time being, but they must yield to other propositions subsequently found to be more adequate. It will readily be admitted that the degree

of cogency of proofs varies; some proofs are regarded as weaker than others. The ideal both in science and philosophy is to prove as many statements or propositions as possible and thus to transfer them from the realm of probability to that of truth. It becomes apparent that a collection of truths is exceedingly valuable and must be regarded as a precious acquisition and possession. One need not refer here to facts, occurrences or events, which it is not necessary to establish by a long series of inferences from the known to the unknown, because any normal human being or higher animal may ascertain them. Thus, both the hunter and his dog may ascertain the presence of a fox. The history of the sciences as well as that of philosophy, is replete with records of successful intuitions and brilliant guesses which could not be proved at the time they were advanced, yet later were fully established. Thus the ancient atomic hypothesis of Democritus, scientifically stated by Dalton, was afterwards amply verified by such ingenious devices as Crooke's spinthariscope and Wilson's chamber. The single atoms are not visible, but the effects of single atoms are made apparent to the senses. The supposition of Lamarck and Charles Darwin that species are not permanent, but change, has become more certain owing to the remarkable results obtained through observation and experiment. As Erich 

Wasmann says, evolution is taking place before our very eyes. It may be said in particular of philosophers, even of the very greatest, that they have not been able to prove all their tenets. If they were to give all the

reasons for the doctrines which they had gathered throughout a lifetime they would fill volumes. The reasons for their convictions have often remained unwritten. Nevertheless the intuitions of a Plato are worth examining even though he may not have proved them to be true. Goethe was justified in comparing 

Plato's philosophy to an obelisk placed on a narrow foundation, but piercing the skies, and that of Aristotle to a pyramid resting on a broad and firm foundation. 

It is true that Plato often relied on his intuitions and took refuge in beautiful myths, although he also proved and demonstrated, whereas Aristotle was more inclined to prove and demonstrate rigidly, yet even he could not escape intuitions and ingenious suppositions.

One of the most celebrated hypotheses that has come down to us from the ancient Greeks is the universally known Parallel Postulate of Euclid. It was generally regarded as true and was not questioned until modern times. The very attempt to prove it by Saccheri may be regarded as the original point of departure of the new geometries of Lobachevski and Riemann. At least 

Riemannian geometry has successfully been applied to the physical universe. Theoretically it is not more consistent and hence may not be regarded as more true than that of Euclid or Lobachevski, but it is far more convenient than the Euclidian in explaining the entire realm of the physical universe. It has become apparent that Euclid's Parallel Postulate cannot be proved; it is but a convenient assumption. Experience has established, however, that Euclidean geometry is more suit-

able for our relatively small earth than any other. Since 

Einstein's most successful theory of gravitation founded on the assumption that the space and time of the cosmos form a four-dimensional continuum has won wide recognition, parallelism may no longer be regarded as absolute as Euclid supposed.

It is apparent that mathematics and philosophy are becoming more closely related than ever before. The recent development of symbolic logic would also indicate this same tendency. It now becomes clear that the ideal of making philosophy as certain as mathematics has vanished, particularly, since the appearance of the new geometries, any one of which may be held to be equally certain. As Henri Poincaré has lucidly explained, mathematics is not based on certitude but on plausible assumptions. Today, as never before, philosophy must be called upon to examine the assumptions of mathematics and supply the theoretical basis of the science of mathematics and all its branches. On the other hand it must be observed that philosophy, in particular logic, which is generally regarded as belonging to the province of philosophy, is beginning to learn from mathematics. Loud protests against the logic of Aristotle, such as those of Eric T. Bell and Alfred Korzybski, have been heard in this country. Others take a more lenient attitude toward Aristotle and the traditional logic which they regard as incomplete. At all events the work of Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead has brought forth permanent results and greatly stimulated interest in mathematical philosophy and

what is often called logistic. The extensive use of symbols in logic, in imitation of mathematics, is unquestionably contributing to the progress and refinement of that science. This is obviously bringing mathematics and philosophy closer together and is bearing significant results as the works of Clarence I. Lewis, Rudolf Carnap, as well as those of Jan Lukasiewicz, Alfred Tarski, and others in Poland testify. The prospects are that this movement will gather momentum and hence further contributions to symbolic logic may be expected.

CHAPTER FIVE  

THE NATURE of the physical world has ever been the subject of the researches of man since the birth of science and philosophy. The amount of knowledge about the cosmos gathered during the last three centuries, or since the time of Galileo Galilei, is truly imposing. The new facts discovered during our own twentieth century have surpassed the fondest dreams of the thinkers of the nineteenth century. Yet the ultimate nature of what is called the world of matter is as baffling as ever. The problem of the ultimate constitution of matter still remains. It has become far more complicated since the discovery that matter is being dissipated in the form of energy, or perhaps better, that radiation is a characteristic of all matter. Thus the very principles of the conservation of matter and energy necessarily had to be modified. At any rate, from present observations it appears that the sum total of matter is not a constant, hence neither is that of energy a constant. For matter is being radiated into space, it is melting away, hence its quantity is diminishing, unless the converse process of radiation reverting into matter occurs somewhere in the cosmos, as Millikan supposes. 

Max Planck's quantum theory according to which 37

energy is sent out intermittently in quanta or whole packets, but not in fractions of packets, has wrought profound changes in modern physics. The most striking form of radiation which we experience is that of light. As far as we are concerned this world is largely a universe of light. The nature of light is extremely difficult to grasp. Does it consist of corpuscles as Newton supposed, or is it reducible to Hertzian waves? Both theories by themselves are unsatisfactory. It seems that the recent researches of Louis de Broglie are paving the way to a more adequate conception of light. A most remarkable scientific prediction has been made by Einstein. He foretold the deviation of light rays in a gravitational field in the proximity of the stars. That was fully established at observations of total eclipses of the Thus it appears that light is subject to gravitational pull like matter itself. The detection of rays hitherto unknown and unsuspected has added to the mystery of radiation, especially the recently discovered exceedingly penetrating cosmic rays. It was the discovery of the invisible radiation of uranium by Antoine Henri Becquerel, the discovery of radium by Pierre and Marie Curie, and also the discovery of polonium and other radioactive substances and strange radiations which threw an entirely new light on the constitution and the activities of matter. Some have been so impressed with the powers and potencies of that mysterious something which is called matter, that they were inclined to regard it as the ultimate reality. However, this shallow view is not shared by more profound thinkers

who emphatically reject materialism. It is true that some scientists refrain from speculating concerning the ultimate constitution of matter, but philosophers may not dodge the issue. Some philosophers find most theories unsatisfactory, among them the matter and form theory of Aristotle and the Scholastics which was formed long before the discoveries of modern science. 

They take the attitude that if the theory is not useless, it at least offers no help or advantages to the scientist. 

Some scientists are content carefully to describe material phenomena and then make certain proximate inferences regarding their nature, but beyond this they will not proceed. One can, of course understand the prudential attitude of the scientist, but the philosopher must go where others fear to tread.

However, since matter cannot be any longer considered apart from energy, the two together appear to some to be either material or spiritual, depending upon the point of view. This theory which permits the cosmos to be viewed from a twofold aspect takes the position that the powers of matter itself are so wonderful that it appears possible to explain every event taking place anywhere in the cosmos as a manifestation of the activities of the one ultimate reality, matter and energy combined. They do not find it necessary to postulate the existence of some immaterial being totally independent of matter and its powers in order to explain even the highest and most complicated mental phenomena. 

Such an explanation would simplify matters to a notable extent, but such a doctrine would be only another

species of materialism. Others, however, are inclined to doubt whether the superior mental achievements of man can be ascribed to matter and the capacities of matter. If the impressive achievements of man in the domain of the sciences are not to be attributed to a material principle, it is probable, nay it becomes a certainty, that this something which constitutes the object of the study of the scientists, ultimately owes its origin to some immaterial principle, the most remote spiritual being, God. Some do not experience the need of seeking the origin of the universe, being satisfied to regard it as eternal. They assume, for they have never demonstrated, the world to be without a beginning and that it will have no end. It may be remarked here that there is less reason for regarding the world to be without a beginning today than in the day of Aristotle. Neither science nor philosophy really permits us to say that the cosmos is illimited in space or time. Even Kant was prudent enough to hold that one cannot demonstrate that the world is infinite and eternal. To be sure he also held that neither can the opposite be established. However, both philosophy and science have progressed since Kant's time. Einstein's world seems to be a limited one, and it should be noted that although some refuse to occupy themselves with the problem of the origin of the cosmos, nevertheless others point out that there are certain facts which would indicate that this world, which we can see and observe, had a beginning. After all, any world subject to change must have a limited duration composed of a series of limited durations to

which another and still another may be added. Some think with Georges Lemaître that the universe is expanding. Others are reluctant to admit this hypothesis, since other hypotheses are now being advanced to account for the shift of the light of the distanct galaxies toward the red end of the spectrum, on which fact the hypothesis of an expanding universe is based. At all events, no one is inclined to say that the universe has been expanding forever. From all the facts which science has hitherto discovered it certainly is permissible to infer that this cosmos, a limited extent of which is subject to observation, is neither illimited in extent or duration. Since being exists, some being must always have existed, for being cannot come from non-being, but this world of ours may not be regarded as a being which had no beginning. The only alternative is to ascribe its existence, and hence its beginning, to another being which had no beginning, the Creator of the universe.

In the past many thinkers have held that the world has had a beginning, some have held the opposite, and finally some have felt they cannot answer the question. 

That is the attitude of some scientists; they are content to limit themselves to the careful description of the cosmos and the interpretation of the data available. However, one may at least examine the various doctrines of philosophers on the origin of the world and evaluate their probability or plausibility. If the cosmos is not from itself, it is from some other being, which may be a being without a beginning, the first being, or from some

intermediate being. At all events everything that is not from itself must ultimately be traced to a being that is from itself and not from another. Some have supposed that the universe is an emanation, direct or indirect, from the being without a beginning. Another teaching which has become widespread, particularly owing to religious influences, is the doctrine of creation from nothing. For some this is quite inconceivable and hence inadmissible. Those who admit creation do not all agree on the manner in which the world was produced. 

Some have supposed that there was but one creation, others have thought that there was a series of creative acts, such as the creation of new species of plants and animals, and especially human souls. This is tantamount to continuous creation. Some take the attitude that these are useless and profitless speculations quite beyond the possibility of verification. One may, however, predict that men will continue to concern themselves with the problem. 

If thinkers may not be able to prove or demonstrate some doctrines, they may succeed in rendering others untenable and thus finally give them a coup de grâce. 

We know today that the remotest parts of the universe consist of the same matter which we find here on earth; of the ninety-two chemical elements, and perhaps of elements of higher atomic numbers than uranium; which appear entirely too unstable to exist under the conditions prevailing here on earth. It is manifestly impossible to examine the states or conditions of matter which are not found on this planet or which cannot be

produced in the laboratory. Just how matter behaves, what phenomena accompany it under such conditions of enormous pressure and extreme temperature which must prevail in the interior of the sun and the hottest stars, can only be a matter of theoretical computations and conjectures beyond experimental control. Astronomers have discovered that in some of the celestial bodies there exists matter so extremely dense that a cubic inch of it would weigh about a ton on our earth. More recently Dr. G. P. Kuiper, of the Yerkes and McDonald 

Observatories, has reported the discovery of matter in the star, Wolf 457, weighing almost nine thousand tons per cubic inch. One has reason to believe that such matter is not as discrete and discontinuous as even the densest matter with which we are familiar on this earth. 

One can only make more or less intelligent guesses regarding the constitution of such matter. From what is known of matter today one may gather that its ultimate constitution is still an enigma; fortunately it is less mysterious that it was a few decades ago, but the problem continues to challenge scientists and philosophers.

It may be said that the future belongs to the scientists. 

One may hope that they will not hesitate to become philosophers and pursue their inquiries as far as possible, boldly presenting hypotheses, without fear of criticism.

There is no doubt that the recent semipopular, but highly reliable books of Jeans and Eddington, have aroused an immense interest among educated people, who will probably be more inclined to support researches revealing new secrets of this material universe.

The judicious publicizing of the construction of the new, two hundred inch telescope has also awakened a healthy curiosity among people who have the means materially to encourage the efforts of the scientists. 

Both science and philosophy thrive where favorable economic conditions are present.

Another important problem of cosmology is the question whether there are any teleological processes in the world. Mechanical processes are universally admitted, but do they satisfactorily explain all events and occurrences and especially the arrangement and order prevailing in the cosmos? Many will contend that the mechanical explanation of the universe is quite insufficient. It is correct as far as it goes, but unfortunately it does not go far enough and hence must be supplemented by a teleological explanation. Many facts are submitted as evidence of purposiveness in nature. But granted that the premature search for final causes may be detrimental to scientific research, as many contend, philosophical speculation must unavoidably concern itself with the knotty problem of finality. It would appear that the recent triumphs of science might strengthen the teleological point of view, especially the remarkable discoveries of the biological sciences. It may be difficult if not impossible definitively to establish purposiveness in the inorganic world, but the world of organisms seems to offer so much evidence of purpose in nature that a purely mechanical explanation would seem puerile. However, an attempt should be made definitely to dispose of the problem. Here is room for

fruitful cooperation between scientists and philosophers. 

Much has been learned about the evolution of small portions of the visible universe. The different stages of the development of galaxies may be photographed and compared. This permits one to gain some faint idea of the orderly development of the immense agglomerations of stars or suns. The various stars in our own galaxy have been classified in accordance with their temperature and color. Some seem to be new stars, some rather advanced in age, whereas others may be regarded as middle-aged stars. It appears that stars go through a regular series of stages which exhibit their development. According to such ingenious and quite probable theories as those advanced by Chamberlin and Moulton, which have superseded the less perfect hypothesis of Laplace, the formation of planets around our sun seems to be due to something like an accident. At any rate there are no cogent scientific reasons obliging us to suppose that all, or even a large number of stars are surrounded by planets. Granting the truth of a regular and orderly development of the universe, one has good reason to suppose that there is some term to that evolution. The mind revolts against the acceptance of an endless and purposeless evolution. Is there no final end, or goal, or destiny? Perhaps there is, but can we find it? Clausius definitely established that heat cannot of itself pass from a colder to a hotter body. 

This famous second law of thermodynamics, according to Eddington occupies the supreme position among the laws of nature. Entropy is steadily increasing, avail-

able energy is decreasing. Perhaps the universe will finally attain the condition in which no events will occur, but is that the goal of the evolution of the universe? 

Can it have no other destiny? There is no known force in the universe today which could possibly disturb that equilibrium and start a new cycle of evolution. It is possible, of course, that we may be standing at the threshold of revolutionary scientific revelations. Philosophers will ever be watchful in order to take advantage of all new discoveries. It is true that from the philosophical point of view there is another avenue of approach to the problem. Once one has established the existence of an intelligent author of the universe, then it must be admitted that a purpose, or purposes, exist even though we may not be able to discover all of them. At least one can establish that the existence of the universe is not for a purpose outside of that great being itself, for otherwise the intelligent maker of the cosmos would be subordinated to some other being, which is impossible if the author of the world is the Supreme Being. Plainly speaking God made the universe for Himself. This question has taxed the ingenuity of great minds. There is still room for the activity of a gifted intellect which would gather and coordinate every shred of evidence, every fact which might throw light on this problem. It is quite probable that philosophers of the future will attain some explanation far more clear and precise and convincing than we possess in this day of ours.

CHAPTER SIX  DESPITE ALL attempts to reduce vital processes to chemical reactions the problem of the nature of life confronts both scientists and philosophers. Some have despaired of ever finding the ultimate explanation of vital phenomena. They regard life as an unfathomable mystery. However, we are not permitted to set limits to the future knowledge of man concerning life and its mysterious processes. One cannot deny that the tendency to do away with the concept of life, or with the view that life is something beyond the physico-chemical forces hidden in nature has clearly manifested itself. 

This has not come about in a day. Psychology was once a part of philosophy or one of the philosophical disciplines. However, Fechner and Wundt have made psychology an independent science which pursues its elusive objects according to its own methods. Philosophy has become psychology, psychology has become biology, biology is being transformed into bio-chemistry, and bio-chemistry is being reduced to chemistry pure and simple. And thus it seems that the famous enigma of life is disappearing like a mirage, like a morning mist dissipated by the powerful rays of the rising sun. Behaviorism has eliminated the troublesome concept of 47

consciousness which according to its votaries should not be considered any more than an ordinary organic process. Of course, the endeavor to simplify explanations is laudable. Besides, one must recognize the tendency of the human mind to substitute monism for pluralism wherever possible. However, when a simpler explanation is not possible it must be abandoned. In other words when irremovable facts stubbornly refuse to sanction a more simple explanation it may not be accepted even provisionally. Without condemning the enthusiasm of those who would demonstrate that life processes are not distinct from chemical processes it becomes necessary, however, to preserve a certain calm and maintain the equilibrium and impartiality expected of philosophers. No one has as yet demonstrated the identity of vital and physico-chemical processes and consequently no one is obliged to deny the real existence of that mysterious something which has been, and still is called life. Neither serious scientists nor cautious philosophers are ready to reject vitalism. Perhaps the best known leader of the vitalists in recent times is the philosopher and scientist, Hans Driesch. Yet he is not alone, for all those who admit some sort of mind or soul not identical with matter must also postulate some vital principle or source of vital activities which they identify with a more or less permanent and substantial entity. 

Some have thought that even in the case of animals it is so permanent that it may survive bodily death. 

Any one will admit that vital, and in particular, mental phenomena are more prominently displayed in the

case of man and the most intelligent animals. However, even in the lowest unicellular forms of life, whether they be bacteria or protozoa, the same fundamental difference distinguishing their vital functions from physico-chemical processes must be recognized. 

Pasteur's epoch-making discoveries, ably confirmed by Tyndall, made the hypothesis of spontaneous generation a chimera. Not one single isolated exception to the now generally accepted biological axiom, omne vivum e vivo, has ever been established by anyone in the history of science. Hence, from the data supplied by science itself we are obliged to hold that everything living on this planet of ours today is derived from some other living being. Despite the dreams of poets and seers everything is not alive, neither is the non-living making any transition to the realm of the living. The problem of the origin of life on this planet still persists. Some suppose that although spontaneous generation does not occur at present, yet it must have occurred some time in the remote past, since all scientists and philosophers acknowledge that conditions prevailing on the earth hundreds of millions of years ago did not permit life, such as we know it, to exist. Others like Svante Arrhenius have advanced the hypothesis of the transference of spores from some other region of the cosmos to our earth as the most probable mode of the beginning of life on this earth. Such a possibility has never been disproved since it appears that spores are able to endure the temperature of liquid air for rather long periods without being killed. However, there is no record, no

single authenticated instance, of any form of life having come to our earth in this fashion. Others have recourse to the doctrine of special creation of the first living being or beings. Many today regard this as the best possible explanation of the origin of life on this earth. To the objection that scientists have not ascertained a single fact of creation it may be replied that this is impossible from the very nature of the case. It should be remembered that St. Augustine did not account for the origin of all living forms by a special creation, but supposed that the powers innate in nature could bring forth life. And thus the problem of the origin of life still remains. It is indeed quite possible that in the future new facts will be discovered which will contribute toward a clearer understanding of this difficult question.

Not only careful observation but especially experimentation has brought to light many new real, astounding facts revealing the marvelous activities of living beings. Perhaps the most generally known and most sensational experiments were those initiated by Dr. Alexis 

Carrel of the Rockefeller Institute of Medical Research in 1912. It is reported that he has succeeded in keeping alive a fragment of heart tissue of a chick embryo from that time to this very day. Strangely enough such tissues not only remain alive, but they are also capable of growth and cell proliferation. Here, at any rate, we have a case of the discovery of new facts hitherto quite unknown. Various attempts to produce life in the laboratory have been made, but all without exception

have failed. The production of life from non-living matter might surely be regarded as perhaps the greatest triumph of science. From present indications, however, the possibility of producing life artificially in the laboratory appears exceedingly remote. From the experiments of Dr. Carrel it would appear that living cells or tissues may be kept alive indefinitely under proper conditions, that is, if his methods are employed. In other words that phenomenon called death can be staved off, but new life cannot be brought forth. Certainly far more is known of life and living cells today than was known two decades or even a decade ago. The biological sciences have advanced rapidly, in particular, the science of genetics has made astounding progress, especially since bombardment by particles emitted by radioactive substances has been employed to disarrange the patterns of genes in the chromosomes of the germ cells particularly of the fruit fly, or Drosophila.

Strangely formed insects have resulted from such experiments. Thus it appears that the possibility of profoundly altering various living beings, beyond anything that has ever been accomplished hitherto, looms large on the scientific horizon. Sex or, more precisely, sex characteristics are altered almost at will by the use of hormones, and the embryonic development of many forms of life has been modified to a very considerable extent.

All these experiments with both life and death have a profound significance for all those speculating on the ultimate nature of life, or of the different kinds of vital

energies, since no one shall say that the different grades of life are the same. There appear to be many degrees of life, not only plant, animal, and human, but even intermediate stages. The distinction between plant and animal life which to Alfred Russell Wallace seemed a yawning chasm, does not appear so any longer to us. In fact in some cases it is rather difficult to say whether a given living being is a plant or an animal. As an instance one may mention the slime molds whose life cycle still remains mysterious. Moreover it appears difficult to make a final pronouncement on the real nature of the life of the euglena since it contains chlorophyll, and hence is capable of photosynthesis which has been regarded as a prerogative of plants alone. Some have thought that the euglena is a case of symbiosis. At all events, life is no longer regarded as static, whether it be plant or animal life. The numerous forms of life are changing incessantly. New species of plants and animals have appeared in the past, and observation discloses that new species are evolving in our day. No one has ever marshalled the arguments in favor of evolution as successfully as Charles Darwin and since his day the doctrine of organic evolution has triumphed over all opposition. Evolution is now universally admitted in scientific and philosophical circles. The opposition in this country to the theory of evolution was not based on scientific observation and experiment, but at least in one case, on the ill-founded views of poorly informed politicians. Yet the world is changing, for today many who looked upon evolution with disfavor are ready to

admit man's descent from some lower form of life, at any rate as far as the body is concerned. Many, of course, particularly Catholic theologians and philosophers, do not admit the evolution of the mind of man from the minds of lower animals. They contend that there is an essential difference between the mind of men and animals, not simply a difference of degree. Many most remarkable facts have been mustered in support of this position. Others, however, feel, without supplying conclusive evidence, that there is no essential difference between the mentality of animals and man. It is their conviction that the alleged special prerogatives of man cannot be substantiated. Moreover, granting the superiority of the mind of man over that of the other animals, which on the whole is readily conceded, they ascribe it solely to the superior development of the human brain. Besides, they decline to admit the existence of some substantial mind, or spiritual principle, entirely distinct from the central nervous system. 

A longer and more careful and detailed study of the mind of animals should supply more information. on this subject. There is no doubt that the minds and abilities of animals have received less attention from psychologists than the mind of man. It is not likely that thinkers taking either one or the other position will notably modify their opinions until new facts are discovered, based on mental tests of the higher animals. 

One has reason to believe that the problem of the relation between the mind of man and animals will be an

especial concern of the philosophers and psychologists of the future.

Many ask the question: Is the principle of life in individual living beings one or multiple? New, rather startling facts are making necessary certain restatements about the ultimate nature of the vital principle in single plants and animals. Some have supposed that in living beings, particularly in the metazoa, there is to be found a plurality of lives or souls. They think that each cell has its own life and hence its own principle or source of life. They feel that death is an indication that there are a plurality of lives or vital principles, since all cells do not die at once. Some continue to live for a considerable time after the phenomenon of the death of the organism has occurred. Just as twigs and slips from plants may be detached and made to grow in a favorable milieu, so also tissues from individual animal bodies may be kept alive, especially when transferred to another living body. This is evidenced by the numerous transfusions, transplantations, bone and skin grafting, and many other skillful operations of surgeons as well as experimenters in physiological laboratories. These experiments take on a very particular significance in the case of man, since according to many, especially the Neo- Scholastics, the one single immortal soul is the unique source of all vital activity in the organism. However, in the case of the transplantation of glands or portions of bone from one subject to another, really living cells and tissues are transplanted and hence their life is so to speak transplanted with them for they are alive and not

dead. The blood that is transfused from one person to another consists of living cells, it is not simply a secretion. Granting that the blood cells are incapable of mitosis and do not contain chromatin material, nevertheless, they do not constitute a lifeless fluid. What has been accomplished by Carrel in the case of the heart tissue of chick embryos may, as far as we know, be done with similar human tissue. In that case presumably the soul would be gone after the death of the organism from which the tissue was taken, and yet some of the organism, even though a small portion, would continue to live, and in a favorable milieu these cells might proliferate freely. If there be no life without some plant, animal, or human soul, those who hold this doctrine must postulate the existence of some provisional soul for tissues which have even temporarily been detached from some organism, and in some cases kept alive without grafting or transplantation to some other organism. 

One has reason to believe that in the future more wonderful achievements by the surgeons and experimenting scientists may be expected. Some of the new discoveries may be relatively unimportant, whereas the significance of others may temporarily escape the notice and attention of competent philosophers. It becomes imperative for philosophers to take cognizance of pertinent new facts to which they must adapt their theories or speculations. These theories may have to be modified or generalized in order to include a vast domain of the facts of nature which in earlier times seemed quite unrelated. 

Obviously all theories or hypotheses will have to meet

every sort of test or criticism. This may occasion controversies which should eventually redound to the advantage and advancement of both science and philosophy.

Not only the normal but also abnormal conditions must be taken into consideration. Thus teratology or the science of monsters offers many striking and rare facts which must be reckoned with by anyone presuming to speculate on the ultimate nature of the various forms or grades of life. One may mention in this connection that the occurrence of a diprosopus, or fetal monster with two faces, offers scientists and philosophers the opportunity of determining whether there is but one individuality, or whether there are two separate vital principles or souls. This is especially perplexing since embryologists hold that human duplicate monsters develop from one ovum. In any case, those who hold to the doctrine of individual substantial minds and immaterial principles of life, will be interested in such cases. 

It is interesting to note here that such a competent biologist as Ralph S. Lillie believes that no merely physical conception of life is adequate or satisfactory, particularly since life manifests spontaneous or creative activity. All will admit that the problem of life still tantalizes both scientists and philosophers. Dr. W. M. 

Stanley of the Rockefeller Institute thinks that the study of disease viruses may point the way to fruitful discovery and a deeper comprehension of the mystery of life. Some think that the day is approaching when a chemical synthesis of protoplasm may be effected.

Young scientists, psychologists, and philosophers should be offered special inducements to devote some time and study to the problem of life. At any rate the present time calls for a friendly cooperation between biologists and philosophers of all countries in order to cope with this most baffling of questions.

CHAPTER SEVEN  

THERE IS one form of life that is of paramount importance, namely, human life. No one will deny the highest manifestations of human life demand special attention. It is true that man is not to be studied apart from the rest of the world; he should not be completely separated from the other forms of life displayed on this earth. After all, living beings have not been found in any other part of the universe. Although it is quite probable that life exists elsewhere even in our solar system, at present, at any rate, it cannot constitute an object of scientific study and research. It is not improbable that life existing elsewhere may be of quite a different nature than that which we can here see and observe. The supreme kind of life that has been found is the mental life of man, especially as exhibited by the superior attainments and accomplishments of the greatest minds the human race has ever known. There have been some men whose intellectual achievements have surpassed those of their less gifted fellow men to such an extent that they seemed to possess a certain character of otherworldliness.

From the philosophical point of view it cannot be asserted that we have any incontrovertible proof of the 58

activities of other minds greater than those of the human beings in this world, with the sole exception of the most remote and the greatest mind God, the Absolute Being. We are reminded that Plato did not hesitate to postulate the existence of minds superior to those of men, and one may not forget that there has persisted the general belief throughout the ages that spirits have intervened in human affairs. Christ unmistakably teaches that such spirits really exist, however, such doctrines are the special object of theology and do not come within the purview of science or philosophy. At most, philosophy may admit the possibility of such minds or spirits, although there are some who would go farther and postulate the occasional interference of such beings in order to explain certain obscure and rare phenomena. 

There is no reference here to disembodied human souls with which spiritists claim to enter into communication. 

In all such matters the prudent position of a philosopher who would peer into those dark recesses is a noncommittal attitude. The only minds with which we can compare the human mind are those of the animals, consequently minds inferior to those of human beings. We cannot study the activities of minds supposedly greater and more powerful than the human mind. One must abstract here of course from the divine mind. Quite in opposition to some relatively insignificant thinkers, some of the greatest thinkers like Plato and Aristotle, Augustine and Aquinas, Descartes and Newton, Leibnitz and Kant, admit the existence of the infinite mind, God. 

Although Kant declined to admit the cogency of the

classical arguments in support of the existence of God, there can be no doubt that he sincerely contended that the existence of God was an unavoidable, necessary, inescapable postulate of the practical reason. 

The ultimate nature of the human mind even after all the centuries of study and research still remains mysterious. For some philosophers the question of its origin is still a mooted problem. Those who hold that besides the material there is an immaterial principle in man have not agreed on the time of the union of the soul with the body. One may observe in this connection that a noted protagonist of Darwinism, Alfred Russell Wallace, regarded the appearance of human consciousness as an insurmountable barrier in the history of evolution, a gap that has never been bridged. There are many today who cannot admit that the mind of man is but the result of the evolution of the mind of some animal. The discrepancy between the minds of even the most exceptional of the most intelligent animals and that of the ordinary minds of the most primitive men on this earth today, does not permit one to admit that the minds of men have been evolved from those of animals. For such an admission, too much must be supplied by the imagination and too great a liberty taken with well established facts. Especially is this so, if one holds that the mind of man cannot be identified with any of the matter entering into the composition of the human organism. It does not appear that the difference between the brains of man and animals can adequately account for the vast difference in mental equipment. It

cannot be said that the chasm found between man and animals has been bridged.

The study of many facts makes it reasonable to postulate that besides the organism there is some other principle which is the primary source of the higher mental activities of man. Simply to call the mind a by-product of the organism or an epiphenomenon is not a satisfactory way of solving the problem of the human mind. 

Sir Oliver Lodge regards it as established that this strange principle, the soul or mind, continues to exist as a consciousness even after death, and that in exceptional cases the organism of a medium, as in spiritistic séances, may be temporarily placed at its disposal, permitting it in that fashion to communicate with men still living. It may be remarked here that survival, if not immortality, is one of Kant's necessary postulates of practical reason. 

One may also mention in this connection a modern psychologist, William McDougall, who, though not desiring immortality for himself, nevertheless cannot escape the conclusion that human consciousness does survive bodily death.

If we look into the past we find that Plato postulated a preëxistence of the human soul and he argued in favor of its postexistence or immortality. Aristotle never satisfactorily accounted for the origin of the soul, but he seems to admit implicitly its existence before being united with a body, since he calls it not only immortal but also eternal, something divine. St. Augustine grappled with this knotty problem of the origin of the soul; while he could not admit generationism he was

inclined toward traducianism; however, toward the end of his life he modified his earlier position without furnishing any definite solution of the problem. He could not very well teach the special creation of each human soul, since he had already taught that God created everything at once. By embracing the old Stoic and Neo- 

Platonic doctrine of the seminal reasons, or innate potencies of nature, he implicitly admitted a far-reaching evolution even more far-reaching than that of Lamarck or Darwin, since he did not need to postulate divine intervention in order to explain the origin of life itself. 

St. Thomas taught special creation as the origin of the human soul, and he supposed that it was infused into the human embryo after that had attained a certain degree of development, apparently basing this supposition on certain observations of Aristotle. Many modern followers of Aquinas have modified his doctrine by teaching that the soul is specially created by God and infused into the embryo at the moment of conception. However, it would have been more prudent not to abandon the teaching of Thomas because certain difficulties have arisen since the discovery of what are called identical twins. It is generally known that scientists report that the Dionne quintuplets are beyond doubt identical. 

That means that the quintuplets developed from divisions of a single fertilized ovum. Incidentally it has been announced that although the quintuplets are identical, in the technical sense of course, they differ to a marked degree in mental ability and personality. This certainly merits the attention of scientists and philoso-

phers, especially in view of the environment in which they are being brought up and their relative segregation from varying extraneous influences. It cannot be said that a single soul was divided into five souls at the time of the subdivision of the embryo. Thus it becomes apparent that the theory which holds that the rational soul is not infused until later, that is after a certain period of development of the embryo, is preferable, especially since it is presupposed that the human soul is an immaterial principle, hence not composed of parts which could be separated.

Some, of course, will say that all such difficulties may be avoided by abandoning the idea of an immaterial soul. As a noted example one may mention William 

James, who did not hesitate to do so, saying that we no longer need the hypothesis of a soul. Others regard the idea of a soul as a remnant of past ages, a relic of primitive animism. This would then mean a frankly materialistic explanation of the mind of man and the necessity of admitting that Dante's Divina Commedia, Shakespeare's plays, or Newton's science are the products of matter. Many, however, will not be willing to admit that, and will either assume a skeptical attitude, or relegate the problem of the ultimate nature of the human mind to the region of the unknowable with 

Herbert Spencer. Finally, one may observe that one is not obliged to admit Herbert Spencer's boundaries of the region of the unknowable. 

No one will deny that the problem of personality is one of the most difficult in philosophy. Despite all that

has been written on this subject by the thinkers of the past, the problem still persists and awaits solution. One may say that there are two ways of looking at the world, one from the cosmic point of view, the other from the vantage point of human personality. In the vastness of the cosmos, human beings are, so to speak, lost and take on a character of insignificance. The duration of the human race on earth when compared with the duration of our own galaxy is exceedingly short, and we must believe quite transitory, since most probably life on this planet will be rendered quite impossible within a limited period of time. On the other hand, if we compare the value and achievements of man with those of other animals, it immediately becomes manifest that man is the highest being in this visible world. The enormous masses of the great stars and aggregate of the matter composing the cosmos is relatively insignificant and of little value when compared with the superlative accomplishments of the human race in science, art, literature, philosophy, and religion. The very sciences which teach us the composition of the cosmos and its extent in space and time are a creation of the genius of man, of human personalities.

The advantages enjoyed by the men of today, they owe above all to the assiduous, intelligent labors of persons, of individual, single, human beings. Thus it appears that the most precious possession of the race itself consists of human personalities, and they are precious above all inasmuch as they are minds, not because they are simply physical organisms. Thus, to the cosmic

point of view, in which human beings vanish into the thin air of insignificance, is opposed the personalistic point of view, in which the cosmos emerges from its paltriness only in so far as it becomes the abode of human beings, of persons. It would appear that these two different ways of looking at the world, which may be called personalism and cosmism, are irreconcilably opposed; yet they are in reality mutually complementary. 

The doctrine of personality was never adequately presented by the ancient Greeks and Romans. In this were the Greeks and Romans similar to the inhabitants of the ancient empires of the East, that they frequently failed to recognize the value, dignity, and importance of free human beings, of persons permitted to think, produce, and create without hindrance. The history of philosophy shows that it was not until the controversies occasioned by the various Christological heresies that Christianity was obliged strictly to define the concept of personality, human and divine. The definition of human personality entailed certain practical consequences, particularly the definition of personal rights and their social and legal recognition. Since that time the world has become thoroughly aware of the difficulties associated with the problem of personality. It was Christianity which recognized more clearly than ever before in the history of humanity the superior prerogatives of human beings of whatever race, color or degree of enlightenment. 

Christianity was the first to discover the new dignity and the sacred rights of human persons, whether they be infants or the aged, or even the yet unborn, to whom

also must be conceded certain rights which no one may violate with impunity. 

Fortunately for the world today there is prevalent a far greater respect for all human beings than in former ages. Despite the recurrence of various forms of oppression there is on the whole more freedom in the world since the advent of Christianity. The institution of slavery has not entirely disappeared from the face of the earth; it still persists in remote regions, but there are fewer slaves today than in the Augustan age. 

Strangely enough there are certain tendencies now manifesting themselves which may be regarded as recessions or retrogressions, returns to a barbaric past. 

Dangers are threatening free human persons again, and the dearly bought liberties attained by centuries of the martyrdom of man may be lost, carelessly sacrificed to fancied political exigencies of our own day. Let us hope that the recrudescence of ideologies inimical to preservation of the rights of free persons are but a transient outgrowth of the unrest which has followed upon the greatest war of all history. One may assert that history itself has evinced that the most important guarantee of progress and enlightenment is the respect for personal rights and the free growth and development of persons. 

There is no time more opportune than ours to restate the doctrines of human rights, to redefine the concept of personality and emphasize its value for human society as a whole. Here is an opportunity and, one may say, a duty for philosophers as well as scientists who have ever stood for freedom, who have defended the rights of

persons, who have endured persecution and the unfriendly attitude of unthinking mobs, infected by varying slogans and virulent propaganda, from the days of 

Anaxagoras and Socrates to our own. Philosophy and pure science can thrive only in this clean atmosphere of freedom which has made possible the greatest triumphs of the minds of men. It is encouraging to ascertain that in some countries the conditions for the advance and progress in philosophy and the sciences are distinctly favorable. In those lands in which freedom and the respect for human persons is not interfered with, there always will be some individuals curious enough to occupy themselves with the problems that have ever tortured and taunted the minds of human beings. 

Among those problems which many regard as the most persistent, one finds the problem of survival, or immortality. Those who may expect to face death soon cannot refrain from giving the question some consideration. We are reminded of Socrates discoursing on immortality with his friends while awaiting the day of his execution. Some, however, study the problem so objectively that they are not even concerned with their own survival. Curiosity alone, it appears, prompts them to attempt to discover if anything of man survives bodily death. Some, however, regard the question as insoluble, taking the position that reason cannot conclusively demonstrate survival or immortality, and consequently it cannot give any definite answer regarding the destiny of human personalities. Of course, there are thinkers who will at least examine the problem objec-

tively and impartially. Some have attempted to dispose of the problem by demonstrating the impossibility of immortality. It cannot be said that they have been successful. Finally, it should be observed that from the point of view of human reason a further study of every possible mental capacity of normal and abnormal persons may yield facts hitherto unknown. These the philosophers of the future may be able to put to good advantage by utilizing them in their arguments. Thus they may be able to furnish new evidence for survival and throw more light on the problem of the destiny of individual human consciousnesses and personalities.

CHAPTER EIGHT  

HUMAN reason has ever sought to gain some knowledge about the furthest, the ultimate and supreme being. It appears to many of the greatest thinkers that in order to account satisfactorily for man and the world, the ego and the non-ego, one is obliged to admit some ulterior being, in fact a being without a beginning. Everyone must acknowledge that since something exists, at least the ego, for the non-ego has been regarded by some as the contents of the consciousness of a mind or minds, some being must always have existed, for being cannot come from non-being. Even a solipsist would be prompted to give some account of himself. In other words there must be an eternal being. But where is it?

There are some who suppose that the eternal being is this visible universe, which therefore includes man. Such an explanation has exerted a powerful attraction on poetic minds who are prone to yield to the seductions of the imagination. There are, however, so many facts which militate against this assumption that earnest philosophers hold that it is possible to demonstrate the real existence of an extra-cosmic being, the one who brought the matter of the world into being and is re- 69

sponsible for the manner of the distribution of that gigantic mass in space. 

In religion the word employed to express that being is God. As among the ancient Greeks, so today it is still conventional to employ the same term in both religion and philosophy, although in philosophy many other terms have been and still are in use. It is generally granted that the problem of God is one of transcendent importance in philosophy, especially since it is inescapable that all thinkers who have gone far enough in their attempts to give an ultimate explanation of being are finally confronted with the problem of God. All scientists and philosophers do not adopt the same attitude when brought to the threshold of this problem. Some hesitate to concern themselves with the question of the supreme being. Others decline to deal with it at all. 

They are satisfied with the description of phenomena discoverable by the senses, and the various aids to the senses consisting of scientific laboratory equipment, with an added limited explanation and interpretation. 

Others critically study and examine all the proofs or alleged proofs of the past that have ever been advanced in support of the real existence of God, not simply as an ideal in the human mind. Some, like Hume, regard it as quite impossible to prove the existence of God, because they deny the validity of the principle of causality. However, Hume is cautious enough not to deny categorically the existence of God, for neither he nor anyone else could furnish conclusive evidence for the statement that there is no God.

Kant critically examined the traditional arguments and found them wanting, because he felt that they lacked cogency. He argued that it was impossible for theoretical or pure reason to establish the existence of God. Many who are imperfectly acquainted with 

Kant's teaching remember this position of the sage of Königsberg, but overlook the fact that he did admit the doctrine of the existence of God, regarding it as an unavoidable, inescapable postulate of practical reason as distinguished from pure or theoretical reason. From this it is evident that his attitude was notably different from that of Hume. This position is similar to that of 

Voltaire and Le Play, who held that if there were not a God it would be necessary to invent one. Even Hume taught that he would not be a prudent statesman who would seek to do away with the scientifically unfounded beliefs of the multitude, such as the existence of God. 

Some, philosophers among them, are convinced there is a God because they have experienced Him, and consequently they feel that they can afford to disregard all arguments based on reason and waive the question of their alleged validity or, on the other hand, lack of cogency. As a familiar example one may mention Plotinus who, according to his biographer, Porphyry, experienced ecstasies in which he came in contact with the One directly and intuitively. It should be remembered that Socrates was firmly convinced of having been internally admonished by a divine command to teach his fellow citizens. Thus the reason why some philosophers admitted the existence of God was not because they were

convinced by arguments. There can be no doubt that 

St. Thomas was thoroughly convinced of the validity of his celebrated five arguments for the existence of God. 

One may ask, however, did he personally need those arguments? From what we know of his life it does not appear so. The great St. Augustine was a convert to 

Christianity, and was not only a philosopher but also a theologian of foremost rank. He is often called the 

Doctor of Grace. He unquestionably acknowledged that men might turn to God not simply because they are convinced by arguments, but also because of the divine influence affecting their lives, nay, even their thinking about God. It should be observed that St. 

Augustine's arguments or proofs of the existence of God are less satisfactory than those of St. Thomas. Curiously enough, although living nearer the time of Aristotle than Aquinas, he was not as well acquainted with the Stagirite's philosophy. Apparently, he was not aware of 

Aristotle's arguments for the existence of God, at least not from Aristotle's own writings.

Herbert Spencer boldly separated the knowable from the unknowable. He drew a small circle around the human mind, saying, thus far shalt thou go, but no farther. Many have wondered how Herbert Spencer could have devoted five chapters to the unknowable, if he were consistent. With due respect for the sincere attempts of Spencer to segregate the unknowable from that which is accessible to human reason, one must declare that he was not successful in drawing the boundary between the two domains, for his reasons for making

the division are not convincing and hence not binding on others. Others contend that it is possible to know something of those ultimate ideas consigned by him to the realm of the unknowable. It is interesting to note that according to Herbert Spencer God is unknowable. 

From all that has been said, it would seem that nothing more can be added to what has already been written by theologians, philosophers, and scientists on God. Some believe that no matter what may be said or written in the future it will not change the problem which will remain in statu quo ante. In other words they refuse to deal with a problem to which they think nothing new can be added, and they judge that no matter what scientific discoveries may be made in the future they will not alter the results of philosophical speculation hitherto attained.

That may be true to this extent, that those who have acknowledged the cogency of the arguments proving the existence of God will never find it necessary to alter their position. They would also confidently predict that any attempts to demonstrate that there is no God will be futile. Of any such attempts one might say what the French Encyclopedist, Friedrich Melchior

Grimm, said of the materialism and atheism of D'Holbach, another one of the Encyclopedists, that it was suitable for barbers and chambermaids; implying thereby that those possessing more gifted and trained minds could never be satisfied with the reasoning of D'Holbach or possibly affected by his shallow arguments. One must observe that those who admit the existence of an

author or architect of the world, will also be inclined to admit that it is possible to increase and augment one's knowledge of that being, in other words, that one may acquire a fuller and more complete knowledge of God. 

They will take the position that as long as the sciences have not uttered the last word on the constitution and arrangement of the universe, the knowledge of the Creator of the universe which the reason of man may attain is susceptible of increase and extension. After all, there is good reason for assuming such an attitude and for keeping an open mind on this subject. Granting that it is true, as was told the Greeks for the first time by Anaxagoras, that mind orders and rules the world, it becomes apparent that the more one learns of the physical cosmos, the more one may learn of the being to whom has been given the title, Architect of the world. The last chapter of natural philosophy has not been written, the final word has not been spoken by those who have specialized in theodicy, as Leibnitz named the branch of philosophy dealing with this subject. We may suppose that the sciences will continue to advance, new facts will be discovered, new interpretations will be forced upon thinkers, all of which will throw more abundant light on the philosophical problem of the 

Greatest Mind, the Supreme Being.

The dazzling results attained by the sciences during the last three hundred years arouse everyone's admiration. During that time some of the most acute and gifted minds which have ever appeared among men have devoted much time to the study of the universe. Despite

all these efforts a great deal remains unknown. The limited minds of men have been studying the cosmos produced and disposed by a far greater, a truly titanic mind, a Being which wills that the universe be as it is and not otherwise, that the phenomena of nature behave according to certain determined ways which are constant and reliable. These are the laws of nature, many of which men have discovered and expressed in words, and more concisely by mathematical equations, or formulas. All will acknowledge that the sciences as such are not found in nature, they are the product of the mind of man. While it remains true that the sciences are not discovered ready-made, but laboriously constructed by human beings, yet reality itself is arranged in such a manner by the greatest mind that it was possible for men to build and erect the imposing edifice of knowledge constituted by the totality of the sciences. 

Facts are discovered and established or ascertained, events occur in a regular sequence, things behave in such a fashion that they permit man to make certain generalizations, the sum of which constitutes a special domain of human knowledge, a science. However much we may admire the conquests of modern science and the genius of the men who have made the advancement of science possible, we must ever remember that their work has not been completed, the end is not in sight. Thus as the prospects of the sciences are simply boundless from the point of view of the human mind, so the outlook for that philosophical discipline treating of God is also boundless. Much yet remains to be learned, and

many erroneous ways of looking at nature will very probably have to be corrected in the future as they were in the past, while still other doctrines will need to be modified to a limited extent. As an illustration one may mention in this connection that one of the principal objections against Aristotle's argument for the existence of God, based on the idea of the Prime Mover, is that his view of the universe was partially erroneous. Moreover, the same objection is urged against the same argument as employed by Thomas Aquinas. It must be admitted that both Aristotle's and Aquinas' view of the physical cosmos was defective, yet a slight modification of the argument, many will hold, makes it as valid as any other. Perhaps our views of the universe will also be regarded as erroneous five hundred years hence. We may be quite sure, however, that as Aristotle's view was not entirely incorrect, so our present explanations will be found to be at least partly true in the ages to come. 

At all events, it becomes quite obvious that the more we learn of the world the more we also learn of the architect and builder of the cosmos. 

It may be asserted that the past of theodicy presents a long vista of discarded anthropomorphisms. Many will acknowledge that the idea of God has gradually become more clear and distinct, and that men have grown accustomed to express themselves of God with greater precision. The Bible itself, for obvious reasons, contains many anthropomorphisms. After all, we shall ever think of God as men are wont to do, that is, in a human fashion. The total and complete elimination of anthro-

pomorphisms appears to be an ideal not to be entirely realized in philosophy. If we are to judge the future by the past we may expect to learn far more of the eternal, the ultimate, the Supreme Being, just as the knowledge which we gain of the world progresses by leaps and bounds. On many minds of even the most celebrated thinkers, the problem of the Supreme Being has exerted a certain fascination. Some philosophers have hesitated to venture into speculations about God. Others, however, are more bold, more curious, more intrepid, and they become more reverential as they seek to learn all that can be known about that being whom more recently Jeans has called the Mathematician, just as others have named Him the Great Architect.

It is worth mentioning that as the sciences have progressed and our knowledge of the causes of phenomena has been extended, it has become less necessary to postulate the direct intervention of the Creator of the universe to account for facts. It is reported that when 

Napoleon asked Laplace about the function of God in his system of the universe, he replied that he did not need the hypothesis. Some have regarded that answer as blasphemous. However, there is no evidence of such an intent. One may say that it is the ideal of science and philosophy to explain as much as possible without appealing to the activity of the Deity. After all, Thomas Aquinas held as a sort of axiom the tenet that when events or occurrences in nature can be explained by the activity of secondary causes, one may not ascribe them to the direct intervention of the primary cause,

God. In this fashion it is possible to establish an agreement between St. Thomas and the Marquis de la Place, who might justly pride himself on his ability to explain the present state or condition of the world by its antecedent stages produced by the activity of secondary causes without ascribing it to the direct and immediate influence of God. Yet far off beyond the comprehension of the limited minds of the men who have so assiduously studied the universe, there certainly exists the greatest intelligence, the unlimited mind and the most powerful being, whose glories are proclaimed by the work of his hands, to use a poetic expression, namely, this wonderful and beautiful world.

CHAPTER NINE  

"THUS WAS Beauty sent from heaven, the lovely ministress of Truth and Good in this dark world," wrote the poet. Plato has shown that human beings may gradually ascend from the consideration of the beauty of this world to the farthest source of the beautiful, God Himself. Human life would unquestionably possess far less value and attraction if man did not possess an aesthetic sense, if he could not enjoy and appreciate the beautiful and the sublime. The ability to appreciate the beautiful must be regarded as a distinctive characteristic of human beings, first of all because they possess a sufficient degree of intelligence, and also because they are animals capable of sense experience. Kant advances the supposition that it would not be possible for human beings to appreciate products of art and enjoy the beautiful scenes and aesthetic objects brought forth by nature, if they were pure intelligences or discarnate spirits. However, since they are sensuous-rational beings, the aesthetic attitude comes within their reach. 

It appears quite natural for man to seek the beautiful, or that which to him appears beautiful and aesthetically satisfying, depending upon his cultural status and degree of enlightenment as well as his taste. All will readily 79

acknowledge that human beings are animals which first of all try more or less successfully to beautify and embellish themselves. Next both men and women endeavor to make their clothing and various articles of apparel as attractive as possible, adding jewelry and, often enough, showy trinkets which are intended to enhance individual beauty and serve the purposes of personal adornment. Then man proceeds to embellish the various tools and utensils which he needs in daily life as well as the furniture in his home. The home itself is made beautiful and may be transformed into a veritable objet d'art. 

The grounds and surroundings of the home are made more attractive by the skillful arrangement of shrubbery and flower beds; fountains and statuary may also be employed to heighten the effects obtained by plants and trees. Besides that men often fill their homes with paintings and bronzes, ceramics and rugs and tapestries. 

They gather about them all those objects which, at least to a limited extent, can satisfy their craving for the beautiful. They also experience the need of poetry and drama as well as vocal and instrumental music in order to live a truly civilized life. Fortunately there have been men who have gone farther than their own homes and their own estates. They have sought to make beautiful entire cities, nay, even countries. It is apparent that it is an advantage for any country to have such enlightened and public-spirited citizens who become patrons of the arts and promoters of good taste. Those who have succeeded in making their city beautiful will attract travelers from every part of the world who will

wish to admire its architectural wonders. Unfortunately some have neglected their cities to such an extent that they have become ugly and hideous. 

This brings one to the consideration of the distinction between the beautiful and the ugly. There is no doubt that the ugly has attracted far more attention in recent times than in ancient Greece. It cannot be said that the relation between the beautiful and the ugly has ever been entirely adequately studied and described. 

Some regard the ugly as merely a foil for the beautiful, since by its striking contrast it heightens the aesthetic effect of the beautiful. Certain it is that the ugly plays a far more prominent part in modern art than in the ancient. Aristotle recognizes the function of the ugly, especially in comedy where it often becomes ridiculous; however, he recognizes it as an element which makes the good appear attractive and beautiful. Thus the ugly itself may become comical and grotesque. The comic is another idea which still needs elucidation and a more adequate presentation. The comic undoubtedly causes pleasure and satisfaction and plays a really important rôle in the life of individuals and even of whole nations. 

People need the comical, especially at certain times of their life, often as a sort of antidote. It greatly helps to make life endurable, particularly in time of stress or danger. Man is an animal which often laughs on the slightest provocation. It appears that man must be an animal endowed with a higher intelligence in order to be able to appreciate the comical, the ridiculous, the grotesque, especially defects and disharmonies which cause

no one any harm or injury. Often enough it is advantageous and truly valuable to be able to see the humor of a situation, to evaluate properly wit and sarcasm, and to detect the incongruous. Thus it appears that laughter is a concomitant of a distinctly human intelligence, a sign or evidence of the power to reason. At any rate, the problem of the relation of the ugly and the comical to the beautiful is one worthy of the attention of thinkers, even today after all that has been written on the subject. However, that branch of philosophy, or philosophical discipline, named aesthetics seems to be the least developed of all the parts of philosophy. Consequently specialization in this enchanting province of inquiry should bring many rewards.

Much has been written on the beautiful, on the characteristics of beauty, on the canons of good taste and on the various arts since the days of the ancient Greeks, and yet much more remains to be written by the aestheticians of today and to morrow. No one will dispute the statement that in the ancient world the Greeks excelled in artistic ability and good taste. They, above all, were competent judges of the beautiful in nature; they could evaluate and appreciate it properly, whether it be the delightful scenes and attractive views supplied by the rock-bound shores of the azure Mediterranean, or whether it be living bodies of men or animals, young or old. They possessed a marvelously fertile imagination which made it possible for them to create a beautiful mythology. They peopled the earth, the air, the woods, the waters, the stars and even the underworld,

with beautiful beings, the product of their own exuberant and rich, and yet tastefully restrained fancy and fantasy. They not only wrote unparalleled poetry, composed superlative dramas, constructed magnificent and aesthetically perfect temples, adorning them with sculptures of such a high degree of artistic excellence that they have scarcely ever been equalled, but they also engaged in theoretical discussions concerning the beautiful and its relation to the true and the good. They inquired into the ultimate nature and the most remote origin of the beautiful; they sought out its essential qualities, irrespective of whether the beautiful object be a product of nature or of man. Thus, for Plato, beauty is the very beginning of philosophy, and the lover of beauty is identified with the philosopher. The contemplation of the beauty of this world of phenomena leads to the awakening and the recollection of the beauties of the world above; the ravishing world of ideas, in which is found the absolute beauty; the beauty without beginning or end; the ultimate source of even the faintest reflections of beauty in this world. 

Aristotle is generally regarded as the father of the philosophy of art and of the beautiful in the West if not in the Far East. It appears that he never had the time and the opportunity to express fully his ideas on the subject of the arts, yet his fragmentary writings have prompted many noted thinkers to further research in this fascinating field. He directed attention to the objective characteristics of the beautiful, such as a certain magnitude which may not go beyond a definite

limit, symmetry, order, and above all, harmonious proportion. He also concerned himself with the subjective element requisite for the enjoyment of the beautiful, namely, the pleasure which beauty begets; that should not be simply an organic pleasure, but a truly rational enjoyment. Of course, Aristotle recognizes degrees of rational enjoyment. At any rate he supplied the foundation for all subsequent inquiries into the nature of the beautiful. Many definitions of the beautiful have made their appearance since the days of Aristotle, numerous attempts have been made to furnish a totally adequate and entirely satisfactory theory of the beautiful, but it cannot be maintained that these results have been entirely successful. Some even hold that it is impossible to give a definition of the beautiful which would possess universal validity. At most, they say, one may speak of the ideas of beauty of a certain country, or race, or of some particular epoch, and even then one may only advert to the prevalent views of the cultured classes. Be that as it may, no one will deny that this matter is still open to discussion. There are others who contend that it is not possible to define beauty satisfactorily because the manifestations of beauty are too complex. There is much truth in this statement. Admitting, however, the complexity of the many manifestations of the beautiful, it still remains possible to classify them and reduce them to several principal groups. Although aesthetics has long ago passed beyond the inchoate stage, it has not yet attained a final or perfect form. The science of psychology has made

notable contributions to the progress of the philosophy of the beautiful, by supplying many facts unknown in the past. Physiology has taught us much regarding the relations between the sense organs and the feeling of pleasure. Some feel that the sense of the beautiful is associated with the procreative tendencies of men. 

These and many other topics are still awaiting further study and further tests and experiments. 

The forms of expression of the beautiful have multiplied in number and variety, and they have become more free and unhampered. Thus the Christian civilization of the West eventually expressed its spiritual and romantic beauty in forms of which the Greeks never dreamed; it is sufficient to mention the wonderful 

Gothic cathedrals with their marvelous stained glass windows. One of the most perfect examples of Gothic art and architecture is the transcendently beautiful Sainte Chapelle in Paris which was erected during the thirteenth century. To the very complex and composite Arabic civilization we owe both the Alhambra and the Taj Mahal, the latter being regarded as perhaps the most perfect architectural gem ever constructed on the face of the earth. It is true that both Plato and 

Aristotle lived in that Athens of the golden age which was largely due to the presiding and directing genius of Pericles. It is justly conceded that such an environment was singularly conducive to beautiful reveries, aesthetic transports and artistic exaltation, as well as to philosophical speculation resulting in various theories concerning the nature of the beautiful. It is true that

we may no longer contemplate the art and architecture of ancient Greece and Athens, in particular, except through the aid of the reconstructive science and reproductive imagination of the archaeologists. Nevertheless, works of art have accumulated to such an extent in almost every part of the world that modern thinkers, aestheticians, and philosophers, owing to the opportunities for travel and the personal inspection and study of works of art and the beauties of nature, are offered advantages utterly unattainable to even the wealthiest men of the ancient world. Today the great museums of the world, those carefully guarded repositories of the priceless collections of the creations of the supreme artists of all nations, and ages, and countries, are at the disposal of those who would philosophize on the beautiful. 

Moreover, today, not only in the great capitals of the world, but even in obscure and out of the way places, thanks to the radio, it is possible to listen to excellent performances of the compositions of Bach, Mozart, and Beethoven, and enjoy the operas of Verdi, Bizet, and Wagner. These make available to millions aesthetic experiences undreamed of by the greatest of the Greeks. 

Modern thinkers since Voltaire, Burke, and Kant, distinguish clearly between the beautiful and the sublime.

According to Kant beauty causes pleasure by its limited or bounded form, whereas the sublime causes satisfaction because it is limitless or boundless. This distinction is quite generally admitted today, although there is room for further discussion of the relation between the two. 

The task of the writer on aesthetics is becoming increas-

ingly difficult owing to the rapid and bewildering succession of vogues and styles in artistic production. Many question the artistic and aesthetic value of some of the poetry appearing today. Others think that some of the recent examples of painting, even with the support of a sympathetic attitude cannot be regarded as aesthetically satisfying, or as works of art. Some modern musical compositions seem to be nothing but a series of vulgar, if not painful dissonances. Some architectural productions seem to verge on the grotesque. Venerable standards of the great artists seem to be passing into oblivion. 

Hence today, more than ever before, the need of some permanent and generally valid standards by which to judge productions, claiming to be works of art, is making itself felt. There should be some universally recognized canons of beauty and norms of artistic creation, as well as criteria of good taste, which would make possible an objective grading and classification of works of art, and well grounded distinctions between the ephemeral and that which possesses a permanent value. The thinker who selects aesthetics as his specialty has singularly abundant opportunities to help make the world more beautiful, life more attractive and the many millions of people happier and more contented. The last word on the fine arts or the applied arts has not been written. There is still much to be taught about the sublime, the beautiful, the tragic, the comic, the pathetic, the ugly, and in general on the different subjective reactions to aesthetic objects.

CHAPTER TEN  

THE IDEAL life from the philosophical point of view is that which is in conformity with human nature. Men are obviously animals, but they are also endowed with reason, hence every man who has attained the age of discretion should behave like a rational being, especially every philosopher. A philosophical life is inseparable from rational conduct, or a life of reason. It becomes apparent that if philosophy is to serve as a guide to life it must furnish a satisfactory Lebensanschauung. Many, however, agree with David Hume who contended that men's lives are not guided by philosophy, but rather by habits, feelings, and emotions. No one will deny that the lives of many millions are at least partially guided and directed by the religion which they profess. It is true, of course, that the vast majority of men have never had the opportunity to study philosophy, hence it is evident that they escape the direct influence of philosophy. Still they cannot elude the influence of their cultural environment, of their own civilization, of the degree of enlightenment of their native land which, at least to a limited extent, is to be ascribed to the influence of the great thinkers of their nation. However, there are many others who have studied philosophy and who 88

simultaneously adhere to some religious beliefs. The problem of the relation between religion and philosophy arises again for each generation. Some philosophers have voiced the opinion that more and more of the spiritual work of the Church is passing to the universities, in particular to the departments of philosophy of the universities. Such a view may appear preposterous to some, yet on closer examination one may find some justification for it. For those of the educated classes who have abandoned all religious beliefs and practices philosophy alone can take the place of religion. No one will deny that many in this country have drifted away from religion entirely. The less well informed often turn to superstition or magic, when they have thrown religion overboard. Even in the most enlightened countries many people wear amulets or charms of some sort, or believe in superstitions akin to those of primitive men. One has but to consult the advertising sections of our large metropolitan dailies to ascertain the number of crystal gazers, clairvoyants, mediums, fortune tellers, and quacks of various kinds many of whom gain more than a modest income. It may not be generally known that their clients are not always the poor and the ignorant. This is not a unique phenomenon found in this country alone; its counterpart is found in all parts of the globe. While it is generally true that philosophy and superstition are incompatible both may thrive in the same country. As is well known some philosophers, Hegel among them, held that religion should be regarded as a transitory phenomenon which is to be superseded by

philosophy when men will have attained a sufficiently high degree of intellectual development. Many of course will not adopt that view. They find that religion, whether natural or revealed, is not incompatible with even the very highest philosophy. 

A century has elapsed since the days of Hegel, but we do not find that philosophy has taken the place of religion. Both religion and philosophy remain, and we have every reason to hold that they will endure as long as there are men on this earth. At all events the problem of the relation between philosophy and religion still confronts us. Of course, there are many who feel they have satisfactorily harmonized their philosophical tenets with their religious beliefs, consequently, they personally are not concerned with the question of the relation between religion and philosophy. At times, religion and philosophy may be identified, in the sense that philosophy becomes the highest religion even of some of the most celebrated thinkers. It may be asserted that Plato's religion was his own philosophic monotheism. Similarly one may say that Cicero and Seneca had no other religion except their philosophy. Spinoza was called by 

Novalis a God-intoxicated man. Certainly Spinoza had no religion other than his philosophy, for he rejected both the Old and the New Testament, admitting no revealed religion whatsoever. This frequently is the case whenever the fact of revelation is not admitted. One may mention in this connection some natural-born philosophers and entirely exceptional thinkers of humble origin, like Epictetus, the slave, Ammonius Saccas, the

porter, and the cobbler philosopher of Görlitz, Jacob

Boehme, whose philosophy was their religion. However, there are other thinkers whose religion and philosophy are not identical. They have accepted revelation, hence their religion is not simply a natural religion, whose truths are attainable by unaided human reason, but a supernatural religion, some truths of which could never have been discovered by reason alone. They regard their religion as being above their philosophy but not contrary to it. They always grant the revelation of God priority over their philosophical tenets, hence their lives are influenced by both their religion and their philosophy.

The number of men whose lives have been directed by their philosophy has always been small and most probably will always remain quite limited. Most men's lives are guided by religion or by superstition, sometimes by both. It has been a difficult task to combat superstition and unreasonable beliefs and practices. The advance of man has been rather slow in this respect. It appears that owing to the limited mental equipment and low intelligence quotients of so many human beings, as disclosed by the more extensive use of intelligence tests, the complete elimination of superstition will remain an unattainable ideal. However, the more exalted religions have succeeded in elevating man, although they have at the same time experienced great difficulties in ridding themselves, or rather their adherents, of various irrational beliefs and magical practices. It is apparent that it is not the task of ridding religion itself of superstitions, or

preventing the adhesion of superstitious accretions, but of prevailing upon those who are members of a religious group to discard their unreasonable beliefs, or superstitions. This is one important function of religion which evinces its immense value in the history of the successive civilizations which have emerged since the dawn of history. Some civilizations, in fact, whole human societies including many nations, have been almost exclusively the product of religion. Despite all doleful predictions about the disappearance of religion, it will continue to live, for men will ever experience its need. 

Many will concede that religions have exerted a greater influence on the human race and have contributed more to the advancement of mankind than philosophy, although others will hold that humanity has gone forward in spite of religion which has been the declared enemy of all progress and enlightenment. Unfortunately, some put all religions into the same category and do not see the vast differences between them. Their attitude is due in some respect to prejudice and preconceived notions, but it is also due to ignorance of the true rôle of religions in the life of humanity, and particularly to the lack of a thorough study of comparative religion and the history of religions. There are philosophers as well as scientists who do not conceal their indifference toward all religions, while others do not assume an entirely objective attitude toward religion. At all events, their attitude appears to be subject to considerable variation. The followers of Karl 

Marx regard religion as an opiate for the people which

helps to keep them in subjection and makes them endure the misery consequent upon their economic exploitation, especially by holding out the vain and forlorn hope of a happier life beyond the grave. Auguste Comte felt that religion was necessary, but being dissatisfied with the religion of his day, he set about the task of forming a new positive religion, whose chief object of worship was to be the Great Being, humanity. Many became disciples of Comte and accepted his system of positive philosophy, but most declined to follow Comte as a reformer, or a founder of a religion. Some looked upon religion simply as a phenomenon, as an historical fact, and judged all religions by their utility, applying to them the verdict of history. Many did not see why 

Comte's religion should be an improvement on the religions of the past, particularly the religion than prevalent in France, the Catholic Religion. That was the position of Emile Littré, one of the most noted positivists, who refused to accept Comte's religious vagaries. 

Others have admitted the social value of religion, like Voltaire, but strenuously fought the Church authorities, or the heads of the organized religions. Montesquieu, although viciously attacking the hierarchy of the Church, willingly acknowledged the superior worth of Christianity. No one will deny that the science of history, specifically the history of religions, has made rapid progress since the eighteenth century. Today more and more philosophers are assuming a quite dispassionate attitude toward religion, in particular toward the Christian religion; this attitude is not always due to

an apathy or indifference, but is often due to a sympathetic appreciation of the tremendous value of religion and its dominant rôle in the life of a nation. Many no longer regard religion as a more or less simple ideology and a bundle of rites and ceremonies reserved for the common people; on the contrary, they recognize that it may play a significant part in the lives of even the greatest thinkers and scientists. They do not see the impossibility of reconciling religious beliefs and dogmas with the data supplied by the sciences and with the firmly grounded doctrines of the philosophers. 

It becomes immediately obvious that, since the most noted thinkers have established the real, not simply ideal, existence of God, a most intelligent and most powerful being, it becomes necessary to acknowledge that Being as supreme and above all, as well as the moral order which is ultimately derived from that Being. This forces one to adopt a loftier view of religion and its function in the lives of all men, learned or illiterate, rich or poor. Some, however, contend that the supposed revealed religions cannot be admitted by any advanced thinker or true philosopher. They take the position that philosophy, along with the sciences, has definitely disposed of any revealed religions, whether Judaism or 

Buddhism, Christianism or Mohammedanism. One is reminded of the alleged quip of the Hohenstaufen, Emperor Frederick II, according to which there were three great impostors in the world, Moses, Christ and Mohammed. However, these religions are not the same. On closer examination it will be found that they are quite

different from one another. It will also be found that it is possible to distinguish true from false revelation and that there exist very good reasons for accepting revelation. Those, for instance, who followed Christ and accepted His teaching did not do so blindly; their adhesion to Christianity often followed a long period of mature deliberation. Thus, contrary to what many have thought, philosophy itself may point the way to the acceptance of revelation. Philosophers who have admitted the cogency of the arguments proving the existence of God must examine the possibility of revelation, and then any seriously alleged facts of revelation, as well as the criteria or tests of revelation. The most cautious attitude or position toward miracles and supposedly fulfilled prophecies should be that of an open mind; this is evinced by an approach free from any preestablished views or notions due to temporary vogues. 

It should be remembered that some of the greatest thinkers of the world, when given the opportunity, accepted revelation or believed in revealed doctrines. One may not doubt that they were actuated by the highest of motives.

Religion has been and still is a powerful influence determining to a notable extent the destinies of nations, and as such it will be the object of continued scrutiny and research. One of the stock objections against any revelation or alleged revealed doctrines is that it is utterly impossible to harmonize them with the data of the natural sciences and the inferences that may be drawn therefrom. It is true that the content of some

supposed revelations has appeared rather trivial, and so intermingled with myth and fantasy that it could not possibly be acknowledged by any serious and mature normal mind. This would indicate, of course, that the content of any alleged revelation should be examined and subjected to certain tests, and that every revelation must be considered separately, on its own merits. Theoretically and a priori there should be no contradictions between the truths revealed by God and those established by human reason from the study and observation of this world, which admittedly is also from God. As a matter of fact, however, there have been notable conflicts between philosophy and the sciences on one side and religion on the other. These conflicts have been occasioned either by mistakes in interpreting the sacred writings containing revelation, or to mistakes in interpreting facts, phenomena, and the data of experience. 

Christian revelation does maintain that its doctrines do not contradict the established facts and proven doctrines of science and philosophy, and that faith and reason are reconcilable. Such claims deserve to be examined and studied if an impartial judgment is to be pronounced on them. Of course, Christian revelation contains or includes mysteries not fully comprehensible to the minds of men; the doctrine of the Trinity and the Incarnation are typical examples. A priori, divine revelation, since it comes from the greatest mind, may contain such propositions whose predicate does not appear to belong to the subject, at least to the limited mind of man, which fails to perceive the nexus between the sub-

ject and the predicate of those propositions, but which accepts them as true on the authority of the revealing God. When carefully examined, these propositions are found not to be patently opposed to reason, or absurd. 

That, at any rate, is the attitude of Catholic philosophers and theologians.

It should be noted that a great deal of philosophy has entered into the different religions, and philosophy has served to elucidate and explain the religious doctrines which have been accepted on authority. Certainly philosophy has, in all these cases, performed a very valuable function and service. Moreover, philosophy may study and observe all religions and judge them, particularly by their results, by their effects on the course of progress and enlightenment, and consequently by their value to the human race. After the completion of so many thorough studies of a great many religions by many men in recent times, the philosopher of religion. has much more material at his disposal than in former ages, and hence this field of the philosophy of religion has become a particularly interesting one, providing a very attractive and profitable occupation for those who would devote themselves to its cultivation. So much still remains undone, so many data are to be correlated, so many inferences to be drawn, that many workers are tempted to engage in this task by the prospects of singularly rich and profitable findings and results. Such labor can only bring about a more thorough understanding of the function and utility of religion in general, and also its value for social life and the stability of

the body politic. Democracies, in particular, cannot thrive without the active support of many upright and honest citizens, and hence the vast importance of religion and its contributions to culture and refinement which have placed civilized men of the leading nations of the world on the heights which they now enjoy. 

Peoples and nations have been elevated to their high status by their religion; they should give heed lest they decline, degenerate, and even disappear, because of their neglect or abandonment of the religion which was the foundation of their greatness. Hence, it is not astonishing that rulers and governments of powerful nations should have interested themselves in the revival of religion as the most powerful support of honesty, probity, and of a moral and upright conduct.

This brings one to another problem which philosophers cannot avoid considering, namely, the relation between religion and morality. Some philosophers hold that religion is a product of morality, while on the other hand there are those who contend that morality, or a certain definite kind of conduct and behaviour of men and women is to be ascribed to the influence of the religion which they profess. Human beings depict and manifest their beliefs and convictions as well as their devotedness and even holiness in their daily lives. In support of this statement one may draw attention to such striking examples of truly beautiful and inspiring lives as those of St. Francis of Assisi and St. Vincent de 

Paul, wholeheartedly devoted to the humble service of God and the welfare of man. One must finally observe

that Christ Himself expected the behaviour and conduct of His disciples and followers to be quite distinctive and easily discernible when He said: "By this shall all men know that you are my disciples, if you have love one for another." John 13, 35. The world awaits with renewed interest the writings of competent philosophers on religion, comparative religion, the history of religions, various religious phenomena and mystical experiences, as well as the place of religion in education.

CHAPTER ELEVEN  

THE CONDUCT of a great many people is regulated by their religion. If they do not have or profess any religion they at least live among people who do, and hence the conduct of all in the community will be guided by the prevailing opinion which exercises a potent influence on the behaviour of all men. Even the educated and thinking classes in civilized regions will not escape the influence of notions prevailing among the less well informed classes, but besides that they find it necessary to have some theory of conduct, especially if they have no religion. Consequently, for them the highest appeal in ethical matters will be philosophy. It is from the above mentioned classes that emerge the leaders of the world, especially those individuals who are particularly endowed with strength of will, capacity for sustained effort, and who manifest unusual tenacity of purpose. 

Such men and women have ever been strong enough to attain a superior position among their contemporaries. 

The conduct of leaders has ever served as a model for others as well as an excuse for reprehensible behaviour. 

Wherever a scientific theory of conduct is lacking, ethical behaviour is inculcated by means of models and examples. However, wherever a theory of conduct, or 100

the science of ethics has made its appearance, moral behaviour and correct deportment are taught by means of general principles and laws. The science of ethics is conventionally regarded as one of the philosophical disciplines, in other words, it is a part of philosophy. 

Despite numerous efforts to make ethics strictly descriptive, it is at present, and in the future will continue to be, a normative science.

It is obvious that ethics as a normative science must be based on the assumption that at least some acts of some men are sometimes free. Of course, all human beings are not free, one need but mention infants and those whose minds are deranged. Human beings are regarded as capable of acts which escape the universal determininism reigning in the cosmos. In fact, we have no other example of free acts except those of man. 

Ethics is also founded on the assumption that there is a distinction between free acts that are good and those that are evil. It is true that men may not always agree on what is good and what is evil, but it appears that wherever we find men, whether civilized or primitive, they universally agree on this, that good is to be done and evil is to be avoided. Hence there arises the problem of the ultimate reason for the distinction between good and evil. It may be asserted, at least as far as 

European philosophy is concerned, that the science of morals was originated by Socrates of Athens, and yet despite its antiquity it cannot be maintained that this branch of philosophy has attained its highest degree of

development and perfection. There are still so many ethical problems which are subject to dispute. go

The determinists, of course, are not confronted with ethical problems, at least from the theoretical point of view. Practically, however, determinism simply does not work, at least no system of jurisprudence can be founded on determinism. It should be remembered that 

Kant regarded the freedom of the will as an unavoidable postulate of practical reason. Many, of course, will farther and contend that it is possible theoretically to demonstrate that man is a free being. All will concede, however, that statesmen and legislators do not ask the philosophers whether they have proved that man is capable of free actions; they must assume that to be true. It is well known, however, that the writings on ethics, especially of the more celebrated philosophers have frequently been studied by those whose duty it is to guide the ship of state. We have every reason to believe that the books of philosophers will also be consulted in the future. Since so many laws are enacted with the special purpose of preventing injustice, the question arises why certain actions are unjust. The ancient Stoics argued that man should live in accordance with nature, in particular with his own human, that is, rational nature. It was formerly assumed that human nature has ever been the same. However, more recently the doctrine of evolution has introduced a disturbing factor by assuming that human nature is changing as it has changed in the past and may be expected to change in the future. Some contend that particularly under the

stress of modern urban life certain concessions may have to be made to human nature, which, they suppose, is rapidly changing, and hence some modifications of formerly accepted standards of conduct will be necessary. 

Others will argue that modern economic conditions have greatly changed former estimates of the honesty or dishonesty of some business transactions. Many sharp business practices which can scarcely be reconciled with the theoretical demands of justice are excused on the ground that they have become quite general. Such opinions, some will say, would indicate that new conditions have certainly arisen which compel a reconsideration of former ethical norms of conduct. On the other hand many will cry out that too much is often sacrificed to expediency, that the valuable norms of conduct and moral standards erected through ages of control, temperance, and discipline, are in danger of being lost to the great detriment and peril of those nations which are lightly abandoning them. Many serious students of history, culture, and civilization detect formidable threats and menaces to the security of the commonwealth in a gradual relinquishing of moral principles and a weakening of the moral fibre of the people. They point to the decline of Greece and Rome, not to mention many other less known cases of decay and retrogression. All this makes manifest that the tasks of philosophy have not all been accomplished, that much remains to be done, many restatements, reconsiderations, and also revisions will be necessary. The prospects of philosophy in the field of ethics and morals are particularly bright. Ex-

perience shows that far more people are interested in the theory of morals and conduct than in metaphysical speculations and ingenious hypotheses regarding the origin or the ultimate nature of the things constituting the physical cosmos. Philosophers of today and tomorrow will be called upon for professional advice and timely warning, and expert opinion will be expected from them.

Thus, many have regarded the indissolubility of the marriage bond as one of the primary conditions for the continuance of Christian civilization which has played such an important rôle in the destinies of Europe. Today the sanctity of the marriage bond is far less respected than in the past. Consequently it is causing some concern in the minds of those interested in the future of the human race. The opinions of learned men, of philosophers, on this problem are often preferred to those of clergymen, who are regarded as biased and prejudiced. Without entering into the question of the accuracy of this judgment, there are people who think the views of philosophers are expected to display a certain note of objectivity and detachment, and consequently some feel that such views will possess greater value and carry more weight. It is not improbable that, owing to the increase in the number of divorces in this country, the question of the permanence of the marriage union will take on increasing significance so that our statesmen and lawmakers will not be able to disregard it with impunity. One has reason to believe that the writings of philosophers on this subject will be con-

sulted. Ever so often, history teaches us, a serious reaction sets in against a real or a threatened dissolution of morals, bringing with it a wave of reforms, and a multitude of reformers and would-be reformers who feel they must save mankind. Ultimately, any judgments on the morals of the day must be based on certain theoretical principles which are to be invoked in support of measures of practical expediency. In every age there appear those who deplore the changes in morals and sigh for the good old times when all were virtuous and honest. However, the past is often idealized and the conditions of the present age misinterpreted and frequently distorted. 

It may be true, however, that in our day as in Athens of old, perhaps too many past practices are being questioned if not totally abandoned. Traditional behaviour is being subjected to criticism and the reasons for prescribing any conduct or norms of conduct are being demanded before the prescribed norms are accepted. Frequently enough the reasons supplied are found to be quite insufficient and unconvincing.

It is above all the task of the philosopher to discover the principles of ethics and furnish the foundation of morals of any epoch. This may be controverted, however, since some will say that as all this work has been done in the past, it is only necessary to present properly to the younger generation the dicta of thinkers long since passed away. There is some truth in this view. Yet the lessons of the past must be relearned by each generation. 

Moreover, the people of today are confronted by situations often quite different and far more complicated

than in earlier times. Modern life is rapidly changing, and yet, despite all changes, ethical conduct is always expected, the rights of others must be respected, and just laws must be enacted. Hence the philosophical discipline which is called ethics, is, so to speak, in a fluid state, for the need of new adaptations of well accepted principles is constantly arising. 

One striking feature of the life of our day is the alrnost unbelievable number of laws being passed by the numerous legislative bodies, and also the rules and ordinances of the various town, county, municipal, state, and federal governments in our country. It has often been asked if the laws which are passed respond to a real public need. There is no doubt that some laws have been so unpopular that they were consistently ignored by the public to such an extent that they soon became quite inoperative and obsolete. Laws have been enacted which were so contrary to public opinion that they were unenforceable. A noted example in recent years was the law prohibiting the use of alcoholic beverages. It is not surprising that as soon as the prohibition legislation went into effect, the question arose whether the law was binding in conscience. Certainly prohibition was, if not the direct cause, at least the occasion or pretext for disregarding so many laws that a general reign of lawlessness threatened the country. It should be observed that there are laws forbidding abortion and yet the extent of the practice according to more or less reliable data is truly alarming. Even a new word, abortorium, has been coined, although it may not be generally accepted.

The word has been applied to veritable establishments devoted to performing abortions, or, one might say, designed to correct the failure of the numerous and varied contraceptives in use on an enormous scale. The sale of contraceptives is also proscribed by law, yet they are readily available. From this it appears that new ethical problems are constantly arising and demanding the attention of philosophers and lawyers. 

The question of the dissemination of sex information to children is something that has been attracting much attention in this country and abroad. Here again is a very delicate problem calling for expert opinion of psychologists, educators, and specialists in ethics. The facility with which sterilization may now be effected, especially in the male, naturally has brought about an increase in the number of cases either voluntary or involuntary. The question of whether the state has the right to enforce sterilization of criminals and defectives has been acrimoniously debated. The morality of voluntary vasectomy is a problem which must be examined by those who specialize in the study of the norms of human conduct. The success which artificial insemination or impregnation has attained owing to the development of an improved technique has raised the question of the morality of such actions. The problem of divorce is daily acquiring a deeper significance for the future of the nation, and the question of the morality of the actions of the judges and the attorneys engaged in such legal practice is demanding attention, especially when divorce is sought for relatively trivial

reasons. History plainly teaches that the family and the welfare of the family is the very basis of all social stability and all progress and civilization. Many are of the opinion that the disintegration of so many families will spell the doom of this nation or any other, where similar conditions prevail.

Apart, of course, from the question of immediate or remote advantage to society or its utility, the question arises whether many actions sanctioned to an alarming extent by practice can possibly be harmonized or squared with firmly grounded principles of conduct which are held to be so permanent that they may never be lightly passed over or disregarded. Every angle of these problems which have been mentioned should be freely discussed in competent circles, and then no doubt the various views expressed will greatly contribute to their elucidation and clarification. It is unfortunate that there are apparently more and more individuals whose conduct is no longer guided by conscience which they seem to lack, or by the lofty precepts of their religion which they disregard, or by the sanctity of the laws established by competent public authority. Too many are ready to go as far as the law allows, and, if chances of detection or being brought to trial are not very probable, they do not hesitate to take the risk of going beyond what is allowed by the law. Such conditions are truly deplorable. There is too much fraud, too much deception, too much false representation, as exemplified, for instance, in the adulteration of commercial products, or in the sale of damaged or deterio-

rated goods, or in the practice of fraudulent advertising. 

Advertisers especially should be brought to recognize certain fundamental ethical principles, which no doubt would eventually redound to the advantage of all commercial interests and of industrial establishments, and the general public as well. This cursory glance at present conditions reveals how much remains to be done in the field of ethics and removes any doubt about the prospects or chances for distinction in that branch of philosophy which is concerned with morals, not to mention the opportunity of rendering a valuable service to the public. Such activity should carry with it a personal satisfaction of duty performed, as well as rich rewards of a rather intangible or spiritual nature.

PERHAPS there never was an age more interested in political philosophy and theories of the state than the present. The greatest war in the history of the world has brought about far-reaching and momentous results which we are not able as yet adequately to grasp and interpret. Future generations further removed from the occurrences of today will probably understand them better than we who are so close to the events. Strangely enough almost every political doctrine known in the past is making its appearance in a more or less modified form in our own day. Some of the most colossal experiments in social organization and forms of government have been tried or are being tried before our very eyes. Many utopias have made their appearance in the past, and we have every reason to believe that men will continue to propose plans of ideal government and dream of the perfect state in the future. Thinking men, if not practical statesmen, will ever seek to describe if not establish the best possible government which would introduce another golden age, an age of peace and contentment for all. Pericles unquestionably exerted a tremendous influence on the citizens of ancient Athens because of his golden oratory. His eloquence made it possible to persuade the Athenians to rebuild their city and embellish it with public buildings of surpassing beauty and magnificence. In our day we find those in whose hands rests the destiny of the ship of state taking advantage of the press, the radio, and the cinema, to influence the minds of their subjects. There are those who contend that a democratic form of government is a luxury which some countries may not allow themselves in the present world crisis, owing to their geographical position and exposed boundaries. The world expects more from the thinkers, from those who have attained a certain degree of excellence in the realm of thought. Especially the great universities of the world and the learned acade-

Let the different plans be studied and thoroughly discussed. Let counter proposals be made, let the consciousness of the peoples of the world be aroused, let the nations and their leaders be filled with some hope of achievement and success. All philosophers should volunteer their services and be ready to assist and offer suggestions that might possibly be utilized by the statesmen and the diplomats who are experienced in conducting negotiations and dealing with international problems. 

By bringing to the attention of the people the various plans for improving international relations, the minds of all will gradually be prepared for the new order. One may not stand by idly, and one is not permitted to be a pessimist or fatalist. Some modern statesmen seem to have appeared too soon to be properly appreciated and supported. Of many of the statesmen it may be said that they are also philosophers even though they may not have published their views. For many reasons, statesmen may not deem it advisable to publish their writings during their lifetime. However, the best thought of the philosophers and statesmen combined with the most prudent and judicious actions of the heads of sovereign states can save the human race from impending calamities and dire misfortunes.

CHAPTER FOURTEEN  

REPEATED attempts have been made, particularly in recent times to relate the history of the human race to some definite philosophical principles. The writings of 

Kaiserling, Oswald Spengler, and Arnold Toynbee have aroused a new interest in history and especially in the philosophy of history. Many have become curious about the goal and destiny of the various civilizations succeeding one another. Some have become prophets of evil, thinking they have detected indications of the impending doom and destruction of Western European civilization. At all events, philosophy of history has ever attracted the attention not only of professional philosophers, historians, statesmen, and writers, but of the general public as well. Recent events, particularly the Great War and the profound modifications it produced, have brought many to the acute realization of the threats to culture and civilization, the possibility of retrogression instead of advance in the arts and sciences, and perhaps even the eventual decay and disintegration of the principal European powers and their friends and allies.

Many have asked themselves the question, is life worth living? Others have asked why should a certain people 131

or nation continue to live. Still others ask themselves if civilization is worth striving for and preserving. 

Why should this strange and unique animal, man, who has been given the appellation homo sapiens by the biologists, continue to inhabit this earth? For what purpose do nations, empires, whole races, fight one another for a place in the sun, which it seems they must very soon cede to another?

The longer the human race exists on this planet the oftener will those questions be asked, and, above all, the thinkers, the learned men, the philosophers will be expected to give some answer which may appear more or less probable and convincing. But one may inquire, have not those questions already been answered? In response to that question one must aver that answers have been formulated by thinkers before our day. Of course, many answers are regarded as quite unsatisfactory, while others have been judged more adequate and plausible. 

Thus it appears that those questions will have to be answered again, especially in view of more recent occurrences. The longer the history of mankind, the wiser do men become, since they profit by the experiences of their ancestors. The human race, estimated by some to have existed on this one small planet, among thousands upon thousands of millions of celestial bodies of the universe, about three hundred thousand years, appears exceedingly insignificant from the point of view of astronomy. On the other hand, if we take into consideration the mental superiority of men and the creation of the very sciences which have taught us of the incredible

extent and duration of the universe, then we must admit that human beings loom large in the scale of being and must be given the highest position among all the beings that we can see and observe. Many have wondered whether or not there are intelligent beings similar to man in any other part of the cosmos. Some have written books on the inhabitants of other worlds, even theologians have concerned themselves with the question, but no one has ever proved that they exist. Some astronomers have advanced the hypothesis that the number of stars, like our sun, which are surrounded by planets, is exceedingly limited. The formation of planets they regard as a rare accident in the universe. 

Since life, as we know it, cannot exist on the shining stars, and since the vast majority of these may have no planets, it becomes apparent that beings similar to ourselves would be rare indeed. Consequently, it would seem that the appearance of man is an unusual event in the history of the cosmos. 

Thus it appears that if we wish to learn something of the purpose and destiny of animals endowed with reason we are obliged to confine ourselves to the study of the races and species of men on this earth, for we have no evidence of their existence elsewhere in the cosmos. 

It may seem strange to some that species of men should be mentioned. While anthropologists and zoologists admit but one species of man in existence today, some have supposed that there were other species which have become extinct. When we consider that only this one animal of the innumerable species of animals on earth

has been provided with an intellect, we are obliged to regard man as a sort of enigma, a sole and unique case, which has never been duplicated. From this we gather that individual man, as well as the human race as a whole, has a loftier purpose and a destiny superior to that of the other numerous species of animals. Although biologists think that some of the insects have progressed further in the evolutionary process, and especially that the colonial forms have attained a more perfect and more stable social organization than men, still it must be admitted, that the insects show far less capacity for adjustment to a changing environment than men, incomparably less progress, and no conscious and deliberate deviations from past ways of living. They lack something that even the most primitive men plainly manifest, namely, freedom, or capacity for deliberate choice. Hence some philosophers of history, such as 

Fichte and Hegel, have regarded the achievement of freedom as the destiny of mankind.

If we look at what has happened to so many species of animals on our earth we have reason to assert that the human race, despite all its cultural attainments, is doomed to extinction. Moreover, if we look into the future of our earth we only find that after a time conditions will supervene which will make human life, as we know it, quite impossible. The extinction of the human race is a foregone conclusion. That is not a cheerful outlook, but we may believe that before that the human race will have achieved its purpose and fulfilled its destiny, and hence will not have lived in vain.

Aristotle insisted that God and nature do nothing in vain, hence even from the point of view of reason alone, that is, abstracting from revelation, one may assert that man, the superior being in this visible universe, has a purpose to fulfill. Others are inclined to grant that there may be some purpose allotted to the many successive generations of mankind, but they also think that we need to wait for many more facts, hence that the attempts to discover the purpose of the human race on earth are premature. It may be necessary to wait thousands, perhaps millions, of years before it may be possible reasonably to assign some destiny to the race. Some feel that the purpose or end of humanity is not manifest but occult. Many, however, will not be satisfied with this skeptical attitude, but will seek to satisfy their curiosity and make some probable inferences about the destiny of man.

It is true, indeed, that the various religions have already answered that burning question, and that answer is satisfactory and also consoling to millions of men and women in the world today. This may be said especially of those who occupy lowly stations in life, who toil incessantly without prospects of advancement, and yet stand at their post and faithfully perform the duties expected of them, because they hope for a reward in the life beyond the grave. They do not concern themselves with the speculations of philosophers on the history of mankind. Today, however, a wave of scepticism and unbelief has swept over the world. To many, the promises of religion are but a snare and a delusion, an opiate

for the people making it possible for them to endure with equanimity, if not with indifference, the evils of social inequality and economic servitude. They reject religion and will rely on reason alone, and hence they expect the learned men, whom they regard as most qualified, the philosophers, to interpret human history for them. From this it becomes apparent that philosophers will have to study again the views of thinkers of the past and even compare them to the teaching of the different religions, and to study the history of states, empires and civilizations, in order to answer the question, Is the human race worth preserving?

One may assert without fear of contradiction that the teleological outlook on history is the most striking and distinctive characteristic of the philosophy of history. It is clear that one may study the history of mankind without regarding it as a realization of one grand purpose. However, those who assign some purpose to mankind must also concern themselves with the ways and means of realizing that purpose. The opportunities for fruitful research in this special province of historical theory are very abundant. Mankind's advance on the way toward happiness and the general welfare of all men has largely been due to the state, hence the importance of the organization of the state. Writers on the state and political theory are grouped in two camps; those who place their confidence in despotism and strict control of all individuals, and those who advocate the greatest amount of personal freedom and personal independence. Both sides feel that the adoption of their

point of view would be most conducive to further progress and advancement. It would appear probable that the truth lies between the two extremes. It should be remembered that men differ greatly today as they have also differed in the past. Perhaps the most striking are the cultural differences. Hence it would appear that governments should always be adapted to the degree of enlightenment of their subjects. Some have held that during the supposed period of infancy of the human race, despotisms were far more advantageous than any other form of government, since men were not yet ready for freedom and hence could not profit from liberal institutions. It is true that despotic governments may achieve greater progress and a more rapid development among less enlightened populations; however, they have not always done so. Experience shows that when the cultural level has risen, a greater amount of individual freedom may be conceded, provided it is consistent with the maintenance of law and order; and when a government besides adequately protects its subjects from external threats, a higher degree of development and progress will be the natural result. 

It is generally conceded that the progress of culture and civilization is due to the leadership and example of the comparatively few, whom the many are willing to follow. These few men possess a superior mind, often associated with a powerful will and an unusual steadfastness. Such leaders are always needed. Unfortunately nations sometimes lack leaders and competent guides when they need them most, in the most critical

periods of their history. Since this is true, it is advisable for every nation to select carefully the more gifted individuals and make it possible for them to acquire the knowledge and training necessary to achieve success and attain distinction. There are countries where talented individuals have few opportunities for a specialized education in the proper milieu and the surroundings most favorable to the intensive development of their powers, their special talents, or their genius.

History has recorded many sad and shameful stories of the pathetic struggles of great scientists, artists, writers, musicians, and inventors, who were obliged to live a life of misery and sorrow, because their superior endowments were not recognized, because they were not provided with a comfortable living which would have allowed them to employ their genius more exclusively for the benefit of their fellow men. The wisest governments, whether democratic or monarchical, or even despotic, were those which, above all, were the munificent patrons of the sciences and the arts. By their assistance and protection of creative individuals they have largely contributed to a better and a richer life for all mankind. Fortunately, many private individuals possessing adequate means like the noted Roman patron of letters, Maecenas, have greatly aided the onward march of culture and civilization by the support and encouragement they have given to artists and scientists, to schools, learned bodies and academies. They deserve proper recognition for the prominent rôles they have played in the history of the human race.

However, one must not overlook the incalculable contributions to the advancement of civilization, to the welfare of humanity, made by the patient toiling masses. It is they, above all, who have literally changed the face of the earth. They are capable of truly marvelous achievements under proper leadership. They have cleared the jungles and the forests, they have built the cities and their noble monuments of architecture, they have constructed the immense factories and industrial establishments which dot the landscape, they have made with their hands the railroads and steamships and airplanes. They have labored and bled for culture and civilization, they have raised the standard of living and hence they merit the thanks of mankind. They, with the aid of the astounding modern technological improvements, can make this earth a pleasant place in which to live.

A great deal is known today about the manner in which civilizations arose, whether they be extinct, like the Minoan and Sumeric, or living, like the Western and Islamic. Much has also been learned about the factors which have brought about the decline and extinction of civilizations. Of course, men living today are mostly interested in their own society or civilization. They anxiously inquire if it is also passing away, never to return. Many today are looking for signs and indications of disintegration of the few living civilizations. Others are interested in the place and significance of each particular civilization in world history. As is well known, some thinkers have agreed with Leibnitz that this is the

best possible world, while others contend with Schopenhauer that this is the worst of all possible worlds. Many others, after having carefully examined the arguments of both sides, find that it is neither the best nor the worst, and they hold that man can make it better. 

Some great thinkers like Plato and Augustine have admitted the doctrine of divine providence. Certainly no doctrine is more clearly and explicitly stated in the Gospels. Other philosophers find no evidence for that very consoling doctrine. It should be remarked, however, that the greatest philosophers have taught that there exists the most intelligent being, God, to Whom the ultimate origin of all being is ascribed. Granting that, the question of the ultimate aim and purpose of human history takes on an entirely different aspect. 

One is then obliged to assume that there has been assigned a very definite destiny to the whole of mankind, which may or may not be correctly divined by historians and philosophers.

Many, of course, will evade the consideration of this fascinating problem of the destiny of mankind, and content themselves with an investigation of matters closer to hand, like the growth and decay of civilizations, and the emergence of their distinguishing characteristics. They seek, if possible, to discover, what one might call, the laws of civilizations. One supremely important factor deserves consideration in this place, and that is religion. Certainly religion, according to all unbiased observers, has played a preponderant rôle in the history of the different civilizations. The acknowl-

edgement of the importance of religion raises the question of what attitude the governments of the various political entities should take toward religion. Should it be abolished by substituting some ideology fomented by those temporarily in control of the government? 

Should it be protected as something valuable, or should governments and in general the thinkers of the world, take a neutral or negative attitude toward all religions? 

The future and the very survival of the great human societies or civilizations in the world today may largely depend on the treatment accorded religion. One has very good reason to believe that the neglect of religion, which history has shown to be so precious to mankind, may be one of the most common causes of the death of empires and civilizations. Religion has ever supplied the loftiest and most exalted motives for subordination to law and authority which guarantees the tranquillity and stability necessary for the advance of civilization. 

It is not possible to maintain order by machine guns alone. The very men manning the guns must be inspired by adequate motives to perform their duty and to remain loyal to their country. It is manifestly easier to maintain order, to build and construct a civilization with the assistance of millions of good and honest human beings, who know how to be unselfish and who are animated by sublime motives to sacrifice themselves for their country and for their fellow men. 

Since religion and morality are intimately connected, the weakening of religious beliefs and practices results in the deterioration of morals. It appears that some

civilizations have fallen owing to the collapse of morals and general corruption of the ruling classes. Even powerful governments possessing unlimited means of influencing their subjects cannot remake morals over night. Some rulers, like the Emperor Augustus, tried to revive the ancient virtues of the people by reviving religious beliefs. However, the reform attempted by the Roman Emperor succeeded only to a very limited extent, it could not prevent a gradual decline. Many may be unwittingly sowing the seeds of disaster by neglecting to heed the lessons of history. At any rate the problem of the threatened decline of our Western civilization remains an acute one for all philosophers of history. Philosophers should be urged to make manifest their opinions on this question. Perhaps they will ascertain that one of the most serious threats to the world of today and to the future of mankind is the growth of selfishness, which will have to be curbed and restrained if civilization is to advance. Let the mature thinkers take heed, and the young and budding philosophers examine the important problems of the philosophy of history and make known their results to the waiting world.

CHAPTER FIFTEEN PHILOSOPHICAL SYNTHESIS THE TIME is approaching when a grand synthesis of all knowledge, a complete unification of all the sciences, will have to be seriously attempted. The history of philosophy furnishes many examples of such attempts in the past, and one must admit that they have served their purpose in their day. There were thinkers in the past who probed almost every domain of knowledge. 

Their works were intended to supply a complete Weltanschauung. In the very beginning it should be noted that whatever may be the verdict of history on their efforts in the philosophical order, their thought should always be treated with respect and proper consideration. 

Too many writers in critizing philosphy different from their own, have employed the qualificative, "absurd,” somewhat indiscriminately. Some of these systems were premature, others were too hastily constructed, and some others were not successfully completed. Again, others have started out with some philosophical preconceptions and followed them more or less consistently, but they have neglected too many facts, and hence have departed too far from the reality which they were designed to explain. Some were too one-sided; as an extreme case one may mention D'Holbach's Système de la Nature, which 143

has never won any general recognition. Such a world view as that of Berkeley may be cited as an opposite extreme, since it reduced the cosmos to strictly mental terms; consequently it has justly been called acosmism. 

The unparalleled advance of the natural sciences since the time of Berkeley, one may confidently predict, will make the creation of another such synthesis quite impossible. One may mention in this connection that Fichte's subjective idealism has now become quite obsolete. The appearance of the philosophies of Berkeley and Fichte can be explained by their historical antecedents to a greater extent than some other systems of philosophy. 

Without a John Locke there never would have been a Berkeley, and the same may be asserted of Fichte's relation to Kant. If one examines the genesis of their doctrines, their purpose is readily made manifest. 

The temporary success and prestige of the ambitious constructions of Schelling and Hegel created a certain vogue and accounted for the appearance of many imitators who are now almost forgotten. In Germany itself, a strong reaction set in, as exemplified by Schopenhauer's voluntarism and other teachings, which eventually manifested itself in such materialistic doctrines as those of Feuerbach, Vogt, Büchner, Moleschott and Ernst Haeckel. From the clouds of idealism, German thought tumbled into a veritable morass of materialism. 

It will scarcely be gainsaid that Auguste Comte was the most influential French thinker since Descartes. Comte regarded philosophy as the sum or totality of the sciences in their organic connection, but, since he rejected all

145 metaphysics, his system of positive philosophy was, so to speak, truncated in the very beginning. His law of the three stages of the development of the human mind and his system of the sciences, soon found a host of supporters not only in France but also in England. However, in France itself, the home of positivism, a reaction eventually revealed itself originally under the competent leadership of Maine de Biran. A more sober appraisal of Comte's system brought to light the shallowness of his doctrines, and consequently a powerful spiritualistic movement gradually developed in France which counted among its adherents such eminent thinkers as Lachelier, Fouillée, Emile Boutroux, and Henri Bergson. Besides one may recall here the notable current of thought which Renouvier and his followers call neo-criticism, a movement perhaps insufficiently appreciated beyond the confines of France, which could nowise be identified with positivism.

In England, Herbert Spencer produced his System of Synthetic Philosophy in which the dominant idea uniting the whole is the law of evolution, namely, the passage from a state of indefinite, incoherent homogeneity to a state of definite, coherent heterogeneity. 

He held that philosophy should be constituted by a related body of general propositions held together by some common bond. Unfortunately like Comte, by whom he was influenced to some extent, he deliberately severed from his system some of the most significant parts of philosophy. Thus he unduly and quite arbitrarily limited the field of philosophy through his refusal to

concern himself with the problem of the Absolute and the problem of the ultimate nature of the ego or self, and also by placing in the region of the unknowable such ultimate scientific ideas as time and space, motion, force, and matter. By such a procedure he also doomed his philosophy to inescapable oblivion. One need not hesitate to declare that there is no satisfactory modern system of philosophy, for every one has met with definite objections. It may be said that all the syntheses ever attempted hitherto are defective in one respect or another. Perhaps the most telling blows to which all of them have been exposed were delivered by the special sciences. For the onward march of the natural sciences, due to their discovery of many new facts, rendered many philosophical doctrines quite untenable. 

Hence the necessity of questioning earlier views, and the desirability of sweeping modifications, as well as the need of continual qualifications and restatements of doctrines even widely accepted. Of course, many individual tenets have stood the test of time, and many doctrines must be regarded as valuable acquisitions, some as permanent contributions to mankind's treasure chest of knowledge. Not everything held in the past is to be discarded, but everything must be criticized, tested, and evaluated. Some critics are much inclined to point out and accentuate the differences between the various philosophical systems and consequently they often fail to detect the agreements between them. One may remark here that even what might be called negative results

Among the mediaeval syntheses there is one which stands out prominently, namely, that of Thomas Aquinas. His system manifests greater cohesion and consistency than any other mediaeval attempt at a complete synthesis of knowledge. It is well rounded out and does not present the fragmentary character of other constructions. Aquinas makes a very clear distinction between the domain of truths accessible to unaided reason and that of revelation, or truths which are accepted on the authority of a revealing God. Yet in his writings he constantly intermingles revelation and philosophy. The two domains are not kept distinct and separate as in modern philosophy. Of course, he admitted revelation and regarded it as a most precious addition to that knowledge which man may acquire by his own efforts. Hence his synthesis as a matter of fact includes both philosophy and revelation. The highest authority of the Roman Catholic Church has commanded that the doctrines of St. Thomas be taught in all Catholic schools, thereby very effectively promoting the revival of Scholastic philosophy in recent times. 

Naturally, many outside the Church have been curious to know the teaching of the man who has been singled out in this fashion, and consequently his philosophy, if not his theology, has been examined from almost every possible angle. One may now ask what has been the result. In answer to this question it may be stated that, like all the other systems of philosophy which have made

their appearance in the course of time, it is in some respects inadequate and not entirely satisfactory, hence it may be perfected by some revisions and additions. As might be expected the most defective part of it is that which has to do with the physical cosmos. Philosophy and especially the sciences have made immense progress since the thirteenth century, hence the reason for corrections and modifications. St. Thomas himself did not think that he had definitely solved all philosophical problems, and he himself hoped for future doctrines superior to his own.

It is generally recognized that what is called Neo- Scholasticism today is to a notable extent simply Thomism, or Neo-Thomism, if to that part contributed by St. 

Thomas himself be added the newer elements founded on the data of modern science. Some have unjustly disparaged the philosophy of Aquinas and belittled its value, while some others have unduly extolled all his teachings beyond measure and without distinction. One may remark here in passing that St. Thomas did not seek originality; he sought the truth whether discovered by himself or others, and he selected what he regarded as the best doctrines wherever he could find them, especially in the writings of the ancient Greeks, the Church Fathers, the mediaeval Arabian and Jewish thinkers, but above all in the writings of Aristotle, for which reason he is rightly considered as one of the most celebrated Aristotelians of the Middle Ages. He took every advantage of the contributions of his forerunners and predecessors and availed himself of the aids of his contem-

149 poraries without directly aiming to be different. His example will have to be followed by anyone who would attempt to construct an adequate philosophical system today. To ignore, as Bacon and Descartes tried to do, all the thought of the past is quite unwarranted, and to endeavor to begin construction of an entirely new system of philosophy from the very foundations would be a loss of time. Much of the material has already been assembled and a part of the structure is already in progress. It may be true that at times the results of the elucubrations of thinkers of former ages have been negative, but even these results may be turned to the profit of the philosophers of our century. 

In order to create some vast, unified totality of all knowledge, one must have due regard for both the philosophical and scientific thought of the past and the present. This makes it apparent that no single man, no individual thinker, can accomplish such a task alone and unaided. Only some institute, a group of most competent men possessing the inclination toward such work, could possibly undertake such a gigantic task with any promises of success. It would be necessary, of course, to assure and guarantee such workers a just competence and an adequate reward for their exertions. Such a group working in harmony would admittedly have greater chances of success than even the most gifted individuals working in complete isolation. There has been so much specialization in recent times that some laborers in separate fields have lost sight of the fundamental unity of all knowledge. It appears that the time to co-

ordinate properly the efforts of all specialists and fully utilize the data supplied by all the sciences has finally arrived. The task of the unification of knowledge has been lagging behind the work of fruitful discovery in our day, hence efforts should be made anew to bring forth a constructive synthesis of all knowledge. 

Perhaps the most favorable milieu for such an undertaking would be a large university in an opulent and cultured metropolitan center. A specially endowed institute, for the purpose of synthesizing all knowledge, forming an integral part of the university would offer quite exceptional opportunities to those engaged in that occupation. In such surroundings they would benefit from the formal and informal gatherings of men representing many sciences, who had already attained a certain recognition and distinction in their respective provinces of research, and they would find the added incentive arising from a limited amount of teaching. One thing appears quite clear, namely, that the advance, progress, and especially the correlation and unification of knowledge should no longer be left to chance and haphazard methods. As all departments of human endeavor are properly arranged, organized and coordinated, so the creation of a more perfect philosophical system than those of the past should be submitted to orderly processes, directed by those who are judged most competent and who are animated by the desire to complete the edifice of human knowledge to its very summit and apex.

It would be preferable that an institute of this sort

The cost would be negligible in comparison with the enormous sums spent for other purposes. It probably would thrive best as a private foundation, free from governmental interference and the pressure of any other racial organizations or special groups, and, above all, free from all demands of different social classes and the exactions of professional politicians, and finally entirely divorced from any species of propaganda. Fortunately we still live in a country where free discussion and criticism are possible without arousing undue suspicion. Some may contend that an organization or institute something like that mentioned above would be quite superfluous, since the great universities of the world are engaged in discharging the functions which the institute would be expected to exercise. It might be conceded that they are doing so to a limited extent.

countries where the academic freedom usually conceded to universities is now limited. Even in the highest schools teachers are sometimes driven to distraction. 

Without being too specific one may say that conditions are sometimes brought about which render it quite impossible for teachers to enjoy the peace and tranquillity of mind which are so conducive to creative thinking and productive endeavor. Some would make even the universities similar to factories, industrial concerns or commercial and mercantile establishments. Those who might be willing to assist in the construction of a philosophical synthesis are frequently obliged to perform so many tasks of a routine character that they lack the necessary leisure which would permit them to devote themselves wholeheartedly to such an undertaking. The object of a philosophical institute would be to provide conditions approaching the ideal in order that the ultimate unification of knowledge be successfully accomplished. Those ideal conditions are not to be found in the world today, although in some countries conditions are more favorable than in others. At all events, with or without ideal conditions the work must go forward, despite all interferences and interruptions. The need for a more perfect and complete philosophical synthesis is making itself felt today more than ever before. There will always be some who will not hesitate to offer their services toward the accomplishment of that purpose.

CHAPTER SIXTEEN PHILOSOPHICAL OUTLOOK 

AFTER HAVING passed in review some of the problems of philosophy, which seem to be perennial, and having examined some of the prospects of philosophy, one may inquire whether any notable increase in those interested in philosophy is to be expected. One may ask also whether there is any probability of a new effloresence of philosophy anywhere in the world in the near future. 

These questions can only be answered if there should be any indications or signs of a wider concern or a more solicitous regard for the questions that have tormented the minds of thinkers since the dawn of civilization. 

Moreover, it is necessary to ascertain whether the social, political, and economic conditions are favorable to the growth of scientific knowledge and philosophical speculation. No one will deny that even after the birth of philosophy there were periods during which it seemed to suffer from undernourishment, or malnutrition, when it scarcely grew at all. If one were to compare philosophy to a stream one would be obliged to admit that there were times when the stream dried up almost completely. Perhaps such a period awaits us in the future.

All will acknowledge that in the West there was a very distinct period of relative sterility during which very 153

little philosophy was written, from the decline of Patristic thought to the Carolingian renaissance. Europe certainly owes Charlemagne a debt of gratitude. It is he who made possible the revival of arts and letters on the European continent. Again, after the downfall of 

Scholasticism until the time of Descartes, there was certainly another period of decline and decadence. Thus there have been ups and downs in the history of philosophy. It may be that we are slowly drifting toward another long period of inactivity and comparative infertility. Some are inclined to think that in recent times men are less concerned with speculative philosophy and that they are turning more toward the practical problems of life. The worst threat to the progress of philosophy would be a growing indifference and consequent disregard and neglect of it. Bitter wrangles and disputes are preferable to a total indifference and disintegrating indolence. If men are still willing to argue about philosophical problems, if they are ready to engage in controversies, even though they may be acrimonious, that is a hopeful sign of life. This may not be the healthiest condition, but it is admittedly preferable to death and the stillness of the grave. 

There is no doubt that scepticism has a deadly effect on the growth and development of philosophy. Scepticism is a species of despondency and despair, it is a renunciation of all hope of ever finding the truth or establishing even generally accepted probabilities. It is true, of course, that scepticism provokes opposition. As a striking example of feeble reactions against scepticism

PHILOSOPHICAL OUTLOOK 

155 one may point out the fideism of Jacobi in Germany, the traditionalism of de Bonald and de Lamennais in France, and the common sense philosophy of Reid and his followers in Great Britain. Today no one will hold that these currents of thought were able to cope with the rising tide of scepticism. Even an eclecticism like that of Cicero and Victor Cousin is really a confession of weakness and an admission of futility closely akin to scepticism itself.

Scepticism has made some so apathetic toward philosophy that they regard all time devoted to its study as wasted, since they hold the results of philosophy to be quite insignificant. They feel that the period of youthful enthusiasm and hopefulness of the human race is past; crabbed and complaining old age is creeping up slowly and preparing the way for a long period of senility. If that be the true condition of mankind, it bodes ill for the future of philosophy. Some have become cynical fault finders severely criticising every system of philosophy. However, sometimes their criticism is unjustifiable. It is easier to destroy than to build, easier to ruin a civilization than to construct it, easier to kill a religion than to provide a better substitute, easier to disintegrate the social order than slowly and laboriously throughout centuries to erect a stable society. History shows that the growth of civilizations is to be ascribed, above all, to superior men who are and earnestly desire to be leaders among men. Some of the most noted leaders have been great philosophers and inspiring teachers. 

They have left a permanent impression on the culture of

their period. Unfortunately, there are some leaders who appear in moments of weakness or during periods of transition who can verily be called blind leaders of the blind. They are not eagles, they are rather loathsome birds of carrion croaking over the impending doom of all that is worth saving and striving for. They appear particularly to the young as standard bearers of revolution and reform whereas they are enemies of true reform and the apostles of defeatism. A philosophy which makes young men feel that they have been worsted before they have begun the onward and upward stride toward a better life here on earth not only for themselves, but for the many, for all men, must be regarded as highly undesirable. This attitude does not imply the stifling of all critique. To be sure critics are wanted and needed, their services may be valuable indeed, for they often point to signs of danger and thus avert disasters and catastrophes; but destroyers, those who blight youthful hopes and enthusiams, who dry up the founts of all inspiration, and sacrifice and devotion, who create an emptiness and a void, since they replace nothing, such are not wanted, especially in the seats of learning. 

The heads of colleges and universities and those responsible for the course and direction of higher learning should give heed to the kind of philosophy that is being dispensed in their schools. If, in the interests of the truly precious academic freedom, certain tendencies should be tolerated, there should, however, at the same time be given other courses which could serve as antidotes. At the universities young men and women should

157 be given the opportunity of hearing not only one side, but both sides, in fact every side. The prudent men in every enlightened country are beginning to see the wisdom, if not the necessity of providing well-balanced departments and schools of philosophy. This is particularly significant in view of the fact that some university students profess no religion at all; hence philosophy must serve as a substitute for religion. There is a certain advantage in furnishing young men and women an approved or official philosophy, especially in the beginning of their philosophical career, but opportunities should also be provided for a thorough study of the history of philosophy. Then at least the approved philosophy can serve as a standard, norm, test, or criterion, in the work of evaluating and critically estimating other philosophies and different currents of thought. At any rate, the courses in the history of philosophy are really fundamental today, whether the approved philosophy be Marxian dialectic or Neo-Scholasticism. It should not be surprising that some governments are concerned about the kind of philosophy which is being taught to the young people of their country. A philosophy which is regarded as favorable to the advancement of civilization and the progress of human society, may be presented to students as the specially recommended, the preferred, provided other philosophies are also examined and freely studied. History shows that philosophy develops best in an atmosphere of freedom, in an environment of free discussion and mutual toleration. 

Socrates has ever been regarded as an ideal teacher

of philosophy, leading as he did a life of sacrifice and renunciation and devoted as he was to the service of his native city of Athens. The progress of philosophy will always be due in a large measure to good teachers in the universities, which should bear some resemblance to the 

Academy of Plato and Aristotle's Lyceum. One may safely predict that if philosophy will prosper and progress at all, it will very probably be in the propitious environment and the favorable milieu of a great university. The only better conceivable surroundings in which philosophy might thrive would be in the sacred precincts or enclosure of some philosophical institute similar perhaps to that of the ancient Library of Alexandria in Egypt, which, owing to the munificence of the Ptolemies, offered a haven of refuge and a placid security to scholars from all regions of the civilized world. 

From the foregoing chapters it gradually becomes apparent that despite all the splendid efforts of the thinkers of previous generations, much remains to be accomplished by the thinkers of the present and succeeding centuries. One may inquire as to the likelihood of successful attacks by the philosophers of our day on the numerous problems which are clamoring for attention if not immediate solution. Are we headed toward an era of dwindling efforts and diminishing returns in philosophy? In answer to this one must state that the general unrest and the resulting anxiety prevailing in the world today do not appear to be singularly auspicious, nor specially conducive to a free exchange of views and untrammelled philosophical discussion. The energies of

However, this seems to be but a temporary condition due primarily to the present misunderstandings among peoples and the antagonisms of the world powers. The removal of the causes of friction and the mutual reconciling of their differences may bring about a new order and a better era, an era of a general rapprochement, of mutual cooperation, and cordial relations, which will keep Western civilization intact. 

The world awaits and needs honest and sincere leaders.

The masses will follow and support them in their efforts to lessen poverty and suffering and to do away with injustice not only national, but especially international. 

Above all, let the thinkers place their most precious thoughts at the disposal of the public, in order to make life better and richer for all men of all races. 

Perhaps the United States offers the most favorable conditions in the world today for a healthy growth and development of all thinking, scientific, philosophical, and religious. There is probably as much freedom of thought and action to be found here as anywhere else on the globe. This country has become a haven of refuge for thinkers from other lands. Scientific thought has attained a truly remarkable degree of development in this country. It is no longer necessary for Americans to journey to Europe to find the best science. Europeans are now coming here to familiarize themselves with American scientific achievements. Although 

American philosophy has not advanced as rapidly as 

American science, its future looks bright and promising.

Some Europeans have blandly smiled whenever American philosophy was mentioned, while others have seriously inquired if there exists an American philosophy. 

It does exist, one may reply without any further explanation, and there is reason to expect a more flourishing epoch of growth and expansion of philosophical speculation in the not very distant future. Owing to the flourishing state of our great universities, possessing huge libraries, wonderfully equipped laboratories, and rich museums, and because of unusually auspicious social and economic conditions, one may await a philosophical edifice of noble proportions. America has definitely passed the pioneering stage, it has attained a timely maturity, and it is now rich enough to support many of the luxuries of an advanced civilization, among which many will always include philosophy. The present philosophical outlook allows one to discern the dawn of a new era. 

The increase in the number of periodical publications devoted to the various branches of philosophy and cognate subjects must be regarded as a clear indication, if not a definite proof, of a broadening public concern with things philosophical. These and other favorable omens do not allow one to doubt that the prospects of philosophy in America are singularly promising. The outlook for the rest of the world will probably improve when international relations will have improved and the dominant powers will have adjusted their controversies and settled their disputes. Then will the sacred fires of higher learning burn bright again, then will a phalanx of eminent thinkers add more lustre to the glorious achievements of the past. All this will redound to the advantage of mankind and will help carry on the work of culture and civilization and enlightenment for all races. Then will philosophy have at least partially realized its lofty practical aims and extended its conquests, then will it have made its most precious contribution to human progress and advancement. Then, finally, will it have fulfilled its destiny. Such is not the improbable outlook for philosophy in our modern world.
