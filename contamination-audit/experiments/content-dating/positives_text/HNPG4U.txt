A MODERN LEGEND

ANGUS gazed out of the window upon a drab city street. He was a slender lad with thoughtful grey eyes and sturdy bearing. No bit of sky, no leaf, met his glance, only the inevitable dull stone fronts of houses in rigid monotony stared blankly back at him. None the less, he knew unerringly that spring was on the way.

Even through the bleak stone he could catch the requickened music of the earth. 

Yet there, on the hard asphalt below him, just as ever, people and motor vehicles alike hurried by in an endless mêlée, outvieing one another in dexterity and speed in their ceaseless race to get somewhere....despite all odds.

Angus's face was intent, overshadowed by a seriousness beyond his years. His eyes rested on the scene as upon the page of a book, and it was evident that he wrestled with some problem which weighed heavily upon him. Under the force of such perusal and the intensity of his concentration the confused medley at length -5-

melted away.... One after another, like flowers upon slender stems, stars appeared, twinkling from the darkness of the earth; and hastening among them to delight in their beauty, he came swiftly upon the land of his early childhooda farm house, among the hills, where from infancy he had roamed a wonderland of high rocky pastures and woodlands, with wind and tree for friend.

Once again he ran upon the sunny slopes, revelling in their wonder and expanse, found the secret place among the rocks where the mosses grew tiny goblets for the gods-watched the eye of the great sun wink goodnight as it dipped behind the hills, and was at one with the pulse and breathing life of earth and heaven. 

Each living thing was gifted then with speech and spoke to him. The world was fair and good and echoed far and wide with irresistible rejoicing. Then one day all had changed. Returning home from one of his wanderings, he was told that his father had been killed in an accident. His mother sold the remote farm house and went to the city as a house-keeper, only soon to leave her little son -6-

an orphan in the home of Professor Hazen, a noted scientist. As he had two small children of his own, a boy and a girl, and had developed a peculiar fondness for the lad, Hazen gladly kept Angus as one of the family. 

So here he lived in a great house amid the noise and seething complexity of the city, far from the breath and language of the hills ... 

Wrenched suddenly back from their refreshing air to his troubled confines, he looked about him once more.

The dim, high rooms were lined with books and paintings of noble ancestors. A faint odor of ageing paper pervaded the atmosphere. In the cellar there was an enormous chamber where the children were forbidden to go, except on rare occasions. It was filled with the most extraordinary objects: glittering vials and test tubes, bright metals and colorful, queer-smelling chemicals, ticking instruments with grinning dials, and mechanisms of the most uncouth and intricate contrivance. Here the Professor shut himself away for long hours at a time to work upon his great invention. -7-

Indeed, this was one of the things that troubled Angus.

Soon after his mother's death he had taken on many burdens of the household and responsibilities for the younger children. Hester was like a bright faery, Archie torn by a fiery nature. The Professor had a scintillating mind of great versatility, but strangely enough, despite his unbending dignity and pent energies, he was much like an awkward and bewildered child. 

He could be uncannily brilliant, or shyly and blunderingly sweet-hearted. Yet wherever he went and this was what weighed so heavily upon Angus-there went with him a shadow.

At least the imponderable being most frequently followed the Professor, although Angus had observed him elsewhere about the house, and suspected the laboratory to be his main place of habitation. At times he was hardly to be perceived, at others he took on various shapes, sometimes hideously cruel, sometimes fiery and insatiable, even looming to the enormous dimensions of a greedy devouring monster. But his presence cast an atmosphere of serious dilemma over Angus's young life. Even now the lad

turned his head to see if the apparition were lurking in the dim recesses of the room. But seeing nothing, he gathered himself together and went about his daily tasks.

No one else seemed to be even remotely disturbed by the creature, least of all Hazen himself.

Apparently it was only Angus who was at all aware of his existence-at least for some time. 

But all the more did the strange being's habits and deportment cause him alarm. Among other peculiarities he seemed to have a fondness for Archie and would hover about the child till he had worked him into a terrible temper, or inflamed him for some dare-devil undertaking. He whispered constantly to Hazen that his invention, when achieved, would reveal the secret energy of life and be a blessing to the world; and Hazen, hearing him unawares, believed him, for again and again he harped upon the fact himself with eagerness and delight. 

One night, roused by a strange uneasiness,

Angus found the monster devouring all the heirlooms, pictures and papers in the great library. 

He was crunching them down with gluttonous pleasure and seemed especially to gloat upon the -9-

portraits of Hazen's venerable ancestors. Angus was in consternation and despair. In the morning, however, everything was in its place again, and the ugly wraith, now shrunken to gossamer transparency, slunk about, grinning from dim corners, with evident relish still upon his lips. Sometimes he was absent for days, even weeks, at a time, to Angus's infinite relief, but he always turned up again with an ingratiating smile and obvious delight. 

As Angus grew older, his concern only deepened; but as he had no one with whom he could share his awareness, he was forced to keep his anxiety to himself, until one day in his eighteenth year he chanced to meet with a travelling player, who came through the streets playing his violin. He was an old man, yet his eyes were young and clear. The music that flowed from beneath his bow bore Angus away upon its wings to the far pastures of his childhood, and in his joy he stopped to talk with the strange wanderer. The pregnance of his speech and his earnest smile made a deep impression upon Angus, and realizing that the old man was hungry, he invited him in. -10-

As they sat at the table talking together, while the fiddler gratefully accepted the food, there was a sudden bang of a door in the cellar. 

Angus thought he heard Hazen's steps, but instead the weird monster came skulking in, with an enormous test-tube clutched to his chest, and began sniffing and clawing about among the books. 

Looking up, the old man fastened his keen gaze squarely upon him. "So he keeps you conpany here!" he mused, half to himself, as the creature suddenly vanished among the pages of a ponderous text-book. 

Overcome at finding someone with whom he could talk about the apparition, Angus poured out his heart to the stranger and begged him for counsel. man.

He laid his hand tenderly on the lad's shoulder and, as their eyes met, Angus was filled with a strange assurance and courage. Then, taking up his violin, the fiddler strode out into the street and went on his way, playing. -11-

"Only a chain as strong and gentle as the sunlight".... Angus pondered long over the old man's words.

Soon after this encounter, a fire broke out in the laboratory. Fortunately, it was quickly extinguished, but not before Angus had caught sight of the Shadow-as he had come to call him, for want of a better name-in the midst of the flames, fanning them to greater intensity by his own breath. Most of the fragile instruments were destroyed in the intense heat; the room had to be extensively repaired. Hazen, distraught, insisted that he must have a larger and better-equipped laboratory. After trying vainly to raise some money and to awaken interest for the importance of his undertaking, he finally borrowed enough to re-equip his cellar and went to work again as before, his determination undaunted. Angus's apprehensions were only the graver, however. Who could know what the 

Shadow might perpetrate next? He began to fear for the whole household, and made up his mind to set out in search of the chain of which the old man had spoken. -12-

His eager spirits bore him swiftly along and soon he began ascending a rugged mountain-side. 

As he climbed, he was filled with courage and enthusiasm. The sun shone more brightly and warmly upon him as he mounted, and he felt sure that as he drew ever nearer to its strong, gentle light he must find what he was in search of. The way grew steeper and more rocky, yet he did not slacken his pace in his ardor. An inner fire sped his feet as upon wings and he scaled one crag after another in his eagerness, leaving the valley far below him. After a time, as he hastened on upward, he caught the sound of voices from the air and sunlight, whispering and calling to him in soft, melodious tones, till at length, as he reached the rare atmosphere of the summits, a host of delicate, fiery beings became visible to him. "Where am I to find a chain as strong and gentle as the sunlight?" he besought the airy creatures. "Only follow us upward into the light and we will weave it for you," they sang in resonant choir. "Come follow, follow us!" - As he mounted swiftly in the wake of their shimmering

"But I want to take it back to the earth to bind and tie the dread monster," cried Angus. "Why return? Take hold of its shimmering wonder-and follow us-follow!" they sang. ....

With a pang of mingled anguish and desperate eagerness, Angus reached for the shining chain, caught it in his hand, and immediately felt himself swinging out into space, borne swiftly heavenward with breath-taking lightness. The ascent rocked him in a sweet rapture and all but soothed his will to sleep.

AT T THE HOME of Professor Hazen life con tinued much as usual. Archie had grown to be a tall unpredictable lad with restless eyes; Hester a beautiful maiden. Hazen, more of a recluse than ever, was glued to his laboratory, and the Shadow was a still more constant visitor at the house.

In the fall of the year Angus entered the university. It was his secret hope that here he -15-

might win knowledge of the chain he was in quest of. He undertook his studies with ardor, but zealous as he was he found little to gratify his wish. Overwhelmed by a super-abundance of facts and theories, he still was unable to discover among their ponderous accumulation and intricate detail-which often seemed to him more like sawdust than nourishment-any substance which could give evidence or even intimation of his goal. Doors of seeming proof were constantly closed firmly in his face, leaving him baffled, imprisoned and terribly alone. The wound in his heart remained ever open and would not heal; while the bit of star-dust, which he harbored with vigilant care, shone ever with the same radiance and filled him with courage and renewed longing whenever he looked at it. 

During the course of his studies he made the acquaintance of two young fellows with whom he developed a special friendship. 

The one,

Anthony, had lived in many parts of the world, had a warm heart and a creative, winning nature. 

He was deeply dissatisfied with modern education, and it was his earnest desire to evolve a more creative method of teaching. With him -16-

Angus found many interests in common. Kurt, the other friend, a foreign exchange student, proud and somewhat limited, was more enclosed within himself, but acquisitive in his studies. 

He had a passion for science and soon became an ardent student of Professor Hazen. He was spellbound by Hazen's brilliant scientific mindas were so many of the students and being keenly analytical and methodical, he gradually became one of Hazen's most advanced pupils; while Angus, to his sorrow, was forced to observe how the Shadow took more and more interest in his friend.

Indeed the Shadow was enlarging his circle of activity. Hester, for whom Angus had always had a place of special tenderness in his heart, had promised her hand in marriage to Wilmer, another of Hazen's intimate pupils. A tall handsome fellow, Wilmer was gifted, ambitious and deeply devoted to Hester. The Shadow, however, was strangely preoccupied with this turn of events. Never before had he paid the slightest attention to Hester, but now he began to hover about her, and it seemed to Angus that she looked increasingly pale in consequence. -17-

As their university study drew at length to a close, Kurt made plans to return to his home overseas, and Anthony to go with him. Overcome by a sense of increasing desperation, yet all the more determined to persevere in his quest for the chain of strong and gentle light, Angus suddenly made up his mind to accompany them. 

The university world in which he was immersed yielded him no help. Only by a broader contact with the world and life itself could he hope for any enlightenment. But Hazen was devastated at the idea. He had become dependent upon 

Angus in countless details, for steering Archie's reckless ways and watching over Hester. "I shall be lost without you!" he criedmaking the decision only the more painful for Angus.

Convinced, however, that it held out the only hope of overcoming the shadowy monster who was becoming daily more sinister, Angus tore himself away, and set sail one fine summer morning with his two companions. -18-

So it was that they left one world behind them and were borne upon the bosom of the mighty elements toward the coast of another. 

Even the sky and clouds changed as they approached the new continent, till at length the land lay before them-tawny cliffs and fertile hills, all so strangely new, with a moist veil of wonder thrown over them to enhance their mystery and gentle beauty. When at last Angus set foot upon the soil and moved among the people, towns and hillsides, the experience was for him much like tasting some new wondrous fruit.

The land was rich with nourishment of such enlivening qualities and savors as quickened all his being with new vigor. And though the people were still worn and weary from a cruel war and poverty was rife, yet music sounded from the hillsides where they trooped along, from the taverns and the houses, and beauty decked each tiny tended garden, each olden village and crooked, cobbled street.

Too many were the tales to tell of all they did! They parted company at length, each upon his separate path: Kurt to pursue his career in science; Anthony, his goal of education; and -19-

Angus, as if his thirst could not be quenched, wandered long amid the streams of art, remoulded and requickened by their living waters. 

Here, indeed, were answers to his quest in flowing hieroglyphs of beauty he could not yet decipher, although articulate and fruitful to his heart. It seemed that he could drink them but the deeper through the open wound that would not heal, till, strengthened by the draught to growing confidence, he now bent all his efforts to school himself in the fine knowledge and practice of the painter's art. And so he throve in joyful work for many months. 

Feeling in need of change and rest one day, he had a longing for his former friends, so sought the town in which Kurt lived, in hopes to find him there. It was a quaint old village. 

Hurrying along a winding street, he reached a little house with low, tiled roof, and on knocking there, the door was opened by a maiden, with clear blue eyes and golden hair, who asked him what he would. It was Kurt's sister-he knew this in an instant. Kurt was not at home. But on being offered a hearty welcome to come in -20-

and wait for him a while, he entered the neat cottage. 

Clarinda gave him coffee and small cakes and asked about the land from which he came. She was most eager to know about the life her brother led across the seas, and finally confided that much of what Kurt learned there in his studies caused her deep distress. "Since his return, he speaks of plants and beasts and even humans as though they were but intricate machines driven by explosive force. 

He calls the sun a mighty powerhouse in the heavens, and predicts that one day, by smashing atoms, we shall discover the secret energy by which it runs. But how," she went on earnestly, "is one to capture the secret of the sun by breaking matter into bits, more than one can win the love and secret of a human heart by breaking it, or cutting it apart? Would we not call such methods torture-the handiwork of evil?" "It is, indeed," cried Angus, "for I have seen the monster! I seek a chain as strong and gentle as the sunlight with which to bind him. 

Oh, can you tell me aught of it?" The words -21-

had left his lips ere he could stop them; and yet Clarinda was not startled by them. "I cannot tell you much myself," she said, "but I know one who can: an old man living in the mountains. He is a doctor, and many come to him from every land. 

I'll take you there tomorrow, if you can bide the time."

So while the dawn still greeted the green earth with its choir of returning light, Angus and Clarinda set out upon a mountain path, leaving Kurt and his aged father still asleep in the valley below. A stream sparkled blithely beside them; a joyful tumult from small birdthroats rang above their heads. With flushed cheeks, her hair caught by the breeze, Clarinda's clear eyes sought the summits far ahead, and Angus thought that he had never seen so fair a sight. For many hours they climbed through the bright air, with quiet steady pace, till they reached a village high in a valley. Here they found the old man of whom Clarinda had spoken.

He was sturdy and gracious of build, with deep, brilliant black eyes and arresting but gentle features; and there with him, among others who had journeyed from afar, whom should Angus -22-

find, to his joy and surprise, but Anthony, who bounded to meet them. "When friends meet thus the stars are friendly," cried Anthony in joy. "What is it brings you here?" "The quest for a chain as strong and gentle as the sunlight," said Angus, turning with an inquiring glance to the old man, who focused his noble eyes upon him in quiet scrutiny. "A quest indeed," he made reply. "Sit down -and I will tell you of it. It is an instrument to fetter evil. It never can be used for harm. 

But all its healing powers bind the monster where he can be seen and recognized; for it is in the darkness that he thrives. The chain cannot be found by one alone, but only by the quest of many who join in free resolve for lofty goals. It can be wrought alone by one far mightier than we-the Being of the Sun Himself. 

It can be wielded only by the pure in heart. 

It binds all human wounds and makes them whole. The evil one is fettered in its gentle light.... The earth is ravaged ever deeper. It is in bitter need of those who can be warriors for this high instrument of good. Gird up your -23-

courage and pursue your quest! Life itself will lead you, if you but heed it, and harken ever for the silent bidding of the One who lights and warms it with His Being. The wounds which you will suffer will be your eyes and ears to know Him by."

He rose and approached Angus, who held the bit of star-dust in his hand. "That is the first link in the chain," he murmured. "There will be others in its making. 

The fruits of heaven and earth must first be wrought to balanced beauty by free human deeds before its power can bloom." "Oh let us all unite in quest of it!" Clarinda cried with gentle fervor. "We shall, indeed," their voices echoed. 

And wishing each the other God-speed upon his journey, they set out eagerly into the world. 

HEN ANGUS and Clarinda reached the cottage once more they found Kurt greatly perturbed at the length of their absence. -24-

"Here is a letter for you," he exclaimed. 

Angus tore it open in haste for the writing was Hester's. Since his departure she had married Wilmer, and now had a baby boy. But all was not well. Her father had gone into debt to pursue his invention; Archie, more reckless than ever, had started a business and failed; her own strength was scarcely enough for her child, who was frail, and, were it not asking too much, she besought his return. 

Life itself pointed the way! His decision was clear. The thought of leaving Clarinda, when their task together had only begun, sent a pang through his heart; but he could not swerve and told her when they were alone. "My path, too, is clear," she said simply. "Some months ago I made application to train as a nurse, and today I received my acceptance." 

So summoning their courage, and knowing they journeyed together in their common quest wherever it might lead them, they went on their separate ways; and Angus returned to the home of Professor Hazen. -25-

It was Archie who opened the door as Angus rang once more the old bell on that grey city street. He clapped Angus over the back and hurried him in, in high humor, his eyes brighter than ever, and a smell of drink on his breath.

But before Angus could even look about him to see that all was the same, Hester had carried him off to look at her child, young Christian. 

He lay in his crib, his arms flung wide like a flying cherub, implicit joy in the breathing flush of his sleeping face; while over his head there rose a great blue-winged Angel with a smile of heavenly quiet. New courage leapt in 

Angus's heart at the sight, and when they had both fed their gaze to the full of refreshment, they tiptoed away, exchanging a glance of glad understanding. 

Angus now sought out Professor Hazen. It was in the cellar he would surely find him. He descended the stairs and quietly opened the door. 

Among the maze of instruments at the further end of the room, Hazen sat bent over his papers in deep concentration. But just behind him loomed the Shadow. He was spinning a gigantic net which he constantly threw over Hazen's head -26-

like a veil as it seemed slowly to melt away into his calculations. No sooner, however, was Angus over the threshold than the creature vanished; while Hazen, catching a sound, jumped to his feet and hurried to greet him, a smile of wholehearted welcome and relief on his face. But his features had sharpened, his mouth had tightened and thinned, and his eyes, though keen and exacting, had still that strange light of an eager child not fully awakened, now tinged with bewildered resentment. He related at length his progress, his hopes and his great need of money to further the work; he had bought new machines, but must have a larger equipment and space while Angus listened in silence and pondered. 

Not long after this night of his arrival, a terrible explosion from the cellar rocked the house. Only shortly before, Angus had observed the Shadow descending in that direction. Mercifully, no one was in the laboratory at the time, for the blast ripped out a side of the wall and the floor of the room just above it, where, only a few rods away, young Christian was playing.

The child was picked up and -27-

blown through the doorway down the whole length of the hall by its force; and though by some wonder he had suffered scarcely a scratch, he was altered, and a serious change caused them each day more concern. Like a bursting storm the child would fling himself down, torn by a terrible spasm and frenzy, an agony of terror and rage, as if waging a mortal battle with some inhuman foe, until, exhausted, he became for a long time silent and speechless, listless as a vacant shell. No game, no smile, no word could seem to rouse him, until at last after some days, like slow, bright water welling from a spring, he would return, with a golden smile and such outgoing sunshine as seldom blessed a troubled city home. 

Everyone was gravely concerned by this change in the child. It must be the shock and would shortly pass, they thought; but it did not cease to recur, and indeed grew only more violent and harrowing in its form. 

The laboratory was rebuilt, with still greater debt. Hazen continued his work with growing bitterness, more of a recluse than ever. If he had only had a proper laboratory, the whole -28-

thing would never have happened! He was on the track of amazing results and the path of science must be followed for the good of mankind. His labor and self-privation for his goal were enormous.

Helping the various members of the family with what courage and comfort he was able to give them, Angus took a well paid position in a big factory in order to earn all he could to contribute to the general need. He hoped thus not only to help relieve the family financially, but to learn more of the world of machines and mechanics, to train his mind in a knowledge of their intricate laws, and to experience himself the hardships and sufferings of those who work among them; all of which might in turn throw light upon his quest. Indeed he found here a new world, in stark contrast both to the mountain heights which he had scaled, and to the wonderworld of art, whose beauty he had breathed. It was one which made him bruised and sore. Yet it hardened and strengthened his powers. He grew to understand the misery and numbness of his fellow men ever more profoundly through constant impact with the sharp contours and -29-

edges of the lifeless. Perhaps it was just in this realm of death, and the infinite suffering connected with it, that he could learn more of the chain he was in search of.
