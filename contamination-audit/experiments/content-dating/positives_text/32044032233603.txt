INSTITUTIONAL FARMS AND FARMING I. DEFINITION 

The term "institutional farm" may include several types of farms. It may be a small truck garden adjacent to an institution. It may be some distance away and conducted as a separate entity, or as a branch of the institution.

The farm may be for the purpose of raising feed supplies for the prison system, or the institutional system, or for all institutions operated in whole or in part by tax moneys-state, county, city, municipal school district, etc. 

It may be a land development project undertaken by the state to clear land and put it in such shape that it may be sold for private use. 

It may be a farm to demonstrate certain facts or experiment in certain types of agriculture in cooperation with the State Department of Agri culture or State College of Agriculture. 

All these factors should be considered when a state is about to establish a farm or farm system.

II. REASONS FOR EMPLOYMENT OF INMATES ON FARM WORK 

1. To reduce idleness. Work prevents trouble-a constant help in discipline. 

2. To produce revenue for the state. A farm properly run can be a financial asset to the state. 

3. To afford training for release. 

A. To inculcate work habits. 

B. To create loyalty to organization.

C. To develop skill or semi-skill in some necessary occupation. 

D. To build character. 

4. To provide rapidly for surplus population. 

5. To give the state a demonstration or experimental farm. 

6. To help support the institution.

Bulletins of the United States Department of Agriculture, the State Departments of Agriculture and recent publications by the various State Colleges of Agriculture, or other institutions on agriculture publishing statistics on the most approved agricultural practice, should be consulted in working out the requirements of a farm or farms in any individual state. 3

III. STEPS TO ESTABLISH INSTITUTIONAL 

FARM The following are factors which must be given careful consideration :

1. Location. 

2. Soil possibilities. 

3. Size. 

4. Adequate water supply. 

5. Good facilities for drainage. 

6. Number of men available. 

7. Produce and live stock products required for institutional needs. 

8. The general plan of procedure. 

9. The plotting and surveying of fields. 

10. Location of buildings. 

11. Whether buildings are to be of temporary or of permanent nature. 

When these matters have been settled, the following procedure should be followed: 

12. Erect temporary buildings. 

13. Lay out administrative plan, including forecast of employees necessary for operation. 

14. If land is to be cleared, plot out plan and arrange time schedule of clearing. 

15. Plan crops to be planted and estimate cost of fertilization and seed. 

16. List farm machinery needed at once and as project progresses. 

17. Build barns and such other buildings as required. 

18. Supply sufficient sheds for protection of farm machinery. 

19. Arrange for disposal of crops on time schedule. 

20. Arrange for constant information as to farm produce, milk, etc., used by institutions, in order that the farm may produce supplies of the kind and quantity needed. 

1. Location. 

In determining the general location of a prison, or other institutional farm, the proximity of other institutions must be the first consideration. 

There are certain definite radii to which farm products may be shipped profitably. This distance will be controlled by rail facilities and the character of products to be raised. Milk can be shipped for a comparatively long distance if rail facilities are available to the points to which the milk is to be shipped. If rail facilities are not available directly to a number of institutions, the truck haulage area will be the limiting factor. 

2. Soil Possibilities. 

A careful analysis of the character and quality of the soil on the farm under consideration should be carefully made by the State Department of

Agriculture, or the State Agricultural College. Again the general policy of the state must be determined, as the kind of crops to be grown or produced must have soil which will yield a fair return from labor and seed expended. In some states, it might be desirable to clear land and plant. orchards. The conditions would then be quite different from those required by a state which desires to develop a large herd to supply institutional needs for milk. 

3. Size. 

Based on the above, the kinds of crops and amount of produce which it is desired to raise, and an estimate as to the number of prisoners to be employed, will determine the size of the farm to be established. 

Oftentimes, in a state of large geographical area, it is better to have a series of 1000-acre farms of different types than one large farm. The number of men to be employed is most important, and the kind of crops to be raised, in large part, influences the number of prisoners who can be profitably employed on a farm. 

In general, in a combined truck and dairy farm, one man to three or four acres is the maximum, but in cases of intensive farming, there may be as high as one man to one or two acres. This illustrates the absolute need of determining the kind of farming to be done. 

If there is an extensive land clearing job, a very large number of prisoners can be used to advantage. 

It should also be taken into consideration that with the establishment of a farm, in all probability, there will be a shack or wing for the old and infirm and perhaps for the ambulatory sick. None of these persons should be included in the estimated number of prisoners necessary to work the farm.

Again, the service necessary in the kitchen and in policing the buildings requires a certain number of men which should not be considered in estimating the size of the farm which can be worked by a given number of men. 4. 

Adequate Water Supply.

Tests should be made as to adequacy and quality of water supply. In a farm area city water is seldom available. The farm must depend on its supply for the use of humans, animals, for fire protection and in certain areas for irrigation. A report should be secured at the same time as the report made on soil possibilities. This report should contain the estimate of costs of reaching water, and also an estimate of cost on a continual supply including pumping and storage. 

5. Good Facilities for Drainage. 

An engineering report should be secured as to facilities of drainage of the property under consideration both as to surface and sub-surface drainage. This survey should be a part of a general report on the availability of the property in conformance with its intended use by the state.

6. Number of Men Available. 

It seems to be true that the farther we get away from the metropolitan areas, the larger the percent of individuals in the population who can be trusted on farms with minimum security. A definite check on these men or women pre-supposes a classification system which can, with a considerable degree of accuracy, determine those who are trustworthy for this minimum security. 

7. Crops.

The institutional requirement for food products can be easily determined by keeping a schedule of the amounts of various kinds of products which have been purchased by the institution. It is a simple matter to work out a table covering the food stuffs which are desired, by selecting those products which can be economically raised in the area as the index for planting and for the size of crops to be planted. It may be rather difficult to get the institutions to supply this information at the beginning, but as time goes on, an accurate check will be available for these various farm products. See No. 15, 19, 20—also page 10.) 

8. General Plan of Procedure. 

After the above information is obtained, constituted authority should lay out a plan for the farm's work. This will include the general plan which is to be followed in occupying and developing the farm. 

9. Plot Fields. 

It is most important that a general engineering survey of the farm should be made and the fields staked out and numbered. The larger the operation, the more important is the need for accuracy in this phase. With a given number of acres in a given locality, and with good and sufficient seed an approximate yield of crops may be estimated. Intelligent farm practice may increase them, but a minimum level can be easily determined by the agricultural authorities in the state. The rotating of crops to secure the maximum from the land under cultivation, is most difficult without a plot plan from which to work. 

10.-11. Location of Site of Buildings. 

The next two headings will be taken together. 

It is necessary to have a definite understanding with the authorities ast to whether the farm is to be a permanent project, or a development which the state expects to turn over to the State Agricultural College, or to sell to farmers, or to use for some other state purpose. 

If the location is to be permanent, the site for the initial temporary buildings should be selected near the site of the permanent buildings. General utility, location in proximity to highways (it is preferable to have the residence buildings a considerable distance from the main highway), the location of farm buildings (at some distance from residence buildings),

the height and drainage of the land, and the proximity of water supply, should all be considered. It is difficult to generalize, as the type of building and type of land vary so much in different parts of the country, that no general suggestion can be laid down for a definite location of buildings, other than the above. Buildings should not be erected on the most fertile sections of the farm. Crops should have first consideration. 

12. Erection of Temporary Buildings. 

In taking over any farm site, it is necessary to do a considerable amount of cleaning up, preparation of the ground for buildings, preparation of the ground for crops, and in many cases, a certain amount of land clearing. 

If the operation can be started in the summer time, a rough camp of canvas or wood can be established as the initial stage. 

The policy involved as to the permanency of the farm will determine the kind of buildings to be erected. Buildings for a period of ten years, with necessary kitchen and dining room, can be easily figured under $100 This figure is high, as shown by actual experience. In many localities where there is little cold weather, the buildings can be built for a lesser sum. 

13. Lay Out Administrative Plan. 

On the occupying of temporary camps, it is well for the authorities to lay out the future administrative plan of the institution. 

As an index for the kind of people to employ, it is well to remember that all employees should have had some experience in farming. It is a ridiculous situation where a farm instructor is only a farm instructor and the gang of men working under him must be in charge of a guard. Practical experience has shown that the farmer-guard type of help is better, more interested in his work, and more economical. Almost any farmer can be made into a good guard if given adequate training, etc. 

It is well to start as early as possible with a general administrative plan. It is as easy to get into bad habits on a farm, as in any other place, and the routine and form of organization should be started as early as possible. With the increase in size and permanency, the administrative plan. must become more fixed and the routine more definite. This is true of any large organization and is particularly true in farm procedure. 

14. Land Clearing.

If there is land which must be cleared, of either forest stumpage or stubble, a plan should be carefully worked out to undertake this work. 

The procedure is so slow when all the work of forest clearing is done by hand labor that it will pay an institution to have the aid of modern machinery. It has been reckoned that, without any pay to the prisoner whatsoever, with a cost of maintenance and supervision, land cleared entirely by hand labor in many areas is so expensive that the value to the state, at the end of the clearing, is not commensurate with the expenditure. 

Reckoning the cost of maintenance, guarding, etc., of a prisoner at 75c a

day, the number of men and the time required to clear an average acre of forest land by pick, ax and horse-operated windlass, is greater than the eventual worth of the land. Figures can be supplied as to the time necessary to clear land under various methods. Of course, the types of land to be cleared vary so greatly that a local estimate is much more valuable than a general one. 

15. Planning of Crops.

It is assumed that the authorities will secure the information as to the produce which is needed. The farm in all likelihood will be unable at the beginning to fill these requirements. It is most important that the plan of planting in its initial stages should be laid out with a view of bringing the land, which may require the building up of fertility, to a standard productivity where normal yields can be obtained. 

A careful estimate should be made of the expenses involved for the initial fertilization and seed. Such tables can be made up with the assistance of the State Department of Agriculture, or the State Agricultural College. 

16. Farm Machinery.

Also with the aid of the State Department of Agriculture, or the State Agricultural College, a careful estimate should be made of the farm machinery required at once and that required as the project progresses. 

The Superintendent should make arrangements that all machinery issued in the morning should be checked before quitting time at night. 

Prison farms are noted for the loss of small tools, such as picks, shovels, hoes, etc. This can be overcome by numbering with burn brand and checking up each night.

The care of farm machinery and its shelter in inclement weather is most important, as with proper care, the life of the machinery can be greatly increased. 

17. Barns.

The policy previously determined upon as to the permanency of the occupancy of the area will determine the types of barns which are to be erected. If the site is to be permanent, permanent barns should be erected at once. The buildings, such as dairy barn, maternity barn, creamery, horse barn, blacksmith shop, etc., will have to be determined in each individual case as the requirements of the farm indicate. 

18. Sheds.

It is most important that there be sufficient sheds to protect farm machinery in inclement weather.

If the farm is to do a considerable amount of canning, the canning should be taken up as an industry, and the canning planned for the requirements which are developed.

19. Arrangement for Disposal of Crops. 

The agency in the state which supervises the industries should be charged with the duty of disposing of the crops produced on the farms. 

20. Arrangement for Information. 

A constant check should be made of produce consumed by the institutions of the state, counties, cities and municipalities, in order that the farm may have adequate information as to the kind of crops and the amount to be grown and for further development. (See Par. 19). 

General Administration of Farm System. 

There is considerable advantage in a centralized control of all stateowned institutional farms. It is obvious that the actual set-up of this organization would depend upon the general administrative plan of the state, or the plan which is being developed by the state for the control of institutions and agencies.

For instance, in the event that a number of herds, aggregating several hundred cattle, were scattered about various institutional farms, a centralized control, the setting of standards for milk, the care of cattle, and the elimination of disease, should be handled in a uniform way, in order that the milk product should be uniform in quality, and that quantity should be developed commensurate with local conditions and that the breed of cattle, which is economical, should be selected. This is true also of the control of planting, both in variety and in quantity. 

The central farm section or bureau should be the medium through which all institutions report their crops, the amounts used, the amounts purchased, amounts available, etc. The control of the general farm policy should be in the hands of the central administrative body. The cooperation with local farm agencies, farm societies, etc., should be carried on by the local farms under the supervision of the central bureau. Very frequently the central bureau can secure greater cooperation for an institutional farm than can be secured by the local farm management. The contact with the 

State Department of Agriculture, or State Agricultural College should be made through the farm bureau. It is most important that the general policy be handled through one agency, in order that the most effective work may be done, and in order that the greatest number of facilities may be in the hands of the local farm administration for effective distribution and exchange of products between institutions. 

In the case of a prison farm, there are two functions: 

FIRST: The custody of the prisoner, his maintenance, his housing, and discipline.

SECOND: The conduct of the farm operations. 

Local Farm Administration. 

It is necessary that those conducting an institutional farm with prison labor have a knowledge of custody and discipline, but it is also important

that they have a knowledge of farming. Employees with only the function of guard should be held at the minimum; such employees should really have the function of policemen. The other employees who guard the prisons should also be skilled in some branch of farming, in order that they may carry on the double function of looking after their charges, as well as instructing them.

The allotment of farm labor will depend upon the kind of farm which is to be conducted. In a large institution, it is well to have a head farmer. 

In smaller institutions, the Superintendents should carry this function in addition to his other duties. The work should be carefully laid out in accordance with the general policy of the farm bureau, and the work of the farm should be carried out under a carefully pre-conceived plan. A guard farmer should be in charge of the dairy herd, another should be in charge of field crops, and the third in charge of keeping the farm in order and in good shape, these functions to be in addition to other general duties. 

A farm is a place where every one has to pitch in and work, irrespective of his main duty, but responsibility should be directly placed upon individuals for the various kinds of crops raised or produced. 

Market for Produce. 

The market available for farm products of all kinds should be available in the records of purchase of the several institutions supported in whole or in part by tax moneys. All farming activities of farms raising fruit products should be predicated on the market as demonstrated by the needs of the several institutions under state, county, city, or municipal control. At times there is a surplus crop that must be disposed of. 

Very frequently, by working out a system of cooperation with the institutions, it is possible to substitute certain articles of diet, which have as much nutritive value and are as pleasant, in a menu, as some of the products now being fed. This may be worked out with the development of the system.

Selling of Produce. 

It is well to consider the produce of all institutional farms in arranging for the economic disposal of the produce from any one farm. If there is a Board of Control, with a State Use Division, or a Farm Division, the central bureau should keep a record of and dispose of all farm produce used. A central sales agency, conducted in connection with state use or industrial department, is usually the best method of allocating and selling all crops raised. 

Farm Crops.

General farm crops may be divided into four categories: 

1. Truck crops, including beans, peas, table corn, tomatoes and other crops which are to be shipped directly to the buyer for use at once. 

2. Crops which are possible for storage, like potatoes, turnips, etc. 

3. Crops for fodder for animals. 10

4. In the event that there is a cannery on the farm, special crops will be raised in compliance with the needs and size of the cannery. This is also true of products for drying-fruit, peas, beans, etc. 

Dairy Products. 

Dairy products include milk and its derivatives. If a very large dairy operation is carried on, it might be possible to manufacture butter and cheese. This would depend upon the locality of the farm, size of the herd, and yearly market prices of these commodities. 

Dairy farming differs considerably from other kinds of farming, The greatest care should be taken in the development of the herd; and whereas it will be necessary to purchase the original cows for stocking the farm, it is important that, when a good strain is developed, the herd should be developed, in so far as is possible, from the stock owned by the state. Outside cattle are liable to bring in infectious diseases which contaminate the herd.

It is most important that excellent veterinary service be available and that the greatest precaution be taken to keep the herd free from such contagious diseases as tuberculosis and infectious abortion. 

The raising of young stock brings up an important question. Very frequently the young stock can be cared for after it has passed the nursing age, in an institutional farm specially designated for young stock. Often women's institutions-women's reformatories, etc., can be used to excellent advantage for bringing up this young stock. 

In planning a herd of considerable size at a farm, it is important to have a maternity barn separated from the cow barn. 

The cooperation of the State Department of Health should be secured in making inspection of all dairies and submitting recommendations for the proper care of the herd and operation of the dairy. 

Chickens and Eggs.

The subject of raising chickens and eggs on a quantity scale by a prison or other institution requires considerable additional study. Its success is partly dependent upon the section of the country, and soil and climatic conditions. Some states or sections of states are able to carry on this industry profitably. Assuming that climatic and soil conditions are satisfactory, it is necessary to have the work controlled by one who is thoroughly conversant with chicken and egg raising. This cannot be too strongly emphasized.

The economic side of this business should be carefully studied and a careful analysis made as to the prices paid for chickens and eggs by the various institutions over the calendar year, and if possible, over a period of the previous five years. Based on this analysis, it is possible to estimate whether an institution can as cheaply raise eggs as to buy them from the large commercial producers.

With proper conditions, housing and supervision, and price range, the chicken and egg business can be conducted successfully in many places. 

In order that there may be expert supervision, it will be necessary to 11

have a flock of birds sufficiently large to warrant the employment of a skilled chicken raiser. In certain places in the East the minimum flock has been found to be a thousand birds to carry on a profitable enterprise. It has also been found that by dividing larger flocks into units of a thousand, the spread of disease has been minimized. Scientific care of the birds cannot be overemphasized.

Hogs. 

Hog raising is a profitable enterprise, provided there is sufficient waste material of crops raised on the farm to make up the bulk of the diet. 

Pork is most popular in many institutional menus, and can be raised profitably if sufficient care and intelligence are used. Inoculations to prevent cholera and other diseases are essential. Food waste from nearby institutions can be used to advantage in the raising of hogs. The best protection against disease amongs hogs is plenty of room and rotation of pastures, even on a full ration of garbage. 

Beef Cattle. 

In certain states, where there is sufficient grazing land, and the climate is satisfactory, the state can well undertake the raising of beef cattle for institutional use. This has proved successful in a number of states and should prove profitable if conditions are right. A farm can well afford to raise fodder for milch cattle. As a rule, it cannot afford to raise the major part of the concentrates used. 

Nursery Stock. 

With the increase of the need for decorative shrubbery and trees about public buildings and parks, and with the increase of reforestation, a nursery is an excellent project. This work requires special training on the part of the man in charge and should not be attempted unless such help is available. Orchards.

With the nursery work, the growing of fruits for institutional use and for canning for institutional use, is profitable and an excellent method of employing inmates.

It must be borne in mind that, of course, depending upon the kind of fruit to be raised, a number of years must elapse between the planting of the seedlings and the harvesting of crops. Part of the land can be used for inter-cropping for the first two or three years. 

Advice can be had from the State Department of Agriculture, or the State Agricultural College, as to the fruits which can be grown in a soil of the farm and what kinds of inter-cropping are possible. Canning.

If large amounts of products are to be raised for canning purposes, an adequate cannery with modern machinery should be installed. Such a 12

vast amount of canned goods is used by the institutions that a very large market is available.

Canning is a special industry and it will be necessary to hire an expert canner for the months the cannery is in operation. 

It is frequently possible to purchase in the area in which the farm is located, various kinds of crops which are a drug on the market at excellent prices, which can be canned and disposed of to the institutions. 

Intelligence on the part of the local farm authorities, the controlling farm bureau, or division in close cooperation with the canner, will make possible the utilization of the cannery to the limit. Certain fruits and vegetables may be stored for a later canning or preserving. Jellies, jams and marmalades can be manufactured for use by the various hospitals.

The extent to which canning can be done depends upon the locality, but with advice from the State Department of Agriculture, or State Agricultural College, the timing of the crops with the facilities of the cannery can bring excellent results.

Public Land Improvement. 

Very frequently a farm enterprise can be coupled with the preparation of public land for agriculture. This will depend, of course, on the facilities. of the state concerned. In one instance, the purchase of abandoned farms, which have good soil, may develop for the state a really valuable farm area. 

Again the question of the eventual policy of the farm should be considered. 

If woodland is to be cleared and put into farming, or if abandoned farms are to be restored, it obviously may be either the establishment of a permanent state farm, or the development of farm land for sale to citizens. 

The development of a permanent farm may again be divided into a farm to raise goods which are required for the institutional needs, or one for experimental and demonstration purposes. The combination of the two aims of a permanent farm are not only possible, but highly desirable. The raising of crops in a given area can demonstrate to the farmers the best method of working on their own land. This has a very large indirect value to the farming community. 

It should be the object of the state to protect itself through the salvage of the men who are committed to its custody. If these men can be used to develop the natural resources of the state, two objectives can be accomplished: Wage. 

1. The salvaging of men. 

2. The salvaging of resources to the state. 

It has been found through actual experience that the payment of a wage to prisoners is a tremendous incentive, not only in character building, but also in monetary value in the volume of crops produced. 

The farm has a number of incentives, most of which are negative. 

FIRST: The return to strict custody of a man who does not behave himself or will not work. 13

SECOND: The restoration of privilege. 

THIRD: The forefeiture of good time. 

If a reward of a positive nature, such as the payment of a wage and the increasing of good time, can be offered, a prisoner has an incentive to do well and to accomplish for himself, as well as for the state. 

Development of Winter Work. 

The number of months in which a comparatively small amount of work is done varies with the latitude and area where the farm is located and the kind of land to be developed. As we go further north, the time which active farm activities are conducted occupies a decreasingly shorter period of the year.

At the beginning of a farm enterprise, the clearing, the preparation of land, and the erection of farm buildings occupies practically all the year, but as the farm becomes established, it is most important that winter activities be developed which will keep the prisoners occupied, and, if possible, give some training in work habits and bring some monetary return to the state. This should be kept in mind in the development of the general industrial and farm program of the prison system. 

Throughout this manual, reference has been made to cooperation between the institutional farm system, the State College of Agriculture, the Department of Agriculture, the Department of Health, and the Purchasing Department.

The following is an outline of the manner in which the Department of Institutions and Agencies of New Jersey is working with these other departments.

New Jersey State College of Agriculture. 

Prior to the issuing of proposals for any or all live stock feeds by the State Purchasing Department, the Farm Supervisor of the Department of Institutions and Agencies and a representative of the State Purchasing Department confer with the various heads of the Live Stock Departments and submit all feeds and mixed formulas for their approval. 

When such feeds or mixed rations are delivered to State Institutions, a representative of the State College of Agriculture visits the Institutions, takes an official sample, and tests such sample to determine if it meets with the specification as formerly approved, and reports the results of these tests to the Institutions and to the Purchasing Department. 

Prior to the purchase of mixed fertilizers or fertilizing materials, the State Farm Supervisor, and representative of the Purchasing Department confer with the representatives of the State College of Agriculture, and work out the most approved formulas, and when such fertilizers are delivered to the State Institutions, a representative of this department visits the Institutions, takes official samples, and reports to the Institution and the State Purchasing Department, the result of such tests. 

Supervision of any official or semi-official test work conducted for milk or butter fat records in State Institution herds, supplying the superintendents and members of farm staff at each Institution with all recent 14

issues of circulars or bulletins pertaining to the most advanced agricultural practices as conducted in New Jersey. 

Very close cooperation is maintained at all times between the County Agricultural Agents and live stock and crop specialists, who are frequently requested to call at State Institutions and advise on any live stock or farm crop problems which may confront the Institution. 

New Jersey State Department of Agriculture. 

1 The Bureau of Animal Industry of this Department is in charge of the control of tuberculosis and infectious abortion on all New Jersey State Institution dairy herds, and make the necessary periodical tests on all the herds, to keep the above mentioned diseases under control. 

Also, if there is an appearance of an epidemic of any form of live stock disease at any of the Institutions, this Department is requested to make a survey of the conditions, to determine what the form of disease may be, and the most approved method of control. 

State-owned poultry flocks are also annually inoculated by this Department for the control of fowl pox. 

Prior to the purchase of any poultry breeding stock, day old chicks or hatching eggs, a list of the most outstanding breeders of the various poultry breeds is secured from this Department, and such poultry plants are visited by the Farm Supervisor and a representative of the State Purchasing Department.

The Bureau of Markets of the State Department of Agriculture fur nish to the office of the Department of Institutions and Agencies, daily and weekly price quotations on all farm commodities in season, and such prices are the basis established for crediting all State Institutions with the value of their commodities harvested. 

New Jersey State Department of Health. 

This Department cooperates in making periodic inspections of all State Institution dairies, inspecting the manner in which the utensils are cared for, physical conditions of the milking cows and submitting recommendations for the most efficient and sanitary method of operating the dairy. 

New Jersey State Purchasing Department.

Where possible, and practical to do so, definite specifications are worked out in cooperation with the State Farm Supervisor and other State Agencies for the purchasing of fertilizers, feeds, live stock, seeds and farm. machinery.

When purchasing live stock, the Farm Supervisor and a representative of the Purchasing Department personally inspect all live stock before being delivered to any Institution, subject to the guarantees and specifications surrounding each form of live stock. 

Prior to the purchase of the supply of seed potatoes for State Institutions, the fields under certification by the State College of Agriculture 15

are inspected by the Farm Supervisor and a representative of this Department, and also a bin inspection is made of the most outstanding certified crops before the final order for the supply is placed. 

There is at all times a very close cooperation between this Department and the Farm Supervisor in order that the highest quality materials consistent with price can be secured for the efficient management and operation of State Institution farms. (This section on the cooperation with the other State Departments has been prepared by Mr. W. A. Houston, Farm Supervisor, Department of Institutions and 

Agencies, New Jersey.)
