PREFACE 

The material represented in this book was compiled primarily as an aid to men taking swimming courses at Springfield College. It has been used as an introductory text in swimming theory in the physical department. 

The nature of the material is such that it represents a. survey made of the historical development of swimming, the more modern research on the physics and physiology of swimming in which Springfield has had a leading part, the status of swimming as an item in the physical education curriculum, the psychology of swimming instruction and practical methods of instruction. The point of view is distinctly from that of a teacher of swimming and diving. The material has been arranged to motivate the student of swimming by developing an interest regarding what can be said in answer to the question, "Why Swim?". This is a question which every instructor should be able to answer with authority. If swimming deserves the important place which has been given to it by recent committees after studying the problem carefully, teachers should know why it is ranked at the top of the list of physical educational activities as contributing most toward the all-around physical development of the individual, It is as yet exceedingly hard to make a complete scientific comparison between any two sports. It is believed that the pioneer theses attempting such a comparison, and which led to some of the first objective data regarding the physiological effects of swimming, were completed by Wellington, Pritchard, Schultz and Pritchard in 1914. These theses were developed under the supervision of Dr. J. H. McCurdy. 

In 1926 and 1927 Professor B. B. Affleck and Dr. P. V. Karpovich undertook a series of experiments on the physiological effects of outdoor water activities. Their experiments corroborated some of the earlier studies.

In 1928 the writer began a series of tests on the students at the American Red Cross Institute, Brimfield, Mass. which culminated in a master's thesis presented at the International Y.M.C.A. College, in 1929. 

In completing this thesis the writer enjoyed the cooperation of Dr. P. V. 

Karpovich, in the physiology department. A summary is included of this work in the first section. It is now possible to speak with much greator certainty regarding the rechanics of the rovements in the various strokes. 

On the concluding page of the summary on Fundamentals is listed a bibliography of six spociál rosearches stressing the mechanics and kinesiology of the movements. In spite of the long history of swimming development, there has been practically no scientific investigation of the mechanics of the different techniques. The accuracy of the teaching instructions has been a matter of much mooted discussion. Not all of the problems have been solved but it is certain that the techniques described have a background of special investigation rather than more or less accurate guessing and personal prejudice. It has not been the purpose to outline in detail all or any one of the mechanical studies. They ray be found in the suggested readings if required. Then too, while these details are covered in the more advanced courses of pedagogy and coaching, this text is an introductory one aiming to aquaint the student with a general introduction to the swimming courses rather than a special and detailed analysis of anything. 

The material presented in those pages is considered to be the background of essential information upon which the student may build toward a more complete lmowledge in the special fields of competitive swimming, life saving, recreational stunts and games and pedagogy. 

The material presented in the section on the Evolution of the Strokes is the result of approximately five years of compilation and study

, FUNDAMENTALS OF SWIMMING AND DIVING Fundamentals of Great Importance Regardless of Stroke

The mastery of the fundamentals is essential before uch progress can be expected in swirming, diving or general watermanship. The list of fundamentals to be learned depends upon the aims of the swirring course but it will be agreed that in the majority of the situations the aims are: 

1. To be at Home in the Water Under Any Conditions or In Any Position. 

2. To Dive Sore So as to Be Able to Enter the Water Efficiently. 

3. To Swin the Latest Strokes with Ease and with Speed. 

4. To Know the Life Saving Strokes Which Are More Efficient for 

Towing. 

5. To Know a Repertoir of Entertaining and Enjoyable Stunts for Recreational Use. 

In order to satisfy these aims it is first hecessary to know how to execute some of the important fundamentals regardless of sex, age or stroke to be learned:

First Fundamental-- ADJUSTMENT TO THE WATER 

Adjustment to the water means being at ease in it when in almost any position. It is largely a mental adjustment to be made in overcoming the fears which every beginner has to some extent. 

The proble is partly physical, however, because a person who is unaccusto.cd to cold water finds it disagreeable and rigidity is produced in the muscles making relaxation i possible. Definite drills must be devised to overcome this situation. Fear may be sublimated as indicated under the section on the psychology of teaching beginners. 

Motivation will overcome this problem through creating a desire to play in the water. A physiological warming-up may be produced by means of land drills. Proper regulation of the environmental conditions

Fundamentals of Swimming and Diving--2 

is known to be a great aid.

Second Fundamental--CONTROL OF POSITION IN THE WATER 

Control of position in the water is of the greatest importance and should be taught before a great deal of work is done in teaching any kind of stroke. In fact, it should begin with the first lesson. 

Fear is caused beyond a doubt by the feeling which a beginner has of being uncertain of controlling the body even in shallow water, a fear of being unable to regain the feet if once lost. It is clear that this should be one of the first things taught. It may be accomplished by submerging drills, gliding on the stomach and regaining the feet, gliding on the back and regaining the feet, gliding on the back and rolling over on to the stomach, somersaults in shallow water, shallow end surface dives, etc. It is advisable to teach the hand sculling movements from the first because they are so important in aiding to control the body position. Drills which require hand sculling should constitute an important part of the beginner's instruction. They are relatively difficult to master but results can be attained almost immediately with some of the easier motions, such as, pushing the water away in a series of short pushes with outward rotation of the hands, short breaststroke sculls, figure eight sculls attempting to maintain the vertical position in shallow water by gradually lifting the feet from the botton, sitting on the bottom and sculling to the top using hands alone, sculling head-first on the back, spinning the top by hand sculls, etc. Control in the water is attained mainly by these movements and not by actual swirming. The dangerous position is with the feet straight downward. An outstanding objective should be to teach what to do in this position. The treads with the feet may also be taught, such as, the stair-climb tread, the frog tread, the scissors tread, etc.

Sixth Fundamental--- COORDINATION 

Coordination is one of the most important problems of teaching swiring. Some people coordinate easily and others do not. 

Coordination means accurate intor-timing of the separate movements for best mechanical action and physiological distribution of work among the body parts. Historical Literature. 

The popular literature is useful in a study of swinning because in it are found the complete records which reflect the status of swimming of the day. The records of speed performances themselves reflect progress in efficient locomotion. 

Much of the material contained in the popular literature,. however, must be taken with a grain of salt, so to speak, due to the fact that many false conceptions are found resulting from the attempts of practical teachers and performers to explain the strokes. It is a well known fact that the majority of instructors have had little training in mechanics or in methods of objective observation. A common fault is that of championing some certain style in preference to another on the basis of prejudice or because the winner of the race used that style. The world has always followed the champion. The popular literature is full of works written Knowledge by the champion of the time. Time has seen the greatest majority of these works forgotten. Few champions have the background to explain the mechanical action of the strokes or the physiology of training to which a large degree of any competitive success must be attributed. of both of these factors is relatively meagre even at the present time. 

It is easy to see thet the explanation of this discrepancy is due to the fact that swimming has attracted the attention of relatively few men of scientific ability and then their works have been little understood by the practical teachers. Testing methods to determine stroke efficiency have been practically unknown. In spite of these facts the historical literature reflects a great empirical progress. It is worthy of study by every teacher. 

The records are of particular importance because they wore made with a certain de no of objectivity.

One of the best methods to promote interest in swimming is by means of an individual analysis. 

This may be done by keeping a careful record of achievement of sach individual as judged by a series of objective tests. These tests must be arranged in difficulty so as to meet the conditions in the camp or school. On this basis the final grades should be distributed according to a normal curve of distribution, approximately 15% qualifying with A, 40% qualifying with B, 30% qualifying with C and 15% qualifying with D. This distribution is a little more lenient than a true normal curve of distribution but its psychological effect is good. Few pupils should receive very low marks.

If this is necessary, it is evident that the tests are not well adjusted to the group. While it is an excellent policy to insist upon a high standard, a fair opportunity should be offered to all to achieve a fair ranking after faithfully attending the class periods. An older idea was to insist that certain definite things be done, the standards depending more upon the arbitrary ideas of the instructor than upon any reliable statistical evidence showing the normal performance of the group. 

However, by constant adjustment tests may be fair and still motivate achievement. The tests should include the real essentials and not be centered around irrelevant or obsolete material. Thus, a maximum of 60 points is allowed for passing the entire number of skills. Improvement is taken into consideration by allowing one point per skill or per second improvement in the time trials but not to exceed 30 points. Attitude and teaching aptitude is allowed 10% of the grade. Absolute speed becomes of importance as ability improves. Ten per cent of the mark is allowed on this basis. A series of scales are proveded to obtain this last score.

SUMMARY 

It may be seen that the greatest difficulty among beginners is with the flutter kicking skills, the misses totaling 29-14-11-11-11 in the five tests including this fundamental. In the water slightly more difficulty was encountered when the kicks were executed on the back. It is also indicated that more difficulty is experienced controlling the legs on land than in the water. The explanation of this is pointed out in a study on the "Mechanics and Kinesiology of the Flutter Kick" where in persons with very poor flutter kicks were found to localize the action in the knees rather than control it from the body and hips. Due to the fact that misses were recorded for all who could not control the legs from the body or hips when lying on the stomach, the results parallel these obtained in the other study. It may be pointed out that while the flutter kick is universelly taught from the very earliest stages to beginners, it is e relatively complicated skill requiring considerable practice for mastery. It might be expected that the most difficulty would be encountered in this department with beginners. 

Swimming an elementary overarm crawl stroke on the back was next in difficulty with 17 misses. There are some teachers who have maintained that persons aro naturally adapted to swimming on the back. It does not seem to be thus indicated for college freshmen at Springfield. 

Observations have seemed to confirm the fact that there seems to be a general fear of sinking while on the back and that persons realize that water gotten up the nose while in this position may run up the nose.

VI. SUGGEST IVE MASS DRILLS 

The outstanding principles governing the construction of mass drills should be: 1. 

The drill should permit systematic organization in the pool. 

2. Proper preparation for the water should always be given. The shower, the warm-up land drills and the explanation of the lesson should preceed the actual attempt. 

3. The first drill after entering the water should be fairly vigorous to prevent chilling. Breathing should be taught largely as a part of recovery drills, that is, each vigorous exercise should be followed by an exercise of recovery. The breathing drills fit into the scheme of instruction in their natural physiological order. 

4. The progressive-part method of instruction should be the basis of construction of the drills, that is, definite drill on a fundamental followed by combining that fundamental with some other fundamental, This represents progression from the simple to the complex. Coordination should be taught in simple combinations and finally with all elements merged together into the complete stroke. 5.

Swimmers should always take a cold shower before leaving the tank and dry well. The instructor should not forget to remind all about this.

Teaching Suggestions for Mass Work 

1. The majority of work should be done across the tank to save time and keep a greater number working at once. 2. 3. 

Divide the group into heats by counting off in "Twos" or "Threes". 

Have those not swimming count widths for the ones in the pool or pick out faults. 

4. A demonstration is helpful if the drill is a new one to the group. 

5. Alternate work with rest or a drill of a different type. 6.

Distribute the work so that practically all fundamentals are covered every day together with some coordinated swimming. 

7. Plan your drill by writing it out before hand on a card in pencil. Modify it only to improve the teaching. 8.

Have your assistants assigned to definite areas of the tank to assist in picking out faults and explaining them after each drill to the pupils.

ELEMENTARY SWIMMING AND DIVING POOL WORKOUT--Life Saving Fundamentals Organization 

1. Roll Call-men report numbers as they come into tank-numbers are crossed off of pad by assistant at door-all not crossed are absent. 

2. Line up on opposite side of tank from instructor and start warming sp、 

3. Count off in "twos"-Number ones take partner on left!

Warm-up Land Drills. 

1. Outward arm circles to warm-up and loosen shoulders. 

2. Demonstration of difference between scissors and reversed scissors kicks. Explanation why reversed scissors is preferrable. 

3. Practice-Reversed scissors kick-on back 

4. Demonstrate Cross Chest Carry on Land. 

5. Practice Cross Chest Carry on Land Water Drills 

1. Demonstrate the Life Saving Stroke-with reversed scissors kick and onc arm out. 

2. In heats-practice 4 widths with life saving stroke. 

3. Demonstrate errors of life saving stroke. 

4. Repeat practice in heats. 

5. Demonstrate head carry stroke-on back-hands out of water at hips as if on subject's head-use frog or reversed scissors kick. 

6. Vertical scull in deep water using hands alone. 

7. Demonstrate four methods of treading water (1) Frog (2) Bicycle (3) Single Scissors (4) Double Scissors. 

8. Practice treading water. 

9. Demonstrate surface dive showing use of hands in aiding to get feet out of water above head. (Jackknife and Tuck types.) 

10. Practice surface dive. 

11. Demonstrate floating-explain why it is impossible for most men to float horizontally in fresh water. 

12. Practice vertical floating with the palms of the hands up. 

13. Surface dive and recover 10 lb. weight. 

14. Approach subject from across tank and carry back to other side with cross chest carry. 

15. Change partners. Repeat. 

16. Witness tank lift and artificial respiration.

Note:
