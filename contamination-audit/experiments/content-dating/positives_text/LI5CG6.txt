FOREWORD 

The National Drug Store Survey attempted to examine problems of merchandising affecting the drug trade, particularly the retail field, over a wide range of subjects. This study of the merchandising requirements of the drug-store package falls within the scope of this survey because of the current discussion of repackaging as one of the means of sales promotion. While the report will be of most immediate interest to manufacturers who may wish to undertake package changes, it lays particular emphasis upon the conditions of retail merchandising which affect the display of packaged merchandise. 

In other words, while treating of packaging as a manufacturer's problem, the report insists that the task is not merely that of putting a product in a package but of placing a packaged product favorably on retail shelves among hundreds of competing commodities. 

In preparing this study the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce has had the aid of many professional package designers and of drug manufacturers actually engaged in package changes. 

The report is the joint product of two sections in the Merchandising Research Division and was carried out under the direction of H. C. 

Dunn, chief of that division.

IV

MERCHANDISING REQUIREMENTS OF THE DRUG STORE PACKAGE 

Systematic consideration of packages from a merchandising viewpoint is a relatively new feature of merchandising practice. A few years ago this movement began to crystallize largely through the efforts of several publications devoted to sales and advertising problems. Before that time packages had always received a certain amount of attention, but from the standpoint of production, however, rather than that of merchandising. The package was designed for protection, and merchandising features were given little attention. 

FACTORS IN THE PACKAGE MOVEMENT 

For a considerable time the principal influences contributing to change and continuous development in the package field were three in number; namely, the growing elasticity of machine production, the development of new packaging materials, and the creation of new elements of package design, such as various types of caps and openings. All three of these influences are exerting visible effect to-day. 

There is another side of package requirements which means a much more fundamental change in point of view and which, as noted in the beginning, is of very recent growth. This is the consideration of the package as a positive factor in sales effort. It means viewing packages from the standpoint of consumer psychology rather than from the standpoint of production possibilities. It is this phase of package analysis with which the present report is largely concerned. 

It is this point of view, also, which has predominated in the activities of the past 10 years which make up the so-called package movement.

REASONS FOR PACKAGE CHANGE 

The reasons for package changes are primarily those of package production and merchandising requirements. On the side of package production it has already been pointed out that change may be necessitated by new materials, new elements of design, or by new possibilities in machine production. These technical aspects of the problem have a merchandising bearing also, since a package which is obsolete in these respects is almost certain to be antiquated in appearance and thus to have an unfavorable effect on the consumer. first reason for package change on the merchandising side, therefore, is to keep the package abreast of technical developments, since an up-to-date package will tend to suggest an up-to-date product. 

Package obsolescence is not a matter of a long period of time; newer developments may outmode a package almost overnight. 1

To keep abreast of new developments the progressive manufacturer must be willing to quickly discard the old and adopt the new. Ability and willingness to do this are shown in the instance of a manufacturer of a toilet accessory. This product had been carefully developed and when placed on the market was packed in the most modern package obtainable at that time. Within a few months of the advent of this product technical developments produced a new type of package much more suited to the product than that which. was being used. Without hesitation this manufacturer adopted the new package, although it meant discarding valuable stocks of materials. Advertising and sales-promotional efforts were increased, and the upward trend of the sales curve in the face of declining prices and aggressive competition has given proof of the wisdom of the decision.

NEW MERCHANDISING REQUIREMENTS 

The second reason for change is to put into effect what has been learned concerning merchandising requirements from consumer surveys and the psychological laboratory. An example of the aid which can be obtained from surveys is to be found in the experience of the manufacturer of a product which is widely distributed through the retail drug trade and the toilet-goods sections of department stores. 

The product, a special-purpose cleaner, was originally placed on the market packed in the conventional type of box usually associated with face powders. An inner seal protected the contents, but caused complaint from some customers because the method of removal caused spilling, ragged edges, and other unpleasant associations. 

Too much of the cleaner was often taken from the box for the intended purpose and further waste resulted. Although the product's adequate performance forestalled many consumer complaints, a consumer survey showed the need for another type of container if old customers were to be retained. The solution proved simple; a new box with a sifter top was designed which permitted the consumer to obtain any desired amount of powder without waste. At the same time a smaller package with sifter top and similar in size, shape, and design to a lip stick was added to the line. This smaller size could be carried in the handbag for emergencies. Sales volume on both packages has been highly satisfactory. 

ARRESTING ATTENTION 

A third reason for a package change may be the merchandising value of the change itself as a means of securing attention and merchandising appeal. The policy of the package change for its own sake is apparent mainly in novelty merchandise with a very perishable style element. In a few items-as, for example, playing cards-new package designs appear every year, just as textile houses base their merchandising appeal each year on newer decorative patterns. 

With more staple products it will not be possible to take frequent advantage of this inherent value of change. With such products the necessity for maintaining the identity of the product so that none of the good will accumulated over a period of years may be lost is a primary consideration. Package change in such cases is usually

worked out very carefully so as to retain in the new form a family resemblance to the original product, no matter how radical the change may be. It is possible even in such cases, however, to capitalize the attention value of the change itself. 

METHODS OF ACCOMPLISHING CHANGE 

There seems to be a predominance of opinion toward making the required change at a single step instead of by means of a series of steps, as has frequently been done. In one instance coming under observation the steps in making the change numbered six, and the time period covered was approximately two years. An executive of this company has, however, made a frank statement that should the company be faced with such a task again the change would be made in one step; that the feared loss of good will which might occur through a radical change at once was more a figment of imagination than an actual fact. So long as the final product can be readily identified with the original package, the intermediate steps do not appear to be necessary. If the change is definite and striking enough it can be made the basis of a new and concentrated sales effort. Such a point of view has sometimes been expressed by the advertiser in the form, "We could not improve the product so we improved the package," or, as was done by another manufacturer who feared loss of old customers through a radical change in package design, "This is product in a new and modern dress." One of the great advertising campaigns in recent months has centered around just such a package change. Many other reasons for package changes may be listed, having to do with one or another type of merchandising problem. All such reasons, however, can be logically grouped under the three considerations discussed above-first, to take advantage of technical developments; second, to adjust the package to new knowledge of consumer reactions; third, to capitalize change itself as a merchandising expedient. 

OBJECTIVES OF PACKAGE DESIGN 

The objectives remain the same whether the creation of a new package or the modification of an existing package is being considered. Fundamental objectives are (1) production economy, (2) usability, and (3) merchandising appeal. These qualities may be incorporated in any new package, and should be retained in changing an old one, whatever the consideration compelling the change. 

PRODUCTION ECONOMY 

Production economy arises in the use of appropriate production equipment and involves such questions as volume to be attained before installing special machinery, and standardization of packages in the manufacturer's line to suit this machinery. As far as the design of a single package is concerned, the considerations involved are the selection of materials, size and shape of the package, and character of сар or closure.

Production economy involves the fact that most products are inclosed by more than one package element. In this report the pack-

age element which serves directly to contain the product and to prevent its escape or spoilage will be referred to as the essential container. In the case of a liquid inclosed in a glass bottle wrapped in paper, the bottle is the essential container. Additional package elements will be referred to as protective wrappers. 

CONVENIENCE 

Usability of the package involves much the same package elements as does production economy. It means, however, looking at materials, size and shape of package, and type of closure from the standpoint of the consumer who is to buy and use the product. Paramount considerations are (a) dimensions suitable to the manner of using the product and (b) a closure which will permit convenient use of the product without waste. 

The screw cap on bottles and tubes can be used as an illustration. 

These caps find ready acceptance if they can be quickly detached and are of such a size as not to be easily lost. This is especially true when the commodity is a bathroom product, as small caps and closures are often lost down the drain. Some study might be made of the types of closure which are attached to the container by hooks or other devices, and the styling of these accessories should not be lost in the broader thought of permanency. 

MERCHANDISING APPEAL 

Merchandising appeal may be defined as that aspect of a package which emphasizes the novel features of the package and suggests the essential qualities or values of the product. Merchandising appeal may arise from usability of the package, particularly if it is secured by novel features of design. An example of this is found, to some extent, in the use by one ice-cream distributor of a package which contains, not a pint or a quart, but rather a definite number of servings-2, 4, 6, or 8. The package is particularly suitable for bridge parties; its general design has attracted customers to it with the result that wherever introduced it has resulted in an appreciable increase in sales volume. In addition to being new and different, a package may secure merchandising appeal from attractive appearance. For this reason the merchandiser is greatly concerned with color and decorative pattern.

This report is essentially an attempt to analyze the sources of merchandising appeal, but it is recognized that this appeal can best be obtained after the package has first been soundly developed with regard to production economy and usability. 

SUGGESTED PROCEDURE FOR PACKAGE CHANGE 

Most manufacturers are aware of the inherent advantages of package change and many are beginning to capitalize them. It is not strange, however, in view of the complexities of the subject, that some executives desiring package modernization should be uncertain as to the course to follow. They can not expect the package designer to supply an outline of procedure, since the designer usually is not concerned with the full problem of production and distribution. Separate phases of package design, such as color and dec-

orative pattern, materials, closures, and consumer tests, have been made the subjects of distinct specializations. In beginning to consider package change, the manufacturer faces the problem of orienting himself to the variety of types of packaging counsel which are offered him. A suggested procedure for package change is given here in the hope it may be of some value to the manufacturer in choosing the type of package service or services which his own problem requires.

PROBLEMS INVOLVED 

It may be worth while to picture the state of mind of the manufacturer seeking package modernization. To begin with, he may confront many obstacles within his own organization in attempting package modernization. Secondly, he may be bewildered by the variety of points of view among the specialists to whom he turns for advice and assistance. One expert talks only about materials, another discusses color, and a third emphasizes new technical features of package design intended to facilitate the consumer's use of the product. Even within a single field of package consideration he will find that opinion is by no means unanimous. In color, for example, a field in which artists and scientists have both performed vast labors, he may seek in vain for a definite body of rules and find them at best elusive. Such apparently conflicting advice may seem an uncertain basis for changing a package radically, and a process of gradual change is not a complete satisfactory solution, since it destroys much of the publicity value residing in the fact of change itself.

POINTS IN THE PROGRAM 

This uncertainty may be cleared up to some extent by following a procedure similar to the one outlined below. The central idea in this package-change plan is to start with a clean slate, as if a new product were being introduced for the first time and not merely that modifications were to be made in an existing package. In this way all important considerations can be taken into account, looking toward the production of a package which is as nearly ideal as possible and in which adjustments to existing conditions can be undertaken later. The procedure as outlined has been followed in some of the most successful package-change programs. 

The points in such a program may be listed as follows: 

1. Determine the size and nature of the contents which the package is to contain. 

2. Select the type of essential container. 

3. Select the protective wrappings, if any. 

4. Select the name under which the product is to be merchandised. 

5. Work out the wording and layout of any printed matter which is to appear upon the package. 

6. Design the color scheme and decorative pattern. 

7. Make a careful comparison with existing practices on related commodities. 

8. Test the package for consumer acceptance.

DETERMINATION OF PACKAGE QUANTITIES 

How does the manufacturer determine the quantity of a product which is to be placed in the package? Does he have an accurate 140734°-32- -2

knowledge of consumer needs which enables him to arrive at the right number of ounces directly? Must he start from the price at which the product may be expected to retail and adjust the contents accordingly? Or does he determine the outside dimensions from the standpoint of eye appeal and then give the amount of the product which can conveniently be placed in this package? All three considerations have their place, but interesting conclusions are indicated when their relative importance is weighed in the light of actual practice. 

CONDITIONING FACTORS 

There are instances, of course, where consumer need or consumer convenience is directly instrumental in determining package contents. In the proprietary medicine field it may be assumed that the package is expected to contain at least sufficient for a moderate course of treatment for the ailment for which it is suggested. The size of the container in toilet goods of all kinds is conditioned to some degree by the convenience of the consumer in making use of the product as packaged and in keeping the product in good condition until used up.

With any product which is in the nature of a tool or instrument there is a definite basis for determining size. On a toothbrush, for example, size is determined within fairly narrow limits by the use to which it is put; yet, within these narrow limits there are actually substantial differences in size. There are a few packaged products in which conditions of this type hold to a considerable degree. 

Cakes of toilet soap vary within relatively narrow limits, probably because of the fact that the cake must be kept of convenient size to be held and used in the palm of the hand. In at least one instance the larger size is so designed that it may be divided into two "hand-size " cakes for use as a bath soap, the larger size being intended primarily for use in fine laundering. A number of tooth pastes have contents of 234 ounces. This exact quantity may not arise from any considerations as to how much the average consumer will use during a week or other definite period. The first point to be decided, no doubt, was the size of package which might most conveniently be held in the hand in squeezing a portion of the contents on the toothbrush. Sanitary napkins are uniformly packed 

12 in a box. This number was probably settled upon in the first instance merely because a dozen is an established unit much used in the drug business. Most companies manufacturing safety-razor blades pack them five in a package for at least one size of package sold. It is difficult to establish any necessary connection between this unit of pack and variations in consumer requirements. The problem of individual variation is eliminated as far as possible when a doctor prescribes for a patient. Nevertheless, most prescriptions call for a few standard quantities. Among all liquid prescriptions tabulated in the prescription phase of the drug-store survey, 27.5 per cent called for 4 ounces of the compound prescribed. The next most frequent amounts prescribed were 3 ounces, 2 ounces, and 1 ounce, in the order given. These four quantities together accounted for a little over 61 per cent of all prescriptions and 75 per cent of all liquid prescriptions tabulated from thousands recorded.

PREVALENT UNITS OF SIZE 

Among the 46 groups of drug-store products surveyed in the present study, of 1,000 packages 19 showed a single size of contents clearly predominating. In almost all these cases the prevalent unit was found in the following: 32 ounces, 16 ounces, 8 ounces, 4 ounces, 2 ounces, and 1 ounce. These several measures may refer to either avoirdupois or liquid measures, or a combination of the two. It is apparent that these quantities are all obtained from the pound or the pint, either by doubling or by successive divisions by two. It may be that the preference for these quantities comes merely from the fact that they are fractions of a pound or pint, in which terms it is easy for the average consumer to think. In the several instances of package modernization programs covered in this survey there was little variation in the new packages from the sizes mentioned above.

Some lines within a commodity group are packaged in several sizes, while other lines have only one or two sizes. The object of the following discussion of sizes or list prices is to point out the size and price concentrations. It is, therefore, a matter of small significance that only one size designation or one list price may be shown for a commodity in some cases, even though that commodity is actually packaged with a range of sizes and list prices. 

The commodity groups in which these quantities predominate are spread over a wide field, and it is difficult to see what they have in common. Several of them are products used in the care of the hair. 

Hair pomades tend toward the 4-ounce size; shampoos, hair-color preparations, and hair tonics center on the 8-ounce size, although on hair tonics there is a considerable scatter around this modal point, the range being from 3 to 12 ounces.

Another group of commodities tending toward these dominant sizes consists of antiseptics, disinfectants, and exterminators. Disinfectants center on the 16-ounce size, as do the personal antiseptics. 

Exterminators are predominantly in 16 and 32 ounce sizes. Other groups with a definite modal size are pile remedies, 1 ounce; indigestion remedies, 4 ounces; talcum powders, 4 ounces; sedatives, 8 ounces; tonics, 16 ounces (but with the 8-ounce size apparently just as important); prepared foods, 16 ounces.

It might be safe to infer that since these products show a modal size and this size is, in each case, a convenient fraction of a pound or pint, they are product groups in which size has been found to be of considerable importance to the consumer. It is not always clear why this should be true. A number of them, however, are products which the consumer will use in large quantities, or at least continuously enough to be aware of the problem of keeping the item in stock. 

This principle would probably hold for all the items listed above with the exception of the remedies. 

What may be offered, then, as an explanation for the standard sizes on tonics? The conditions which these remedies are designed to aid are chronic in character, so that the buyer probably does not demand the immediate and positive action which he expects to obtain from headache remedies, pain remedies, or laxatives. Tonics are usually taken to remedy a long-standing condition, and the user judges the value of the remedy in terms of the results obtained by

taking a full bottle. This undoubtedly explains the trend toward the 

16-ounce bottle, it being desired that the tonic may have full opportunity to bring about the effect claimed for it before it is discontinued by the user. A bottle of the remedy is likely to be thought of as a course of treatment by the consumer. It will be noted that there is missing from this list most toilet items except those having to do with the care of the hair and all remedies on which the desired therapeutic effect is of a direct and positive nature. 

It has already been indicated that the scatter of content sizes is considerable, even in groups where there is a definite mode. The prepared-food groups, for instance, have little size concentration except in the 16-ounce or 1-pound package. Also, in the oral antiseptic group the smaller sizes show considerable quarter and half ounce variations around the 3 and 5 ounce sizes, although the 16-ounce container is consistent in most groups. 

The modal point is 8 ounces for more groups than any other size. 

In groups with other modal points the off sizes tend up or down in the direction of 8 ounces. A somewhat different distribution of sizes might have been expected, with a scaling down immediately below the modal point. For example, on packaged grocery products packed in the 8-ounce size, the off sizes are mainly 6 and 7 ounce units. 

In such cases there is an apparent desire to have the package accepted as an 8-ounce size, and they save somewhat on its contents. 

The determination of size of contents in practice usually means the selection of a range of sizes rather than a single size. This is one way of meeting individual variation in consumer requirements. 

On new products it is no doubt frequently necessary to offer several sizes in order that consumers may register their preferences as to the most convenient size. The most frequent selection of a range of sizes is 4, 8, and 16 ounces or 3, 6, and 12 ounces. In both these size ranges, each size is just half of the next larger size. While this ratio is arbitrary, it is doubtful whether the average consumer would be capable of estimating any more accurately varying size requirements. This ratio is successfully used in many fields of packaged commodities, so that the jump from one package to another twice its size seems to be psychologically accepted by the consumer.

Such considerations of size as have been mentioned do not hold for special packages, such as those designed for first-aid kits, travel-. ing bags, or vacation use. The basis of size determination can not be fully settled without considering two related matters, namely, list prices and external measurements of the package. 

PRICE RANGE 

The price line has come to be an important phase of merchandising, particularly in the department store. To an increasing extent in the drug field, also, articles are made to sell at certain anticipated retail prices rather than having the contents determined first and the price set on the basis of contents, but because of frequent and intermittent cut-price sales in the retail drug trade, true price lines have not become established on many items. The list prices printed on packages represent an effort on the part of manufacturers

to establish such price lines, but this device has not sufficed to bring about stable and uniform retail pricing. It is worth while, nevertheless, to examine these list prices as at least indicating what the manufacturer considers a proper price for his product, and one which will be acceptable to the consumer. 

Just as size of contents has been based on convenient fractions of a pound or pint, list prices have ordinarily been based on convenient fractions of the dollar. The most frequent combination, no doubt, for a product made in three sizes is that of 25 cents, 50 cents, and $1. When the range is scaled downward, the usual practice has been to drop the $1 size and add a 10 or 15 cent size. When it is scaled upward it is usual to add a $1.25 or $1.50 size. In one instance coming under observation the 35-cent size was reduced in price to 10 cents, and a new 35-cent size was added, this new size containing approximately three and one-half times the quantity previously given. This particular shift in price and size was made because of the development of new uses for the product; a lower price trebled volume and cut into production costs to such an extent that the rather drastic changes could be profitably made. 

There has also apparently been a definite need for a price line between 50 cents and $1, so the 75-cent price is found quite frequently. A similar gradation between 25 and 50 cents has resulted in a number of articles being manufactured and marketed over a long period at 35 or 40 cents. During the World War list prices became still more varied because of a desire to have the price cover the special tax imposed on pharmaceutical products at that time. 

A number of manufacturers formerly using the 25-cent, 50-cent, and $1 prices changed them to 30 cents, 60 cents, and $1.20. Some of these list prices were changed back after the tax had been repealed, but others remain at the same level to-day. As a result, the list prices now shown no longer reflect the simple price structure which originally prevailed. 

List prices do not usually hold in actual sale on most of the leading drug-store products. An important feature of price cutting which must be considered by the manufacturer in establishing list prices for a product is the fact that the extent of price cutting varies on different price lines. Analysis of special sales made at cut prices in the stores surveyed in St. Louis indicates that the 50-cent price is cut more deeply than the 25-cent, 75-cent, or $1 price. The 19cent price is low for the 25-cent size, while the 50-cent size frequently appears at 29 cents. In other words, the 50-cent price is being cut over 40 per cent, as against almost 25 per cent on the 25-cent size.

This is no doubt due to the fact that the 50-cent size is the most popular one on a great many products. 

It is the prices at which products are actually sold which identify them in the mind of the consumer when these prices have become established at new levels through successive cut-price sales. There is sound reason for a somewhat greater percentage of cut on the 50-cent size than on the 25-cent size, since many of the costs of handling are the same for both sizes. The disproportion is greater, however, than would appear to be justified on the basis of cost considerations alone. 

It is a fact, also, that the percentage on the 75-cent and $1 size is

less than on the 50-cent size, although cost considerations would. permit an even deeper percentage cut on these latter sizes. This analysis of list prices is based only on list prices printed on the packages, prices being shown on about 440 of the 1,000 packages. 

Out of 46 commodity groups studied, there are 15 on which list prices are seldom shown, and consequently no central tendency could be determined. These are after-shaving lotions, baby powders, dental-plate brushes, dusting powder, disinfectants, eye medicines, face creams, face powders, hairbrushes, finger-nail preparations, plasters, shampoos, skin lotions, talcum powders, and sanitary napkins. 

The groups in which there is a very definite modal price are 12 in number. Even on these groups, however, list prices are not uniformly indicated. These groups and the usual list prices shown are candy bars, 5 cents; toilet soaps, 25 cents; headache remedies, 25 cents; laxatives, 25 cents; vermifuges, 35 cents; deodorants, 50 cents; toothbrushes, 50 cents; tooth pastes and powders, 50 cents; antiseptic douche powders, $1; depilatories, $1; prepared foods, $1; and sedatives, $1.50. The scatter of off prices in these groups is, in general, downward-that is to say, below the modal price instead of above it. An instance in which the scatter is upward is the group of deodorants, on which the only off price shown is 60 cents, a divergence which undoubtedly came about through the imposition of a tax in the manner already described. 

Eleven commodity classes out of 46 show a definite tendency toward modal prices, but with two or more modal points instead of a single modal point. These groups and the modes indicated are corn remedies, 15 and 25 cents; cold creams, 25 and 50 cents; hair pomades, 25 and 50 cents; liniments, 30 and 35 cents; shaving preparations, 35 and 50 cents; razor blades, 35 and 50 cents; indigestion remedies, 50 and 60 cents; exterminators, 50 cents and $1.25; diuretics, 60 and 75 cents; hair tonics, 75 cents and $1; rheumatism remedies, $1 and $1.50. The remaining eight commodity groups are ones in which a definite price structure seems to prevail, although there is no definite modal concentration on one or two prices. These groups are personal antiseptics, asthma remedies, cold remedies, cough remedies, hair-color preparations, pain remedies, patent tonics, and pile remedies.

It is difficult to say exactly what these facts should mean, but one obvious interpretation will no doubt occur to the reader. Where there is a single modal price on a commodity group, it may be reasoned that the consumer is sharply conscious of price and that variations from this price are likely to be in a downward direction in an effort to make an economy appeal to the consumer. 

In groups where two or more modal prices appear, it is probably a reflection of the fact that quality or therapeutic effectiveness, rather than price, is the center of consumer attention. The divergence from the basic price structure is therefore in the direction of adding a few items more expensive than the average, which make a claim to more than ordinary effectiveness. To a manufacturer considering the design of a package it probably means that price structure has been somewhat overemphasized in determining package size and that it can be assigned a minor place except on the limited range of commodity groups on which the modal price is sharply defined.

EXTERNAL DIMENSIONS 

The first significant point developed by examination of the external dimensions of packages is that there is a much greater tendency toward uniformity in this respect than in either list prices or size of contents. This uniformity is, no doubt, due to some extent to the desire to have new packages in a commodity field resemble in general appearance packages which have already succeeded in the same field.

Out of the 46 commodity groups studied, definite typical dimensions are found in 32 groups, and two modal points appear in 6 additional groups; even in the 8 remaining groups in which no clear mode appears divergence is not so great as it frequently is on either size of contents or list price. The smallest package to show a modal size is the razor-blade package. All razor-blade packages examined for the purposes of this study were 2 inches long and 1 inch wide. 

Two packages in the group were 1 inch thick and the remaining 18 were about one-fourth inch thick. In describing dimensions for some of the remaining groups of packages, the three dimensions will be shown together as, for example, 2 by 1 by 1. It will be understood that the first number represents height, the second width, and the last depth; 2 by 1 by 0, on the other hand, will mean that the package is cylindrical, 2 inches being the height, and 1 inch the base diameter.

In the first division are found 10 commodity groups. These groups and the dimensions of the modal package in each case are as follows: 

Cold remedies, 2 by 1 by 1; pile remedies, 2 by 2 by 1; laxatives, 3 by 1 by 1; headache remedies, 3 by 1 by 1; finger-nail preparations, 3 by 1 by 1; toilet soaps, 3 by 2 by 1; cold cream, 3 by 2 by 2; hair pomades, 3 by 2 by 2; face creams, 3 by 2 by 2; and antiseptic douche powders, 4 by 2 by 2. Although these dimensions characterize the typical package in each group, there is in most cases a considerable divergence from this mode. The scatter from the mode is largely in the upward direction. On height, for example, each of these 10 groups includes one or more packages 7 inches or more in height. 

The second division comprises 13 commodity groups. The groups in this division and the dimensions of the typical package in each group are as follows: Baby powders, 5 by 2 by 0; dental-plate brushes, 5 by 2 by 1; pain remedies, 5 by 2 by 1; after-shaving lotions, 5 by 3 by 2; tooth preparations, 6 by 2 by 1; rheumatism remedies, 6 by 2 by 1; talcum powder, 6 by 2 by 1; skin lotions, 6 by 2 by 1; shampoos, 6 by 3 by 2; toothbrushes, 7 by 1 by 1; liniments, 7 by 2 by 1; shaving preparations, 7 by 2 by 2; indigestion remedies, 7 by 3 by 2. The scatter around the typical package in this division seems very evenly divided in most of the 13 groups. 

The divergence is not great, however, in either direction, so that the typical dimensions can be given considerable weight in determining the size of a package as far as this division of 13 groups is concerned. 

In the third division of packages there are six groups, and the package width or diameter is usually 3 inches. These groups and the dimensions of the typical package are hair-color preparations, 8 by 3 by 2; personal antiseptics, 8 by 3 by 3; cough remedies, 9 by 

3 by 2; sedatives, 9 by 3 by 2; plasters, 9 by 6 by 0; and patent tonics, 10 by 3 by 2. The scatter is uniformly downward in each of these

groups. In cough remedies, however, there are two distinct types of packages. The dimensions 9 by 3 by 2, which predominate in the group, apply to cough sirups, while the typical dimensions for the cough-drop group are 3 by 2 by 1.

In considering package dimensions for all three divisions it is apparent that the same tendency to avoid extremes, observed with regard to size of contents, is at work. This is shown directly by the fact that scatter is evenly divided in the middle division, upward in small packages, and downward in large packages. It has been suggested that the standardization of dimensions by commodity groups may have resulted from a tendency to follow the leader, and the tendency might be defended on the grounds of the general value of package standardization. It is doubtful, however, whether standardization of this type is of very much value from the retail operating standpoint, since it does not extend beyond the boundaries of a limited commodity group.

There are eight groups of commodities with two modal points, but the variation is not great and exists for the most part in only one dimension. These groups are corn remedies, vermifuges, asthma remedies, face powders, dusting powders, depilatories, prepared foods, and hair tonics. However, in three of these instances one typical package is rectangular and the other is round. On the remaining eight commodity groups-candy groups, deodorants, disinfectants, diuretics, exterminators, eye medicines, hairbrushes, and sanitary napkins-no clear mode could be determined. 

SUGGESTIONS BASED ON FINDINGS 

From the entire analysis of size determination, it is apparent that external dimensions of the package are most significant, list prices of secondary importance, while size of contents has the least weight of all. Some suggestions with regard to the process of size determination may be hazarded on the basis of these data. This does not purport to be an ideal process and yet it is in line with the best existing practice as reflected by packages now in the field. 

Size of package. First, decide what impression or suggestions it is desired to convey to the consumer on the basis of the external dimensions of the package. On some packages it may be desired to convey the thought of generous quantity, while on others it may be expedient to suggest daintiness and high quality in the product by keeping the package small.

Second, consider whether to make the package of the same size as or of a different size from the leader in the field. If the similarity in appearance goes no further than external dimensions of the package, it could scarcely be claimed that there was an effort to counterfeit the package of the leading product, and this is perhaps a legitimate device for suggesting that the product is of a grade similar to the leader. There is the further point that the consumer has registered approval for this size package by buying the leading product. 

Third, if it is desired to diverge from the typical package dimensions, it appears that the divergence can more safely be upward on small packages and downward on large packages. This fact is clearly reflected by the data and probably means that it is expedient to avoid extremes for fear of making the divergence too conspicuous.

Quantity of contents. With the outside dimensions of the package established, it would appear convenient to determine next the quantity which the package is to hold. The first consideration is to avoid the appearance of trying to create a false impression. The essential container should fit rather snugly inside the outer package. An outer package which is obviously oversize is likely to create suspicion. 

Any available evidence as to the size which is most convenient for consumer use should be introduced at this point. Size requirements as regards packages for use in traveling cases and hand bags is an important consideration. In one instance of package modernization a special package to be carried in hand bags was created as an addition to the line. Sales on this special size have been extremely satisfactory. There is a distinct advantage in having a package contain an even number of ounces if such an amount of the product can be made to fit into the package size chosen. This is particularly true of such quantities as 2, 4, 8, or 16 ounces, which are convenient fractions of the pound or pint, to which the consumer is accustomed. 

Price.-List prices seem logically to be the last point for consideration rather than the first, as is usually the case. The package dimensions and the size of contents are intimately related, and together have more weight than list price. List prices should be worked out in terms of what the product is likely to sell for in the majority of instances. A list price which is not too far in excess of this figure will tend to equalize the amount that different distributors make on the product and will thus increase satisfaction in the trade. The usual procedure seems to have been to set a list price high enough to cover the highest likely resale price. Package dimensions or content. sizes indicated by previous consideration can be modified if it is particularly important to meet an established list price. Definite modal prices, however, are found on only a limited number of commodity groups.

PSYCHOLOGICAL PRINCIPLES 

There are some important psychological principles affecting the impression of size which are obtained from looking at an object. 

Two objects of exactly the same cubage will vary greatly in bulk appearance. Height is the dimension which contributes most to an appearance of size, and depth the dimension which contributes least, when the product appears in line on the shelf. Drug-store packages usually follow this principle in the proportions between dimensions. 

Height is, on the average, about three times as great as width, while in the great majority of cases depth is kept down to 1 or 2 inches. 

The conditions of securing bulk appearance will be radically changed if the product is commonly placed on display fixtures in the drug store. The importance of depth is then increased, whatever the method of display. If the package is laid flat on the display table the depth is particularly important, since it is really the height of the package in its new position. There is some evidence pointing to serious consideration being given this point by package authorities, although it must be admitted that up to the present time the vast majority of drug-store packages hold to the older mold. Round packages are somewhat less adaptable to open display, and would tend to stand in the usual position. Therefore, the top surface be- 140734°-32- -3

SELECTION OF THE ESSENTIAL CONTAINER 

Mention has already been made of the two distinct types of package elements. One of these is the rigid air-tight or water-tight container which is really essential to the handling of the product. This package element has been called the essential container. In addition to this container there is frequently an element which appears either for decorative effect, to protect the essential container from soiling, or to supplement the protective features of the essential container. 

This package element may be shown as the protective wrapper. The usual types of essential containers are the bottle, jar, tin can, tin box, metal tube, paper box, paper tube, and paper carton. Practically all the 1,000 products covered by the survey had essential containers of these types, although there were a few unusual elements, such as the wooden box. Developments in the field of package material may provide some new possibilities and may make the selection of these essential containers a less rigid matter.

BASIS OF SELECTION 

The selection of the type of essential container depends, in the first instance, upon the physical nature of the product which the package is to contain. The products covered by the study may be classified from the standpoint of their physical nature in the following groups-liquids, powders, creams, salves, tablets, pills, capsules, cakes, plasters, and miscellaneous. The miscellaneous group of packaged products included such items as toothbrushes, razor blades, and hairbrushes.

Predominance of bottle type.-Most numerous of all among the 

1,000 products surveyed were liquids, 366 of all items being of this type. In this class it is possible to observe the very close connection between the physical character of the product and the type of package used. The most common package type in this group, and hence in the entire 1,000 products, was a glass bottle in a rectangular paper carton, 236 liquid preparations being packaged in this fashion. 

Sixty additional liquid preparations were in bottles without an inclosing package. The third choice for packaging liquid was a bottle in a paper wrapper, and the fourth choice was a tin can with no additional wrapping. Fifty-four liquid products were packaged in various ways, including such forms as a glass bottle wrapped in glassine, cellophane, tissue, or other wrapping.

It may be said that there is a high concentration of package type among liquids, since 296 out of 366 items were found in the first two types, namely, a glass bottle in a paper carton and a glass bottle without other wrapping. Other types of products with high concentration in package types were creams, salves, and plasters, while powders, tablets, and cakes showed a wide variety of possible combinations of materials and types of containers. 

Other types of containers. It has been pointed out that the essential container for most liquids is the glass bottle, although a few items, such as insecticides, appear in tin cans. The essential con-

tainer for salves, creams, and pastes is usually a tin box or can, a jar, or a flexible tube. The tube and jar are of equal importance with the cream and the tin container predominates with the salve, whereas the paste is usually packed in tubes. A few creams are packed in bottles, while the tin box or tin can is used in 30 out of 72 salves. 

The tin can is the leading type of essential container for powders, as a whole, although there is a considerable variety in package type, with the bottle, paper box, and still other types serving as the essential container. The greatest variety in type of essential container is for tablets, pills, and capsules. In this type of product the essential container may be a bottle, paper box, tin box, paper carton, or almost any other type of container. Products such as soaps, which are manufactured in the form of cakes, do not require a rigid protective element deserving to be designated an essential container. Some of these products are presented with no container at all, while others are wrapped in paper, glassine, tissue paper, or other protective wrappers. In 6 out of 55 instances a double container is used, the article being wrapped in wax paper and placed inside a paper carton. One package-modernization program coming under observation continued the use of the waxed paper inner wrapping for the cake product, discontinued the outer wrapping of foil, and adopted a metal box for the outer cover. In this particular instance the product was of such nature that it was used only upon occasion, and the foil wrapper failed to provide the protection needed between periods of use.

LIQUID FORM OF PRODUCT PREDOMINANT 

Liquids predominate in 19 out of 46 groups. In all these groups the character of the essential_container is rigidly determined by the character of the contents. In 19 of the groups the essential container is a glass bottle, as follows: Vermifuges, hair tonics, cold remedies, finger-nail preparations, pain remedies, sedatives, aftershaving lotions, rheumatism remedies, skin lotions, shampoos, liniments, hair-color preparations, personal antiseptics, cough remedies, laxatives, tonics, diuretics, disinfectants, and deodorants. In pain and cold remedies a number of products are also in the form of salves and are packed in a tin box or flexible tube, and some cough remedies and diuretics are packed in tablet form. Some liquid disinfectants are packed in tin cans, and the tin can is the typical container for exterminators. Exterminators in powder form are packaged in paper cans or paper boxes. 

GLASS AS A CONTAINER MATERIAL 

The basic reason for packing liquids in glass is that liquids are frequently chemically active, and glass is the most inert of the common container materials. It should be borne in mind, however, that two types of glass containers are used for the preservation of pharmaceuticals. One is the type made from mixtures of silicates and subject to decomposition, while the other is of more durable materials and able to withstand the disintegrating properties of the contents. Glass differs from certain metals in that the decomposition of silica glass may in some cases destroy the therapeutic value

of the preparation, although the products are not toxic. Serums and antitoxins are products requiring special containers in order to preserve their effective value.

Exterminators and the types of disinfectants packed in tin cans are not intended for direct human use and the hazard of chemical reaction with the container is therefore not so important. However, some prepared foods in liquid form are packed in tin cans. The average consumer will accept glass as a safe container-and, in fact, tends to demand it-without stopping to inquire whether the given product is chemically active or not. Transparent glass also enables the user to see the liquid and decide for himself whether it is wellmixed before taking or whether deterioration has set in. Also, it continually discloses the quantity remaining in the bottle.

Another reason for the extensive use of glass as a container is its molding adaptability. This fact tends to lessen the restrictions surrounding the design, and much can be accomplished toward this end by molding the essential container along those lines which will add distinction to the product. 

PRODUCTS IN POWDER FORM 

The next largest division is a set of commodity groups in which the usual form of the product is powder. These nine groups are face powder, dusting powder, baby powder, talcum powder, antiseptic douche powder, prepared foods, asthma remedies, headache remedies, and indigestion remedies.

Types of container. The usual container is the tin can, which is somewhat cheaper than glass, and can be made sufficiently airtight to preserve a powder without deterioration. Most products are comparatively inert in powdered form and will not react to the metal container. Exceptional package forms for powdered products are the paper box for face powder, paper can or carton fo· antiseptic douche powders, the paper envelope for headache_remedies, and the glass bottle for indigestion remedies. Headache remedies are also presented as tablets or liquids in glass bottles, and indigestion remedies as liquids in glass bottles. 

Factors determining packaging for powders.-An important determining factor in packaging powders is the manner of use. Those which are to be sifted are put into tin cans because the tin can can be fitted with a sifter top and closure more economically than any other type of container. Furthermore, loose powders do not adhere to metal surfaces as they do to glass.

Special elements of design deserving consideration may make for ease in pouring, counting out, preventing spilling, or adapting the package to the place where it is likely to be found in the medicine chest, on the dressing table, or in the bathroom. Also, there is no doubt a special consumer appeal in increasing the usability of the package. This may be done by making the container itself permanently usable, either for refilling with the same product or for containing entirely different products after it has been emptied. 

THE CREAM TYPE OF PRODUCT 

Five important commodity groups are prepared mainly in the form of creams. These groups are cold creams, face creams, shaving

preparations, dentifrices, and depilatories. This is the field in which the flexible tube and the glass jar have been struggling for dominance, or at least where such a struggle has been recently dramatized by national advertising. Actually, however, there is a very definite determining factor in choosing a tube or jar for creams. Face creams and cold creams are packaged in wide-mouthed jars, thus permitting the insertion of the tips of the fingers, which seems to be the most convenient means of application. In this connection it might be well to call attention to the fact that in some instances cold creams, especially those made for theatrical use, are packed in tin boxes; also, these same creams are available for general use packed in the usual type of wide-mouthed jar. Some lines of cold creams and face creams are packed in flexible tubes. Shaving creams and dental creams are typically presented in tubes, since this offers the most convenient means of transferring the product to a brush. The exception group is the depilatory creams, in which there has recently been a decided swing from the jar to the tube, although the usual method of application is with the fingers. The jar is still used to a considerable extent, however, and is the predominant type of container for depilatories in powder form. Both powders and creams make some use of the small wooden paddle for application.

MISCELLANEOUS GROUPS 

The three product types-liquids, powders, and creams-account for the greater number of items in 33 commodity groups. The remaining 13 groups are scattered through several physical types of products. Five groups which might be labeled as instruments or appliances are usually packed in paper cartons. These five groups are toothbrushes, dental-plate brushes, hairbrushes, razor blades, and sanitary napkins. Three groups, namely, pile remedies, hair pomades, and eye medicines, which come in the form of salves or pastes, are usually packed in tin cans or in tin boxes. 

It has already been pointed out that only on liquids, powders, and creams is there any rigid determination of the character of the container from the physical nature of the product. The remaining physical types are free from spoiling, deterioration from exposure to the air, or chemical reaction with the usual type of containers. In these groups, therefore, a broader range is permitted to creative ingenuity in exploiting the possibilities of production economy or merchandising appeal.

SUMMARY OF DATA 

Selection of container.-The determining factors which establish the basis for selecting an essential container may be summarized as follows and assigned an importance in line with their rank in this list: (a) Physical nature of the product; (b) method of application; and (c) production economy. The selection is rigidly limited on the more important physical types of commodities, liquids usually requiring the glass bottle, powders a tin can, and creams either a jar or tube. The type of mouth or closure is determined by the methods of application, and ranges through corks, screw tops, sifter tops, and, more recently, various novel mechanical contrivances. It is possible, though, to obtain less rigidity in the selection of the essen-

tial container in some instances by a change in the nature of the product. In at least one instance coming under observation a liquid product was changed to a cream with satisfactory results both as to sales and effectiveness in the purpose for which it had been developed. 

Packaging materials. The materials used in packaging are, on the whole, well established. From the merchandising standpoint, however, there is some reason to expect changes in this field, although most of the new materials proposed are more adapted to protective wrappers than to essential containers. The use of colored or opaque glass is likely to show some increase, for reasons which will be discussed in a later section (see p. 20). 

SELECTION OF PROTECTIVE WRAPPERS 

Protective wrappers are quite as rigidly determined as essential containers. A protective wrapper may actually supplement the protective value of the essential container or it may be added as a base for the required printed matter and for the application of color and decorative pattern. However, the determining factor is the character of the essential container. 

The two dominant situations with regard to protective wrappers is to have no wrapper at all, as is typically true in 15 commodity groups, or to make use of the paper carton, as in 24 commodity groups. On the seven remaining groups the typical protective wrappers are paper, waxed paper, tissue paper, glassine, transparent cellulose products, and some new types of materials in the field of silver or corrugated paper. Some types of protective wrappers are inside the essential container.

THE PAPER CARTON 

To what extent does the manufacturer who is considering a new package have an open choice concerning the use of a protective wrapper? Certain essential containers, notably the tin can, are usually presented with no protective wrapper. Other essential containers, notably the glass bottle, the glass jar, and the flexible tube, are generally presented in paper cartons. The answer to the question concerning the future use of the paper carton probably lies in the points of contrast between the tin can and the other types of essential containers mentioned. The principal points of contrast are as follows: (1) The tin can presents a flat surface which takes printing and color effectively and economically, (2) the tin can is not so fragile as other types, and (3) the tin can is opaque. 

The paper carton, then, is intended to add to the bottle, tube, or jar one or more of the essential quantities of protection, flat printing surfaces, or opacity.

Utility and protection. Which of these qualities is of greater importance? The paper carton is undoubtedly a protection against breakage in certain types of glass containers. When handled by the retailer or the customer the bottle which is most likely to slip from the hand is the large or medium size bottle with a smooth surface. 

This, however, is precisely the type of bottle which it is most difficult to pack securely in a paper carton, and many instances are reported

of such bottles slipping through the bottom of a carton which was not of sufficiently rigid construction. Security in handling could undoubtedly be more readily secured by designing the bottle with a square or triangular cross-section or with paneled or corrugated sides. The usual type of glass jar is not subject to breakage to any great degree, both because of the thickness of the jar and because of the size, which allows it to be readily held in the hand. 

There seems to be a better case, however, for the protective feature of the paper carton in the case of the flexible tube. The tubes are subject to denting, bending, or cracking, and might soon be rendered unfit for sale without the protection which the paper carton offers. 

The flexible tube, however, is the only type in which the need for protection seems to be sufficient to make the retention of the paper carton likely on this ground alone. This would be the only possible basis for retaining the carton in the case of the tube, since in its other characteristics it is similar to the tin can, being opaque and taking color and printing very readily.

A related problem is the protection of the package in shipping. 

The paper carton undoubtedly facilitates packing to some degree and offers some protection to the glass articles during the process of shipping. Many glass bottles and jars, however, are shipped about the country without great loss, lacking such a covering. The use of corrugated paper and related protective elements in the shipping case seems to have gone a long way toward a solution of the shipping problem. It seems likely that for most glass containers the paper carton is merely an alternative form of protection as far as transportation is concerned.

Decorative effect. If these protective elements are held nonessential and decorative effect and merchandising appeal alone are to be considered, the manufacturer is free to choose between the possibility of using no protective wrapping at all or selecting from a wide range of materials. For example, if the product is a liquid of attractive color packaged in glass, there may be a strong point in presenting the product in its essential container without any additional covering. There comes to mind in this connection the experience of the manufacturer of a long-established toilet preparation which had been packed in a bottle and then inclosed in a cardboard carton. The product had always been an excellent seller but had never obtained much display value because, primarily, of the rather old-fashioned type of carton which had been in use for many years. In undertaking a package-modernization program one of the first moves made was to discard entirely the use of the protective carton and to depend upon the attractiveness of the newly designed bottle and label to secure for the product the displays it rightfully deserved. The wisdom of this move was apparent; displays were easily obtained and the sales curve showed a decided upward trend. 

However, if the appearance of the product is not an appealing feature and it is desired to use an emblem or a picture designed to indicate its value or authenticity, wrapping, such as a paper carton, may be required to carry out this effect. 

Printing surfaces. The printing surfaces of the paper carton would appear to be a much more important advantage. The paper carton is, in a sense, an individual billboard going with each pack-

age, on which the manufacturer inscribes his message to the consumer in the form of either statement or suggestion. Not only do the surfaces of the paper carton readily take color and printing, but they are usually flat and hence are good reading surfaces. The importance of this consideration depends to some degree on the significance of the message which the manufacturer may have to present. He will almost uniformly wish to present at least the name of the product in as large and legible letters as the package will permit. This consideration is undoubtedly served more directly by the paper carton than by any other type of protective wrapper. 

Opacity. The quality of opacity which the paper carton contributes to the transparent glass container has been in the forefront of discussion for some time. It has been held by some observers that this feature is injurious to the sale of commodities. These arguments have been based on the well-recognized value of visibility, which has been advancing the cause of open display and otherwise influencing the course of merchandising. There are instances of manufacturers of drug-store products who have discontinued the use of the paper carton for this reason, and with results which they report as being highly satisfactory, especially from the standpoint of securing better displays on the shelves and tables of retailers. It would appear, however, that for visibility of the product itself to have a favorable influence, the product must be pleasant in appearance or suggest pleasant sensations in use. There are a great many drug products for which these considerations do not hold. Only a limited number are pleasing in color, and a still more limited number pleasing in taste or in sensation if applied externally. It seems to be a strong possibility that on such commodities concealment rather than visibility is the proper basis of appeal, and that the surfaces of the package might be used to suggest pleasant associations connected with the anticipated results of the remedy rather than its application. 

As to whether the usual style of decorative pattern actually gives this suggestion is another matter, which is to be discussed in another section of this report (see p. 30).

There has been some tendency to drop the paper carton as a protective wrapper in some product groups, particularly in the toiletgoods field. In such items as perfumes and lotions the color of the product is usually attractive and the application is for the most part pleasant. Some groups of proprietaries, however, although having an attractive color and pleasant effect, prefer remaining in the field of proprietaries in the mind of the consumer. The paper carton seems to provide this result. Oral antiseptics are outstanding in this instance, although for special merchandising considerations one manufacturer has provided a special fillable bottle for use in the home. The special bottle is sold empty and is designed to fit into the general ensemble of toilet necessities found in the home.

Some proprietaries, however, are discarding the heavy carton and replacing it with a waxed paper wrapping. This wrapping gives a sense of visibility, for while it reveals the name or other identifying mark, the contents of the bottle are not clearly seen. This combination of opacity and semivisibility might be said to be the

middle ground between the two extremes of clear glass without a protective wrapping and the carton-inclosed product.

Before much increase in the direction of visibility can be expected, there will have to be some improvement in the appearance of many products. Some progress has already been made in this direction in some commodity groups. Another possibility would appear to be the introduction of opaque or colored glass, particularly with flat sides. Such packages could be made to present a very attractive appearance and could be used on any stable product which tended to retain its essential ingredients in suspension. On the whole, the paper carton seems likely to retain a hold on approximately the same field in which it is now used as a protective wrapper. 

This is particularly true of use with the flexible tube. The opaque glass jar is the type which can most readily drop the paper carton. 

A number of leading manufacturers whose products are packed in the opaque glass jars have already dropped the paper carton and, so far as can be determined, with satisfactory results, especially in the matter of availability of display values. Liquids in bottles will not ordinarily dispense with the paper carton unless the liquid is of the nature of a toilet accessory, or unless color is introduced in the product, or color or opacity into the glass container itself. 

THE FLEXIBLE WRAPPER 

Many of the flexible wrappers prove an unnecessary expense to the manufacturer because they do not stand the usual wear and tear from packing and stocking. They tear easily and often have to be removed from the package before it can be placed on the shelf or display table. The customer may, in time, be educated to demand this type of protective wrapping when it serves a real purpose, such as making the package air-tight or waterproof, but it should be borne in mind that appearance is a dominant factor in gaining customer acceptance. The fact that many of these wrappers do not subject themselves to price-tag adherence may also become a subject for further research.

Since the opaque and transparent flexible wrappers are similar in function, the decision between them will rest on the same considerations of visibility versus opaque printing surfaces which have already been fully discussed. The important point to recognize is that the question between the opaque and the transparent flexible wrapper is a minor issue which is not presented for solution until the problem of visibility versus opacity has been decided.

SUMMARY OF DATA 

In summarizing the data concerning protective wrappers and essential containers, it seems probable that the leading combination of package elements likely to continue for some time will be the glass bottle with the paper carton. In the present study this combination was found to occur in 236 of the 1,000 packages studied. 

Visibility will be desired on some products and will be secured either by abandoning the paper carton or by replacing it with transparent flexible wrappers. This applies particularly to the toilet goods.

With respect to other products visibility does not appear to be desired, and the paper carton will be retained except where the essential container is opaque glass or where some considerable improvement in the appearance of the product has been made. The adoption of visibility then becomes a means of publicizing the change. For most products, opaque flat surfaces to be used in advertising are likely to be retained. 

The basic package combinations are likely to remain (a) the glass bottle and paper carton for liquids, (b) tin cans without protective wrappers for powders, (c) glass jar with or without the paper carton for creams applied with the fingers, and (d) flexible tube with a paper carton for creams applied with a brush or other instrument.

For products which are in the form of cakes, tablets, pills, or capsules, where the requirements do not appear to be rigid, the ruling consideration will continue to be production economy and flat printing surfaces. 

SELECTION OF THE PRODUCT NAME 

The outline of procedure which this report follows is that which might be appropriate in introducing a new product for the first time. In bringing out a new product a very important subject for the manufacturer's consideration would be the selection of a name. 

While the manufacturer who is merely changing his package will not ordinarily be changing the name of the product, at the same time some considerations concerning the problem of selecting a product name are pertinent to the problem of package modernization. 

It is worth while, at least, to describe the present situation with regard to product names and to suggest the complications which are brought into the process of package change by considerations affecting the name.

IMPORTANCE OF THE TRADE-MARK 

The selection of a name, if the manufacturer expects to have it trade-marked, is hedged about by many legal restrictions. Most packages merchandised through drug stores bear names which are protected by trade-marks. A manufacturer's trade-mark, however, may be a symbol or device rather than the product name itself. In the 1,000 packages studied, the name was trade-marked in approximately 800 cases. The great majority of drug product names, therefore, must first of all conform with patent law and regulations concerning the types of names which can be used. The ideal trademark from the standpoint of Patent Office procedure is the combination of syllables which is a pure invention. 

Each class of name has advantages and disadvantages in use and reacts upon the problem of package modernization in various ways. 

With the growing number of consolidations among manufacturing concerns during several years past, many trade-marks have come into the hands of centralized concerns. It is frequently desirable from the merchandising standpoint to drop the old name and to use the name of the manufacturer as the identifying mark. The purpose is to have each product in the line enjoy some advantage from advertising done on a particular product. This question arises when a

manufacturer wishes to stress the family relationship of his products and have every product appear in the same type of dress so far as color and general appearance are concerned. 

In the outstanding cases where this has been done, however, some exceptions have been made. While the manufacturer may put most of his products into a uniform package, there are some on which the original name will be retained because of the consumer good will which has been built into the name by years of advertising. There are several outstanding examples of this procedure which have come to notice recently, and the probable outcome is for every item manufactured to be put out eventually under the name of the consolidated concern. While at first the trade-mark names of the various products have been carried in a prominent position, they have been definitely coupled with the name of the new concern. Gradually a change in position importance has been made; and while it will require years to make the change complete in some instances, such a change will eventually be made and the old name will either disappear entirely or be mentioned in a very minor way. In the meantime, however, it is virtually impossible to bring all the products into the general scheme of package modernization. 

DIFFICULTIES ATTENDING CHANGE OF NAME 

Changing the name of a product is, on the whole, a more hazardous undertaking than changing the appearance of the package. Consumers are aware that the name of a product is protected and will accept a new package readily if it still carries the identifying trademark. Changing the name of the product, however, requires that it be sold to its consuming public a second time. Striking instances have appeared during the course of the drug-store survey in which customers refused to buy a product when merchandised under a new name, because they could not be convinced that it was identical. 

Where the name is to be changed, there will almost certainly need to be an intermediate stage in the package change for the purpose of identifying the old name with the new.

An actual example of a successful handling of this problem is presented in the food field, where a recent merger brought an independent product which had enjoyed a wide distribution and heavy sales into a company which did not have in its line a similar product. 

The first change made was a simple one and consisted in merely superimposing the name of the new firm in a contrasting color diagonally across the regular label. The second change occurred when the new name was made more pronounced and the old name given less prominence; finally, through a series of such changes, the old name has practically disappeared and the new name has been generally accepted by the consuming public. 

The relation of the product name to package modernization is, therefore, largely of a complicating or obstructing character. These considerations apply only to the selection of the name itself, while the manner of showing the name on the package or the number of times shown are considerations which are discussed elsewhere (see p. 24).

PRINTED MATTER ON THE PACKAGE 

The package carries a message to the consumer which is couched partly in the form of suggestion, but which also relies to some degree on definite printed statement. The printed message on the package includes such varied elements as the product name, statement of amount of contents or suggested list price, description of the product and its merits, and directions for use. This message is usually placed on the surface of the package itself, but is sometimes amplified by means of a package insert. Some of the elements in this message have already been discussed in other connections, but are brought up here for the purpose of commenting on the problem of placing them effectively on the package.

FEATURING THE PRODUCT NAME 

Normally the name of the product is featured more prominently than any other item on the package. A central problem of layout, then, is that of securing the desired prominence for the product named. The front face of the package, on which the name will usually appear whether shown elsewhere or not, fulfills the function of a small billboard placed before the eye of the customer. This billboard, however, is only 2 inches wide and 6 inches high in the typical drug-store package. Even in the larger packages on the druggist's shelves it is only 3 inches wide by 10 inches high. This does not provide a very generous space for the service which must be performed. The three important factors conditioning the solution of this problem are the number of letters composing the name, the manner in which the name is to be set up, and the distance from which it will generally be seen when on display in the store. The distance may be assumed to be 10 feet, considering the basic situation of the customer standing before the counter and looking at the package on the shelf. Taking into account the Snellen tests for acuity of vision and such other factors as differences in lighting and such distractions as neighboring packages, it would doubtless be safe to set a minimum of half-inch letters for reading under display conditions.

ELEMENTS OF PRODUCT DESIGNATION 

The full designation of the product really consists of three partsthe name of the product, the name of the manufacturer, and the trade-mark. Of the three elements in the complete name the name of the manufacturer is used and featured least on the package. On 

60 out of 1,000 packages it is not even shown and on 500 more it is mentioned but not featured. This is in sharp contrast with the name of the product, which is featured on all except 24 packages, and the trade-mark, which is featured on all but 23. Furthermore, the tendency is to feature the product name and the trade-mark more than once, each being featured 8 times in several instances and 2 to 4 times in about 500 cases each. The name of the manufacturer is featured only 440 times all together, and in about one-fourth of these cases it is featured more than once. The tendency toward featuring name and trade-mark is especially noticeable in the toiletgoods field. On the typical drug-store package, therefore, space

must be found for the product name and the trade-mark each to appear twice in bold letters, while the manufacturers' name will appear in small letters. The rectangular package has six faces, and each face is likely to be visible at some time, depending on the manner in which the product is displayed in the store. 

AIDS TO LEGIBILITY 

So far as visibility is concerned, any one face may be set up as a separate problem. It may have to carry a name of 5 to 10 letters on a space only 2 inches wide so that they can be read at a distance of 5 or 6 feet. The name may use most of the face of the package by running across the package diagonally from corner to corner. 

Symbols or elements of design may assist materially in the identification of the package. Regular users of the product will be so familiar with the general appearance of the name as set up in print that they will be able to comprehend it without the necessity of seeing each letter clearly.

Spacing. Careful spacing will, of course, aid materially, it being readily demonstrable that the same space allowance can be used to better advantage with small, well-spaced letters than with larger letters crowded more closely together. Color contrasts between the printing and the base or trim decoration will assist or hinder legibility.

Simplification.-Another aid to legibility is the simplification of a decorative pattern. The modern package tends to have less superfluous decorative detail to distract the eye from the words which are to be read. The tendency is to seek distinction in color combination but to keep the arrangement of lines as simple as possible so that the name may stand out.

There are several examples of simplification along these lines which stand out in the field of modernization of drug-store packages. On one rather high-priced product a sort of concentrating device which had been placed under the name was eliminated; next, the name itself was made more prominent through the use of a type face more modern in appearance than the former type in use; lastly, a slight change was made in the color tone used, and the result was highly pleasing to the manufacturer concerned. It has been reported that better displays have been obtained for the product since the changes were effected. Worthy of note is the fact that when the new and the old packages are placed side by side it is difficult, from a casual glance, to determine the differences, so slight were the necessary changes. In another instance practically no change was made in the package fundamentals. The color scheme is the same as in the original package; the two real changes made were to take the directions from the front panel and place them on the side panels, and to take from the lettering some of the semidecorative borders. 

Greater visibility was obtained, as was also greater display in retail outlets.

INSTRUCTIONS FOR USE OF PRODUCT 

While the designation of the product is likely to be the most conspicuous item of printed material on the package, the longest portion in terms of number of words will be that which describes

the product and gives detailed instructions for its use. Instructions are printed on only 325 out of 1,000 packages examined, and on 439 packages there are printed statements making claims for the product or suggesting conditions which it may be used to cure. 

The practice on these two points is not uniform and varies greatly as between commodity groups. Out of 25 candy bars analyzed, for example; there are no instances where instructions are given, but one package out of the 25 carries a printed statement concerning the merits of the product. Instructions are absent also on dusting powders, pain plasters, razor blades, dental-plate brushes, hairbrushes, and toilet soaps. Claims and suggestions of cures are missing on shaving preparations, dusting powders, face powders, cold creams, face creams, razor blades, dental-plate brushes, sanitary napkins, and finger-nail preparations.

Groups in which instructions are given with a fair degree of uniformity are headache remedies, cold remedies, exterminators, laxatives, shampoos, and prepared foods. Groups on which claims and cures are described on the majority of items are indigestion remedies, cold remedies, cough remedies, exterminators, personal antiseptics, laxatives, and pile remedies.

In Claims, cures, and instructions are undoubtedly printed on the package less frequently than formerly. A number of factors tend to bring this about. One factor is the increasing use of the package insert. Package inserts were found in 75 per cent of the cases where a double container was used. Another factor is the greater familiarity of consumers with drug-store products and their uses. this connection, however, it might be well to point out that while the present consumers of a product are, no doubt, generally familiar with its uses, the thoughtful manufacturer will not overlook the fact that each year sees a loss of some customers and the gaining of others who are not familiar with his product except possibly through seeing it advertised or hearing it mentioned by friends. It may be, also, that proprietary medicines are taken under a doctor's instructions to a greater extent than formerly. All these tendencies are probably in the direction of improving the appearance of the package since crowded areas of printed matter are difficult to handle in decorative pattern.

On all products where the manner of application is at all involved, full instructions should no doubt accompany the product, regardless of such information as the user may be expected to derive from other sources. The package insert may not meet this situation fully, since it is likely to be lost when the package is opened. The best place, no doubt, for detailed instructions is on a label firmly attached to the essential container itself. It may then be retained as long as anything remains in the bottle, while printed matter is by this means removed from the surface of the outside container where it might act as an obstacle to decorative pattern.

OTHER DETAILS 

There are some minor details of printed matter appearing on the package which do not consume space in the same measure as the name, the instructions, and claims for the product. Such elements are size designations, list price, and the names of other products

made by the manufacturer. If these items are to be shown, the obvious place for them is the surface of the outside container. They are intended for the attention of the consumer at the time of purchase, rather than at the time of use, as in the case of instructions. Each of them may affect in one degree or another the consumer's decision to purchase.

The extent to which these details are thought to affect consumer choice is perhaps roughly indicated by the number of times that each occurs in 1,000 packages. Size designation is shown 490 times, list price 438 times, and other products of the manufacturer 148 times.

Size designation.-Size designation is affected to a slight degree by legal requirements. Sizes or quantities are shown on all the 30 prepared foods and the 25 candy bars examined, the designation being required by law in these instances. It is perhaps significant that both these items make use of sizes commonly understood by the consumer, 2 ounces being typical for candy bars and 16 ounces for prepared foods.

Price designation. Price designation is controlled entirely by merchandising considerations. It is usually left off toilet items and tends to be shown in at least half of the cases in proprietary remedy groups.

Other product names.-Carrying the name of other products made by the manufacturer may help the immediate sale if the customer is familiar with the other products, or may suggest additional sales. 

Until the structure of the drug-manufacturing industry is considered, it is somewhat surprising therefore to find that this practice is followed in so few instances. Many manufacturers make only a single product; and even where consolidations have taken place, the separate companies may still be handled as distinct concerns from every standpoint except that of financial control. Commodity groups in which it is customary to show the names of other products made by the same manufacturer are hair tonics, shampoos, pile remedies, exterminators, tonics, and vermifuges.

ADVERTISING APPEAL 

The character of advertising appeal in the message on the package is general in character in about three-fourths of the cases. An attempt to appeal directly to women was observed in 207 instances and an effort to appeal to men in 47 instances.

The important thing is for the package to become identified instantly with the advertising of the product which the consumer may have read elsewhere. The first principle in securing this identification is to give the fullest possible emphasis to the designation of the product. The distinctive color, shape, or decorative pattern of the package will also be of material aid, especially if the package is reproduced in the advertising material. On the whole, it would seem desirable to show a size designation on all products which are used continuously, but to limit price designations to products where the central aim is to stabilize the possible earnings of various distributors on the same product. These designations, where used, should be shown on the outside carton.

COLOR AND DECORATIVE PATTERN 

Color and decorative pattern constitute the most difficult of all phases in a package-change program, but at the same time one of the most important fundamentals. Size of contents, selection of essential container, protective wrapper, and trade name can all be referred to fairly rigid determining factors. The determination of color and decorative pattern, which includes the printed matter, involves artistic creation as well as logical reasoning. For this reason it is suggested that questions of color and decorative pattern be left until all other features of package design have been determined, so that the application of color and decorative pattern may be worked out with the limitations in mind which these other features impose.

PRINTED AREA AS FACTOR IN DESIGN 

The consumer's attention is caught and held by the appearance of the package if the design and blending of colors are harmonious and pleasing to his sense of art and proportion. Many designs on packages give the impression that the lettering or the message conveyed through words was not intended to form an essential part of the design. In order to have the design attractive in all points it is necessary to incorporate the lettering as an integral part of the design. 

If the printed message can be eliminated without causing a sense of artistic loss, then the message does not perform its function in the composite whole.

PLACE OF FUNCTIONALISM 

One of the most important trends in the whole field of fine or applied arts in recent years has been that known as functionalism. 

This method or manner of thinking may be safely said to dominate the thinking of the leading workers in all branches of industrial art. It is the central topic of discussion in architecture, textile weaving, and furniture design. It is not surprising, therefore, to see reflections of this movement in the work of package designers. 

Functionalism in art may be defined as that point of view which asserts that color and decorative pattern should grow directly out of the purpose served by the object to be decorated and should be in accordance with the inherent qualities of the material of which it is made. The function of a package for drug-store products is usually a very simple one. The package is intended merely to inclose economically the space which is to contain the product and to protect the product from various deteriorating influences. 

To inclose space economically for such a simple purpose as that served by packages requires only the simplest of geometrical forms. 

A shape with acute exterior angles, such as a bottle with a triangular cross-section, is obviously not good functionalism on this basis, although it may have some temporary appeal from the standpoint of novelty. Prisms or cylinders with square or circular cross sections are obviously economical means of inclosing space, so long as the height is not too exaggerated in proportion to the cross section. The same may be said for any shapes intermediate between these two, as, for example, a regular octagon.

Other shapes are permissible where the package is to serve some use other than that of merely a container. The attempt to introduce functionalism in relation to consumer use is seen in recent instances where a broad pedestal is provided for the purpose of making a package stand up firmly on the shelf or dressing table. 

Functional considerations of this type are so varied, however, that no general discussion of them will be attempted. Each is a special problem in the case of the particular product involved. 

Several instances may be cited, however, to indicate the experiences of manufacturers who have been faced with this problem. In one instance the problem was to so package the product that ease in use could be obtained without detracting from an already attractive package. A careful study of the actual use of the product developed a method of packing which involved individual inner packages. The outer package functioned as a protective device, while the inner packages functioned as added conveniences for use. 

In another instance that of a beverage-the product had always been packed in rather large units in wooden cases. A new package was developed which contained a lesser number of units and was so equipped that the package itself became a most convenient carrier. 

The new package of units functioned as an added convenience and in no way interfered with the older pack in wooden cases; in fact, a new type of consumer was developed through the use of the newer and smaller unit package.

DECORATIVE DESIGN 

Requirements. Decorative pattern may be defined as the set of lines or masses laid upon the surface of the package for decorative effect. At least three requirements may be set down for governing the creation of decorative pattern. Decorative pattern for a package should be (1) simple, (2) convergent, and (3) consistent. Simplicity of pattern is demanded by the function which the package is supposed to serve. Ornate outlines detract from the prominence of the package name or other identifying mark and render it more difficult to read. The outlines of a decorative design should converge toward the principal point of interest, presumably the name or identifying symbol. This is one of the most time-honored rules governing artistic creation. Designs are sometimes seen which appear to explode because of the fact that all the stronger lines lead the eye out of the design and away from the object rather than inward toward its central point of interest.

Sources.-The sources of decorative pattern for use on packages are as broad as the general sources of art. Some of the sources which are seen to influence package decoration are the shape of the object, geometrical forms, conventional decorative motifs, natural scenes and objects, and the human face or figure. The 1,000 packages covered by the survey were analyzed from the standpoint of the character of the pictures, emblems, or portraits appearing as part of the decorative pattern. Such decorative elements were found on 463 packages, of which 264 were pictures, 139 were emblems, and 69 were portraits (some packages carrying more than one such element). An emblem might be found in combination with the portrait or pictures

of two different objects which might appear in different parts of the design.

Of all the pictures appearing on packages the largest class was made up of those which represented the product itself; that is to say, where a bottle is inclosed in a paper carton a picture of the bottle and the contents appears on the face of the carton. The principal justification for the use of such a picture would be to identify the package to a consumer who is familiar with the essential container as seen in use rather than with the package as seen on the drug-store shelf. It is doubtful, however, whether such a picture is a very effective means of identification. After all, when reduced to a picture one bottle filled with a colored liquid is likely to resemble many other such bottles. The fact that it is used on only 7.6 per cent of all packages is a fair indication that this type of design has not proved to be an essential means of identification. 

Next to pictures of the product representations of the human face or figure provide the most numerous classes of pictures. Seventy-five packages showed pictures of young girls, 43 of men, 42 of women, and 11 of children and babies. If portraits are also considered, the human face or figure appears 237 times in the 1,000 packages. The function of pictures and portraits, however, is quite distinct. The word "picture" as here used refers to an idealization which is supposed to convey some impression, usually concerning the effect which the product will bring about. The type of picture shown depends on the type of person who will use the product. Hair pomades use pictures to a considerable extent, as do related preparations such as shampoos, hair tonics, and hair-color preparations. 

As a matter of fact, pictures of this type are largely confined to toilet items. Pictures shown on packages of proprietary remedies do not as a rule take advantage of the opportunity for picturing glowing health, but seem by preference to portray a person suffering from the ailment for which the remedy is designed. This fact arises from the number of years over which many of these products have been marketed. The packages were designed before modern merchandising psychology was developed, and the manufacturer has been reluctant to change them.

Other types of pictures frequently used show landscapes, animals, insects, the manufacturer's factory, or suggest the use of the product.

The portraits already referred to are generally for the purpose of substantiating the use of the manufacturer's name as a trade-mark. 

For this reason the proportions are very different from those shown in the use of other pictures, there being 51 portraits of men, 19 of women, and 6 of girls.

A number of packages use emblems of one type or another. A great majority of these emblems are the manufacturer's own mark, such as entwined initials or the manufacturer's name shown in connection with a distinctive symbol. Other types of emblems used are those indicating acceptance by medical and other professional associations, which are 6 in number, 12 showing acceptance by manufacturers' and other associations and 14 awards and royal favors.

SELECTION OF COLOR SCHEME 

The phase of package design on which determining factors are least tangible is, no doubt, the selection of a color scheme. The number of theories offered as to the proper point of departure in selecting colors indicates the extent to which the question is debatable. The manufacturer can do no less than take account of all the color suggestions which come to him concerning his product, the materials of which it is made, the uses to which it is put, or the results which it is expected to achieve. No single factor, such as the color of the product itself, the sex of the consumers who are to buy it, or findings concerning the psychology of color attention and appeal can be relied upon exclusively. The most significant fact appearing in an examination of colors found on a thousand packages is the great variety of colors which seem to be successfully used at present.

Aside from the impression made on the consumer by the aspect of a commodity when first viewed under display conditions, there are other considerations of a more practical nature to be weighed when making color selections. The resistance of colors to fading and discoloration due to many elements of use are important, as well as phases of chemical reaction to which the package may be subjected.

RELATIVE IMPORTANCE FOR VARIOUS USES 

The consideration of the use of colors on these packages has been divided into base colors, trim colors, and printing colors. Without regard to the manner in which they are used, colors ranked as follows in number of times appearing: White, 1,069; black, 1,062; red, 570; blue, 547; yellow, 298; green, 291; orange, 218; gold, 205; brown, 184; gray, 151; violet, 76; pink, 50; and silver, 32. These various appearances, however, differed greatly in their importance. Some colors appeared mainly as base colors, others as trim, and still others mainly as printing. Even within these groups the use of the color may have been either bold, medium, or inconspicuous. It was necessary, therefore, to devise a means of weighing the importance of each color appearance. This was done by assigning values to bold, medium, and inconspicuous appearances, of 3, 2, and 1, respectively. 

These weights are not merely arbitrary, since a bold appearance represented approximately three times as much area on the face of the package as an inconspicuous appearance.

Base colors. The rank of the base colors when judged in these terms was as follows: White, 942; yellow, 379; blue, 340; orange, 314; green, 251; red, 232; gray, 174; black, 172; brown, 165; pink, 60; violet, 48; silver, 23; and gold, 14. The importance of white alone as a base color is almost half as great as that of all other colors combined. This fact probably arises from the obvious economy in printing where white is used as a base, since this is the usual color of the original material. There are apparently some sharp breaks in the rest of the list, which readily divides itself into groups of approximately similar importance as background. There seems to be some logical basis for these groups in each case. 

For example, yellow, blue, and orange are of approximately equal importance,

yellow and orange being neighboring colors on the spectrum and blue the complementary of orange. The complementaries red and green are of approximately equal importance, as are the neutral colors gray, black, and brown. 

The place of colors other than white in this list is to some degree subject to interpretation. Blue, appearing in the first group, has consistently shown up in most laboratory tests of color preference as the favorite color of all classes of people. The frequency for yellow and orange, on the other hand, which have usually proved to be low in color preference, is undoubtedly increased by their use for contrast where blue is used in the trim or printing colors. Green and red, which are shown in the present study to be of next greatest importance as base colors, frequently fall next to blue in tests of color preference. The use of gray, black, and brown as base colors has undoubtedly been stimulated by the emphasis upon neutral tints which has marked the work of some of the modern decorators and designers, and the tendency to assign a suggestion of sanitary character to neutral tones.

Trim colors. The relative importance of trim colors was as follows: Black, 870; white, 829; red, 569; blue, 495; green, 273; gold, 268; yellow, 252; brown, 172; silver, 155; gray, 153; orange, 139; violet, 94; and pink, 53. It will appear at once that there have been some striking differences in the rank of colors when used in the trim and when used as base. The principal changes are the raising of black from a very subordinate position to the head of the list, and the depressing of yellow and orange from near the head of the list to the lower half. No one color stands out quite so strikingly as white does among the base colors. As in the list of base colors, there seem to be definite groups of nearly equal importance. 

Printing colors. The relative importance of colors used in printing is as follows: Black, 1,044; white, 933; red, 512; blue, 420; gold, 158; yellow, 146; green, 142; orange, 130; brown, 120; gray, 54; violet, 43; silver, 27; and pink, 16. It will be observed at once that this list is very similar to the list of trim colors. The important distinction is a considerably greater concentration on the four colors at the top of the list. This would seem to indicate that there is a slightly higher determination of printing colors on the basis of legibility, although, in general, printing colors will be chosen to obtain proper contrasts with base colors, just as in the case of trim colors. Some differences in the two lists are no doubt due to the fact that printing colors are sometimes placed over trim colors rather than over base colors.

MULTIPLICITY OF COLORS 

Sometimes a number of colors are used on the same package, although the tendency is to keep the combination as simple as possible. 

Normally there is only one color on the package which might be called the base color. In a few instances, however, space was so evenly divided between two or three colors that each had to be considered as a base color. Forty-one packages used two base colors, and four used three base colors. Multiplicity of colors is of much greater occurrence in the trimmings of the package. Two or more trim were used on 617 packages, and on 49 of these there were

five or more trim colors. Seventeen packages made use of no trim color. Slightly less variation was found in the number of printing colors used, with only 533 packages showing more than one printing color.

Despite this considerable variety in number of colors used, the typical package from the color standpoint would carry a simple combination. Three colors might be considered typical, one being used as base, and two colors being involved in trim and printing. In some cases trim and print would use separate colors, while in others there might be two trim colors, one of which was repeated in the printing. 

COLOR PREFERENCE TESTS 

Color-preference tests have been made in a number of psychological laboratories. These tests have been designed to measure variations in preference for many types of subjects. Among those tested have been whites, Mexicans, negroes, Japanese, men and women, psychopathic patients, children of various school grades, college students, and adults graded by age, and persons grouped as to normal, inferior, or superior intelligence. Neither the methods nor the results of these tests have been entirely uniform. There are some tendencies, however, which appear to run through all of them. 

The rank of color preferences which seems to occur most frequently and which might be taken as representing a normal cross section of the population is as follows: Blue, green, red, violet, orange, yellow, and white. There are many variations in the intermediate rankings, but white is nearly always the least-favored color and blue the most-favored, regardless of age, sex, or racial origin. 

The tests show that a number of popular beliefs concerning color preference have not been well founded. It has been stated, for example, that red is the favorite color of women. 

The tests appear to indicate that blue is the favorite with both men and women, but that red tends to obtain a somewhat higher ranking with women than with men. Color preferences with whites and negroes appear to be very similar. There does not seem to be any sharp distinction by levels of intelligence, except that superior white children have been found to distribute their preferences more evenly over the entire color range than do those of inferior intelligence. The whole body of data tends to indicate that experience with color is the primary determinant of color preference. In general, those of more favorable social environment seem to distribute their color preferences more broadly. There seems also to be a definite shift in color preference with increasing age. In general, it may be said that the warm colors-red, orange, and yellow-lose in preference as age increases, while the cool colors-green and blue steadily climb to the top of the preference scale. Red is the favorite color only with very young children.

The history of art gives some further reflection of a similar change in color preference. The schools known as primitives have typically worked in bold and unrelieved colors. Later an appreciation of softer shades and more subtle harmonies is developed. At the present time a strong leaning toward neutral and metallic tones may be observed, particularly in the industrial arts. From the standpoint of trends in color preference therefore, it would appear that a pack-

age making use of dominant neutral tones and cool colors would be most assured of continued acceptance. General color education will no doubt result, however, in a broad distribution of preferences which will in time allow greater freedom of choice for package use. 

The point has been made by some students of color in advertising that general color preferences are not sustained when it comes to use of colors in packages. Tests were conducted, for example, to determine color preference for use on coffee cartons. The results showed 

50 men and 23 women agreeing on color preference in the following order: Orange, yellow, red, purple, green, blue, white, and black. It will be observed that some colors which are high in this list do not score strongly in the general tests of color preference. Unfortunately, coffee color was left out of the test, so that it was not conclusive on such points as to whether the color of the product should furnish the dominant color note for the package. These tests, however, are highly suggestive as to methods to be used in package development, since color tests for specific products can be more directly used than indications of general preferences. Other tests were carried through concerning the selection of pairs of colors for use together on packages and difference in reactions between tints and saturated colors.

COLOR COMBINATIONS 

Some tests made in a French laboratory, which have been widely quoted in this country, covered the question of best color combinations for legibility of printed matter. The 14 most legible combinations of colored type with a background color were as follows: 

Black on yellow, green on white, red on white, blue on white, white on blue, black on white, yellow on black, white on red, white on green, white on black, red on yellow, green on red, red on green, and blue on red. These favorable combinations occur very frequently on drug-store packages. The most surprising feature of this list perhaps is that the combination black and white is in sixth place, while the combination black and yellow was determined to be the most favorable one from this standpoint.

There seems to be a definite color preference, at least as to base color, in some of the commodity groups covered by the 1,000 packages. White is the base color most frequently used in 33 out of the 

46 groups. This probably does not indicate preference in these groups, however, but merely reflects the factor of economy referred to before. On such packages the really characteristic color note is in the trim colors rather than in the base. For example, on cold creams, where the base color was white on 10 out of 20 packages, the trim colors were typically black, silver, gold, and blue. On most of the proprietary medicines, where white is the typical base color, red, yellow, green, and all other colors are found in combination. Other groups in which a dominant color note was found to prevail to a greater or less degree were as follows: Blue (shaving preparations, toothbrushes, and tooth preparations); red (face powders, dusting powders, and razor blades); orange (hair pomades and diuretics); yellow (exterminators); green (after-shaving lotions); gray (sanitary napkins).

All the points considered heretofore are, of course, merely limiting conditions tending to determine the range of possible color combinations. For more positive inspiration in selecting a color scheme the manufacturer or the package designer must look to the product and the variety of color suggestions that may grow out of its materials, use, or anticipated effect. The symbol chosen by the manufacturer to distinguish his product may determine the point of departure in the color scheme. Once a point of departure is established, the pattern may be worked out in either neighboring or complementary tones. The choice of these colors and the amount of each color to be used will be determined by the artist on the basis of wellestablished laws of color balance. The fact that there are several systems for obtaining color balance does not appear to affect the result materially, since each system is able to produce attractive color combinations.

TESTING THE DRUG-STORE PACKAGE 

In the package-change program outlined in an earlier section (see p. 5), the last two points mentioned were comparison of the package with existing practice and tests of consumer reactions. Comparisons with existing practice are for the purpose of making sure (a) that the package does not differ so much as to be unduly conspicuous in its commodity group, and (b) that it is sufficiently distinctive to identify the product. In making this comparison it is not sufficient merely to collect a number of packages of competing manufacturers. Some means should be adopted for giving each of these packages an appropriate weight in relation to the amount of stock which will usually be found on the retailers' shelves. One method which has been successfully used is that of the laboratory store, in which a set of shelves of a design similar to that usually found in the retail outlet is erected and the package under consideration is placed on the shelf along with a collection of competing packages such as will be likely to surround it. 

Nothing short of a series of consumer tests and reactions will serve to properly check the package-change program. In the first instance all possible data should be collected which will throw any light on the consumer's choice with regard to quantities. Such data will include both information concerning the sales of various package sizes already in use and inquiries directed to consumers calculated to establish the rate of use and the most convenient intervals of repurchase.

SIZE AND SHAPE 

Once the quantity is determined dummy packages should be made up in various dimensions and proportions, with each inclosing sufficient space to contain the required quantity. Consumer tests of the most unbiased sort obtainable should be secured as to which package shape and type is most acceptable to the consumer. Dummies for such use should always be made up in a neutral gray, since either black or white or colors will tend to distort the appearance of size and shape. When a name is selected it should be subjected to similar tests as to consumer appeal. One method of obtaining a name

with such appeal is the familiar one of a prize contest among users of the product. In the same way subjects for consumer reactions are frequently obtained, either by a small cash payment or by giving as a premium a free package of the product itself. 

ADVERTISING MATTER 

Printed matter appearing on the package should be subjected to the same close scrutiny as other advertising copy. Such an advertising message should, as a matter of fact, be tested more thoroughly than the usual piece of copy to appear in the magazine or newspaper. 

It will usually have to stand for a much longer period and be read by all classes using the product rather than merely those of a particular group making up the readers of a given medium. A safe rule with regard to price and size designations and other such items sometimes placed on the package is to include no elements except those which are required by law or those intended to serve definite and carefully considered purposes.

THE CONCLUSIVE TEST 

When the fully completed package has been created, nothing less than a test under actual selling conditions should be considered conclusive of its value. Laboratory conditions can never include all the conditions under which the package will actually be sold. A selling test in actual retail outlets may frequently reveal some fatal defect, no matter how carefully the whole program may have been developed. It should be relatively easy for manufacturers to develop arrangements for such selling tests. A moderate amount of free goods or a small cash consideration should prove a sufficient inducement to the retailer to undertake such tests. Tests should be carefully controlled, however, so that it will be possible to interpret the results, and they should preferably be made in several types of markets and sales records kept for several control stores in similar areas.
