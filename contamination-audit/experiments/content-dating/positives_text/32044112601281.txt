Practical Aspects of Resuscitation By EDWARD STEIDLE INTRODUCTION 

Fundamental knowledge relating to the physiology of breathing was not discovered until late in the seventeenth century. Before this time little was known except the muscular mechanism of breathing and the fact that death will occur if breathing is interrupted for a few minutes; also that breathing is increased by labor and by some diseases. Harvey's epoch-making treatise in 1628, in which he announced and demonstrated the circulation of the blood, gave no positive clues as to the physiology of breathing, and it was still generally believed that the main function of respiration was to cool the blood. 

It is interesting to note that physiological discoveries have depended upon corresponding discoveries in chemistry and physics. 

Rapid progress has been made during the past fifteen years in methods of resuscitation. Many brilliant papers on this subject have been presented by physiologists and by members of the medical profession. These papers naturally contain medical terms and physiological discussions which are not generally understood by the laymen. On the other hand, numerous first-aid instructions for administering artificial respiration have been prepared, but they merely tell how; none explain why we breathe; consequently in some cases these instructions have not been carried out effectively. 

This paper does not present any new theories, but is a correlation of facts expressed in plain language which may bridge the gap between the medical profession and the layman, and it is hoped that it will meet the needs of men engaged in industry who are qualified to study advanced first-aid methods, also that it may lead to the saving of life. 

RESPIRATION 

The act of respiration is an involuntary physical function which sometimes can be made voluntary by an act of the will. It is accomplished by enlarging the chest cavity by means of the muscles between the ribs (intercostal muscles), which raise and spread the rids, and by means of the diaphragm (a flat or disc-shaped muscle dividing the lung cavity from the abdominal cavity), which automatically descends as the ribs are spread apart and raised. 

This process of enlarging the chest cavity creates a negative pressure in the space between the lungs and the inside of the chest (pleural-cavity), and air is drawn into the lungs. This process of drawing air into the lungs is called inspiration. By depressing and contracting the ribs and raising the diaphragm the chest cavity is diminished and the air is forced out of the lungs. This process is called expiration. Expiration and inspiration together are what we know as respiration or breathing. 

The lungs of the average person hold about six quarts of air. At rest a person breathes about twelve times each minute and inhales about one quart of air with each breath. During exertion the rate of breathing increases to about twenty breaths per minute, and the volume of each breath is about 1.5 quarts. [ 3 ]

CIRCULATION OF THE BLOOD 

In order that the process of breathing may be thoroughly understood, it is necessary first to describe the circulation of the blood in the body, as it is the blood that carries the oxygen of the air to all parts of the body. 

The Heart 

The chief organ of circulation is the heart (see Figure 1). The heart is a hollow, muscular organ divided into four chambers or cavities. The two upper chambers (right and left auricles) are smaller and have thinner walls and collect the blood. The two lower chambers (right and left ventricles) are larger and have thicker walls and serve as "pumps" for the blood. The blood, after having been collected from all parts of the body by the veins, is finally poured into the right collecting chamber by the two main veins. (superior and inferior vena cava) through inlet valves. The blood then passes through an inlet valve (tricuspid) into the right pumping chamber. 

This chamber, in contracting during a heart beat, forces the blood through an artery (pulmonary) to the lungs. What happens to the blood in the lungs will be described a little later. In returning from the lungs, the blood is carried back to the heart through two veins (pulmonary) and enters the left collecting chamber of the heart. From this chamber it passes through an inlet valve (mitral) to the left pumping chamber, and this chamber, by contracting, sends the blood through the arteries to all parts of the body. All veins contain numerous check valves (venous) that prevent back flow of blood between heart beats, or in other words, strokes of the pump. The body of the average person contains about five quarts of blood. 

Process of Circulation 

The arteries divide and sub-divide, getting smaller with each sub-division, and finally reach all parts of the body. The smallest blood vessels into which the arteries divide are called "capillaries." The capillaries in turn unite together to form the veins. 

Oxidation of Waste Products

It is mostly in the capillaries that the oxygen which the blood carries is given off to the various tissues of the body and where the carbon dioxide (CO2) is taken up by the blood. This giving off of oxygen and taking up of carbon dioxide by the blood is accomplished by osmosis, which signifies the interchange of gases through a very thin membrane. Vital reactions in the tissues of the body are carried on by a process of oxidation, or in other words, a process of burning; and as in any burning, oxygen is necessary, and carbon dioxide is given off. This is the reason why the blood must carry fresh oxygen to the tissues and take away the waste products of combustion. 

FUNCTION OF THE LUNGS 

Ordinarily, air consists of about one part (or volume) of oxygen to four parts of nitrogen, or 20 per cent. oxygen and 80 per cent. nitrogen. Nitrogen is an inactive gas and takes no part in supporting life or combustion. Oxygen [ 4 ]

is the important constituent, because without it there can be no life or combustion. Air containing only 15 per cent. of oxygen causes deep breathing, 12 per cent. causes heavy breathing, and air containing less than 10 per cent. of oxygen may cause death. In experimental tests when the percentage of oxygen is slowly reduced, men at rest have lived in a mixture of 4 per cent. oxygen and 96 per cent. nitrogen.

The Lungs 

The windpipe (trachea) is a hollow tube through which the air passes to the lungs (see Figure 1). This hollow tube is surrounded by rings of gristle, which keep the tube from collapsing. A familiar example is the windpipe of a chicken, which everybody has seen. This windpipe divides into two main air passages (bronchi), one of which goes to each lung. The main air passages divide and sub-divide, getting smaller with each sub-division, and finally terminate in tiny sub-divisions (bronchiole) and very thin-walled aerating cells or sacks (alveoli).

Reoxygenation of the Blood 

The extremely thin walls of the terminal aerating cells of the lungs are covered with thousands of microscopic capillaries, which form from veins and again build up as arteries. By a similar process to that by which the blood takes up carbon dioxide and gives off oxygen in the tissues of the body, here in the lungs the carbon dioxide is given off by the blood from these cells and oxygen is taken on. This is the same osmosis referred to in the tissues, an interchange of gases through a very thin membrane. Exhaled air contains about 4.4 per cent. of carbon dioxide.

Reaction of Carbon Dioxide in the Lungs The best authorities state that it is really the carbon dioxide in the lungs which stimulates the involuntary portion of our respiration. This can be easily understood if it is remembered that when breath is held for half a minute or more there is an intense desire to take a breath. This is due to the stimulation of the respiratory center which is situated in the voluntary section of the brain, by the carbon dioxide that has accumulated in the lungs. The act of respiration is controlled by the pneumogastric nerve and is kept going at a normal rate by the constant production of carbon dioxide by the continued burning process in the body. In this connecton it is logical and interesting to note that at any time when more carbon dioxide than normal is produced, as in severe physical exertion or in fevers, the rate of breathing materially increases. 

It is to be noted that at no time can the lungs be entirly empty of air. 

There is always a portion called “residual air" which can not be expelled even by the deepest act of respiration. This residual air is oxygenated little by little during respiration, but its relative quantity does not change. 

ASPHYXIATION 

It is obvious from the above that for respiration we must have an atmos phere to breathe which contains sufficient oxygen to supply the needs of the body. If for any reason at all there is not sufficient oxygen in the atmosphere, or if air or oxygen fails to reach the terminal aerating cells of the lungs, then [ 5 ]

the blood can no longer be re-oxygenated in the lungs, and what is known as asphyxiation or suffocation results.

Asphyxiation or suffocation is a result of the body tissues not receiving sufficient oxygen and at the same time accumulating an excess of carbon dioxide. The first tissues of the body to suffer from this condition are the higher centers of the brain, and when these are sufficiently affected by asphyxiation the victim becomes unconscious. 

The most common causes of asphyxiation are as follows: (1) Inhalation of carbon dioxide (CO2); (2) inhalation of smoke; (3) inhalation of carbon monoxide (CO); (4) petroleum vapors; (5) miscellaneous gases; (6) drowning; and (7) electric shock. 

Carbon Dioxide

It is obvious that in inhaling an atmosphere deficient in oxygen and heavily impregnated with carbon dioxide the body can not get sufficient oxygen. 

The lungs are only breathing a mixture of the same gases which they should discharge. The absence of oxygen and the excess of carbon dioxide finally produces unconsciousness, absence of breathing, and in time if unrelieved, death. Smoke

It should be remembered that when a smoky atmosphere is inhaled part of its bad effects may be due to carbon monoxide, and especially to oxygen deficiency, as a big fire uses up even more oxygen from the atmosphere than the human being does in breathing. There is also another important feature in smoke asphyxiation; the smoke itself is so irritating to the air passages that a person is unable to get the atmosphere down to the terminal aerating cells of the lungs, and coughs so violently that he literally can not breathe. Hence he is getting virtually no air into the lungs, and what little air he is breathing. contains little or no oxygen. 

Carbon Monoxide

In poisoning by carbon monoxide, asphyxiation takes place chiefly for the reason that the hemoglobin of the blood corpuscles has a great affinity for carbon monoxide and will take it up more quickly (300 times) than it will take oxygen. If you think of each corpuscle as a little boat and the oxygen as a number of passengers, it will be easy to see that if the boat prefers carbonmonoxide passengers to oxygen passengers how quickly the oxygen passengers will be crowded out. When each corpuscle then is loaded or saturated with carbon monoxide there is no more room for the corpuscle to carry oxygen to the tissues, and asphyxiation results. Unconsciousness results when the blood of a victim is about 50 per cent. saturated with carbon monoxide, and death at about 65 per cent.

Petroleum Vapors 

The physiological effect of petroleum vapors is very hard to define. In most cases it is quite similar to anesthesia caused by chloroform, ether, and alcohol. Inhalation of petroleum vapors causes headaches, dizziness, intoxica- [ 6 ]

tion, anesthesia, and death. Some of the vapors are actually absorbed by the blood, and asphyxiation is due to resulting nervous disorders. It appears from published data that air containing 0.5 per cent. will cause headache and dizziness in about 15 minutes. One per cent. inhaled for a period of 15 to 30 minutes will cause serious physical disturbances. Anesthesia, and finally death, may result after exposure of 30 minutes to 2.5 per cent. The stage between unconsciousness and death is very narrow, making the vapors of the higher hydrocarbons dangerous anesthetics.

Miscellaneous Gases 

Other poisonous and asphyxiating gases encountered in the industries are oxides of nitrogen and hydrogen sulphide. Sulphur dioxide and ammonia fumes are highly irritating to the air passages, cause violent coughing and prevent a person from getting air down into the lungs. 

Chlorine and bromine gases, acid and miscellaneous chemical vapors and gases from carbon tetrachloride fire extinguishers such as phosgene, hydrochloric acid gas and carbon tetrachloride vapor have an irritating as well as corrosive action on the walls of the air passages which decreases the ventilating area of the lungs. Drowning

In drowning there is not, as is generally supposed, a great deal of water taken into the lungs because the pressure of the air within the lungs prevents the water from getting much further down than the main air passages. The water in these passages can be drained out by the process described in any manual on first-aid methods for drowning. However, when the victim is submerged in the water, he is not getting any oxygen at all, and the excess of carbon dioxide in his blood soon renders him unconscious and stops his breathing. 

Electric Shock

Electric shock is not a true asphyxiation at all, but a paralysis of the muscles of respiration due to the paralysis of the vital centers which control it. 

This paralysis is accompanied by a paralysis of every other vital organ, voluntary or involuntary, in the body. Some electric shocks merely go far enough to render the person unconscious or to render him unconscious and stop his breathing. Artificial respiration is beneficial in such cases. If, however, the heart and the other vital functions have also been stopped by the electric shock, the patient may be said to have been killed instantly, and in such cases artificial respiration is of no value. This, however, should not deter a person from applying artificial respiration and other first-aid measures for at least four hours after each case of shock, no matter how severe it may be.

By making a person breathe artificially we get oxygen back into the lungs, where it can be loaded by the blood and taken to the various vital centers of the body. As respiration improves, and more oxygen is brought to the vital centers of the body, in time the higher centers are re-oxygenated and the person becomes conscious once more. 

History of Artificial Respiration 

The act of administering artificial respiration probably dates back to Biblical history. In the Old Testament (I King, xvii. 17) it is stated that 

Elijah restored breath in a boy. The first manual method for administering artificial respiration is known as the Sylvester method, but in later years this method has lost favor and the Schaefer prone-pressure method has come into general use. About ten years ago the United States Bureau of Mines and the 

United States Public Health Service determined by experiment that the Schaefer method is simpler and affects a greater exchange of air in and out of the lungs, and consequently those Bureaus recommend that this method be used in all cases where possible. The Sylvester method may be used advantageously in the case of an injured back. In presenting these methods it is only necessary to refer to a standard publication such as "Manual of First-Aid Instructions for Miners," published by the Bureau of Mines, which can be obtained by writing to its Publication Section, Washington, D. C. 

Training in Artificial Respiration 

Men, women and children in all walks of life should be trained in manual methods of artificial respiration and first aid. Systematic training courses have been prepared by the American Gas Association, the National Electric Light Association and the American Red Cross as well as the Bureau of Mines. 

Many steel works, mines, petroleum refineries, chemical plants and other industrial companies are training their employees in artificial respiration and first aid. The public utilities and fire and police departments are not only training their employees, but are equipping rescue trucks with approved breathing and resuscitation apparatus to answer emergency calls. These trucks are manned by well-trained and experienced men. 

Schaefer Method 

In using the Schaefer prone-pressure method, place the person on his abdomen, remove from his mouth all foreign bodies such as false teeth, tobacco and gum; the tongue usually falls forward of its own weight, but see that it is forward; turn his head to one side and rest it on his forearm, so that the mouth and the nose will not come in contact with the ground, and extend the other arm forward. If the person is thin, prepare a pad or folder of clothing [ 9 ]

or blankets and place it under the lower part of his chest. Do not make this pad too thick. Do not wait to loosen the victim's clothing, but begin artificial respiration without delay. An assistant may remove all tight clothing from the victim's neck, chest and waist; blankets, hot-water bottles or hot bricks well wrapped in paper or cloth, should be placed about the victim by an assistant. 

Kneel (see Figure 2, A) and straddle the victim's thighs, facing his head.

The palms of your hands are placed on the small of the back with the fingers over the ribs, the little finger just touching the lowest rib, the thumb alongside of the fingers, the tips of the fingers just out of sight (see Figure 2, B). 

With arms held straight, swing forward slowly so that the weight of your body is gradually brought to bear on the person (see Figure 2, C). This operation, which should take about two seconds, must not be violent, lest the internal organs be injured. The lower part of the chest and also the abdomen are thus compressed and air is forced out of the lungs. Now immediately swing back slowly to remove the pressure, thus returning to the original position. Through their elasticity the patient's chest walls expand and his lungs are thus supplied with fresh air. After two seconds, swing forward again and repeat deliberately, about fifteen times a minute, the double movement of compressing and releasing, thus causing a complete respiration in about four seconds.

Continue any method of artificial respiration, if necessary, for at least four hours without interruption, or until natural breathing has been restored or a physician has arrived. Even after natural breathing begins, carefully watch that it continues. If it stops, start artificial respiration again. Do not give any liquids whatever by mouth until the person is fully conscious, when you may give slowly aromatic spirits of ammonia (a teaspoonful in a half a glass of water), hot coffee, hot tea or water. Do not permit bystanders to crowd around; cases are on record where crowding has had a bad effect on the patient. Do not allow patient to walk when revived; keep patient on his back and warm for several hours thereafter. 

Special Treatment for Corrosive and Irritating Gases 

When ammonia gas has been inhaled, dip a handkerchief or gauze, folded once, into vinegar, wring out lightly, and lay loosely over the mouth and nose. 

Keep patient quiet.

Sylvester Method 

To employ the Sylvester method, place the patient on his back. The tongue must be held out, as otherwise it will fall back and block the windpipe. 

Grasp it in a dry cloth or pincers. Have someone hold it out or, better, hold it out by a bandage or rubber band over the tongue and under the jaw. Place a rolled-up coat, a pad, or something else of the same shape and size under the suffocated man's shoulders. This will straighten his windpipe. Kneel just above the patient's head; catch both his arms at the wrists. Draw the arms outward and upward gently and steadily and hold them as far as they will go above the head. This operation requires about two seconds. This motion opens and expands the chest to the greatest possible extent. Certain muscles [10]

are attached to both arms and ribs, and when the arms are raised these muscles raise the ribs and so enlarge the chest. Then bring the arms down till the elbows press against the chest, arms crossed; with a little pressure diminish the size of the elastic chest as much as possible. This movement also requires about two seconds. Continue these motions about fifteen times per minute. 

When done properly, this treatment is hard work for the operator and he should be relieved by someone as soon as he grows tired. 

HAZARD OF CARBON MONOXIDE 

Carbon-monoxide gas is considered the nation's greatest gas hazard. It is a product of incomplete combustion and one of the chief constituents of illuminating gas; also of producer, coke oven and blast-furnace gases. Some substances when burning are known to give off excessive amounts of carbon monoxide, such as celluloid films, pyroxlins, paper storage, etc. It has no odor, taste or color, nor does it give any warning by irritating the throat or nose. 

It was said previously that carbon monoxide has an action on human beings which is peculiar to itself. In other words, when inhaled it comes into contact with the blood as it is brought to the lungs and unites chemically with hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying constituent of the blood, which plays an important part in circulation. After this chemical combination has taken place the individual who has inhaled the gas is deficient in oxygen, the allimportant part of the blood, just as much as though the same amount had been lost by the opening of one of the blood vessels or his supply of air had been completely shut off. A few breaths of air containing 2 per cent. of carbon monoxide will cause unconsciousness and death in three or four minutes. Death is likely to occur when a man breathes air containing as low as 0.2 per cent. of carbon monoxide for two to four hours. As a matter of fact, relatively short exposures for 20 or 30 minutes to any percentage above 0.1 per cent. (the average carbon-monoxide content of the black smoke from a burning building) must be regarded as dangerous, and particularly during hasty and vigorous exertion, when the volume of breathing is greatly increased, and consequently saturation of the blood with carbon monoxide is effected more rapidly.

RESUSCITATION DEVICES 

In 1908, a German device called the pulmotor made its first appearance in America. This mechanical device is automatic in its action, and actually pumps an air mixture rich in oxygen into the lungs under positive pressure, and out of the lungs under negative pressure. In about 1907, a "so-called" lungmotor was designed in America and was put on the market in 1911. This device also pumps air rich in oxygen in and out of the lungs, under pressure, but the pumping action is accomplished by hand. Both machines were sold in large numbers and, unfortunately, have been given wide publicity by the press. 

Harm in Using Positive Pressure Resuscitation Devices 

As early as 1914 the United States Bureau of Mines and the United States Public Health Service tested and condemned the use of both the pulmotor and lungmotor for administering artificial respiration; as a matter of fact, these two Bureaus do not approve of any positive-pressure device for administering artificial respiration. In the course of exhaustive investigation [11]

it was found that mechanical devices of this character do not conform to nat ural breathing; on the other hand, they disturb the natural rhythm of breathing, which is controlled by carbon dioxide in the different portions of the lung cells. Furthermore, it was found that the excessive pressure and suction developed by mechanical devices in due time rupture the delicate lung tissue, and actually do more harm than good. 

Theories Relating to a Resuscitation Device 

Physiologists now agree that pure oxygen tends to retard breathing, but that oxygen containing a small amount of carbon dioxide will stimulate breathing, especially in cases of asphyxiation. This mixture may be administered by an inhalator which is controlled by respiration. 

As a result of research and experiment, Drs. Yandell Henderson and 

H. W. Haggard, physiologists in Yale University, several years ago discovered that air containing 5 per cent. of carbon dioxide excites the respiratory organs in a natural manner; also that a 93:7 per cent. mixture of oxygen and carbon dioxide is best for resuscitation, and especially in cases of carbon monoxide poisoning and ether or alcohol intoxication. This theory is substantiated by many other eminent physiologists, including Dr. J. S. Haldane, of Cambridge, England.

Carbon Monoxide Asphyxia 

In the case of carbon monoxide asphyxia, the union of carbon monoxide with hemoglobin is reversible; in other words, by mass action of oxygen the carbon monoxide, which has been absorbed, may be displaced. Oxygen inhalation is theoretically the best method for displacing carbon monoxide from the blood, and thus restoring the normal oxygen-carrying capacity of the hemoglobin.

THE H-H INHALATOR 

The inhalators which conform with the foregoing theories are the Atmos Inhalator, Model C; the Industro Oxygenator, Universal Model, and the 

H-H Inhalator. Each is of about the same general type. The last named is now mostly in use and the development and operation of this inhalator is of particular interest.

In 1920, Doctors Henderson and Haggard enlisted the services of the 

Mine Safety Appliances Company, Pittsburgh, Pa., in designing an inhalator which would embody all of the accepted principles for such a device. This company applied the lung-governing principle of the McCaa reducing valve used in oxygen-breathing apparatus, and with that as a basis developed the H-H Inhalator in its present form. It consists (see Figure 3) essentially of a steel cylinder containing a 93:7 per cent. mixture of compressed oxygen and carbon dioxide. This mixture passes through a reducing valve and a low-pressure regulating valve into a breathing bag, and thence through a tube direct to a face mask containing inhalation, exhalation, and fresh air valves.

EFFICACY OF THE OXYGEN-CARBON DIOXIDE TREATMENT 

In 1923, after careful physiological study, a Commission of the Amer ican Gas Association made a report, which contains three important recommendations: (1) That the use of positive-pressure resuscitation appliances such as the pulmotor and lungmotor be discontinued; (2) that the use of the Schaefer prone-pressure method of artificial respiration be adopted; and (3) that in cases of carbon-monoxide poisoning the Schaefer method be sup plemented by the use of an inhalator which will pour a 95:5 per cent. mixture of oxygen and carbon dioxide into the lungs, it being recognized that a rich oxygen mixture is doubly effective in purifying the blood and a small percentage of carbon dioxide will stimulate breathing. 

The inhalation treatment of a 95:5 per cent. mixture of oxygen and carbon dioxide is also invaluable for relieving headaches induced by partial gassing or exposure to smoke. [14]

Since this study was made, it has been found that a mixture of 93:7 per cent. oxygen and carbon dioxide is preferable, as quicker and better results are obtained.

Record of Experience 

In December, 1925, the American Gas Association Monthly published a report by Dr. C. K. Drinker, physiologist in Harvard University, covering the efficacy of the oxygen-carbon dioxide treatment of carbon monoxide poisoning. 

This report covers the experience of 180 organizations possessing one of the three inhalators mentioned above. All evidence obtained indicated (1) that the inhalation treatment of this mixture is exceedingly valuable in mild gassing and relieves or prevents the occurrence of headaches, nausea, etc.; (2) that serious cases, still breathing, revive rapidly and thoroughly; and (3) that it is entirely practical to supplement a manual method of artificial respiration with the inhalation treatment.

CARBON MONOXIDE DETECTING DEVICES 

Nearly every daily paper contains an account of one or more cases of carbon monoxide asphyxia and the majority occur from illuminating gas, which contains from 5 to 30 per cent. of carbon monoxide. Today, members of the medical profession, safety engineers and municipal officials all over the country are constantly on the lookout for new inventions and improved apparatus that may be utilized in fighting this hazard. It is appropriate, therefore, briefly to discuss the approved carbon monoxide detecting devices which are now employed. 

Carbon Monoxide Detector

The carbon monoxide detector (see Figure 6) is designed to determine the presence of carbon monoxide in dangerous proportions. The detector tubes used with the instrument are filled with a chemical which changes color when air containing carbon monoxide is passed through it. The shade of the color (bluish-green) varies with the concentration of carbon monoxide. By comparing this with the standard color scale, which is included with the instru ment, not only the presence but the approximate amount of carbon monoxide in the air under test can be quickly determined. About 0.07 per cent. can be casily detected with this device. 

The barrel of the instrument is filled with activated charcoal, which absorbs any other gases in the atmosphere which might affect the chemical in the detector tube. Ten squeezes of the bulb are all that is needed to make a test. The color produced in the detector tube soon fades upon standing, permitting its re-use for seven or eight ordinary tests, when it should be replaced with a new one.

Continuous Carbon Monoxide Indicator 

The continuous carbon monoxide indicator (see Figure 7) is a portable instrument which provides a continuous analysis of carbon monoxide in air with exceeding accuracy in the low concentrations that are normally difficult to detect.

A small motor driven pump, operated by storage battery cells arranged in the case, draws the sample continuously into the instrument. [18]

SAMPLING LINE 

The sample first passes through a flow meter, consisting of a capillary and manometer, in order to maintain a constant flow. This is accomplished by having a constant differential on the manometer through the adjustment of the volume control valve. The sample then passes through a canister of chemicals which removes any moisture. From the canister it now enters the cell containing a catalyst known as "Hopcalite" and then passes out the exhaust valve of the pump. In the cell, any carbon monoxide is oxidized to carbon dioxide by the cataly tic action of the "Hopcalite".

Heat, liberated by this oxidation, is directly proportional to the amount of carbon monoxide present and is measured by a

The instrument can be designed to indicate as much as 1.5% carbon monoxide in air. The accuracy of the instrument, for low concentrations of carbon monoxide, is not affected by the presence of other combustible gases in the sample under test.

Carbon Monoxide Detector Ampoule 

The carbon monoxide detector ampoule (see Figure 8) is a safeguard for use in testing areas, before entering and while at work, where carbon monoxide is suspected. It is for use only in indicating the presence of carbon monoxide. 

It consists essentially of palladium chloride in an acetone-water (non-freezing) solution, sealed in a small tube which is surrounded with absorbent cotton. 

To use the ampoule, simply crush it be tween the thumbs and forefingers, making sure that it is pressed flat so as to secure uniform saturation of the cotton. Suspend for a minimum of ten minutes in manhole or other area where carbon monoxide is suspected. 

In the presence of carbon monoxide, the originally yellow coloring of the solution on the cotton will turn dark. The depth of discoloration, which runs from light gray to grayish black, depends upon the concentration of carbon monoxide present. The discoloration is compared with scale on the standard color chart which accompanies each package.

CONCLUSION 

With our present knowledge, based on reliable experiments, methods of resuscitation have resolved into rather systematic procedures. A great deal of attention has been given in the last few years not only to improved methods of resuscitation, but also to methods of preventing asphyxiation. The various appliances discussed in this paper are related to the general problem, and an approved inhalator in particular should be kept ready for immediate use wherever cases of asphyxiation are liable to occur from any cause. 

Manual artificial respiration, preferably the Schaefer prone-pressure method, should be employed at once when respiration stops, and this method can be simultaneously assisted by the inhalation treatment of a rich oxygen mixture plus carbon dioxide, especially in cases of carbon monoxide poisoning. 

It is evident that until physiologists have discovered more definite knowledge of the conditions in the lungs, brain, etc., following asphyxiation, this procedure is the most practical and effective.
