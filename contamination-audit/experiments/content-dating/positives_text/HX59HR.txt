When Shadows Disappear CHAPTER I 

On Battery Mountain

In the early Autumn it was, yet the mildness of late Summer had not given way to the crispness that usually accompanies that particular season, though some of the profuse foliage had begun to take on the magnificent coloring that foretells the approach of frosty nights, and warns of the coming winter. The panorama of scenes as one forestclad hill after another appeared from this vantage point to rise above the other closer at hand was beautiful to behold.

The calm peacefulness of this artistic spot was marred this morning by what seemed to be an endless procession of grim-visaged men, mountaineers, in 'most every conceivable mode of conveyance, and above the clatter of horses' hoofs and the continual rattle of wheels over the rough stony road, one could feel, rather than see, a silent tenseness and grim determination upon the tanned features of each passer-by and the natural sounds of the wild life of the hills were suspended as if in awe of the intrusion upon this quiet sacred realm. 

Farther north a few hundred yards the road dipped and wound around that side of the mountain until finally it came out far below, upon a beautiful valley or basin known as Crystal Valley.

No one knew exactly why it was called Crystal

Valley, any more than they knew why this parti cular hill was called Battery Mountain. Nobody cared.

The procession continued. As fast as the travelers would disappear around the bend in the road, others would appear from the south, all traveling in the same direction. Rough, uncouth mountaineers they were, rugged like their ancient hills, but silent all. A principle was at stake; the old, old story of right and wrong, a principle of honesty, of uprightness, of square dealing, against that of cunning outlawry. Bands of outlaws had always been active in these hills where beauty, love, and fellowship should have reigned supreme. Yet they were being suppressed or driven from the country -slowly, to be sure, but just as truly. This would be the supreme test; not, perhaps, a test of strength of numbers, but the test of honesty as a principle 

Would it pay?

Glen Randall's cabin home was located on the summit of Battery Mountain, in the heart of the

Missouri Ozarks. He had lived here all his life and knew the hills and the hill folk. This morning he was doing most any odd job around the place, where he could be near the road and keep a shrewd eye on all who passed without appearing to be doing so. He was a young man of twenty years, six feet tall, and carried, with grace, one hundred eighty pounds of bone and muscle. Some job, he reflected, picking out a man from all these people who are passing, with no other description than "he is the best rifle shot in the hill country." But Jim

Lyon had spoken just these words, and Glen knew, expected results. He wanted to know just exactly the time this man passed this way. Why the information was desired did not matter. No doubt, some plan was in the formation; a plan, perhaps, the success of which depended upon his ability to gather the information requested.

It was becoming more difficult each minute to distinguish and study each traveler. More people were coming; more disappearing. The road was crowded-men on horseback, in wagons, in buggies, some walking, all dusty and many evidently tired out from the journey they had made. But that same determined expression was upon each countenance. Some waved a greeting while others did not appear to notice him at all, but Glen knew that he was being watched; knew that the ones who apparently gave him the least attention were really looking him over closely. It was the way of the hills, to see everything and say nothing.

A team pulled out beside the road. It stopped within a dozen yards of where he stood. A quick glance: This team of fiery little bays had been driven long and hard. They were fine little animals, too; true as steel, and game, wet with sweat, sides heaving, had been driven fast and over the roughest road in the world. They started from somewhere yesterday, perhaps, or the day before, hadn't received the best of care last night, probably had been driven 'most all night and started before day this morning. A rough mountaineer was driving, forty years of age perhaps, clad in regulation mountain attire, blue shirt and overalls. He was tanned,

of course, but not a bad looking sort. Two boys were in the back seat of the rig, a two-seated hack. "Howdy, stranger". "Good morning, sir", Glen replied. "Could you tell me how fur it might be to the 

Crystal Valley Race Tracks"? the stranger continued. "Four miles, down the mountain and across the valley. From a short distance down the road and around the next curve you can see the tracks". "Live here, I reckon"? indicating by a wave of his large hand the mountain home fifty yards or so from the road.

Receiving a reply in the affirmative, he continued, 

"My name's Crawford-Zeke Crawford. What might yourn be"? "My name is Randall, and I'm very glad to know you, Mr. Crawford", as he came closer and extended his hand.

Each man was studying the other closely, very closely, and speaking slowly. "Acquainted around these parts, I reckon; seein' as how you live here". "Yes. I have lived here all my life", guardedly. "I see. 

Know Billie Adams"? the stranger asked.

Somehow, Glen thought, he detected a softer note in his voice as this question was asked, and decided that he rather liked this man, even though he was a stranger. Yet one could not be too sure. "Yes, I know Billie Adams very well", he replied.

Zeke moved his team a little farther to the side of the road; perhaps because he could hear better

farther from the rattle of wheels of passing vehicles and possibly because he did not want others to hear too much of what he had to say. Glen moved that way also, yet always glancing quickly at each passer-by. He was glad this man stopped for he could appear to be talking and at the same time give everyone the "once over". "I hear this will be his last race", Mr. Crawford continued. "Perhaps", was the reply. "It must be mighty fine to know a man like Billie

Adams and to see and talk with him on occasion. 

I've drove sixty miles jest to get to see him and maybe say 'howdy'. I live down Rock Bridge Way and don't generally get this fur from home, but I hearn he might go away after tomorrer, and I wanted to tell him how we all appreciate what he done fur us down there. Caint tell, might not have 'nother chanct. Wanted to see him anyway. Aint many like him; fact is, there aint no more like him, in these here hills, anyways".

That was a long speech, Glen thought, for a man like Zeke Crawford and he decided instinctively that he was all right. Couldn't be anything else with that soft look in his eyes and the almost beautiful expression upon his rough features when he referred to some kind act of Billie Adams. Too,

Glen decided, there was some other object for Zeke 

Crawford's having driven sixty miles to see Billie 

Adams' last race. True, folk were coming from even greater distances to be on hand for this occasion, but-well, they were not like this man; not many of them anyway.

"Reckon you know a heap more 'bout him than I do", Mr. Crawford continued, "but he done a good turn fur us that nobody won't ever fergit".

Funny, Glen meditated, about hill folk; just a sneer or a wise crack, misdirected, would often mean a gun fight, perhaps a killing; while a kind word or a friendly act would create lifelong friendship. And a friend in these parts always meant all that the word implies and many times more; true as steel and loyal. But, an enemy-that was a different story. The trouble just now was the fact that the two factions were very strong, even powerful. On the one hand was Billie Adams and a loyal following; honest, law-abiding citizens, always progressive as hill folk go; gamblers, horse-racers-true-but fair and white all the way through. On the other hand there were the outlaw gangs, headed, just now by the Altons, cunning, shrewd thieves and robbers.

A few years before our story begins Billie Adams, only a youth, and single-handed, had determined that this great beautiful country should be made a place of joy, of pleasure, as God had intended, no doubt, when he created it. To do this it took money; much money,-schools must be had, churches, too. Billie Adams' father was a gambler, but a very poor one. He almost always lost at the races. He was killed five years ago by this same band of outlaws. Billie vowed at that time (he was not yet seventeen years of age) that outlawry and killings must come to an end and be replaced by law and order.

His father at the time of his death owned a

large tract of heavily mortgaged land and a few race horses, more or less worthless. Billie took up his burden fearlessly and plied the only trade he ever knew, racing horses, not because he liked the game but more because he must have money. Folk must be educated and, too, they must attend church. 

There must be churches and schoolhouses. Billie

Adams shall build them. He did not attend church himself, not yet. He was not a hypocrite, and he did not let it be generally known that he was particularly interested in this endeavor but his donations to such enterprises were always many, many times larger than was expected of him. 

His fame grew. If there was a schoolhouse or a church building being erected anywhere within a radius of fifty or seventy miles, he knew of it and his donations were heavy. His generosity was never imposed upon and he did not advertise. If one learned anything about him, the information came from someone else, to be sure, not from him. 

Day by day folk began to understand. Men came to him for advice on almost all subjects. They were following in his footsteps but did not realize the fact. Neither did he. Many attempts had been made upon his life but always he had avoided them with honor.

Zeke Crawford was speaking again in his slow drawl, "Guess I better be gittin' along but 'fore I start, I want to show you somethin' ". He reached down and from under some grain sacks, partially filled with feed, and drew out a shining rifle of a very late model. "A gift from a friend", he said, "that went to Oklahoma a few years back and

struck it rich in oil. Knowin' as how I was a tarnal fool about fire arms, he sent me this", indicating the rifle by a nod.

Glen had almost decided before that this was the man he was looking for. Those quick-shifting eyes and sudden movements, even though accompanied by a slow drawling speech, almost told the story. "I think you are a young man that can be trusted," he continued. Anyways I can see that you are a friend of my friend and that's enough. If you will throw a small rock in the air-yes, that's about the right size"-as Glen picked up a small stone about the size of an ordinary marble. 

He was holding the rifle across his knees when he instructed Glen to toss the stone. A sharp report and the stone was burst into many pieces. 

A smile hovered about his lips as he gently laid the rifle back in its place. "Reckon I'll see ye tomorrer", he said, as he spoke kindly to his team and drove away. 

Randall watched after him until he had turned the bend in the road and dipped down the mountainside out of sight. "Fine little animals he was driving", he thought, "fiery and game; nothing like a good horse".

Glen turned toward the house, as he was not interested further in the people who were passing by. 

He must get the message to Jim Lyon that his man would be over there within an hour. Jim was a loyal soul who owed not only his life to Billie Adams but everything else which he possessed. He wondered just what Jim's plans were, just how the

stage was being set, and why he wanted to know exactly when to expect Zeke Crawford. 

His telephone was connected by direct line to the paddock office where he expected Jim was waiting for him to call. "Hello", a deep voice answered in reply to his ring. Glen knew that voice as well as he knew his own. Without further remarks he said, "Two golden queens at ten forty-five”. "Fine", was the reply over the wire and both parties hung up. 

Mr. Randall, Glen's father, was an invalid.

His lower limbs were useless and his condition would not respond to the best medical attention that could be had. However, he could propel himself around in a wheel chair after he had been helped into it. As Glen turned from the telephone he noticed that his father was sitting in his chair near the north window with field glasses in his hand. 

From his position he had a very good view of the race tracks, except the east end where the side of the mountain cut off his view. With his field glasses he could see the start and finish but could not see the third quarter of a race. "That message, my boy, sounded a little like old times, sorter mysterious like", he said. "Can't tell who might have tied a 'phone to the line, Dad. Guess it wouldn't make any difference but Jim understands anyway. How you feeling, dad"? "Oh, fine, fine. Maybe I can walk again some day."

Glen knew that his father was only trying to be

cheerful for they both knew his condition was hopeless; spinal trouble of some kind. "School will begin right away, dad; then we will get along all right. It has been quite a pull and I'm sorry I haven't been able to give you the attention and comforts you should have had and I don't know what we would have done if Billie hadn't made it possible for me to attend high school last year”. "Of course, I'm mighty proud, Glen, that you have enough education to teach school and mighty thankful to Billie, too, but don't forget, my boy, that you yourself deserve some credit. It almost drove me crazy with pride seeing you come home every night to be with dad and mother. Eight miles night and morning through the rain, snow, and sleet all winter long will test a boy's grit. And speaking about getting along, we've been all right. 

Mother and me have had everything we needed and we're right proud of our boy".

Glen thought he saw a semblance of a tear on his father's pain-drawn face and thought it best to change the subject. Hill folk, even though they have big, warm hearts, do not generally show affection. "Can you see very plainly with your field glasses this morning"? he asked. "Jist as plain as if I was there, but I aint seen 

Billie about yet. But such crowds! Glen, look", as he handed Glen the glasses. "And the race", he continued, "not till tomorrow. 

What do you think, my boy, about the people you saw going over there"?

Glen knew what he meant by the question and answered promptly. "I would say seventy-five percent with us. Not so bad, do you think"? 

Mr. Randall pondered for a moment. "Not so bad, no; but not so good. Wolves travel by night".

Glen handed back the glasses without further comment. "But Billie must win, Glen, he just must. He is too white to lose to them skunks". There was deep passion in the old gentlman's voice. "Now, dad, don't get excited. sible, that's fair, will be done".

Everything pos- "But you know Billie will not resort to unfair methods even to win and that Kentucky horse is mighty fast and hard to beat under the best circumstances. Of course, we know that all kinds of crookedness will be resorted to by that Braxton and Alton gang. I believe though", and he spoke very slowly and with much feeling in his voice, "that honest methods will win".

The road down Battery Mountain was rough and winding. "How like life", meditated Zeke Crawford as he bumped along over rocks and ledges and swung around sharp curves. "Part of the time you will sail along nice and easy", he muttered to his team, "while at other times the goin' is mighty hard and it seems almost impossible to get anywhere.

Yet the more difficult it is now, perhaps the smoother it will be later".

As if to prove the logic of his reasoning, he suddenly came out upon a wide valley or basin which he knew to be Crystal Valley. Far across the slightly rolling but practically level valley he could distinguish the race tracks with their white paddocks and stables. He had heard a lot about the vast tract of land owned by Billie Adams and surely the story had not been exaggerated, judging from what lay before him. He judged it to be at least two miles across the valley, going north, and perhaps twice that distance east and west. 

It ran east to the river, but was flanked on the other three sides by the forest-clad mountains. Nearly all, he noticed, of this vast valley was in a perfect state of cultivation and there were tenant cottages here and there which were well kept and of a very neat appearance.

It was perhaps a mile from the tracks that he noticed a young man on horseback galloping carelessly toward him. The young man glanced in his direction as he passed and galloped on. Half a mile farther there was another young man, dressed in overalls, standing by an open gate, directing the travelers through that way to a camp ground. "This way folks", he was heard to say, "drive in and make yourselves as comfortable as possible.

Place to camp, plenty of water and feed for your horses. You are our guests and we want you to feel at home while you are here. No charge", he said, as someone asked him about that matter, "ride right in. After you have camped, go all over the place if you like. You are welcome here". "Hospitality to the limit", Crawford thought.

Many were taking advantage of the privilege and riding or driving in while a few others continued on their way toward the tracks and paddock. 

As Zeke Crawford was about to turn in at this gate, the same young man whom he had met a few minutes before, galloped up from the rear, caught his eye and with a slight, almost imperceptible, gesture, indicated that he was to continue along the main traveled road which led around the east side of the race course where it turned north, and almost zigzagged around the stables and passed within a few hundred feet of the paddock. Tracks, stables, and paddock, he noticed, were at the extreme north side of the valley. In fact, all the buildings were upon a natural elevation which rose gradually until within another mile or less the hills appeared which flanked the beautiful valley on the north.

The young man, having delivered his silent instructions, galloped carelessly back toward the stables.

The information had been spread that Billie Adams would give The Chief a light workout at this hour. Zeke Crawford had arrived just before noon and had become intimately acquainted with Jim Lyon since that time. Though the two had never met before, it just seemed natural that they should become great friends. Jim had also taken a great interest in Crawford's two boys who were with him and had taken time to show them many interesting things. They went back to the paddock and Jim left them for a while, after locating them where they would miss nothing of the proceedings. The crowd was milling around, all expecting and anticipating the privilege of being able to see Billie and their favorite in action. While this was the first visit of many of the spectators, they had heard much and, while many had seen a number of races here and had seen The Chief perform gallantly before, they were as thoroughly interested as the folk who had come a hundred miles or more for the occasion. 

There was a sudden stir as people were being asked to stand back. "No use taking a chance of an injury when it is not necessary", the young man was saying who had met Zeke Crawford out in the valley and had directed him to where Jim Lyon was waiting. Bobby 

Land was his name. He was Billie's jockey, when 

Billie did not fill that important position himself. 

However Land had never attempted to "ride" The 

Chief, but with the other horses he was a past master.

A door to the paddock was suddenly opened and two men stepped out quickly, each holding a rein, and, as if a cyclone had been suddenly turned loose, there dashed from the stall within a huge, dark bay stallion, eyes flashing fire, rearing and plunging, a veritable dynamo of strength and power. The men attempting to hold him were, many times, lifted clear of the ground, but doggedly hung onto the heavy leather reins. The Chief was going into action.

A wild cheer arose as a door to the paddock office opened and a figure clad in a clean white jockey suit came quickly to the spot where The 

Chief was being detained, greatly against his will; a young man, almost boyish in appearance, slim and tall, clean through and through-Billie Adams. He acknowledged the cheers with a brilliant smile, poised gracefully for an instant and with one graceful bound landed in the saddle of the plunging horse. 

They dashed down the incline, through a gate and were on the track almost instantly. At the starting point The Chief was held up, but reared and plunged so viciously that it was necessary to bring him around sharply. Finally he was made to hesitate momentarily at position. The next second they were off with a burst of speed that was seldom seen on any race course. 

It was thrilling, the power, speed, and energy thus displayed. At the first quarter Billie tightened the reins, leaned far over on the big animal's neck and spoke encouragingly to him. As if in response, the speed demon seemed to settle a little closer to the ground and Billie knew that between the first

It is impossible for one who has not had the experience to understand just what this mystic contact, between horse and rider, means, but those who have had the experience will tell you that without it there is seldom a winner. 

At the three-quarter post, Billie held him up and at the mile, the speed had slackened but was still terrific. It was a mile and a quarter track and on the home stretch The Chief was fighting for the opportunity, evidently, to make a record. The cheering crowd had made its impression. The great animal had become serious and had decided fully that there must be an important occasion at hand. 

It is a known fact that a race horse actually understands, when he is entered for an important race, about when the race will be and something of the effort he will have to make to win. These things are made known to him in different ways, his grooming, the seriousness of his master, and perhaps his steady training, and the gathering crowds. Another great reason why a horse understands these things is because his intelligence tells him. He knows, and it is not intuition either. 

As they flashed under the wire this loyal of loyal animals was trying to say to his master, "I am ready and will win for you or die in the effort". Billie knew the peculiarities of this horse and was con-

vinced that this condition existed. A thoroughbred will do just that.

He was finally checked and returned, amid the wild cheering, to the paddock. Billie and Jim Lyon went into the stall where attendants were giving him a "rub-down". The big fellow was quiet now and his sides were heaving from the strenuous exercise. Too much so, Billie thought. He should have made that mile and a quarter without much more than a long breath. Jim was thinking the same, but neither expressed his views. "Be here a while"? Jim asked. "No, Jim", Billie replied. It was the first words he had spoken since arriving at the track. "Think I'll go back to the house. Have some work to do and I can work better where it is quiet". 

His voice was soft and pleasant.

Jim knew what the work was that Billie thought had to be done. He also knew that Billie always avoided crowds as much as possible. Spectators seldom saw him except during a race. He abhorred "grandstand plays".

He went across the stall to where The Chief was standing, very quietly now, and breathing easier. 

The big horse rubbed his nose against Billie's breast affectionately as Billie stroked his forehead.

The attendants were still busy with their grooming.

While the Cannons Roared

When it became generally known that Billie was serious in his statement that he would enlist in the United States Army, the young men of the hill country hastily did likewise. More than two hundred of the stalwart, robust, loyal sons of the hills offered their services the same day, eager to step out and show the world just the kind of metal they were made of. Included in the number were Glen 

Randall, Bobby Land, the Murphy boys, the Crawfords, the Davis boys, the Denney's, and Leonard 

Miller, and many, many others. 

They were detained only a short period of time at the training camps, until they were designated for early duty abroad with the Third Division and directed to sail in November. They embarked for 

Europe in December 1917. In the critical days of 

March 1918 they were fighting shoulder to shoulder with the British and bringing honor and recognition to the Division with which they were destined to fight through the war.

In May the Division was placed at the disposal VI Army, which turned it over to the 38th Army Corps (French) operating in the Chateau-Thierry region.

The movement of the Division into this sector was commenced by the motorized machine-gun Battalion when it left its station on the afternoon of 172

May 30 for Conde-en-Brie. The movement of the 

Infantry Brigades, which was by train, began in the night of May 30 and was completed June 3, when the last unit arrived in the new area, having marched from its detraining point at Provins, many kilometers to the south. The divisional trains made the journey over land, arriving at their destination in good time.

During the months following there upon that blood-stained field of battle our boys became seasoned veterans and with a grim determination known only to themselves and their comrades, they covered themselves with an undying glory.

The wisecracks and light, good-natured attitude had long since disappered. The "Good-bye Broadway, hello France" was heard no more. Instead, the din of battle, the ear-splitting, bursting of shells, the groans of wounded and dying companions was on every hand. 

Let it be said, too, that no braver soldiers, no troops more loyal were to be found than those

French and British lads. True, perhaps, they were discouraged from their long years of fighting and America's entrance into the war came at an opportune time, but those great armies were not ready to quit as some misinformed reporters had stated. 

Our moral support, however, was far reaching in its effect, as well as our physical support. 

Billie Adams and Glen Randall had been made

Lieutenants and their bravery had been recognized on different occasions by their superior officers. 

It was in October our infantry received orders to attack and clean up the Bois des Rappes and the 7th

Billie and his brave boys set out upon this deadly mission. Grim-faced and detremined, they knew that many of their number would not return. They advanced with glistening bayonets and hearts of steel. Slowly, stubbornly, they gained ground in the very face of a raging hell. Men were falling everywhere. They fell back slightly and dug in, holding most of their gain. The 7th Infantry organized a counter-attack and for three days they endured what seemed impossible for any human flesh to stand and survive. 

Attack after attack was made against those stubborn enemy lines, with only slight gains and with huge casualties. Shrapnel, machine-gun fire and bayonets thinned their ranks, but they pressed on gallantly.

They alighted from the car at the place near the top of Paradise Mountain known to him as the

Lookout and almost directly under the huge tower-ing cliff which had held over his consciousness throughout the many years memories of a sin which he would have given his very life and soul to have erased.

Just a few hours ago they had watched from here the sunset glories and had felt the reuniting powers which had subtly intertwined their lives and hearts to such an extent that there could not possibly be the faintest resemblance to a dissenting cord. 

He did not look toward the cliff as he gently led her to the little ledge where he had known his greatest happiness and his most profound sorrow.

Seated there they listened to the quaint sounds of the night magnified by the shadowy things and the darkness. 

Far below them there was the faint call of a whippoor-will. There was the chatter down in the valley below of a tractor and its long shaft of light playing across the fertile field as the farm's night shifts drove their work furiously. The sound would die away faintly in the distance only to be borne to them again presently upon the wings of a gentle spring breeze. The night was pleasantly warm and the palest of pale moons rode high in the heavens. 

She wound her arms about his neck and shoulders as he kissed her upturned lips and clasped her in his arms, crushing her to his bosom. As he released her, she began her story.

"Perhaps", she said softly, "the happiness of this hour will be an inspiration to me through an otherwise sad future and if I am a disappointment to you, dear, I hope and pray your forgiveness for I have had very little voice in fashioning my own life. Yet 

I would not have planned it any other way had it been in my power to have done so".

She was speaking slowly, meditatingly in that cultured tone he knew so well. 

He pillowed her head against his breast as she continued. "To begin at the very beginning, my name is Virginia Braxton. I was born a few weeks over nineteen years ago, March 27, 1913". 

He calmly interrupted her. "Please repeat the date of your birth", he requested. "March 27, 1913", she repeated.

To him that date was written high in the burnished heavens by a giant hand and stamped with brands of fire upon his heart and soul. It was the day of her death, and the date of her birth. The drama was unfolding before his eyes. The very day she had been taken away from him in sorrow, plans were in the making to return her to him in joy and happiness by all-powerful Creator. "The part of the story", she continued, "which

Seven or eight months later this daughter of hers was born. It wasn't a story she was telling him, he reflected, it was a revelation. 

The girl in his arms was speaking again. "Mother died when I was only a few days old and

I was reared by the kindest, most loving father in the world. His was a devoted life since that event here in these sacred hills. Your influence, Billie, though perhaps you did not know, changed his entire career. He loved and cherished your memory more than anything else, perhaps, except possibly my dear mother, and myself. To him you were an outstanding character, bordering upon the Divine". 

For a moment she was silent, while the subdued calls of the whip-poor-wills came faintly to them.

After a little while, she continued, and her words came soft and low, "The first words I learned to speak were 'Billie Adams', taught me by my father. 

When I was old enough to comprehend, father told me in glowing terms of you. I loved you when I was only a little child and that love became greater through the years. I liked to imagine I was here with you, and in later years I could feel that we were companions. Father told me of your great, almost unbearable, sorrow and I have been convinced that in recent years I have been able, through my silent understanding and love for you, to help you bear that sad burden. I have tried so hard to do so".

For a moment she again hesitated. Then continued with the most amazing revelation he had ever heard. "The old gentleman", she said, "whom you knew as Uncle Dick, was my grandfather".

She then related the sad story of the old patriarch as her father had told it to her. 

Continuing, she said, "When you were in France, grandfather came to visit us in Kentucky. I was only a child, but I remember him well. He would never repeat that visit. Something, I do not know what it was, kept him here. When I was fifteen, he sent us a photograph of you. He also kept father informed of your every activity, and the condition of your health. When your dear mother died, we were deeply grieved. I wanted to come to you to try to comfort you, but father said it could not be, then".

She reflected for a while before continuing. Her voice was low and she was speaking slowly. "About a year and six months ago, father's health began to fail. He thought little of it at first, but as the months dragged along and he did not improve, I became alarmed. There was nothing, however, that could improve his condition".

Her voice broke and she sobbed softly as memories crowded upon her. 

Billie had been silently holding her in his arms throughout her strange narrative. He spoke gently to her now. "Please do not tell me any more, now, Virginia. 

You are tired and it makes no difference anyway. 

I have you and that's all that matters".

"I would rather finish", she said, "if you don't mind". 

She continued, "One day father called me into his room and asked the nurse to retire. He went into detail about his financial affairs, his investments and things I did not understand. His business ventures had proved very successful and he had converted most everything into bonds except our home, as it seemed he realized he would not get well again. He was so weak he could scarcely speak, but he insisted upon my knowing all his affairs. Finally he was through, and I started to get the nurse, as I knew he needed her skilful attention. He detained me again and told me more about you, Billie. He asked me to promise him that one year from his death I would come to you. 

Of course I hesitated because I did not know what to do. He said you would be waiting and expecting me. At last I said I would grant his request.

I did not know what else to do. It is so hard to refuse a request of a loved one who is dying. 

Though I love you, Billie, and had loved you all my life. I did not want to intrude. He kept saying the way would be made easy, but I did not know just what he meant. A year ago today, he died".

She was crying softly. Billie consoled her with fervent whispers of love and endearment.

At length she continued, "I have been so lonesome during this last year. Yet I kept up my college work and recently received my degree. Grandfather, Uncle Dick as you knew him, communicated with me often. He wrote me of the great work

She looked up into his face and smiled that halfsad smile. "That is all of the story, darling. You may make the decision now".

They were sitting upon the miniature ledge facing Crystal Valley, with the great towering cliff behind them.

For an answer he stood up and gently lifted her to her feet. Confidently he faced the cliff upon which the threatening shadow had hung through the years. He directed her attention there. The cliff was as brilliantly white as the driven snow.

"My answer", he said. "The shadow has disappeared".

It was true. The shadow was there no more. 

The shadow of doubt, the shadow of uncertainty, the shadow of shame, the shadow of sin, had all vanished, and their hearts, minds, and souls were as clean and pure as that of a new born babe. 

He took her dainty form in his arms and kissed her fondly. "Your life", he murmured, "has been fashioned by the Supreme Power of a just God and in His own natural way". 

She smiled her happiness as the night sounds were suspended and hushed as if in awe of the joyful scene. "Yet there is one thing", he said soberly, "about which I had given no thought". He looked back over the years and meditated upon the events of bygone days. 

To him the beautiful girl by his side was his same darling whom he had loved years ago and whose memories he had worshipped loyally. The same soul, perhaps, in a new physical body, just as lovely, just as sweet as ever before. 

He remembered the night before he had left for the capital, long, long ago. She had asked him to tell her again that he loved her.

He realized now how true was that prophecy. 

Her restoration to him he knew was the act of an all-merciful Creator. Through death that immortal soul had been cleansed from sin, a sin for which he assumed full responsibility. To him, unworthy as he was, she had been restored in all her regal beauty and just as she had been in the days of his youth. Her face, her eyes, her hair, her form, her voice, even her smile, were the same.

She was so peacefully quiet nestling there against his breast that he thought she had fallen asleep. 

He spoke gently to her. "No, dear", she said. "I am not asleep. I was just wondering if our people here will love me". "Certainly they will love you, Virginia. They love you now with all their hearts". "Oh, I'm so happy, Billie"!

Down in the valley below them the chatter of the tractors had been silenced, their sweeping lights extinguished. The men, no doubt, were on their way to their cottages and to bed and to rest. Dim lights from a cottage window could yet be seen down there.

The blissful silence of the romantic hills had settled about them, and with it, life, joy, happiness supreme.

A gentle spring breeze sighed softly, whisperingly, through the stately pines and giant oaks, adding a touch of color to the blissful happiness of the scene.

He closed the Bible and in a deep voice continued, "The tomb was empty. To mortal man the greatest of all miracles had been performed. The 

Christ, who had suffered untold agonies and died upon the cross that we might inherit everlasting life, had arisen from the tomb. To the ordinary intellect, mysteries bordering upon the miraculous are to the great Living God only commonplace occurrences. The sun rose upon three women who were frightened by what they saw. It rose also upon a world that should never be the same again. 

The light had overcome darkness as easily as the sun had conquered night. Life had put death to rout. Eternity had proven its mastery over time. 

Why, then, should those three have been afraid?

Only because they had not yet learned to trust Life's

unconquerable power. They still believed that darkness was able to put out the light. They were still living under the tyranny of time and the oppression of space. We, too, are standing at sunrise before a sepulcher. For sunrise and sepulcher are always meeting, facing each other, always at the dawning of each day. Angels are always there to tell us of a resurrection. We, too, find the heavy tombstone rolled away. 

Shall we, then, also, be afraid? 

Afraid of what? Of the light? Of the messenger? Of the fact? Not so, surely, for we have recaptured the Radiance! Not in doubt, not in fear, but in faith and hope and love, we shall turn from the sepulcher to the sunrise with a song of triumph and a prayer, amazed not at God's manifestation of power today".

The short wedding ceremony was soon over, and, as he pronounced them husband and wife, Virginia looked at her husband with those large brown eyes, and a smile from which all traces of sadness had vanished. "My husband"! she whispered. "My angel from Heaven", he murmured as he folded her to his breast.
