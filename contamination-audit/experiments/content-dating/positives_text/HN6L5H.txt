THE BOGGY ROAD TO SAN JACINTO By Captain Hobart Huson, S-1, 21st Bn. TSG 

Colonel Travis' last appeal for help reached Washington-on-the-Brazos at breakfast-time, March 6, 1836.

The Alamo had fallen that morning, but the delegates at Washington had no means of knowing it. Within a few hours after receipt of Travis' message, General Sam Houston and his adjutant, Colonel George W. Hookley, set out for Gonzales where what was left of the Texian regular army was encamped. The commander-in-chief arrived at Gonzales at about 4 o'clock in the afternoon of the 11th. His first inquiry was for word of the garrison at the Alamo. Tho 

Gonzales was but 80 miles from Bexar it was still ignorant of the Alamo's fate. A Mexican had entered town, claiming he had left the Alamo the morning of the 6th and that Travis' command was exterminated. The story was thought probable as Travis' signal guns had not been heard since the 6th.

Houston sent out a scouting party in which were Captains Henry Wax Karnes, Erastus (Deaf) Smith and Robert Eden

Handy, to approach as close as possible to Bexar and learn the facts. Until definite information could be obtained, Houston waited at Gonzales. On March 11, he ordered Fannin to abandon Goliad and fall back to the Colorado. On the 12th he ordered Captain Dimmit to bring his company to Gonzales. 

Houston found that the army which he had come to command consisted of only 374 men under Captain Mosely Baker. With the fortitude which characterized his course during the sub-

sequent campaign, the general immediately proceeded to mold these undisciplined troops into a military organization.

A regiment was formed with General Edward Burleson as its colonel. It was designated the 

First Regiment of Texas Volunteers. A month and ten days later, at San Jacinto, Houston commanded an army of 1350 organized and disciplined men. This remarkable feat was achieved while on a long and arduous retreat. 

Definite news of the fate of the Alamo came on the 13th when Houston's scouts encountered the widow of Lieutenant Dickinson and some servants about 20 miles from Gonzales. Santa Anna had allowed them to depart from the Alamo. 

Mrs. Dickinson further advised that a Mexican army of about 

750 men was then on its way to Gonzales; that she had passed it at the Cibolo. Karnes' party sent the refugees in to Gonzales, where their arrival caused a panic. The scouts continued towards Bexar to reconnoiter the enemy. 

Houston acted with promptitude upon receipt of the information.

With the superior Mexican army but two days march away he resolved to keep a safe distance between it and his own. 

Orders were given at 9 P. M. to prepare for an immediate retreat. 

The artillery was sunk into the Guadalupe, and some of the military stores were destroyed, so as not to impede a rapid march. 

Before midnight of the 13th the little Texian army, now numbering slightly over 400, evacuated Gonzales and headed for the Colorado. Early the next morning all caches of ammunition which had been left were blown up, and the town itself was burned.

The Texian army marched throughout the night of the 13th, finally halting for rest at daylight on the 14th at 

McClure's, on Peach Creek, about 11 miles east of Gonzales.

The exhausted men threw themselves on the ground and slept on their arms. Here the army was reinforced by a local company. After good sunrise the army resumed its march. and his adjutant surveyed it as it moved out. The columm seemed to them to be but "a speck on the vast prairie". 

Turning to his adjutant Houston remarked, "Hockley, There is the last hope of Texas. 13 Houston

Sunset found the army at Daniel's, on the Lavaca, where it halted for the night. The army was up before the sun and on its way. A halt was made at Rocky Creek. The Navidad was reached about one o'clock on the afternoon of the 15th. The tired men rested until next morning. At this point reinforcements from the Brazos arrived. Houston sent to Brazoria for artillery and munitions, and ordered all troops in that area to join the main army on the Colorado. 

The Navidad was reached at one o'clock on the 15th. So far the Texians had followed the principal road from Bexar to 

San Felipe which crossed the Colorado at Beason's. 

The army stayed overnight at the Navidad, resuming its march the morning of the 16th. After leaving the Navidad, Houston ordered the column to turn off to the left on the little used old La Bahia road, which led to Burnham's, on the Colorado, near present La Grange. Moseley Baker, afterwards denounced his general for this movement: "You crossed the Colorado fifteen

miles above the great crossing at Beason's; taking a solitary and unusual route, one certainly which the enemy would not think of taking, and which they did not take. " 

The army reached the west side of the Colorado opposite 

Burnham's at 4:30 o'clock the afternoon of the 17th. 

It now numbered 600.

The rear guard came up with refugee families on the 18th, and the families were immediately crossed to the other side. 

General Houston about this time received information that Santa 

Anna had gone back to Mexico. No information to the contrary was received until the night of April 11th.

The army remained on the west side of the Colorado until the 20th, when it crossed. The buildings at Burnham's were burned to the ground, while their owners who, were in the Texian army, looked on. Moseley Baker and others of Houston's enemies excoriate him for the burning of Gonzales, Burnham's, Beason's,

San Felipe and other places along the retreat. It should be noted that places burned were at or near crossings of the principal rivers. All buildings in such places were destroyed by fire to prevent the enemy using them for crossing the flooded rivers or for shelter from the biting wintry weather. 

After crossing at Burnham's, the Texian marched down the east side of the Colorado to a point opposite Beason's, where they went into camp. The buildings at Beason's were burned. 

Beason's was the central of three river crossings in that

vicinity by either or all of which the Mexican army could pass to San Felipe. The Deweese crossing was located about three miles above and the Atascosita crossing about four miles below. In mid-February, 1836, somewhat over 6500 Mexican troops had entered Texas in two separate divisions. under General Jose Urrea, consisting of 1500 men, crossed the Rio Grande at Matamoras, and took up position at San Patricio, on the Nueces, after exterminating Johnson and Grant's small commands. The other, under Santa Anna, consisting of 5000 or more troops, crossed the Rio Bravo at or in the vicinity of Laredo and marched directly to Bexar. 

Santa Anna states that he considered it necessary to reduce the Alamo, defended by 183 Texians, before his armies advanced further into Texas. In the reduction of that place the Mexican army sustained about 500 casualties, dead and wounded, The various reinforcements received by Santa Anna while at Bexar, probably made up the losses suffered at the Alamo. Hence, for the purpose of this dissertation Santa 

Anna's strength at Bexar, after the fall of the Alamo, will be placed at about 5200.

The extermination of the Alamo garrison was the signal

for a general advance of the Mexican Armies. Santa Anna proposed to strike at the right (Urrea's) by way of Refugio, Copano, Goliad, Victoria, and Matagorda, to Brazoria; the left, General Antonio Gaona with 750 men, advancing via Bastrop to Nacogdoches; and the central column, under the jefe del estado himself, through Gonzales to San Felipe, on the west bank of the Brazos River. Santa Anna originally intended to establish his headquarters at San Felipe. 

A map of colonial Texas will disclose that San Felipe was ideally located for such purpose. The river on which she stood was navigible to the gulf, and troops and supplies could be transported up and down the river. Almost every principal land route in South Texas passed through Austin's capital. It is interesting to note that the road leading from San Felipe to Liberty was the top of a pocket formed by the rising are of the gulf coast and the deep inland-recessed Galveston and Trinity Bays and the Brazos River. A superior force operating above the top of the pocket was in a position to pull the zipper shut on an inferior force caught operating within the pocket. The force operating on the outside of the top had its way of withdrawal secure. It will be seen that Houston constantly manoeuvered for position so as to be at all times on the outside of the top of the pocket. Both Santa Anna and Houston regarded 

San Felipe as being of strategic importance. 

The general movement of the Mexican armies began on March 11. On that date Urrea moved from San Patricio towards Refugio, General Antonio Gaona began his march for Bastrop, and Generals

Sesma and Woll with 725 men left Bexar for Gonzales.

About the same time Colonel Juan Morales with 500 men was sent to reinforce Urrea at Goliad, as Santa Anna feared the governor would have to attack Fannin in his fortress which bristled with artillery. After Urrea's attack on Refugio Mission, Santa Anna sent him a further reinforcement of two or more battalions under Colonel Cayetano Montoya. This reinforcement reached Goliad the eve of the Battle of Coleto. The Sesma's central column reached Gonzales the night of the 16th. Captain Dimmitt and his company, in ignorance of Houston's retreat, crashed headlong into the advancing Mexicans.

Texians were roughly handled and lost many of their horses, but Dimmitt skillfully avoided a disaster. Sesma informed Santa 

Anna that Houston's strength was thought to be 1300. Santa Anna, on or about March 17, therefore, dispatched General Eugenio Tolsa with two battalions of infantry and 40 dragoons, about 675 men, to reinforce Sesma, who was continuing his slow, cautious march to the Colorado. Sesma's strength was raised to about 1400, when the two units joined on the 24th.

The Mexican President remained at Bexar during these preliminary operations. He was undecided whether to remain in 

Texas or go back to Mexico. decided him to leave Texas.

Henry W. Karnes rank with the greatest of American scouts. 

Many of the colonists had a knowledge of the Spanish language. 

In the patriot army were numerous Texas-Mexicans, including Captain Juan N. Seguin, a brilliant and able man. Thus the 

Texian commander was able to make the most of captured Mexican soldiers and dispatches. They

Throughout the campaign Texian scouts scoured the territory between the two armies and fanned out around the flanks. were as indeftigable as they were capable. The success of the campaign was largely, if not entirely, attributable to the competent military intelligence of the Texian army. Most of the 

Mexican officers, in after years, commented upon the efficiency of the Texian scouts and spies, who, they said, "seemed to have been everywhere." 

On March 20th Karnes scouts contacted a reconnoitering party of Sesma's column at Rocky Creek, about 20 miles from Beason's. They brought in a prisoner who gave valuable information. Sesma, however, acted with discretion. He was informed that the Texian army was probably larger than his own unit, and he loitered in the vicinity of Rocky Creek until Tolsa could come up. He, however, sent out parties to examine the adjacent crossings. Texian scouts reported his proximity and that he might be expected to appear at the Colorado the night of the 21st. A detachment of 100 Texians crossed the river to hinder his advance, but he did not come. On the night of the 22nd Sesma

camped within three miles of the river. On the 23rd he appeared at the river in force. Tolsa joined him on the 24th. 

It appeared to be Houston's policy to force the enemy to take the lower routes, while he himself took the upper ones. The policy was furthered by the rainy weather, which converted the prairies into quagmires and brought watercourses up to flood-stage. The display of military strength at places already difficult because of natural phenomona seemed sufficient to divert the enemy movement to other places. On the 22nd a detachment of 200 was sent to Deweese crossing, above Beason's, with orders to defend the passage at all hazards. A smaller detachment was sent to the Atascosita, lower down. Finding the passage at Beason's barred by the main Texian force, Sesma sent a detachment to Deweese's to seize that crossing. The attempt was frustrated and three Mexican soldiers were captured by the defenders and sent to headquarters. One prisoner reported that Santa Anna had left for Mexico. Texian scouts about the same time captured two enemy scouts (March 22). Sesma then probed the Atascosita crossing but found it, too, defended. 

Captain Teal and some regular troops joined Houston's army at Beason's, bringing the strength up to about 700. On the 23rd an express arrived with the first news of Fannin's disaster. He reported that Fannin had been surrounded on the prairie and that the battle was continuing into the night when the express+ man took off. Houston had a premonition that was the last of Fannin. "He is an ill-fated man", he wrote.

When the Texian army learned the news of Fannin, there was great excitement. Many demanded to be led against Sesma. 

Houston then had about as many men as the Mexican, and seriously considered acceding to the importunities. 

Reports that

Tolsa was within supporting distance of Sesma ended the thought of attack. Hot-headed soldiers, however, refused to perceive the danger of the course suggested by them and berated their general when he declined to fight. Houston felt depressed. 

He wrote Colonel Rusk, "I have found the darkest hours of my past life! My excitement has been so great, that for fortyeight hours, I have not eaten an ounce, nor have I slept."

On the 25th Peter Kerr arrived with the first news of Fannin's surrender. Houston, fearing that disemination of such a report would affect the morale of the troops, had Kerr arrested as a spy and spreader of false-alarms, and sent him away under guard. 

The contending armies faced each other on the 24th, 25th and 26th. On the afternoon of the 26th Houston abruptly gave orders that camp should be broken at sunset. The soldiers were to light their evening fires at the usual hour to conceal the movement. The army protested but obeyed. At dusk the troops quietly withdrew from the camp, leaving their fires burning. A march was made to a lake in the prairie, a distance of about six miles. There the night was spent, bivouacing without fires, and the "army grumbled itself to sleep." Here the commands of Turner, Fisher and Roman arrived bringing the strength to about 1400.

Leaving small detachments to watch the crossings and make demonstrations, the retreat was resumed at daylight of 

March 27th. Marquis James in his classic The Raven, states: 

The first light of morning saw the column pressing on, Staff officers rode up and down, Close up, men! Close up!'

Major Ben Fort Smith of the staff asked Captain Mosely Baker what he thought of the movement. Captain Baker replied in a loud voice. He thought little enough of the movement, and unless reasons for the retreat acceptable to the Army were forthcoming, Sam Houston would be deposed from command before the day was over. The march was so relentlessly pressed that 

Captain Baker did not find an opportunity to carry out his plan. That night with thirty weary miles behind them, the men were too tired to care. They had covered the whole distance between the Colorado and the Rio de los Brazos de Dios, and were bivouaced a mile from San Felipe de Austin."

The army sulkily moved off, but Baker and Martin's companies stood fast, Houston seeing this situation, then ordered Baker to defend the crossing at San Felipe with his company, and Martin to take his company down the river to Old Fort to defend the crossings there. The two insubordinate captains consented to obey this order and departed for their assigned posts on the east side of the Brazos. 

The weather was bitter. There had been a drizzle for hours, and the ground was soggy and slick. Late in the afternoon the army began crossing Mill Creek, at a point about three miles above Cummins' mill. As it did so the leaden skies opened and a veritable cloudburst fell on the soldiers who were struggling to get the wagons and teams through the boggy creek bottom. The officers, including the general himself, labored along with the men in pushing the baggage wagons through the slough of mud, By sunset of the 28th the crossing was over and the army made a camp in the timber. The rain was still pouring down, but the men gathered piles of logs and soon had blazing fires going, by which they thawed themselves and dried their soaked clothing.

The country into which the Texians was penetrating was uninhabited. There were no roads of any kind above Mill Creek, and the 29th was spent in hacking a way through the timber thicket so that the wagons could get through. Only three miles

were gone that day. On the 30th the army marched eight miles, camping at Bracey's, near the edge of the Brazos bottom. James says, "On March 31, 1836, he (Houston) halted in a 'bottom' by the Brazos with nine hundred demoralized and mutious men remaining of the thirteen hundred he had led from the Colorado five days before." The entire distance traversed was only 18 miles, but three "terrible days" had been required to make it. The campsite was near Jared Groce's Ferry, on the Brazos. Here the army remained miserable and grumbling the night of the 31st. Houston received reports that Sesma's column was beginning to cross at the Atascosita, and that San Felipe had been burned, Scouting parties were sent out to watch the movements of the enemy.

The next day, April 1, the general selected a spot in the bottom for a permanent camp. It was an elevated place in a thick growth of timber around a clear-water lake or pond--just off the road to Groce's Ferry. In order to get to the site a trail had to be hacked through the timber and spaces had to be cleared for camp spots. Captain Baker later accused the general, "You pursued your route up the Brazos and finally encamped amid some lakes at a spot to which no roads ran. Will you tell me why you went there?"

The army moved to the new camp on the 1st. Thereafter the flooded Brazos rose so high in its banks that the camp site was converted into an island,-"The Texas Isle of Lobau", so to speak,--and Houston devoted it to purposes similar to those of

its Danubian prototype.

After the men had washed and dried their clothing and got cleaned up, the army underwent a rigorous reorganization. 

A second volunteer regiment was formed with Sidney Sherman as its colonel. A medical corps was organized, and a field hospital was established on the east side of the river near Groce's. 

Many of the men suffering from chills and fever and exposure were hospitalized. The effectives went a thorough course of discipline, despite the incessant rains. As artillery was daily expected, an artillery company was trained. Detachments were sent down the river to send back stragglers, harrass the enemy, and to obtain military information. The latter was never neglected. 

One of the first acts of the general upon his arrival at 

Groce's was to "impress" the steamboat Yellowstone, then loading cotton at Groce's Landing. Thus the general secured the means of quickly getting his army across the flooded Brazos when occasion might demand.

From his position at Groce's, Houston could keep watch on the movements of both Gaona's column, (which was "lost in the sandhills" above Bastrop), and Sesma's column, which was having difficulties at the Atascosita, below. His army was poised for mobile action. Although hidden from enemy sight, he had open lines of communication with the Texian government and with the 

Redlands and the United States. side of the Liberty road.

prepared to leave for the front. He decided to converge his three columns on the Brazos and unite them into one command. 

On March 25th, orders were sent to Gaona to abandon his march to Nacogdoches and proceed to San Felipe instead. This order was not obeyed. General Juan Jose Andrade with 400 men, besides the wounded of the Alamo battle, were detached to garrison Bexar. 

On March 29, Colonel Augustin Amat with two battalion left Bexar for Atascosita. Two days later, (the 31st) Santa Anna, with his staff, and the remainder of his effective troops under General Vicente Filisola, followed. Amat's and Filisola's contingents aggregated some 2,000 troops. Had these contingents united with 

Sesma's division, the total strength of the central column should have approximated some 3,400 men.

When Santa Anna and Filisola got to Gonzales on April 2nd, they found Amat's van-guard held up by the swollen Guadalupe, unable to cross. Santa Anna ordered barges to be constructed, but he did not remain to see the matter concluded. 

On the 3rd, he with his staff and an escort of cavalry, swam the torrential Guadalupe and proceeded rapidly to the Colorado. He reached the 

Atascosita crossing on the 5th. There he found that Sesma had succeeded in crossing his troops, but had been unable to cross his baggage and artillery.

General Santa Anna immediately detailed General Woll with a detachment of 200 to construct barges or rafts upon which the impedimenta could be floated across, and also be in readiness when Filisola's division came up. Taking the remaining 1200 of

Sesma's troops, the commander-in-chief, on the 6th, began a march to San Felipe, where he expected Gaona to join him. He camped the night of the 6th at the San Bernard. The Mexican army reached San Felipe at daybreak of the 7th. 

Santa Anna's information was that Houston had only 800 men, and that he was in the vicinity of San Felipe. From one of Baker's men captured at San Felipe it was learned that Baker was on the east side of the river with 150 men, for the purpose of barring a crossing; and that Houston was at Groce's, fifteen leagues up the river with 800 men. Santa Anna, as well as other Mexican officers, seem to have deduced, because of the disposition of Baker's men, and the location of Grece's residence, that Houston was on the east side of the Brazos, and evidently did not know that the Texian army was concealed in the bottom only 18 miles away on the same side of the river as themselves. 

Informed that the Mexican army was at San Felipe, Houston sent Baker a reinforcement of 45 men, and also sent out a cavalry detachment to shadow the enemy. Santa Anna on the 7th undertook to force the crossing of the Brazos. The attempt was turned back due to Baker's determined stand and the high water. The Mexican artillery was brought into action and Baker's fortified position was vigorously shelled, but to no avail. The attack was kept up for three days, Santa Anna at the same time sending parties up and down the river to discover another crossing. In desperation 

Santa Anna ordered some unburned houses torn down and put his men to work constructing barges. After a few hours this project was abandoned, and the generalissimo, on the 9th, left San Felipe.

On the evening of April 11, Houston received his first knowledge that Santa Anna was on the Brazos, and that the Mexicans were in process of crossing at Old Fort. This information was

THE BATTLE OF SAN JACINTO By Captain Hobart Huson, S-1, 21st Bn. TSG. 

The purpose of the present article is to give a military history of the sanguinary and decisive Battle of San Jacinto, stripped of myths and heroics, and let the facts speak for themselves.

It will be remembered that General Sam Houston with about 400 Texian volunteers began their retreat from Gonzales on March 12, 1836; that the army went into an isolated camp at Groce's, on the Brazos, on April 1, and remained there until April 11, when General Houston first learned that 

General Santa Anna, President of Mexico, was still in Texas, and at that moment was crossing the Brazos at Old Fort (Richmond) and headed for Harrisburgh; that Houston perceived that 

Santa Anna was about to get himself separated from his main army and was likely to get himself caught within a military "pocket" (which Houston had contrived to stay out of); that

Houston had immediately broken camp at Groce's, crossed the Brazos, and made a forced march through the mud in the hope of intercepting Santa Anna; that the Texian army arrived near 

Harrisburgh on the morning of the 18th, shortly after the Texian scouts had captured Mexican couriers who bore dispatches revealing that Santa Anna was below Harrisburgh with 750 troops and was expecting a reinforcement of at least 500; that Houston had established a camp and depot on Buffalo Bayou, opposite Harrisburgh, and on the morning of the 19th learned definitely that Santa Anna intended to cross the San Jacinto River at

Lynch's Ferry and march to Anahuac where he expected to be

General Houston, having been informed of the plans and position of Santa Anna, acted with energy and dispatch. He established a depot on the north or west side of Buffalo Bayou, opposite the burned town of Harrisburgh. There he left all baggage and impedimenta, and all sick. A field hospital was set up in charge of Doctors Phelps, Anson Jones, Anderson and There were some 150 hospitalized soldiers, most of Davidson. whom had the measles. 

Major McNutt with 75 effectives were detached to guard the depot. With the coming of additional recruits the personnel of this depot eventually increased to between 300 and 350.

On the morning of the 19th the remainder of the army was ordered to draw three days rations and prepare itself to march immediately. Nothing was to be carried along which could be dispensed with. Houston even considered leaving his two sixpounders, known as the "Twin Sisters", but finally decided to take them, which eventually proved to be wise. The Texian flying column consisted of approximately 1000 men (not counting those left at Harrisburgh). 

The indefatigible Texian scouts, Messrs. Deaf Smith, Karnes & Co., had rounded up three or four flat-boats, ferry-boats or rafts, and had assembled them at the confluence of Sims Bayou with Buffalo Bayou. Captain Karnes and his scouts were sent to the south side of the Bayou to protect the crossing from that side, while Deaf Smith and his little band were fanned out in the direction of New Washington keeping watch on Santa Anna.

The crossing began as soon as the flying column reached the place where the flat boats were. The first unit to cross was the engineer company. The men and equipment and the artillery were floated across on the boats. The mounted units swam their horses across the bayou. The crossing was completed by sundown. When the last had crossed General Houston addressed a few words to his army. He told them their objective was to find and fight the enemy. 

Before leaving the depot opposite Harrisburgh, General

Houston had advised Major McNutt that some 500 reinforcements for Santa Anna were due to pass through Harrisburgh within the next several hours; that under no condition was such reinforcement to be attacked or molested, but should be permitted to pass; that McNutt and his men should stay out of sight as nearly as possible so as to avoid a clash with Cos; and finally 

McNutt was not to offer battle unless himself first attacked.

The Texian flying column took up the line of march for Lynch's Ferry immediately after crossing the bayou. It marched 

By one all night, without refreshment, stopping only once. o'clock of the 20th it had made 12 miles. A halt was then made in the prairie, and the weary men slept on their arms. Smith's scouts came in about that time and reported that the Mexicans had not yet attemped to cross the San Jacinto. At dawn the march was resumed. The men were awakened by a tap of the drum, the reveille being prohibited.

At this point General Houston detached a number of mounted men with orders to get to Lynch's Ferry as rapidly as possible,

and to seize and hold that point until the column could come up. This advance guard consisting of some 30 or 40 men got to the ferry before 8 o'clock, and just in time to capture a new flat boat loaded with provisions for Santa Anna's army.

Further preparations for breakfast were abandoned. The column made a rapid march to the ferry. It was found that the Texian advance guard was already in possession there. .

Mexican reconnaissance parties came in sight, but disappeared when they saw the Texians. Houston's army then fell back about a half mile into a belt of timber skirting Buffalo Bayou, and established camp. Breakfast preparations were resumed, and, with addition of the provisions captured by the advance guard, the army enjoyed a sumptuous repast. 

The position chosen by General Houston was an ideal one. 

The elevation was high and the entire prairie for miles around could be kept under constant observation. On the other hand the position was so luxuriantly wooded that the Texian camp was practically concealed from enemy observation. In fact Santa 

Anna was not certain of its location until late the afternoon of the 20th, and seems never to have learned the number or

disposition of the Texian units. 

Back of the campsite was

Buffalo Bayou, which was very wide at the point. The woodlands and the bayou gave security to the flanks and rear. 

The one disadvantage was that in case of defeat the Texians would be caught in a trap, unless they could escape across the bayou. For this reason Houston kept the flat-boat, captured that morning, behind the camp; and later had the other flatboats and rafts brought down from the camp at Harrisburgh.

About 10 o'clock a. m., of the 20th, the Mexican army, under Santa Anna, Presidente of Mexico, was seen entering the prairie from the east. Santa Anna had spent the preceding night at Clopper's Point, about eight miles below. His cossack post at Lynch's Ferry, commanded by Colonel Barragan, had notified him of the Texian approach. The report had reached Santa 

Anna at about 8 a.m., and, as is seen, he lost no time in getting his force up to Lynch's. 

General Santa Anna was apprised of the fact that the Texians were in the belt of timber along the bayou, and he promptly and shrewdly appraised Houston's position. "When

I arrived, Houston had taken possession of the woods on the 

banks of Buffalo Bayou. His position would force him to fight or take to the water." The Mexican commander-in-chief decided to make an immediate demostration against the Texian position, with the view of coaxing the Texians out of the protection of the woods into the open prairie, or failing that, to probe and learn their dispositions. Santa Anna had at that time only 700 infantry, 50 cavalry and one piece of artillery, and was aware that Houston must have 1000 or more troops. ever, he knew his own troops to be seasoned veterans, highly disciplined, and that his officers were competent and experienced; whereas, the Texians were volunteers, most of whom had never been in a pitched battle; their officers mere frontier fighters

without technical military training. Santa Anna himself had been a professional soldier since he was fourteen, and had thirty years military experience back of him. Despite the tirades made against him, Santa Anna possessed high talent as a military strategist, and had proven his worth in hundreds of battles. Sam Houston, on the other hand, was a politician and not a soldier. He had been in one battle as a lieutenant, and the campaign of 1836 was his first experience in commanding an army in the field; and the battle he was about to fight was to be the first in which he commanded.

Santa Anna, therefore, desired nothing better than to lure Houston and the Texians out of the woods and on the open plains, where military skill and discipline would be pitted against inexperience and lack of discipline. The general features of the San Jacinto battleground have been noted already. Reference to the map will immediately disclose all peculiar or distinctive features of the terrain. It will be seen therefrom that the area involved is within an angle formed by 

Buffalo Bayou on the west with San Jacinto River on the north, and by the river with San Jacinto Bay on the east. A prairie about two miles in width is framed by the waterways named, the margins of which were either marshes or timber or both. Along the margins

of San Jacinto River and San Jacinto Bay were wide expanses of swamp-marches, which were practically impassable. About a mile from Buffalo Bayou and at the margin of the swamp with the prairie fast land was a pond called McCormick's Lake, which was fed by a rill running through the swamp. The area around the pond was well wooded, and the area along the rill was also densely wooded. 

This fringe of woodland connected with the fringe bordering Buffalo 

Bayou in which the Texian camp lay.

Between the two hostile camps was a prairie in which were several islands of timber, some of which have been heretofore mentioned. In the prairie were several low places or depressions covering considerable areas. The time of the year was the zenith of Spring, and the prairie was covered with tall, luxuriant grass, in some places between knee and hip height. The road between New

The Mexican camp site was adjacent to McCormick's Lake, and was high, healthy, well shaded, and convenient to ample fresh water. 

Its right flank and rear were secured by the river and bay marshes.

After dinner General Houston was sitting on the grass conversing

Captain Karnes was squatted with his back against a tree. The general asked him to slip through the fringe of woods which connected with the enemy's camp and take an observation of what was going on in Santa Anna's camp. The scout left and the conversation was picked up where it had been left off. In a short while Karnes returned with a rather disgusted expression on his face. There were several officers of Mexican descent present, and also any number of old settlers who were married to Mexican women, or had lived in Mexico, and Karnes report started a discussion of Old Spanish and Mexican Customs, including that of the siesta during the hottest part of the day. Some of the Mexican officers, who had served with the Spanish and Mexican armies, remarked that the custom of the siesta had been often observed by armies while in the field, if they were confident of no immediate danger. 

General Houston listened with interest, and appeared to be amused by what was said, but made no comment.

General Houston had implicit confidence in their loyalty and judgement, and these frontiersmen believed in the general. These two captains of scouts undoubtedly enjoyed more confidences of the commander-in-chief than any other officer or man in the camp, not even excepting Rusk. 

It was to Smith and Karnes that the first intimation of the day's plans were given. Smith was instructed to watch for the arrival of Cos' reinforcements and to make as accurate count as possible of their number. He was also ordered to hold himself in readiness to destroy Vince's bridge after Cos had passed over it. 

Karnes instructions were to unobtrusively slip through the woods after the noonday meal and keep watch on the enemy camp and see whether or not they repeated their siesta episode of the day before. 

The greatest secrecy was preserved as to all of these matters, and

no one save the general and the chiefs of scouts had an inkling of what was in the offing. 

About 9 o'clock the morning of the 21st, General Cos column was seen coming across the prairie. Shortly thereafter Deaf Smith reported to his general that according to his count Cos had 540 men. Whether this estimate included the 100 men which Cos left with his baggage at Sims Bayou, is not clear, If the figure did not include said 100, then the arrival of Cos increased Santa Anna's strength to 1290 men. If the figure did include the 100, then 

Santa Anna's strength was only 1190 men. From Houston's official report it appears that Santa Anna had on the battlefield about 

1360 men.

The reinforcements were of no immediate help to General Santa Anna. Cos' troops had marched most of the night and all the morning, with very little rest, and little or no refreshment, and were fagged out when they marched into the Mexican camp. It was out of the question for Santa Anna to make any plans until these reinforcements had rested. Probably Houston realized this situation as well as 

Santa Anna.

The suspense became awful in the Texian camp. About noon of the 21st, a committee of officers waited upon the commander-in-chief demanding that a council of war be held. General Houston graciously acceded, and ordered the council be held instanter. Colonels Burleson and Sherman, Lieutenant-Colonels Millard, Somervell and Bennett, and Major Wells, constituted the council. Houston and Rusk merely sat on the sidelines. This conference was held under a tree, the officers sprawled upon the grass or ground. The question was debated

and put: Shall we attack the enemy in his position, or wait an attack from him in our own position. Lt. Col. Bennett and Major 

Wells voted to attack the enemy; the other members voted to wait and receive the attack. The council was then dismissed, without 

General Houston indicating what course he intended to pursue.

After dinner Deaf Smith and his companion Reeves were dispatched with their axes to destroy Vince's bridge. The general remarked that they had better hurry and get back if they did not want to find the field already bloody. already bloody. Colonel Bennett was sent through the camp to ascertain the spirit of the troops and their willingness to attack the enemy. Bennett reported that he found the spirits of the men high, and all eager for the fray. 

About 3 o'clock in the afternoon Karnes slipped into headquarters and whispered a few words to the general; they were to the effect that Santa Anna and most of his army were enjoying their siesta. Houston then perfected in his mind the plan which had been intriguing him since the noon before. He then called 

General Rusk aside and submitted his plan, which was promptly approved by the Secretary of War. The troops were ordered to parade at 3:30 o'clock.

The general, having made his final decision, now found it necessary to make certain changes in the formation of the battle front. As before stated, since the camp had been established on the 20th, the artillery under Colonel Neill had occupied the center of the line; behind it was Colonel Millard's infantry units; and behind the center the cavalry squadron. General Houston now moved the cavalry, (presently commanded by Lamar), to the rear of the right, and moved back Millard's infantry and the artillery, so that Burleson's regiment could close upon Sherman's and compose the center. The artillery, (now under Colonel Hockley, due to Neill's wound) was then moved to Burleson's right, and Millard's infantry moved to the right of the artillery. The little cavalry company was then shifted to the right flank. The Deaf Smith scouts were away at Vince's bridge, whilst the Karnes scouts were stationed in the woods to the left to signal back any undue activity, of the enemy before the battle-line was formed. 

Practically all of the foregoing movements took place under concealment of the timber; as did the conference which the general next held with his field officers. He explained his plans and purposes in detail. The movement was to be a strict surprise. 

Absolute silence was to be maintained. Everyone was to keep as much out of sight as possible and as long as possible as the front advanced. Fire should be withheld until the word to fire was given, and the officers were enjoined to enforce this phase to the letter. The success of the movement depended absolutely upon the fidelity, and the intelligent and courageous cooperation, of each officer and man. The field officers were to instruct their captains in these orders; and the captains in turn were to instruct the men.

After the field officers had returned to their commands and relayed instructions to their subordinates, General Houston, mounted on his gray stallion, rode across the entire front of his 1

army, and stopped and spoke personally with every captain, and gave words of encouragement within the hearing of all. 

The entire army, less one detachment, was to participate in the action. The one detachment consisted of those who were to guard the camp, and the doctors and medical assistants. 

Needless to say, when the army moved out, practically all of the detachment moved out with it, Dr. Anson Jones included. 

As Sherman's regiment had the farth' rest to go; its route being circuitous, it moved off first from the flank in column formation, turning through the woods which skirted the bayou and the river. As this regiment was to deliver the first attack, the movements of all other units were made to depend upon it. 

The cavalry then moved off from the right flank, in a rather leisurely manner, heading in the general direction of Harrisburgh. 

This movement was in open view and was intended to attract attention of the enemy, and district attention from all other movements. 

From appearances the little cavalry troop was jogging along on some routine mission to Harrisburgh, As soon as the cavalry moved out, Burleson's and Millard's units moved forward simultaneously each in column formation, using the timber islands in front to screen their movements. 

The grass was high enough to hide a man lying or crouching in it. 

It was also difficult for a man to be seen after he passed into the depressions or low places.

When the columns reached the timber islands in their fronts, they proceeded to deploy rapidly into battle-front, creeping and crawling through the high grass while accomplishing this movement.

When each unit had deployed it lay or squatted in the grass, while its officers observed the progress of Sherman's left wing. 

The artillery was advanced through the grass to within 200 yards of the enemy's breast works, without being detected. 

Only the general and his staff officers were mounted, (besides the cavalry), and they remained in the edge of the timber concealed from view, while observing the deployment of the troops. 

When it was seen that Sherman had finally worked his way through the woods to the battle line, and that part of his troops were ready to debouch into the open to connect with Burleson, the general and his staff put spurs to their horses and dashed forward. The Mexicans began to fire when the Texians got within 

300 yards of the entrechments. The Texians had been ordered to withhold fire until they were within point-blank range. The men religiously obeyed orders. Moseley Baker chides the general, "You are now drawn up before the enemy in battle array. * 

Mexican artillery and the Mexican musketry are playing upon your line, but your men obedient to orders, move not, fire not, "Trail rifles and forward!' is the word. The first fire was delivered by Sherman's left wing advancing through the woods. The firing then became general, the "Twin Sisters" opening up and blasting away. The little cavalry company ceased dawdling along, and under the command of Colonel Lamar turned in and charged the Mexican cavalry, which was caught unprepared.

The defeated cavalry made a dash towards Vince's bridge, Lamar's troop giving chase. Most of the Mexican cavalry was killed e'er the bridge was reached. 

After the command, "Fire!" was given the Texians fired one blasting volley at point-blank range, and then surged forward with a mighty shout, "Remember the Alamo! Remember Goliad!" They entered the enemy entrenchments, using rifles as clubs. The attack was a real surprise and a tremendous success. Organized Mexican resistance was broken within the next ten or fifteen minutes. 

It goes without saying that despite the skillful management exhibited by General Houston and his officers, someone had to be at fault with the Mexican army in order that such a debacle could happen. The Texians lost only two killed and 23 wounded, of whom six afterwards died of wounds. The Mexican loss on the other hand was 630 killed and 208 wounded.

It is evident that Santa Anna did not err in postponing giving battle under such circumstances.

Several hours were required to rest the troops, and Santa Anna knew, therefore, that he would be unable to offer battle on even terms until the middle of the afternoon.

He also truthfully points out, "My duties as general-in-chief did not forbid my resting, for no general is forbidden this necessity.

He states that before retiring for his siesta he appointed his veteran Manuel Fernandez Castrillon as major general of the camp and entrusted its security to him, and cautioned him to be vigilant. 

If Santa Anna did give such orders and make such assignments of duty, it is hard to see how he was to blame in taking rest before and expected battle, especially when it should have been impossible for his camp to have been surprised in broad daylight if ordinary vigilance was observed. 

Santa Anna charges Castrillon with gross neglect. Although he was responsible for the security of the camp, "the said general occupied himself with making his toilet and changing his clothes; and that when the enemy attacked and surprised our advanced guards, he was whiling away his time in a party with other officers of my staff. He did not inspect our outposts a single time, and his example was followed by the other officers. Thus, part of the troops were sleeping, while those who were awake, not being vigilant, permitted the enemy to carry out a surprise that could not have been more complete had it taken place in the middle of the night." 

Of course, Castrillon was dead, killed in the battle, and could not reply to Santa Anna's accusations. However, if Santa Anna entrusted the security of the camp to him, the facts speak for themselves as to his neglect. 

Santa Anna does not mention that he, like many other military men of his day, was addicted to the use of opium or morphine to induce immediate sleep while in the field, so that complete and quick relaxation might be obtained. It would seem that on this fateful day he took an overdose of the drug before lying down for his siesta, and was sleeping soundly when the entrenchments were

crashed.

Most of the accounts of the battle were written by Texans, who had an undying hatred, not unjustly, of Santa Anna, and Mexicans in general, for that matter. The accounts are, therefore, colored and filled with prejudice. They play up the idea that a 

much superior Mexican force was defeated by a much inferior Texian force, some 1500 by 783. The Mexican officers are pictured as fools, and both officers and men as cowards. Very little credit is given General Sam Houston for his splendid generalship. has been seen, the Texians outnumbered the Mexicans in substantial numbers until mid-morning of the 21st, yet Santa Anna did not hesitate to attack the Texians and invite them to battle in the open. 

At the time of the battle Houston had almost 1100 men to Santa Anna's 1300. Thus the odds were not so great in favor of the Mexicans, The Mexican leaders were guilty of gross negligence, but they and their men were certainly not cowards. The Mexican army had chased Houston from Gonzales to San Jacinto, and had hurled challenge after challenge to him, some of the challenges being personal and insulting; but Houston had declined to fight. Santa Anna had offered battle on the morning of the 20th, and Houston had avoided it.

Under the circumstances it was natural that they expected no such thing as did occur from such an enemy. 

The noise and confusion of the Texian attack aroused the Mexican officers too late to save the day. The Mexican right wing was driven in by a fierce attack of Sherman's regiment. Santa 

Anna's, Castrillon, Almonte, Cespedes and Luelmo attempted valiantly to rally the men and form a line of defense. The Matamoras battalion,

good and veteran troops, commanded by Colonel Luelmo, stood like the Rock of Gibraltar, after the flanks collapsed around it. 

An attempt was made to form a column under Colonel Manuel Cespedes composed of the permanent battalion of Guerrero and detachments from Toluca and Guadalajara.

It actually moved forward to support the Matamoras battalion, but Cepedes fall badly wounded, and Luelmo was killed outright at the same moment. 

Castrillon--and there was none cooler or braver--moved from place to place trying to reform the confused masses. He deliberately and bravely accepted death in the forefront. The mortality among the Mexican officers was exceptionally high, attesting their bravery, There were killed, one general officer, four colonels, 2 lieutenant-colonels, 5 captains and 12 lieutenants; there were badly wounded, 5 colonels, 3 lieutenant-colonels, 2 second-lieutenant colonels, 7 captains and 1 cadet. Colonel Almonte acted coolly and succeeded in rallying 730 men and led them to a place of safety and procured for them an honorable surrender. 

While the Battle of San Jacinto was a glorious victory for

Texian arms, it was converted into a decisive victory only by the capture alive of General Santa Anna, the head of the Mexican state, and the idol of the Mexican nation. war would have been far from an end. 

Had Santa Anna escaped the 

Forty miles away he had four or five thousand veteran soldiers, including General Urrea's army which had defeated every Texian army it had theretofore met, and flushed with victory. With their idol Santa Anna again at their head, Houston would have been compelled to fall back towards the Sabine, with only the hope of again catching a superior Mexican

army off-guard, which was not likely to occur again. 

Santa Anna undoubtedly realized that his person was the real prize of the battle; and he quickly sensed that all was lost at San Jacinto, and there was nothing further he could accomplish there, except to surrender or die a hero-martyr's death. His life meant more to his country's cause than his death, as he was the only leader who could hope to bring victory to his people. He attempted, therefore, to get back to his armies then on the Brazos, one long, hard day's ride away. 

That he failed in his attempt was Texas' greatest piece of luck, and the real fruit of decisive victory.

The Battle of San Jacinto is rated one of the decisive 

Strange to say, its decisiveness did not rest on the number of men engaged, the extent of the carnage, or the strategy or tactics employed, but on the one factor alone, the capture alive of General Antonio Lopez de 

Santa Anna, head of the Mexican state and commander-in-chief of the army. 

Had not the personality of Santa Anna been so intimately involved, and had not that personality been what it was, San Jacinto would have been just another hard fought battle, and many more such battles would have been necessary before the fate of Texas was finally determined.

Santa Anna was, in my opinion, one of the most remarkable and glamorous figures ever to cross the stage of Western history. 

Irrespective of what our ancestors may have thought of him, his must have been a superior and impelling personality to have held the veneration and obedience of his countrymen even while a captive. The Mexican government, a month after San Jacinto, proclaims its attitude toward him: "Soldiers: One of the events, very frequent in war, has placed in the power of the enemy to our independence, the herioc [sic] conqueror of Tampico, the President of the

History has presented but few instances when the capture of the head of the state was the equivalent of the subjugation of the country. Mexico herself furnished the two outstanding instances, those of Montesuma and the Spaniards, and Santa Anna and the Texans. In each case the personal power of the captive to impel immediate obedience to his orders was not affected by the captivity. The orders of Santa Anna to Filisola and Urrea to exacuate Texas and remain south of the Rio Grande were implicitly obeyed. Such an order obtained from Santa Anna was equivalent to a victorious war, and much less costly.

The Texans instinctively realized the importance of the possession of the person of Santa Anna to their cause. The battle was yet new when the search for the dictator began. He was sought not for summary vegeance, but for a greater purpose then scarcely comprehended. The search continued during the night until well into the next day, and for a while it was feared by the texans that Santa Anna had made good his escape to Filisola's army.

Negotiations were had with the captive dictator for the cessation of war and recognition by Mexico of the Independence of Texas. 

At Velasco, on May 14, 1836, two treaties were made with Santa Anna, one public, the other secret. The public treaty related to an armistice, disposition of troops and prisoners, and liberation of Santa Anna. The secret treaty provided that Santa Anna should be released to go to Mexico to prepare the Mexican cabinet to act favorably in Texan independence and enter into a commercial treaty with her.

The Texan authorities undertook in good faith to perform their part of the compact. The Texan warship "Invincible" was brought to Velasco for the purpose of carrying Santa Anna and his suite to Vera Cruz. On June 1, 1836, the Mexican captives were officially liberated and permitted to embark on the "Invincible" for their home land. At the moment of sailing a steamer came into the harbor loaded with recruits under Thomas J. Green for the Texan army. 

The recruits were outraged that Santa Anna should have been so

leniently dealt with. They asserted that he should be court martialed for the Fannin massacre, and that the sentence of death should be executed on the very spot where Fannin had died.

The recruits took charge of the situation, flaunted authority of the civil officials, boarded the "Invincible," and took possession of the body of General Santa Anna, and brought him back to shore, where from that time until General Houston became president, the lot of Santa Anna was indeed a hard one. He thus described his treatment: "I endured exposure to the cowardly insults and affronts of the soldier-like mob; I endured the closest confinement; I endured the severe vigilance of those who were most enraged against me, the prisoners that had escaped from Goliad; I endured a succession of attempts to assassinate me; I suffered them to place a heavy ball and chain upon me; and, lastly, on the 30th of June, I suffered them to order me to march to Goliad to be executed in the place where Fannin and his men had died. "I saw death approaching without regret * * *. Notwithstanding, life was offered to me; and, convinced that its sacrifice on the scaffold would not benefit my country, I listened to the generous advice of the colonist, Stephen F, Austin***who decided to save me. 

The only means of saving myself in his opinion (and my opinion could not be contrary to his under the circumstances) was to invoke the name of the President of the United States, Andrew Jackson.

He was

While Sylvester was thus engaged in projecting across the continent shadows of personalities who were to later follow in person, those personages remained at Columbia faced with an indeterminate captivity. Santa Anna appears to have understood the realities clearer than the other Mexican leaders. aware of the power of the United States, and her interest in Texas. He realized the futility of a long and costly struggle for Texas, and frankly expressed himself to Austin, Houston,

Burnet, Rusk, and the other Texan leaders, that he doubted the ability of Mexico to reconquer Texas, and that he personally believed that facts should be accepted and the independence of 

Texas recognized. 

While friends of Texas in the American Congress had clamored for recognition by the United States of Texian Independence even before the first shot was fired at Gonzales, President Jackson officially, at least, took the position that the United States was friendly to Mexico and should do nothing officially which would disturb these friendly relations. He, therefore, officially

frowned on all efforts made in Congress for recognition of Texas or aid to her revolution.

Recognition by the United States of the Independence of Texas meant for all practical purposes a guaranty of that independence, and perhaps, eventual annexation. 

However, the one obstacle to such recognition was the traditional and long uninterruped friendship between the United States and Mexico, and the sincere reluctance to overtly disturb that status. There were also political interests which were opposed to a break with Mexico over Texas. These were the anti-slavery and commercial interests of the United States. President Jackson was steering a cautious and non-critical course. 

It was apparent to the leaders of both Texas and the United States that if the head of the Mexican nation and its most powerful political and military figure would himself personally inform 

President Jackson that Mexico could not hope to reconquer Texas, and that in his opinion, recognition of Independence would be the best interest of all concerned, then all obstacles to recognition of 

Texas by the United States would be removed. 

Santa Anna agreed that he was willing to communicate those views to the President of the United States, and correspondence looking forward to a conference in Washington was opened. On July 

4, 1836, from Columbia, Santa Anna addressed a letter to President

Jackson. This was forwarded by General Houston through General Gaines. 

President Jackson answered by letter dated from the Her-. mitage, September 4, 1836, which reached Santa Anna in due time.

It was arranged that if General Santa Anna found it convenient to visit Washington he would be received by the American President. 

General Houston, upon becoming President of the Republic of 

Texas, after the September, 1836, election, relieved Santa Anna and Almonte of most of the hardships of their captivity and arranged for their release, it being understood that Santa Anna would visit Washington and explain to President Jackson, Santa Anna's own views of the Texian situation. 

On November 25, 1836, Santa Anna and Almonte were "liberated" at Columbia by orders of President Houston. They were given an escort by Texas army officers. Colonel George W. Hockley, Inspector General and Chief of Staff to General Houston, Colonel Barnard E. Bee, later Secretary of War. (It was he who gave 

Thomas J. Jackson the soubriquet "Stonewall") and Captain William 

H. Patton, who had been Santa Anna's jailor at Columbia. He had served as aide with rank of major on General Houston's staff at the battle of San Jacinto. There were also a Mexican servant or two.

During the progress of this strange party, the status of the Texian officers were widely discussed. Some took the view they were guards. The Whig press resented the fact that Texas army officers had the audacity to bring their prisoners into neutral territory. Others took the view that the Texian officers were in the party at the express desire of Santa Anna, who desired their company and protection. It would seem that the Texian officers travelled at all times heavily armed. 

The party left Columbia in a stage coach, and headed for the

Mississippi River. En route they passed the battlefield of 

San Jacinto, at the sight of which Santa Anna is said to have shuddered. 

The party approached the Mississippi by way of Attakapas, and at Plaquemine, boarded the steamer "Tennessee", for Wheeling, Virginia. This was on December 12th. On the 14th the steamer put in at Natchez where John M. Ross joined the party and accompanied it as far as Vickburg. Ross wrote for publication his views of the significance of Santa Anna's trip to Washington. 

The steamer arrived at Louisville on Christmas Day. The little party went to the famous Galt House for rest, intending to resume the journey to Wheeling by water. Despite the intense cold, great crowds assembled before the hotel and Santa Anna and Almonte obligingly stepped out on the balcony, to acknowledge the greetings. As an object of popular interest Santa Anna was second only to General Houston. 11 The comments of the press on Santa Anna are of interest; at Vicksburg it was said that "if it had not been known that he was a villian he would have been taken for a refined, dignified gentlemen. At Louisville, "He is very reserved, plain, modest in his conversation and manners as far as I have observed him. 

I can see nothing villainous or deceitful in his countenance", An attempt was made to resume the trip to Wheeling by steamer, however, the floating ice in the Ohio presented a continous danger, and it was decided to abandon the river route. General Santa Anna and party arrived in Washington City on the afternoon of January 18. He and Col. Almonte took lodgings at Mrs. Ulreck's boarding house, and the presence of the party in Washington was shortly made known to President Andrew Jackson. 

According to the "Intelligencer" of the 21st, "It is understood that General Santa Anna had an interview with the president of the United States at the Palace, on Thursday, and was kindly and courteously received by him. Jackson on the Texas question.

Santa Anna remained in Washington for over a week. He had several conferences with President Jackson. Just exactly what was said or done at these conferences will never be known. But we do know that history was made; that Santa Anna kept his promise, at least sufficiently to justify President Jackson in transmitting all correspondence to the United States Senate with recommendation that the independence of Texas might now be recognized. Recognition

followed as a matter of course, and Texas became a nation of the world. It was here that one substantial benefit of our victory of San Jacinto was realized.

During his journey and sojourn, Santa Anna was front page news. This fact was commented on by the Albany Argus: "There is a strong propensity in the mind of our worthy Public to worship the "lion" of the day, whatever it may be; and the propensity is not less strong to vibrate from the extremes of abhorrence and sympathy. Thus Santa Anna, who, one year since, was the object of universal execration, and whose unparralleled atrocities excited a burst of indignant feeling that would have led, at that time, to summary and condign punishment, and he placed himself within the reach of nearly any portion of our citizens, is now the object to respect, of sympathy, and of public attention." (Albany Argus.) 

The "National Intelligencer" observes: "There is much truth in the first sentence of this paragraph; and so much of that antiquated and un-court eerlike quality in the residue of it, that we felt something like suprise at seeing it in a quarter distinguished for its loyalty to the present ruling authority of the Government. For, if from anyone, it is from the Executive of the United States, of whom the Argus is the unhesitating supporter in all cases, that Santa Anna has received respect, sympathy, and public attention. He has been received in the presidential halls with at least all the respect due to his position, with the sympathy of a brother soldier, and with the public attention of supplying him a Government vessel to take him

back to Mexico, in the face of a reported decree of outlawry against him. "To any of all of these circumstances of hospitality to an unfortunate stranger we do not mean hear to take any exception. 

We are glad, for the character of our own country, that he travelled through our territory in safety, and that he has set out from the seat of Government under auspices which insure his safe journey beyond our limits. "But, reminded of it by the paragraph in the Argus, our thoughts involuntarily turn to the remarkable contrast between the courteous reception given to the EX-President of Mexico by the President of the United States, and that which if any reliance could be placed on public demonstrations, might have been expected at his hands. For, it is not much more than six months since the 

Government paper in this city, suggested that we had taken a bribe, or something of that sort, because we were unwilling to beleive at first in the putting to death, by order of General Santa Anna, of Fannin's command in Texas; and hardly half that space of time had elapsed since the same paper very plainly intimated it to be the opinion of the President of the United States that Santa Anna, being then a captive, ought to be put to death by the Texans, as a miscreant unworthy to live." 

Destiny had been

Santa Anna's mission had been accomplished. fulfilled. The history of the new world had been remade. His guard of honor had gone home; naught remained to Santa Anna and

Almonte but to do likewise. They expressed their desire to be taken to Vera Cruz, and President Jackson obligingly placed a war

vessel at their disposal. 

On January 26, Santa Anna and Almonte, accompanied by

Captain Tatnall, U.S.N. left Washington City "in the cars for 

Baltimore", where they arrived that afternoon. The party immediately embarked on a steamboat "Pocahontas" for Norfolk, where the "Pioneer" (One of the smaller class vessels which had been lately built for exploring voyage) was waiting to take 

Santa Anna and his secretary to Vera Cruz. The steamer "Pocahontas" left Baltimore at 3 P.M. of the 26th. "The boat proceeded that afternoon to the mouth of the river, where it remained in consequence of the ice in the bay", until the afternoon of the 27th. "The southerly wind having by that time caused the partial breaking up of the ice, the Pocahontas continued her course for some distance down the bay; but, on account of the difficulty of working her way through the ice, was eventually compelled to return to the "Rocks" where she remained at anchor "until the morning of the 28th, when the weather becoming more pleasant," the journey to Norfolk was continued. 

The "Pocahontas" reached Norfolk the morning of February 11, 1837. The party transferred to the "Pioneer" of which 

Tatnall took command, A news item states: "The Pioneer having on board Santa Anna and Colonel Almonte, was towed to sea, on Tuesday morning, last February 2nd., by the steamboat "Patrick Henry".
