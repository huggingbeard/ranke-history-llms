PREFACE And Indian Lyric of the Ozarks where myth and tragic legend have played the harp of the Centuries so long with discordant wierdness. 

Here the Bluff Dwellers with huge hammers of stone flaked the mountains of flint, with axes of granite clave the forest asunder, and with baskets of woven willows patiently carried the earthen mould and builded their temple mounds whereon the worshippers offered up their myriads of human sacrifices.

A Lyric of that bewitching land where once the Sky people and Earth people had met, wooed and wed in the dim haze of the early morning of Life when Waconda walked softly as the breath of the whispering east wind, kissed the wand of the 

Day Dawn and endowed their children with a flickering flame to guide their sandled footsteps. 

At a time when white winged birds were skudding the billows of the great waters and were plunging their brazen beaks into the forests of the New World and the Spanish Conquestadors were being lured on by the spirit of Adventure and 

World Conquest, this drama was enacted. 

Here and then it was that Maumewa (Laughing Water) came of princely lineage to the household of the Chieftain Flying Arrow and Tacabewi 

(Black Deer) of the Tsizhu or Household Clan of the Osages.

This was the Peace clan and was given to works of hospitality and the peaceable settlement of difficulties between warring factions. 

Maumewa patiently waited upon her old blind father, led him in the gloaming to his council house, fed him and prayed to Waconda for his healing. 

She extended the hospitality of her clan to De Soto, taking him to be Waconda's messenger. She implored him to heal her dying father, but he replied through Ortiz, the interpreter, "Only God is Able."

The centuries have witnessed the intense struggle of the Indian Conscience to get a glimpse of God and his power to heal, and help benighted humanity.

A glossary of terms is herewith given:

Wakota-The Osage Great Creator of the Universe.

Waconda-The Pawnee Spirit of the Waters and Life. 

War Eagle-The War Spirit of the Indian. 

Tsizhu Clan-The Household Clan of the Osages given to deeds of Good Will, Hospitality and

Peace. 

Tacabewi-Black Deer (female) represents the struggles of the Indian Conscience to flee from war and live at peace.

Maumewa (Laughing Water) Daughter of Fly ing Arrow and Tacabewi, Princess of the 

Spirit of Peace of the Tsizhu Clan.

Howineetah-(Young Deer) (Eastern Cherokee) 

The Industrial spirit of the Red Man, who passed the Omens of the Spanish Conquerors to their kinsmen across the Father of Waters. 

Casqui-Chieftain Guardsmen at the River who plead with De Soto for Mercy in the Name of Justice.

Juan Ortiz-Spanish vagabond scout of the Narvaez expedition who had wandered among the Indians for years and had gotten their view points of eternal life and sympathized with them in their benighted condition. He is shown as the last personage of the caravan to leave Maumewa and her dead father, Flying Arrow.

DeSoto The Spanish explorer who crossed the Mississippi, met Casqui, was succored in the

time of need by friendly Indians, marched thru northern Arkansas to the village of

Tanico in northeastern Oklahoma, near the Buffalo and Grand Rivers where he pastured his horses and stock before his return down the Arkansas to the Mississippi and to his death.

MAUMEWA LEGENDS OF CREATION 

Far back in the Eon Creation

When Light and Darkness were warring 

With scimeters made out of star-flame 

Of the stars that fell with Lucifer, That Manitau kept in his smithy 

Where Pluto wrought wrath for the Devil, And chains for the howling Cerberus, That Watched at the gates of Hades; 

Then Wakoda, the God of Creation,

Walked forth on the roof of the heavens With a step that sounded like thunder That echoed through rifts of the mountains 

Of the newly made earth below him, That muttered disconsolate warnings

As it clave to the sides of the craters 

Where fire-seas raged in their fury 

Through the void of infinite distance;

Then it was, it is said, that the Sky-people 

Came forth from the hands of Wakoda, Made of soul-dust up in the Skyland, Kissed by clouds where fountains were forming Up close to the sources of waters, Where Waconda, the Spirit of Spring time,

In his lodge of infinite cycles, Woven out of the mist of the east-wind, Sat planning the sources of rivers. 

To lock in the ever-green mountains 11

To water the firmament valleys 

And make beautiful vales for Waconda 

To bloom in the sheen of Tomorrow.

Then it was that the Sky-people builded 

Their mystical lodges in Sky-land 

Out of shafts of earliest day-dawn 

Shot up through the clouds where fountains were forming 

Where the swan had mothered her children. 

Then it was that Waconda, the Spirit, The God of the Whispering-spring-time,

Walked forth from his lodge in the Sky-land From his lodge made of mist of the east-wind, Walked softly in his swan-down sandals Through the gloam on the breath of the night wind

By the couch of his weeping Sky-people; 

Lit their lodges with radiant candles 

Made from attar of redolent roses 

That grew at the threshold of Day-dawn. 

Then it was the Sky-people sorrowed,

Wept aloud at having no children 

To play in their lodges in cloud-land, Like the gray wolf baying his anguish 

To the new moon weeping above him.

Then it was that Wakoda the Maker 

Made Earth-people out of the pebbles

That lay on the tops of the mountains, Made them wife for the Sky-people husband; 12

But the newly made Earth-people hungered 

Till the Elk swam out in the waters, And blew up the mist with his nostrils 

Till the sea went up in the heavens,

And the mountain tops rested amid them; 

Then the Elk in the ooze of the valley Shed his coat at the coming of spring time 

When the sun shone forth in the heavens, And the hair blossomed out in the maize bloom 

Bore food in greatest abundance,

So the Earth-people garnered a harvest, When they wedded their Sky-people husband With a green corn dance on the mountain. 

Then Wakoda made plains for the pastures, For the buffalo wallows and thunder 

At the call of the herd-bull's bellow

When affrightened by the fires of Wakoda As they swept in a haulicaust skyward Over the waste and blue stem meadows; 

Made vales for the cradles of rivers 

Where their murmurs in lullaby cadence 

Soothed to slumber the weeping Earth-people

Rocked to rest by the hand of Waconda As he came from his lodge in the Sky-land; 

Came to earth in a cloud of the east wind,

Sowing seeds of beauty and goodwill In the minds of the Earth-people children; 

Went visiting springs in the mountains 

And adding his voice to the music 

Of carroling Sky-people anthems;

Made trees for the shelter and shadow 13

Of life worn weary with journeys, Weary winged by the blast of the north-wind,

Undisturbed, could rest in their gloaming; 

Then it was that the Earth-people builded By the streams at the edge of the forests Their lodges of bark and of rushes 

That grew by the murmuring rivers,

Out of grass that grew in the waste land; 

Made ready for their Sky-people off-spring 

To come and play in their lodges

That their Earth-people mother, had builded. 

So they builded barken wigwams, Lodges out of hempen rushes, Out of silken weeping willows

In the forests of the Ozarks 

Where War Eagle went a wooing

Through the groves along the rivers, Where the laughter of the waters 

Soothed the fauna to its slumber; 

Went a wooing for the maiden

Tacabewi in the forest, "Black Deer" maiden with her tresses 

Blacker than the raven feathers; 

Fleeter than the fleetest fairy;

Ran away among the pine trees To her lodge among the cyprus 

Where the moonbeams pierced the shadows 

With a glow of melancholy, Wierdly wonderful and lonely

In the fastness of the forest, Where the Spirit Chief Waconda, God of youthful, Whispering-Spring-Time 14

Came and taught his dusky children 

How to make their bows and arrows

Out of bodoc and of cedar; 

Make their bowstrings out of sinews 

Of the venison and reindeer; 

Make their spear shafts long and shapely, Toughened by the storm winds vesper

So they sang upon their mission 

Whirring through the leafy branches 

With their shining heads of jasper; 

Make them arrow heads of copper, Of obsidian, and sapphire, Cleft as jewels from the mountain 

Web-weft as with seraph sunshine 

Iridescent in their beauty.

Then Waconda taught his people 

How to use their bows and arrows

As their fire drills in their lodges; 

How to plant, and how to harvest; 

How to spin and weave their blankets; 

How to make their dancing dresses, And their moccasins of buckskin, And to beautify with spangles, Suited to their savage fancies 

With their shining flakes of copper, Tiny shells and bits of wampum 

Gathered from some distant sea shore; 

Make the head-dress for the Chieftan 

Out of stately eagle feathers

From some crag-loft in the mountain, Where the storm-king left the trophies 

Of his struggle with the Titans. 15

TACABEWI In a shady little covert 

Near the vale of Tacabewi, Where she loved to chase the black deer 

To the crag-loft of the mountain, And to pluck the luscious berries

In the jungle of the wildwood. 

There it was the wood-nymphs gathered 

In the mystic mellow shadows 

Of the full moon in her splendor; 

And would listen to the music

Of the ripple of the water 

Of the tiny icy brooklet 

Gushing from the rocky crevice 

Running, singing to the river 

Where Waconda once had gathered 

With his children of the forest; 

Told them legends of Creation 

Of this great primeval forest

Clinging to the Ancient Ozarks 

At the gates of Buena Vista;

How they came from neath the waters; 

How the Elk swam out in gladness; 

Blew the sea up with his nostrils; 

Lifted up the weeping mountains 

Groaning in their ocean prisons; 

How he wallowed in the marshland; 

Left his shed-coat in the valley; 

How the hairs grew into cornstalks; 

How these bore the grain abundant

For their green corn mountain dances 

For their little Sky-land cousins. 17

FLYING ARROW 

In the land where Tacabewi 

Flecked the silver sheen with shadows, Phantom shadows of the black deer 

With her shining raven tresses 

Waving in the mellow moonlight,

While she chased the wood-nymph fairies 

Through the vale of "Laughing Water" 

To the melody of rain drops 

Scothing nature to her slumbers 

On the tom-tom Whispering-Spring-Time.

Here beside the rippling river 

Dwelt the chieftain Flying Arrow

In a cleft-cave of the mountain, Stone craft master Arrow Maker,

With a nearby lodge of rushes Covered with a black deer buckskin, Blacker than the blackest midnight 

Softer than the softest velvet; 

Glistening in the silver moonsheen 

Like the teepe of Waconda 

On a border cloud of Sky-land, When the sun had gone to slumber 

And the full moon rose in splendor 

To the zenith in the heavens. 

Here it was that Flying Arrow 

With his host of Arrow Makers

In their teepes by the river 

Shaped their arrow points and spear heads 

Out of flint flakes from the mountain; 

Famous Tsizhu arrow makers

Were these clansmen of the Household. 18

In the teepe of Wakoda

Many days they dwelt together In a grass lodge by the river, Covered with a black deer blanket, Near the vale of Tacabewi; 

Lived they where the songs were sweetest, Where the sunshine glow was brightest, And the voices from the woodland

Lent enchantment with their murmur As they sat and crooned together 

Indian melodies idyllic. 

Oft he heard the boatman calling

During hours of weary waiting, And he beckoned to Maumewa 

To prepare him for the crossing 

With his choicest bows and arrows 

For his long hunt in the Sky-land;

But as oft he seemed to linger 

With his footprints by the river. 33

THE COMING OF DE SOTO 

One day when the sun was weary 

Shooting arrows at the shadows, And had started to his slumber

In the highlands of Hereafter, Then Maumewa on the mountain 

Stood communing with Waconda; 

Praying him to heal her father 

Of his blindness, ere the crossing

To the Hunting Grounds Eternal; 

Then it was she saw a phantom 

From the cloudland coming valeward, Clothed with glowing sheen of armor, Twisting like a fiery serpent 

Down the defiles of the mountain 

On a myriad jaded chargers. 

Now perchance she thought Waconda 

Comes in answer to her pleading, With his healing herbs from skyland, Or the vales of Howineetah.

In the far-off smoky highland. 

TSIZHU HOSPITALITY 

At a signal from Maumewa

Came a host of Tsizhu Elders 

From their many smoking teepes, Or from hunting in the woodland; 

Telling them in queenly manner 

In the name of Flying Arrow,

Chieftain of the Arrow Makers 

That Waconda must be coming 34

​As their spirit guest from Sky-land; 

Told them to prepare their baskets,

Woven of the weeping willows 

From the vale of Tacabewi;

Fill them with the very choicest 

Bits of venison and turkey;

Fruits and green corn in abundance 

From the icy mountain store house 

Where they kept their winter larder. 

With a myriad baskets laden, Hastened they to greet the strangers 

Who were coming down the valley, Weary from their weeks of marching

Through the marshes and the jungles. 

THE WELCOME 

Then the elder Tsizhu leader 

Bade them welcome with a gesture 

In the name of Flying Arrow, Chieftain of the Arrow Makers, Blind and helpless in his teepe. 

Now De Soto saw and pondered; 

Was bewildered with the welcome, And the presents set before them; 

Grazing meadows for his horses, Icy water from the fountains,

Grass for beast and food for body 

Given freely without asking 

For the "Governor" and Captive, Who had crossed his path in anger; 

They alike had claimed the mercy 35

Of the Tsizhu Arrow Makers 

In the hour of direst hunger. 

All night long the Tsizhu Clansmen 

Watched the camp fires of the weary; 

Kept them burning thru the gloaming 

Till the coming of the morning; 

Watched them lest some poisoned arrows 

From the bows of unknown foemen 

Should assail the careworn sleepers.

THE PRAYER FOR HEALING 

Brave Maumewa sent a message

By her elders to DeSoto, As a messenger from Skyland, "That he hasten to her father: 

Grant him healing for his blindness,

Else his day might close in darkness, And he could not see the boatman 

In the mist along the river."

THE RESPONSE 

Touched by such unheard of kindness 

Shown to them upon their journey

In their quest for golden treasures, Knights were told to spare the Tsizhu, Who had given of their bounties 

In the time of direst hunger. 

At the coming of the day-dawn 

Drest in robes of shimmering opal, All set forth to greet the Chieftain 36

Flying Arrow in his wigwam

On his cot of black deer blankets, And erect a cross of cedar 

At the doorway of his teepe; 

To bestow upon him blessings, Sightless, yet he might remember 

Till the boatman cast the anchor; 

Kiss their shining coats of copper; 

Touch, perchance, the priestly vestment; 

Feel the golden cross on cassock 

Woven by monastic weavers; 

Hear the chanting of an unction 

Drooned in sacred mystic minors. 

Greeted by the brave Maumewa, Dressed in priceless robe of black deer,

Mentor of the Tsizhu Household 

In the name of Flying Arrow, Guardian Spirit of her clansmen,

Princess of the Arrow Makers, 

Then the trusted Spanish leader 

Gave the answer to her pleading 

For the healing of her father; 

Tho a chosen Prince from Skyland 

And a priest in golden cassock, He could not bestow the favor 

Saying "Only God is able." 

But in honor of her kindness 

Crowned her Princess Mintahama, Indian Princess "Giving Gladly;" 

Bade their farewell to the Chieftan; 

Blessed him in his dying moments;

Laid him close beside the cross-tree 

As he heard the boatman calling; 37

Then departed toward the sunset, While Maumewa in her anguish 

Left the lodge of "Laughing Water;" 

Lifted up her dying father 

So he touched the cross tho feebly, And responded to the boatman 

Calling loudly "Flying Arrow.”

THE CRY OF MAUMEWA 

As the boatman rowed his spirit 

With his trusted bows and arrows 

To the forest of Forever, Then Maumewa stood bewildered

At the question mark of Living, Calling loudly in her anguish, "Come back Chieftain, tell me truly 

What means this that 'God is able' 

And this cross of mountain cedar 

To my father Flying Arrow

Chieftain of the Arrow Makers? 

Tell me Ortiz, tell me quickly
