Preface 

This summary of earnings series presents in one tabulation selected measures of trends in hourly and weekly earnings of factory production workers and the Consumer Price Index since the beginning of World War II. This summary was published in a different format and distributed by the Bureau of Labor Statistics for over a decade. As in the past this summary will be kept current by quarterly supplements. 

The report presents revised earnings figures issued by the Bureau of Labor Statistics late in 1961; and in accordance with recommendations of the Bureau of the Budget, changes the base of the index series from 1947-49=100 to 1957-59=100. At the same time that the indexes were computed on the basis of revised figures and converted to the new base, the constant weights used in the series of earnings excluding the effects of premium pay for overtime and interindustry shifts were revised as explained on page 11. 

To conserve space, information is shown for only a few months in each year from 1939 through 1958; monthly data are shown for subsequent years. Average earnings in some months are shown because of the importance in the economy of these months. The basis for selection of these special months is shown below: 

1939 average Beginning of World War II. 

January 1941-Base period of the "Little Steel" formula.

October 1942—Effective date of the Wage Stabilization Act. 

January 1945-Wartime peak in hours and earnings.

September 1945-Used for V-J Day since the 2-day holiday in August affected the earnings data. 

February 1946-Executive Order 9697, providing a more flexible wage-price policy. 

July 1946-First month following lifting of World War II 

price controls.

January 1950-Base date used for wage stabilization purposes. 

June 1950 Start of Korean emergency.

Appendix: Methodology Average Weekly and Hourly Earnings 

A detailed discussion of the methodology, concepts, and coverage of the earnings series is contained in a technical note on "Measurement of Employment, Hours and Earnings in Nonagricultural Industries," which is available upon request. A more limited discussion is provided in the "Explanatory Notes" of Employment and Earnings 

Statistics for the United States, 1909-60, BLS Bulletin No. 1312, pages 1-7E.

For an explanation of the effects of the recent revision of the earnings series, see "The 1961 Revision of The BLS Pay roll Employment Statistics," Monthly Labor Review, January 1962, pages 59-62. Briefly, the revised earnings series differ from those previously published for three reasons: (1) The industry structure has been converted to the 1957 Standard Industrial Classification, (2) the series have been adjusted to a March 1959 benchmark, and (3) beginning with January 1959, the estimates were prepared from a sample stratified by establishment size and, in some cases, by region. In addition, data included Alaska and Hawaii beginning in 

January 1959. These factors had little effect on hours and earnings in manufacturing as a whole; average hourly earnings dropped by only 

3 cents and average weekly hours remained unchanged.

Average Hourly Earnings Excluding Overtime and Interindustry Shift 

These indexes are compiled by assigning constant weights to the earnings figures excluding premium pay for overtime for each industry. Except for a relatively small number of industry groups in which there are several important industries to which separate constant weights have been assigned, the indexes exclude the effects of shifts among 2-digit SIC groups, but are affected by any shifts among industries within these groups.

The indexes for months prior to January 1961 were compiled by using the earnings series based on the 1945 Standard Industrial Classification and average 1954 production man-hours; the only industry groups within which constant weights were assigned to exclude the effects on earnings of interindustry shifts were transportation equipment and electrical machinery. Within these two industry groups, man-hours were held constant for each 3-digit SIC classification. 

Beginning in January 1961, the weights used were average 1958-59 production man-hours, and shifts in employment and man-hours among industries were removed in four industry groups: Primary metals, transportation equipment, food, and apparel. Within each of these four industry groups, man-hours were held constant for each 3-digit 

SIC classification. 11

To form a continuous series, the index for January 1961, derived from the new earnings data and new weights, was linked to the index for the same month computed on the previous basis. It was decided not to recompute the series for earlier years, since tests made for January 1958 indicate that use of the new weights and revised earnings series would have changed the index by only 0.15 of 

1-index point.

Major Factors Influencing Measures of Wage Changes 

A list of factors influencing the Bureau of Labor Statistics measures of wage changes are shown in the accompanying table. Although this list is not exhaustive, it is believed to include the most important factors influencing wage changes in a group of industries. (As applied to individual industries or establishments, the list would require, modification.) Nonincentive bonuses, vacations with pay, and similar factors have been excluded from the list because they are rarely reflected in measurements of wage changes.
