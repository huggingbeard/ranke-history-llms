INTRODUCTION 

Just before Shabbos, one Friday in April, a wee little boy was born. His parents were very happy. They asked all their relatives to come and see the dear little baby.

Soon, two grandmothers, two grandfathers, aunts, uncles and cousins came to see the child. "What shall we call our dear little baby?" one grandmother asked. "Yes, what shall we call him, the dear little thing?" the second grandmother cooed. "Let's call him David," one grandfather said. "Not David; that name's much too common," the other grandfather replied. "Let it be Joseph," Aunt Judith suggested. "Or Harold, or Robert," said Cousin Elaine.

Uncle Peter, gruff and determined, stood off by himself.

"Look at that child!" he exclaimed. "How can you say 

Harold or Robert or Joseph. He'll be an athlete, sure as you're born. See how his feet curl up from down under. He's a young champion, just wait and see. Give him a name that sounds strong. He's no sissy. Look at his fists! He's no ordinary child.”

They argued and argued but couldn't agree until Mother's head went round and round like a wooden top. "Now look here," she said when at last there was silence, "you can name him anything you please. To me, he will always be Habibi, no matter what you call him. He's my 

Habibi, my beloved one." 

And that's how Habibi got his name.

Mother never did remember what they finally decided to name him. Neither did the two grandmothers, the two grandfathers, nor the aunts and uncles and cousins, for everyone called him "Habibi," which means "My beloved!" 

And that's what he's called to this very day.

Habibi grew and grew. He was a beautiful baby. His cheeks were pink and his eyes were blue. His hair was red and curled up on his head, and he smiled and gurgled the whole day through.

Then came April again, and little Habibi was one year old.

Again the two grandmothers came and the two grandfathers and all the aunts and uncles and cousins. And Uncle 

Peter came, too. They all brought gifts,-rattles and blocks and a little red cart. Habibi's eyes twinkled as he reached and he rocked and he rolled on the floor.

Uncle Peter stood quietly by and said not a word until all the excitement was over. Then he said: "I've brought a 10

www gift that will be with him longest, a gift that will grow more precious with time."

Uncle Peter stepped out of the room, and what do you think? He returned with the cutest little, blackest little puppy tucked under his arm. "A puppy, a spaniel!" the aunts and uncles exclaimed. "Now what will a baby do with a dog?" "Don't you think he's too young for a pet, Uncle 

Peter?" Mother, too, was surprised beyond words. 

Before Uncle Peter could open his mouth, down jumped the little black puppy, right out of his arm. It made its way directly to Habibi. 

Habibi's red curls stood straight on his head. His eyes opened wide and he stared and he stared at the little black dog.

The little black puppy blinked awhile and then opened his black little mouth, and out of his black little mouth rolled a pink, pink tongue. "YOW-!" shrieked Habibi. "YOW!" 11

He must have been startled by the pink, pink tongue. 

Each time Habibi shrieked, "YOW-!" the little black puppy came closer and closer and soon he began licking 

Habibi's plump little legs with his pink, pink tongue.

Habibi timidly touched the soft, black fur of the little puppy. The puppy curled up and rested his little black head in Habibi's lap.

Habibi stroked the puppy's back, so soft and warm. In an instant they were friends. "What a friendly little dog," said Mother. "He didn't mind Habibi's screeching at all. He came right up to him just as though he had been called." "Maybe he thought the boy called him," Uncle Peter said. "Maybe the puppy thought 'Yow' was his name." "Why not?" asked Mother. "Though that's a very queer name for a little black dog, he seemed to like it. Let's call him 'Yow'!" 

And that's how Yow got his name. 12

Chapter I UNCLE PETER COMES FOR SHABBOS 

Habibi and Yow grew up together. They loved each other very much. Wherever Habibi went, Yow was at his side. Whatever Habibi did, Yow was sure to do. "The two pals," Mother called them, and Father said, "We never need to worry about our little boy as long as that dog's around." 

When Habibi was two years old, his mother remarked, "I don't know who is growing faster, you or Yow, or which one of you can get into more mischief."

That made Habibi giggle. He closed his arm tighter around the little black spaniel who blinked his eyes and let his pink, pink tongue roll out of his mouth in his cute little impish way.

When Habibi was three years old, Uncle Peter came 13

to spend the Shabbos with them. Habibi was thrilled. He loved the Sabbath and he loved his Uncle Peter. "We've got to get this house spic and span," said Habibi. "We've got to get it spic and span for Uncle Peter.” "This house is always spic and span for Shabbos," 

Mother replied. "I know, you want it extra spic and span because Uncle Peter is coming."

The house was spic and span. Everything had been cleaned and polished for Shabbos. Every nook and corner smelled of freshly cooked fish and broth and roasted chicken. 

Habibi and his little black dog sniffed and sniffed all afternoon. It made them hungry. "Don't you think it's time to set the table?" Habibi asked. "Uncle Peter will soon be here." cloth.

Mother let Habibi help her spread the snow-white table- "I'll get the shiny candlesticks," the little boy said, and he placed two large, handsome silver candlesticks right in the center of the dining-room table. 

Mother came in with two twisted golden-brown Hallahs, the Sabbath loaves. She put them at Father's place. "Shabbos Hallahs, golden brown, Shabbos Hallahs, twisted down,"

Habibi sang. And Yow began to bark. He always barked when he saw the Shabbos loaves, for Hallah was Yow's favorite bread. "You may cover the Sabbath loaves," Mother said. And 

Habibi covered the Sabbath bread with Mother's beautiful

Hallah-cloth. The Hallah-cloth was pure white linen with two twisted Sabbath loaves and two Shabbos candlesticks embroidered in gold-colored thread. All around the border, 14

embroidered in red, was a Hebrew prayer. Habibi could not read the prayer but Mother told him it was the "Motzi" prayer, the blessing which Father recited over the Sabbath bread at the beginning of the meal. 

Habibi went to get the silver Kiddush cups, one for each person at the table. 

It was then that Yow thought he should be doing something. No one was paying any attention to him. Mother and 

Habibi were so busy they had forgotten him completely. 

He sprang up on Father's chair and with his little black mouth, pulled down the white linen Hallah-cloth with the gold and red embroidery. He was going to have his fun, too! 

Mother came in with a bottle of wine for Kiddush. "Habibi!" she exclaimed. "My nice, white Hallah-cloth on the floor!"

Habibi couldn't understand how the Hallah-cloth could have fallen from the table. "I covered the Hallahs with it. 

It must have slipped off," he said. "Oh-h-h, that beautiful Hallah-cloth." He picked it up and replaced it once more on the two golden-brown twists of Sabbath bread. 15

A few minutes later, when Mother and Habibi returned to the kitchen, the little black dog again sprang up on Father's chair and with his little black mouth pulled down the embroidered Hallah-cloth. 

When Habibi saw the Sabbath loaves uncovered, he was really puzzled. "This is three times I have covered those Hallahs," he said. And then he looked down at Yow. There sat the little black spaniel grinning up at him, his eyes dancing merrily in his little black head, and his pink, pink tongue rolling out of his mouth. His stubby little tail was wagging back and forth. "So you're the one who has been pulling off the Hallahcloth," Habibi said.

Yow scampered away, satisfied that he had had his fun. 

If he could have no part in setting things up for Shabbos, he could at least have the sport of pulling things down. 

When Uncle Peter arrived, the sun was beginning to set. Father had already left for the synagogue. 

Yow remembered Uncle Peter and jumped in the air to greet him. 66 

You funny little dog," Uncle Peter said. "You funny little dog. You're such a little fellow yet you have a memory like an elephant's."

That must have pleased Yow for he barked and he wagged his stubby little tail and he licked Uncle Peter's hand with his pink, pink tongue. "It's time to bless the candles," Mother said.

Like a flash Yow turned and ran to the table. He was the first one there. Standing on his little hind legs, just as 

Habibi had taught him, he waited for Mother to kindle the lights. Habibi bowed his head. The little dog bowed his head, too. 16

​Mother blessed the candles in a low, sweet voice: "Bo-ruh a-taw a-do-noy e-lo-hay-nu me-leh ho-o-lom a-sher kid-sho-nu be-mitz-vo-sov ve-tsi-vo-nu le-had-leek nayr shel Shab-bos.. "Blessed are Thou, O Lord our God, King of the universe, who hast sanctified us with Thy laws and commanded us to kindle the Sabbath lights. "O Lord, bless Thou our home with Sabbath cheer and 

Sabbath peace, and send Thy blessings of cheer and peace to all the families of the earth." "Amen," said Habibi and Uncle Peter. And Yow barked once. That was his way of saying, "Amen." 17

"What a remarkable dog," said Uncle Peter. "What a cute little, clever little dog!" Uncle Peter had never seen a dog bow his head for the kindling of the Sabbath lights. "But Yow is a Jewish dog," Habibi told him. "He does everything we do."

While Uncle Peter said his evening prayers, Mother took Yow and Habibi in her arms and settled down in their favorite armchair. "Tell us about the two angels, Mother," coaxed Habibi, "the two angels that pass every Jewish home on the Sabbath eve."

Mother was sure that Habibi knew every word of this charming legend for she told it to him every Sabbath eve while they waited for Father to return from the synagogue. "Very well, I'll tell you about the two angels," Mother said. "On Friday at sunset, when Father returns from the synagogue, two angels accompany him home. One is a good angel, the angel of peace, and the other is the spirit of evil. 

If Father finds the table spread with Sabbath food, the candles lit, and everyone dressed in their Sabbath clothes, and all within festive and peaceful, the good angel smiles and says: 'May all your Sabbaths be as this one. Peace unto this home,' and the spirit of evil is forced to say, 'Amen.' 999 "But what if the home is not peaceful?" Habibi asked. "What if it is noisy and the Sabbath candles are not lit?" 

Mother smiled. Habibi asked the same question each time she repeated the legend. "If the house is not peaceful and festive for Shabbos," 

Mother said, "if Sabbath peace and Sabbath cheer are not within, then the spirit of evil speaks and says, 'May all your 

Sabbaths be like this,' and the angel of peace weeps. Against her will she is forced to whisper, 'Amen.' 999 18

Father returned just as the story ended. "Shabbat Shalom!" he said. "Shabbat Shalom!" replied Mother and Habibi and 

Uncle Peter.

At the table Yow stood beside Father. The little black spaniel always stood beside Father for the Sabbath meal. 

He wagged his tail and barked all through the singing of "Sholom Alayhem, Peace be unto you, ye angels of the King of kings on high." But when Father and Habibi sang the Ayshis Hayil, their weekly song of praise to Mother, the dog didn't make a sound. He sat and listened until the very last word was sung.

When the family rose for the chanting of the Kiddush, up came Yow. He rested his forelegs on Father's chair and remained standing until Father had finished chanting the 

Kiddush. Then he waited for Father to bless the Sabbath bread. "A Motxi for Yow," said Father after he had passed a portion of the Sabbath bread to each one at the table. 

Yow caught the Motzi in his mouth and devoured it `at once. "That dog is absolutely wonderful," said Uncle Peter. "He should not be sitting on the floor by himself. He should have a place right here at the table with the rest of us. Any dog that rises for Kiddush and bows his head for prayer and begs for his Motzi, should have a place at the Shabbos table. He should have his own Shabbos chair. I shall build him a Shabbos chair before I leave."

And that's how Yow got his special Shabbos chair. 19

Chapter II HABIBI AND YOW GO FOR A SHABBOS WALK 

Every Shabbos afternoon Mother would take Habibi and his little spaniel, Yow, for a Shabbos walk "just to see what we can see." Habibi loved these Shabbos walks. Mother would never hurry and she always explained all the things they saw. They would stop and admire the flowers. They would watch the beautiful butterflies circling lazily above their heads. When they were tired they would sit down on the grass and rest. And always they would sing the loveliest

Shabbos songs. Habibi knew them well for he practiced them all week long. "Yom ze l'Yisroel, La, la-la, la, la, Shabbos m'nuhaw, La, la-la, la, la" 20

One Shabbos afternoon Mother could not go for her

Shabbos walk. She had a headache and told Habibi to go out and play with Yow.

Habibi and Yow were very sorry. They tried to be as quiet and helpful as they could. But Habibi missed his 

Shabbos walk. "Can't we go for a Shabbos walk by ourselves?" he whispered to his little dog as soon as Mother had dozed off to sleep. 

Yow leaped with joy. He, too, missed his Shabbos walk.

So they started off together. They walked and they walked until they reached a large, open field. In the field were some bright yellow flowers. "I wonder what these flowers are," said Habibi. "They look like golden feathers!"

Habibi bent over to smell the golden feathers that seemed to be nodding in the breeze. "Ker-choooo!" he sneezed. "Ker-choooo!" He sneezed and sneezed, and Yow who had poked his nose among the yellow flowers began to sneeze, too. 

Fat, round bumblebees buzzed around Habibi's ears. "Go away!" he cried waving them off and running away. "Go away!"

It was lonesome without Mother. The field looked so large and the flowers no longer interested him without Mother there to tell him their names and explain how they grow.

The boy and his little dog were both very tired. They sat down in the grass to rest.

Habibi began to sing the Shabbos songs that Mother had taught him, but somehow, the songs did not sound the same without Mother there. Nothing seemed the same without Mother. 21

He lay down in the grass and soon fell fast asleep. The little black dog lay down beside him and soon he was fast asleep, too. When they awoke the sun was low. Patches of dark gray were gathering in the sky. "Oh!" cried Habibi. "It will soon be dark! We better get back before three stars come out. When the three stars come out, Shabbos will be over and Father will be looking for us for Havdallah."

Yow looked worried. He knew that Father always expected them home for Havdallah, the prayer he says when Shabbos is over. The little spaniel looked up at the sky. 

There was not even one star there, so he knew it was not yet time for the Havdallah prayer.

Habibi wanted to start for home immediately, but he had no idea where he was or which way to go. He looked to the right and he looked to the left. He didn't remember from which direction they had come so he didn't know how to return. 

The boy was frightened. His big eyes filled with tears and he began to cry.

When Yow saw his little master cry he came closer and licked the boy's hands with his moist, pink tongue. "Don't cry," the dog seemed to say. "Trust me. I'll get you home."

But Habibi was too frightened to heed what the little dog tried to tell him and he sat down on the grass and cried and cried.

Soon a large raindrop splashed on his nose. Another raindrop splashed on his curly red hair. "It's beginning to rain," the boy said, jumping up from the grass. "It's beginning to rain.” 22

Yow sniffed the ground and ran first in one direction and then in another direction, trying to discover which was the way home. He seemed to be going around and around in circles. It was getting dark and chilly.

Suddenly the dog began to bark and scamper. He had located the path that would lead them home. He tugged at 

Habibi's trousers but the boy would not follow him. "How do you know that's the right way?" asked Habibi. "How do you know, Yow? What if you're wrong?"

But the little dog kept on barking and barking. He tugged at Habibi's trousers and he tugged at Habibi's blouse. 

He ran on a few yards and then returned. He barked softly and then he barked louder and louder. Still the boy would not budge. 

The raindrops came faster and faster and Habibi's red curls stuck flat on his head. 

Finally, the boy started to follow. "I hope you're right, Yow," he whispered. He was too frightened to stay there alone. "I hope you're right because it's getting dark and soon we won't be able to see at all. Then how can we get back for Havdallah?"

Yow was sure he knew the way home. He ran on friskily ahead. At times Habibi found it difficult to keep up with the little dog. The boy's legs were so tired and the rain trickled down his forehead and got into his eyes.

Yow seemed to understand the plight of his master and he poked his warm nose into the trembling hands of the boy. "You have me," he tried to say. "Now don't you worry. 

I'll get you home." 23

They walked and they walked and they walked. It was getting darker and darker. Habibi knew that way up in the sky, under the black clouds, were three little stars struggling to come through so that Father could say the Havdallah prayer. He knew that Father must be looking all over for him.

Soon they came to a street that seemed familiar. Habibi knew he had seen the houses and stores before. Suddenly he recognized the store where Mother bought her bread. "I know where we are now!" the boy cried joyfully. "I know where we are, Yow! This is the bakery where 

Mother buys her Shabbos Hallah. Our house is just across the way, on the other street."

The little black dog began wagging his tail. He was as happy as the boy. He barked and barked at the top of his voice.

It may have been Yow's barking that brought Father hurrying toward them, for out of the dark, Habibi heard 

Father's voice. "Habibi, where have you been?" called Father. "We have been looking for you all afternoon." "Father!" cried the boy. "Father!" His voice quivered. 

He couldn't say another word.

Father lifted the dripping-wet boy in his big, strong arms and Habibi felt safe and warm in Father's embrace. "We were lost," Habibi repeated over and over. "We were lost, we were lost-" "I told Mother you two must have gone for a Shabbos walk," Father said. "And I told her no harm would come to our little boy as long as he had Yow with him." 24

Father carried Habibi into the house and Mother quickly helped him change into warm, dry clothes. "Have you said the Havdallah prayer?" Habibi asked as soon as he was comfortable and dry. "I looked for the three stars but they didn't come out." "You know we wouldn't say the Havdallah prayer without you," said Father. "How could we take leave of Queen 

Sabbath without our little boy to hold the candle and light her way and send her off with song?" 

Father brought the silver spice-box and the red and white braided Havdallah candle, and Mother brought the wine and the olivewood Havdallah cup. Habibi held the candle high while Father chanted the prayer that bids farewell to Queen Sabbath and sends her on her way with light and song and the fragrance of sweet-smelling spices.

When the lights went on, Habibi's face was full of smiles again. His voice sang out cheerily:

Sho-vu-aw Tov! Sho-vu-aw Tov! Sho-vu-aw Tov! Sho-vu-aw Tov! which means a good and a pleasant week. 

Before the spice-box and the candle and the olivewood 

Havdallah cup were put away, they all joined in singing about Elijah, the Prophet: 

E-li-yo-hu ha-no-vee, E-li-yo-hu ha-Tish-bee, E-li-yo-hu ha-Gil-a-dee, Bim-hay-ro yo-voh ay-lay-nu, Im Mo-shee-ah ben Do-vid.

"I'll never go for a Shabbos walk without you, Mother dear," said Habibi after he and Yow had had their supper. "If it had not been for Yow, I never would have found my way back home." 

Mother hugged her little boy and kissed him. She patted the little black dog who lay cuddled in the chair beside them. "You're a good pal, Yow," she said. "I knew I could trust you to take care of my little fellow. We'll have to put a medal on you." 

And that's how Yow became a hero. 26

Chapter III YOW CELEBRATES ROSH HASHANNAH IN HIS OWN WAY 

Yow, Habibi's little black spaniel, was too young to understand everything his master told him. He could not understand, for instance, how the world could have a birthday. The world was not like a boy or a girl or a lady or a man.

Yet, that's just what Habibi had told him when he returned from the synagogue with his parents and Uncle 

Peter on Rosh Hashannah day. "Shanah Tovah! Happy New Year!" Habibi had called in his cheeriest voice. 

The little dog did not know what Shanah Tovah meant, so Habibi proceeded at once to explain. 27

"Today is the world's birthday," he said. "Instead of 'Happy Birthday!' we say 'Happy New Year!'"

Habibi could see by the expression in Yow's eyes that the little spaniel still did not understand, so he sat Yow on a high, high stool and he repeated the words that the rabbi had spoken after the Shofar was sounded that morning. "This day the world was created," Habibi said. "On this day all stand before God. And God opens a big, big book and everything,-EVERYTHING we have done is written there." 

The little dog tried to look intelligent. He blinked his eyes and cocked his head. He raised one ear and then he raised his other ear. He shifted this way and he shifted that way. He tried patiently to follow what his master was saying. "Do you see this finger?" Habibi said, wiggling his forefinger for the little dog to see.

Yow perked up and began wagging his stubby tail. He thought the lecture was over. He thought Habibi was ready to play. "This is no play," Habibi said sternly. "Just look at this finger. Every night it goes to heaven and writes in a big, big book all the things I have done all day, good and bad. On Rosh Hashannah, when the big, big book is open, everything is written there. Not a single thing is hidden, not one thing is forgotten. Our own finger writes all our good deeds and all our bad deeds in that big book."

The little dog began at last to understand. He had visions of a huge book hanging overhead. "Does my forepaw go to heaven each night?" he wondered, glancing down at his little black paw. "Is there a 28

record of all the things I have done? Did my forepaw write about the little squirrels I chased and their nuts that I scattered in the park?" His face became troubled and serious. "Beginning today, we have ten days in which to make good the wrongs we have done," Habibi continued solemnly. "We have ten days before the great day of Yom Kippur when we fast and ask God to forgive all our sins. If we want 

God to forgive us, we must go to those whom we have wronged and get their forgiveness. And we must resolve never, never to repeat our sins."

Habibi was impressed with the sound of his own voice. 

He sounded so grown-up and important. In his ears still quivered the shrill sounds of the Shofar, "T-T-T-T00000!" and the sweet singing of the choir. "L'Shanah Tovah Tikosevu!" called Grandmother, bursting into the room. "May you be inscribed for a good year!" "Grandmother!" cried Habibi. "When did you come? 

I didn't hear you-" "Of course you didn't," said Grandmother. "You were too busy with your little dog. We've come to have Rosh 

Hashannah dinner with you. We're all here."

To be sure they were all there, two grandmothers, two grandfathers, aunts, uncles and cousins. They were greeting each other joyfully: "L'Shanah tovah tikosevu, a good and happy year. 

L'Shanah tovah tikosevu, a year of health and cheer."

The house was full of flowers which they had sent for 

Rosh Hashannah, and stacks of New Year cards lay unopened on the desk.

Uncle Peter was starved, as usual. He was the first to go into the diningroom. 29

"Round, red apples," he said, admiring the bowl of shining apples in the center of the table. "And honey, too!" "Apples and honey for a round, sweet year!" said Mother.

Everything on the table was round. The Hallahs were round. They were not twisted like the Shabbos loaves. The egg barley in the Yom Tov soup were round. The tayglah, the honey-balls, were round. Even the honey-cake was round today. "Round and sweet for a round, sweet year," Habibi kept repeating. "Round and sweet for a round, sweet year."

Although the dinner was delicious and Yow sat in his Shabbos chair that Uncle Peter had built for him, the little spaniel was impatient for the meal to end. He wanted to get away, to run and frisk and bark.

When the last word of the grace, the prayer after the meal, was said, out rushed the little dog, wagging his stubby tail and barking and barking at the top of his voice. "Happy New Year!" he barked at a little girl. 

The girl was frightened and began to cry. "Happy New Year! Happy New Year!" barked Yow to a man, jumping up at him in the friendliest way. "Go 'way!" said the man. "You're dirtying my suit with your muddy paws." "Happy New Year! Happy New Year!" barked Yow at everyone who passed by, but no one understood him and everyone brushed him off. 

He ran out into the street. "Happy New Year! Happy New Year!" he barked at the automobiles that drove by. But the drivers of the automobiles did not understand either. They scowled and swerved to one side to avoid running the little dog over. "Get off the road, you crazy little dog! Do you want to get killed?" they shouted. 30

One after another the drivers of the automobiles skidded and scolded as Yow ran into their path, barking and chasing each car. Yow barked louder and louder for he wanted the drivers to understand he was greeting them on this Rosh 

Hashannah day.

A little speckled setter soon joined him in the middle of the street. He knew what Yow was saying, so he barked, "Happy New Year!" too.

Now there were two dogs chasing and barking at each automobile. What a noise they made barking together! They barked so loud, that a third little dog, a bushy brown collie ran into the street and added his greeting to theirs. "Happy New Year!" they all barked together. "Happy 

New Year!"

The whole street was in an uproar. Three dogs barking together, automobile horns blowing, gears shifting and scraping, angry drivers shouting,-what a turmoil!

One by one the automobiles lined up after each other, unable to proceed. The drivers got out of their cars and tried to force the three little dogs off the street, but the dogs barked louder and jumped up at them trying to make them understand.

It was precisely at that moment that Habibi appeared. 

He saw the long line of cars and heard the threats of the angry drivers. He spied his little dog, Yow, at once. "YOW!!!" he cried, panic-stricken. "Yow! come here this minute!" 31

Yow was an obedient dog. Much as he wanted to continue his fun, much as he wanted to continue barking, "Happy New Year!", he knew that when his master calls, he must respond immediately. "What's the meaning of this?" asked Habibi, very, very angry at his little dog. "Do you want to get killed? Haven't

I taught you never to play in the street? Those automobiles could have run you over." 99 The other two dogs, seeing their leader leave his post, followed him off the street.

The automobiles started moving. Order and quiet were restored.

Yow followed Habibi into the house. Habibi would not speak to him. The little dog knew that his master was angry with him but he could not see where he had done any wrong. 

He looked pleadingly into Habibi's eyes and tried to tell him that this, being Rosh Hashannah, he wanted merely to be friendly and to greet everyone "Happy New Year!" If 

Habibi would only understand. If Habibi would only smile and tell him he was forgiven. How can a dog celebrate the 

New Year? He cannot go to the synagogue to pray. He is not allowed to hear the blowing of the Shofar. He can only be very happy and bark his New Year's greetings. 

And that's how Yow celebrated Rosh Hashannah. 32

Chapter IV YOW MAKES SURE TO BE IN THE SUKKAH FOR KIDDUSH 

This was the eve of Sukkos, the great thanksgiving festival, the holiday of joy and gladness. "Give thanks unto the

Across the sky, from one end to the other, flashed a streak of lightning like a cord of fire, seeming to split the heavens in two. "Mother! Father! Uncle Peter!" called the boy. "The sky has opened! The sky has opened! Come, quick! I can see the Ten Commandments!"

Mother, Father and Uncle Peter came running up the stairs, startled by the boy's sudden outcry. "I saw the Ten Commandments, Uncle Peter, just like you said I would." "Habibi," said Mother gently, taking the boy in her arms. "You have had a dream. You must have eaten too much at dinner." 105

"It wasn't a dream," Habibi insisted. "It wasn't a dream. Just at the stroke of twelve the sky opened, and I saw the Ten Commandments!" 

Mother looked at Father. Father looked at Uncle Peter. 

Uncle Peter smiled at Habibi. "To be sure," said Uncle Peter. "This is Shavuos. 

This is the night when the heavens open. Do you mean to tell me, young fellow, that you and Yow were awake all this time, waiting for the sky to open so that you might see the Ten Commandments?" "You should have seen that sky, Uncle Peter. You should have seen that sky," said Habibi. 

And that's how Habibi and Yow saw the heavens open at midnight on Shavuos. 106

Aadir Hu. Song, "Glorious is God!" sung at the Seder ceremony on Passover eve.

Ayn Kelohaynu, Ayn Kodonaynu. The first words of the hymn, "There is None Like Our God," sung in the synagogue on Sabbaths and Festivals. 

Ayshis Hayil. Part of the last chapter of the Book of 

Proverbs, sung traditionally on the eve of the Sabbath in tribute to the woman of the household. 

Bar Mitzvah. Literally, "Son of the Commandment."

A boy becomes Bar Mitzvah on the Sabbath nearest his 13th birthday at which time he assumes his religious responsibility. 

Blintzes. Thin pancakes filled with cheese; traditionally eaten on the Shavuous festival. 

Bo hay-naw. Come here! 

Boker tov. Good morning. 

Bokser. St. John's bread. A hard, dry fruit which grows in Palestine and is eaten on Hamishah Asar 

B'Shevat, the Jewish Arbor Day. 

Dreidle. A Hanukkah spinning top on which are the

Hebrew letters, nun, gimmel, hay and shin. 

Eretz Yisroel. The Land of Israel, Palestine. 

Esrogim. Plural of "esrog," citron. The citron is one of the four products of the earth used on the Feast of Tabernacles. 

Gefilte fish. Specially prepared fish; usually eaten on the Sabbath and on the Festivals.

Grogger. A noise-maker used by children each time the name of Haman is mentioned during the reading of the Book of Esther on Purim. 

Habibi. (pronounced Ha-bee-bee.) A term of endearment meaning, "My Beloved."

Had Gadyoh. Song, "An Only Kid," sung at the conclusion of the Seder ceremony on Passover eve. 

Haggaddah. Narration. The name of the book which contains the Seder service. 

Hallahs. The two loaves of Sabbath bread. 

Hamentaschen. Three-cornered cakes filled with poppy-seed and honey; eaten on Purim. 

Hamishah Asar B'Shevat. The fifteenth day of the month of Shevat; Jewish Arbor Day in Palestine which is also called the New Year for the Trees. 

Hanukkah. The Feast of Lights; also called the Feast of Dedication. This festival commemorates the victory of the Maccabbees over the Syrians, 165

B.C.E. 

Hanukkah gelt. Coins given to children on Hanukkah. 

Haroses. A mixture of grated apples, nuts, cinnamon and wine, eaten during the Seder ceremony on Passover eve. It symbolizes the mortar mixed by the Israelites during their bondage in Egypt.

Havdallah. Literally, "Separation." The prayer re cited after dusk on Saturday to declare that the

Sabbath is ended and a new week begun. 

Kelev raw. Naughty dog. 

Kelev tov. Good dog. 

Kiddush. Sanctification prayer ushering in the Sabbath and the holidays.

Kittel. White ritual robe worn by the head of the household when he conducts the Seder service; also worn by the pious on New Year and the Day of Atonement.

Knaydel. Matzoh-meal dumplings eaten on Passover.

Omer. Observed as the "Scholars' Festival" on which picnics are arranged for school children who usually carry bows and arrows.

Mitzvah. Literally, "Commandment." A meritorious deed. 

Modeh Ani. "I give thanks." First words of a morning prayer recited by children.

Motzi. Prayer made before eating bread. Colloquially used to denote portion of bread over which the Motzi prayer has been made. 

Nahit. Chick-peas; usually eaten on Purim. 

Ness godol hayah shom. “A great miracle occurred there." 

Pesah. The Festival of Passover commemorating the exodus from Egypt.

Rosh Hashannah. The Jewish New Year; also called the "Day of Judgment," the "Day of Remembrance" and the "Day of the Blowing of the

Shofar." On this day the Jew considers his past deeds and resolves to improve in the coming year. 

Seder. The "Order of Service" contained in the 

Haggaddah. The first and second nights of Passover are called "Seder nights." The Seder service is a charming home ceremony in which young and old re-enact in song and story the miracle of 

Israel's departure from Egypt.

Seudah. Family feast usually associated with Purim. 

Shabbat Shalom. Sabbath greeting used by the Jews in Palestine meaning, "A Sabbath of Peace."

Shabbos. The Sabbath, beginning at sundown on Friday and continuing until sundown on Saturday. 

Shabbos Shirah. The Sabbath on which the Song of 

Moses is read.

Sholom Alayhem. "Peace be unto you." A Sabbath song of peace sung on the eve of the Sabbath.

Shanah Tovah. New Year greeting meaning, "A good year." 

Shayo al ha-keesay. Sit on the chair! 

Shavuos. Feast of Weeks; Pentecost. The festival when the first fruits were offered at the Temple in Jerusalem; also commemorates the giving of the Ten Commandments on Mt. Sinai.

Rosh Hashannah and at the conclusion of Yom Kippur. 

Shovuaw tov. Greeting for the new week: "A good week!"

Sukkos. The Feast of Tabernacles; also called the

Festival of Ingathering, in celebration of the harvest. It is a festival of joy and thanksgiving. 

Talis. A prayer-shawl with fringes; worn during worship. 

Tayglah. Honey-balls; a confection eaten on Rosh Hashannah. 

Tayn lee sefer. Give me a book!

Torah. The Five Books of Moses. Commonly applied to the whole Bible and the entire field of Jewish learning.

Tzimmes. Carrots cooked with prunes or potatoes. 

Torah is die beste schoire. (Yiddish) from a folk-song; "Torah is the best merchandise." 

Yom tov. A holy day.

Yom Kippur. Day of Atonement; the most solemn day of the Jewish year. A day of fasting and prayer. 108 ..
