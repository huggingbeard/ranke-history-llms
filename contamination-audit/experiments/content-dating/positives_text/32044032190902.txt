HOW APPELLATE COURTS DECIDE CASES. 

PART I. 

By KARL N. LLEWELLYN. 

I take the position that the arts of law, the ways of doing the jobs of law, lie at the present time very largely in an unspoken tradition which we, even those of us who are in the middle of doing any piece of law work, don't know too much about in terms of isolating the particular parts of it for communication to anybody who doesn't know as much as we do, or more. And I regard that as a lamentable misfortune. I think that any craft is better handled when its practitioners know something articulate about what it is that they are doing and how it is done, and I think that the neophytes as they come in desperately need information as to how any craft of law is practiced. 

Among those crafts of law which thus rest in a practically unspoken tradition is the art of deciding cases in appellate courts. The one thing that the appellate court has got on all the rest of us is that the appellate court doesn't renew itself all at one chunk, but it gets one new judge and another new one or two new ones at once and breaks them in to the tradition of that particular court. 

And slowly, they do break in to that particular tradition, and you will find that in any particular court the tradition is distinctive in that it is somewhat different from the way in which the same job is handled in a court just across the state line, for example.

Those who have attempted to tell us about it, who have been in it, I suppose, are chiefly Cardozo and Hutcheson. 

I don't see how anybody can read the opinions of either man and feel that his writings on the subject of how he (1)

was doing his job communicate at all how he did do it, much less how it was being done by his mates. When the practicing man turns to give an exposition of what it is he does, commonly you will find that he gives you a picture of what he did in his tough or puzzling cases, the ones that caused him much trouble. The thing that he practically leaves out in his account to you and even in his account to himself is what he does in the run-of-the-mill stuff that turns up every day. Now, the practicing lawyer is not interested particularly in what happens in the decision of the tough, difficult, peculiar, nontypical case; the practicing lawyer is interested primarily in what happens in the typical, ordinary, run-ofthe-mill, day-to-day case that he is likely to have tomorrow, and nobody who has written on this subject, as far as I am aware, has paid attention to that case. That's the case I want to talk about, because that is what sets the standard pattern from which the peculiar cases vary. 

I want, in my own study of jurisprudence, to bring out a Ben Franklin kind of jurisprudence, the kind of thing that he would be giving us if he were alive today and were a lawyer, giving us Poor Richard's horse sense on how to practice law or how to do the other legal jobs. I have got no use for what my friend Thomas Reed Powell calls "jurisprudence on stilts"; the jurisprudence I am interested in either is no good or else it is of use to you and me in the case we are going to have tomorrow, and for the purpose of trying to get at that I want to test what I have got to say and build what I have to say on and out of the run-ofthe-mill stuff.

Back in 1939 the advance sheets from New York rained on my desk and I happened to turn up a tremendous output that was handed down on July 31st of that year-about 311 pages in the final reports, it was. And as I happened to read those through in moments of relative leisure I found them very exciting, and I worked out the theories I am going to put forward tonight pretty largely on the basis of that material.

Not satisfied with finding what was done in one court or on one day (although I was delighted to have material that varied as much as it did inside one court on one day), I turned over two volumes further, to 241 New York, where the same court was sitting nine months later with three new judges out of seven and a new chief, and I handled an equivalent body of material there to see whether I found the same stuff. I did, practically item for item. 

Wondering whether New York was peculiar, I thereupon took up an equivalent job on the last volume I could get hold of from Massachusetts, the last volume I could get hold of from North Carolina, the last volume I could get hold of from Ohio. I had been out in Ohio and had heard their Chief Justice explain that in Ohio this business was much easier because in Ohio the syllabus stated the law, and not the opinion, so I wanted to see whether it was different under that situation. It wasn't. Then I looked around for a court that was sitting in departments and went to Washington and looked over an equivalent body of law there to see whether it made any difference that there are two courts, for substantial purposes, instead of one. I found no substantial difference. 

But I thought it would be nice to try it out all over again when I was coming down here, on Pennsylvania, so again I went to the last volume on the shelves and I picked out the first hundred pages of that volume as they came in the report, and after I had gotten well into the first hundred pages and had done most of my work I discovered that all the opinions were handed down on a single day, and that the opinions of that day ran through to page 143, so I added some study of the pages from 100 to 143 which is not as intensive as what was done on the first hundred, but which I have drawn on, too. I stopped with the single opinion day of March 20, 1944. You will agree that that is current? You will agree that it is beautifully nonselective to establish any thesis? On the subjects which were up, I won't run through the whole list, but as suggesting that they range around reasonably there is a review of action of a State board, there is a problem on a construction contract, a foreclosure of a conditional sale, four negligence cases-mostly on contributory negligence, control by the court of the handling of a charitable trust, the rights of an informer about an escheat, stockholders' liability after a merger of two corporations only one of which had stockholders' liability, winding up of a partnership, the Labor Relations Act as applied to a Salvation Army shelter, a suit for recovery of a commission from a political boss, a close corporation's acquisition of a minority holder's pledged stock, and a large number of cases on trusts and estates. But when you go through those cases that are headed, "Estate of Jones", or "Estate of Smith", you find that the actual legal points are beautifully varied. There is a remedy of a contestant of a will, rights of tenants in common, apportionment between life tenant and remainderman, the book account rule, attorney's collection, and the statute of limitations, allowance of attorneys' fees, and so on. 

The first question in regard to how appellate courts decide cases is why there should be any such problem. Why doesn't the opinion tell you all you have to know about why

5 appellate courts decide cases? Under certain conditions the opinion does tell you all that; as a practicing man, you need to know and, indeed, it tells you almost all a later court needs to know, under certain conditions. It happens that those conditions are no longer commonly present in these United States. I shall not develop why I think they are not present; I am going to stop with my opinion that in the main they are not present. Under certain conditions, I say, the opinion tells well enough, and it tells well enough for lawyers and it tells well enough for later judges, and it tells well enough for the general public. All of the following three conditions must coincide in order that that may be the case as a regular matter. 

Condition No. 1: All of the relevant rules of law must be well adjusted to the current needs of the times, and they must also be well drawn technically. That condition is rarely true, and even if the others are true the failure of that one to be true commonly knocks out the opinion from giving you the whole story. When that condition is true, it is surprising how sweet and clear the story is, on the face of the opinion, about how the case was decided. To which we shall come later. 

Condition No. 2: When the relevant judicial personnel are well trained, able, upright, and wise. The wisdom is the tough one there. It isn't hard to find them pretty able; it isn't hard to find them upright; it isn't hard to find them pretty well trained; it is tough going to find the wise ones.

Condition No. 3: When the prevailing style of opinion writing in the judicial craft is to seek for the sound rule for the type of problem, as being the first job of the court, instead of seeking for the best available disposition, as between these particular parties, which is technically available. Do I make that distinction? The rule for the type of problem, "Cases of this kind should be decided this way", as distinct from, "Confound it, Jones is a skunk in this case, and Smith ought to win it". Now the court has to

wrestle with both of these things, but I say that when the prevailing style is to pick the rule for the type of problem as the first job, then the other job falls into line by itself. 

But if the court looks first for how to dispose of this particular controversy, then it is likely to be so clear about that that it will reach for anything in sight to "hang its hat" on-which makes trouble later. 

Now I say that there was a time in American law when all these three conditions typically maintained, and it was from that time that our current ideology about the meaning of opinions derives, in the first instance; to wit: that the opinion shows how the court decided the case. That time was, roughly, from about 1820 to about 1850, and the chief exponents probably best known to you, from desultory reading, at least, as well as from your tradition, are Tilghman and Gibson, and I am delighted to be able to pick Tilghman and Gibson as the exponents because they will show that I am dealing with a manner of decision and not with a content of decision. Tilghman and Gibson were politically completely at odds, as you are aware. But both were exponents of the type of judicial manner and craft performance, which I am discussing, and you will find, I think, as you read the opinions of either man and, indeed, of their colleagues, that you get a pretty good idea from the face of the opinion as to why and how the court decided its case. I call that the grand style and it does not depend upon the men being great. In New York, for example, we then had operating in that style a great commercial judge, probably the greatest America had ever seen until this century. We had Cowen. We had also Savage, a good but relatively mediocre man; Nelson, a good but relatively mediocre man; 

Bronson, a complete commercial dumbbell, all writing in substantially the same manner of work, and you can tell why each of them did what he did, except with Bronson, who sometimes obscures it because he was kind of club-footed in his expression.

If you want to take it out of your own State, take the contrast between Marshall and Storey. There is greatness and there is desperate mediocrity in constitutional law, but both in the same "style".

Now, then, for reasons which we do not fully understand—at least I don't and I don't know anybody else who does, but we hope to find out before we die-for reasons which we do not fully understand there came a change in the style, both of judging and of opinion-writing which we can date roughly as marked clearly throughout the country between about 1880 and 1910; a wholly different style from that which prevailed and dominated between 1820 and 1850. 

The marks of the new style are not the mark of your reason on the face of the law, your rules changing always to fit the reason being tested not from within the law but from the life outside the law, to see whether the reason made sense and the rule made sense and wisdom for the country, open discussion of policy, careful attention to procedure but always under the common-law judge's duty of reshaping them to fit their reason and to continue to make sense for the country. Those are the marks of 1820 to 1850; the grand manner. By 1880 to 1910 we find that the marks of judicial opinions and of judging run like this: Order comes first, not justice. Shelter yourself under the law. It is enough to give a precedent. You need not give a reason either for the precedent or for the rule. Denial of one's power in court to create at all; denial that one does any creation at all; denial of one's power to reshape at all; denial that one does any reshaping at all. And the "principles" against which you test specific decisions are not rules-of-life wisdom, but they are rules of order found inside the legal system itself and as such; you don't look outside.

A classic principle of those days, for example, is that preexisting duty, God help us, defeats consideration. You don't ask, "Does it make sense?" You ask, “Is it a legal principle?" And policy, under the style of 1880 to 1910,

is no open concern of the court; if you mention it at all, it is to say, "That is not our concern".

It is from that period that our current ideology derives to the effect that the rules of law alone-alone-plus our dogmatic techniques of distinguishing, telling dictum from decision and the like, are enough to decide our cases. 

This, of course, is hopelessly false ideology. They never have been; doubly, they are not now, but the notion that they are enough derives from that period. You will notice that almost the same form of statement said real truth under the older style, because in the older period you did not say that "the rules" decided cases, you said "the law" decides the cases, largely irrespective of who the judge may be; but you meant the rules plus the reasons for the rules plus the horse sense of the court; whereas in the period 

1890 to 1910 it means the rules alone, and as they sit upon the books; which is insufficient, as we shall see. 

And I may say that out of that period when the court thought that it could do no creation of course came the vicious habit of judges of feeling that their first, if not their only, duty to justice lay in doing the best they could "as between these litigants". Since they had (they thought) no duty to better law and no power to perform that duty to the law if they had it, but since they still felt, being judges, a duty to justice, why, the best they could do was to do the best that they were technically allowed as between the poor devils that were in front of them. And that feeling that the major job of an appellate court is to dispose of this case as between these people, the best they can, still curses the courts today. As we shall also see.

At present we are on our way back to the style of 1840 and have not yet wholly got there. We have a mixed style, in process of vigorous transition. Fifteen years ago if I had made the speech that I am making tonight I would have shocked every one of you to his very gizzard and you would have thrown me out or even lynched me. Todayyou shall see. Maybe you still will.

The first evidence that we are getting back to the style of the grand manner is the recurrence to sense, horse sense, sense outside the law, tested from outside the law and stated quite openly on the face of the opinion as a guide to decision.

Now, does that hold in Pennsylvania? I quote from page 94: "The problem is obviously one that does not lend itself to solution by the application of any particular rules of logic, but calls rather for an answer dictated by practical considerations so far as consistent with legal principles."

On page 81: "The practical question was," (to which the Court will give the answer "What was best for all parties in the circumstances?") That was a matter involving the reservation of a certain portion of a trust and putting it aside to pay rent as it may accrue-rent owing by the estate.

On pages 64 and 65 they don't say, "We want to be practical", but just see if you can build a better practical scheme for solving the situation than they offer you-not in terms of law am I judging, but in terms of sense. "The things to be taken into consideration in determining the compensation to be recovered by an attorney"-this comes home; you ought to understand it-"are the amount and character of the services rendered, the labor, the time, and trouble involved, the character and importance of the litigation, the amount of money or value of the property affected, the professional skill and experience called for and the standing of the attorney in his profession; to which may be added the general ability of the client to pay and the pecuniary benefit derived from the services." Now, that may not be law until the court lays it down, but believe me it was sense before then.

On page 26: "It is a rule of common sense as well as law not to attempt to construe that which needs no construction." hi

I have more illustrations, but I want to know on this particular point, which is quite vital to my argument, whether I am testing the court or whether I am testing individual judges. My major instances derive from opinions by the following. I shall from here on mention no names, but at this point it is vital to mention names. I have an opinion with a nice passage of this kind clean on the nub from Linn, from Hughes, from Drew, from Allen Stearne, from Horace Stern, and from the Chief. I derive the conclusion that it is general to the court. I had only one opinion from Patterson to play with, you remember, so it isn't strange that that single one didn't happen to touch on this particular question. Every other one had within 

143 pages handed down in one day. I say I am entitled to claim that this piece of recurrence to the grand tradition is in full swing in Pennsylvania today, and I expect to see more of it. That last is a guess.

There is, in addition to this matter of horse sense which is referred to, a very serious trouble to be observed on another line, if you please, of material to be looked at. 

Contrary to the ideology inherited, then, from 1880, the rules and the precedents plus the accepted techniques for handling rules and precedents do not today, in most cases, alone decide. You see the appeal to horse sense outside the law on the face of the opinion. The rules and the precedents don't tell you, then, what the rule of law in Pennsylvania is, all by themselves; neither do they, all by themselves, decide the case. I shall demonstrate that at comfortable leisure in just a moment from the other end, not showing simply that there is another factor in the picture but showing that out of the rules as they stand plus the techniques of handling them and the authorities you cannot determine what the rule in Pennsylvania is. 

But pursuant to the ideology inherited from 1890 and contrary to that of 1840 the court does still, because the transition is not complete, recurrently act and talk as if the rules and precedents and the accepted technique for

handling them were enough to determine what the rule is and to decide the case. This greatly confuses all but the absolutely best of the lawyers, and it often enough confuses the court itself.

Thus, where you have a case or a set of cases which seem to state the law or to contain it in their holdings, let me show some of the things that could have happened to you in regard to that case or cases on which you had thus relied if you had hit the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania on March 20, 1944. And I may say that the same thing could have happened to you if you had come before any other Supreme Court in these United States under the same conditions on any current day.

You may find that the case cited stood for the whole theory of a whole situation that it laid down, reaching infinitely beyond the exact holding of the case. That is what happened on page 31. The case is the King Estate, and it reads, "In Buist's Estate, 297 Pa. 537, 541, 147 A. 608, we decided 'The merger of two or more corporations is neither a sale nor a liquidation of corporate property, but a consolidation of properties, powers, and facilities of the constituent companies.' We specifically held (542): 'the issuance of the new capital stock is merely the issuance of new evidence of ownership to the shareholders. Accepting shares of stock in the merged company is not tantamount to a distribution or division of assets which calls for an apportionment between a life tenant and remainderman'." Now, that is just one thing. Now comes the wide theory. "Also (543), 'The life tenant is not entitled to any division until (1) the increased value is declared as a cash dividend, or (2) distributed in the form of a stock dividend, or (3) the affairs of the corporation are wound up so that assets are distributed to those entitled to receive them."". You will notice that you can't hold (3) in the same case that you hold (1) and (2), or don't you notice that? ""Or (4) there is a sale of this stock so that the connection of shareholder is entirely severed.'" Now, that is the rule reaccepted and

laid down to test all the other Pennsylvania cases, and it isn't a "holding"; it is a statement of a theory of the entire situation out of which one small piece, the prior holding, flows. The court proceeds to test two Pennsylvania, one 

Delaware, and one English case by that theory, and to treat those cases accordingly. 

On the other hand, you may find that you can't trust the case that you have even for what on its face and its stated facts it held itself out as holding. This is on the same page. On the same page the broad theory that I have just read to you proceeds to be applied as follows: "Reliance is placed upon what we stated in Daily's Estate, 323 Pa. 42, 186 A. 754, in connection with the respective rights of life tenant and remainderman in a merger and reorganization. But here again, if the opinion of the lower court (24 D. & C. 628) be examined, it will be found that the stock was 'being distributed in kind to the parties entitled'." 

That is not in the prior opinion of this court. If you had rested with that you would have had to think that this was a shift from the rule there laid down, but we go into the opinion of the lower court and determine from that the way to distinguish our own prior case. Now, this is one page, one prior authority like this (clean on) and one prior authority like that (clear off).

Or else you may find with regard to this case that you have got that the rule of law will prove to be taken from any language used by the court anywhere without regard to whether that language represented the holding or a dictum. 

Thus, for example, on page 10 language from prior cases is indiscriminately cited as "we said", and "we held", and is used equally to lay down the rule of Pennsylvania which then governs the reasoning. And on page 127 we find that the rule is laid down in terms of what "we held", and again in terms of what "we said", and then in terms of "the tendency of our decisions" (which means that we neither held nor said what we now will take as the law of Pennsylvania). I may say that I have not done a good job of chas-

I will guarantee that for anybody that wants to risk enough money to make it worth my while. 

Or else what you find the court terming as giving Pennsylvania's law may not be the language nor yet the holding of a case, but something altogether different, and to me very lovely-a thing more prevalent, I think, in this Pennsylvania body of material than with any other material I have intensively examined. 

You may find that what it states the law of Pennsylvania to be built on is not the rule laid down anywhere along the line, but the reason given for a prior rule or holding or announced in the course of its discussion, which is then developed and applied in terms of the reason and not of the rule, and this occurs on page 46, on page 61, and on page 62. 

Or else, when you get your authority that you are relying on, you may find that the court instead will seize and develop analogies from other cases as being the law, though the cases are concededly not in point and have no language quotable that is in point. Thus, for example, on page 80 we "compare" three cases, give no other authorities, and use the proposition for which the "compare" reference was made. On page 84 we do the same. We are stating the law of Pennsylvania, you see, as we go along, and using it to decide the case. On page 40 we "see" three cases and no other authority is given, and on page 71 we "see" no less. than nine cases which the court does not feel it safe or decent to cite as flat authority, but from which the proposition is derived on which the court builds. 

On the other hand, the fact that a point was actually held under the stricter tests of holding and dictum is so important to its being authority for the rule that the court finds a real value on pages 39 and 68 in setting forth the

facts and precise issue, so that any one can see that that point was really and exactly decided.

Indeed, the court will distinguish your case if the actual holding is not sharply in point on the facts. So it distinguished on page 31 two prior cases that had language that had been relied on by counsel, and on page 104, again, it distinguishes because of a difference on the facts. 

On the other hand, the court may also-always on the same day-decide that it will not distinguish in terms of a distinction which you press upon it and which concededly is present on the facts, as was done on page 110 (after page 

104 had done the requested distinguishing).

So that the specific ground on which the court chose to rest the case can either be played up as vital, as it was on page 30, or it can be wholly disregarded and thrown out, as it was on page 31, or it can be reformulated, as it was on page 117. That last is not quite a fair citation because in the original case it had not been a major ground. What the court did do, however, was to take what was done in the prior case and reformulate it with a materially different emphasis to produce the rule laid down and used in the case in hand.

Finally, and somewhat charmingly, the court reminds us on page 31 (my italics and not the court's) that, “We have never overruled or modified this decision." I have to judge from that that the court is familiar even with the modification and overruling of other decisions. 

To cap this particular thing, when you have finally worked out what the rule is, then comes the question of certainty of decision in the particular case, and on this I am so glad that I got into the latter portion of the material, because I now want to quote you from page 135. "The rule in such cases is, as Judge Gest said in Williamson's Estate, 3 D. & C. 1, adopted by the Superior Court, 82 Pa. Superior 

Ct. 444, 446, that' such a broad interpretation will not be given to the bequest, unless it clearly appears from the context to have been the meaning of the testator, or

unless the will would otherwise be inoperative.'" So we now know what the law is, and I may note that that one comes not from this court but from a lower court. "On the application of that principle to the record before us, the six members of this court who heard argument are evenly divided."

I am now prepared to urge that I have demonstrated that the rule of law which prevails in Pennsylvania in the case that you are going to bring up tomorrow is not to be determined from the authorities plus the accepted techniques for handling the authorities alone, and also that after you have got the rule of law you are still not certain what effect the rule of law will have upon your case. 

That sounds, when you put it that way, pretty rough, and why does it sound rough? The reason it sounds rough is because every last one of you has been trained in the silly ideology that the rules of law decide cases, which they never did and never will. For the courts are not running wild around here. As I went through these cases I found that at least eight out of 10 of the actual decisions were practically foregone conclusions; they seemed so to me. There were about two cases in 10 where I thought people could differ. 

Suppose it was three out of 10: what of it? On about half the cases through here I threw up my hands wondering either how the court below could decide the way it had (when the case was reversed), or how there could be an appeal after the decision of the court below; cases so absolutely clear for any court-maybe not for the lawyer that was looking at them for a client-but for a court. 

Look at them: one answer. The only question is, what ground will it be rested on? There runs, I say, a rather pleasant feeling of predictability and considerable certainty of result throughout the material. It does not rest either upon the certainty of the rules in detail nor yet upon our techniques for handling them alone. 

What are the certain things that we can see? You can see without any question that the court is moving and mov- ני

ing unmistakably inside the frame of a legal system which you and I are both familiar with, and which limits and guides and at times, indeed, dictates the decision; just no question about it. The court does not look at the facts in front of it the way a layman looks at facts, it doesn't see issues the way a layman sees issues. The court knows that there is a difference between an executor and a trustee, and that is an extremely important difference to the court. 

What layman knows that or feels it? The court sees conditions in contracts and thinks promptly in terms of substantial performance—that is, of doctrine, a doctrine with cases and law on it. What layman thinks in those terms? The court knows the difference between a life tenant and a remainderman, things about tenants in common and their very queer respective rights, sizes things up in terms of these categories which are to a layman Greek. In other words, the whole seeing of the facts is conditioned by the accepted categories of our legal system, the peculiar categories of the American legal system, and if I had gotten into equity cases I would have found that the court was seeing things in the peculiar categories of Pennsylvania equity, which differs from any other equity on earth. Through those spectacles you and I see with the court and we differ therein from the entire City of Philadelphia outside and from most of the State of Pennsylvania, and that is a tremendous factor in holding decisions steady and predictable and in making the particular individual legal officer relatively unimportant: to know that every one of the judges is seeing his cases in these ways.

There is a case in volume 348, which I have no business to discuss tonight but which I shall just mention, in which the majority and the dissent go into each other's hair with a vigor quite unusual in this court's opinions. It had to do with the application of the parol evidence rule, and interests here because both the difference and the righteous indignation rest upon the failure of just what we are here discussing: a common way of sizing the prob-

lem up. This time that common sizing up of what it was about broke down completely. But they were so used to it that they couldn't be reasonable with one another when it muffed. The Chief, dissenting, thought he saw a "con- tract", judged accordingly, and was outraged. The majority just didn't see any contract and stood firm. And because of their classification in that picture, "contract" or "no contract"-a matter of legal classification-the two sides just went at each other with fists. It is a lovely thing to read, exciting for the outsider. It must have been for the insiders, too, I imagine. But for us here its rarity is enough to bring out the fact that steadiness of classification of issue is of tremendous importance in holding decisions steady. Doesn't it make that point? And that the fact that the court is seeing these things in our given legal categories common to them and us, holds things level to a huge degree.

Now, that sets up the picture of the appellate judge in his aspect as a lawyer and it simultaneously sets up the first principle for advocacy which I can derive from this material. You see, I built this talk not for the judges who are here and who embarrass me by being here-though I am glad they are—but for you lawyers who are here. The first principle for advocacy is plainly that to be safe you require as one condition—as one condition-to make a case on the law as technically understood in our system which is a technically solid case. Your case must stand up under technical classification and legal results.

The thing which lawyers tend to overlook is that there commonly may be made on any matter worth litigating at least four cases which are thus technically sound on the law. Frequently, there are more than that, but I put four as a conservative estimate. So that your opponent commonly has a case which is technically sound on the law, too, if he is any good. And lawyers are constantly fooling themselves into thinking that merely because they have a case that is technically sound on the law, therefore they ought

to win. We have now demonstrated that that is no basis. for winning. It is simply a precondition of safety. It is one condition. But it is a condition. 

The second thing that I find running quite clearly through this material, both in the conscious expression which I have quoted and also in other phases, is that within the field of the technically available possibilities (which are always more than one unless the case has been brought by a lunatic) the court reaches for that solution, within the field of the technically available possibilities, that makes sense, sense to the court, and when the sense is clear the court feels pretty free to exploit and develop prior language though it be mere dictum by analogy, by distinction, by any recognized use of any prior authority, by any other recognized line of technical work. And when they do that and when they don't do that depends in first instance on whether the sense is clear. And the direction in which they do it is practically always in the direction of what they see as a sensible result. And they include in the lines of work they are willing to exploit and develop the general field of authorities, which is what "compare" means when they cite some cases or, making a fresh ruling, citing no authority at all. PART II. 

May I call back to your mind the trend generally of what was said last time when I was talking about the simple and easy pieces of analysis of the process of deciding appellate cases and the simple and easy answers or helps that one got for advocacy out of the analysis? You remember I put it to you on the basis of an intensive study of the work of the March 20 opinion day of last year that there showed in the Court a continuing and frank and open quest for sense in the result of a case, and a frank and open quest for sense in the rule that was laid down or that was to be laid down, a thing which would have been regarded as completely disreputable either to do or admit doing fifty years ago, but which a hundred years ago was the standard procedure-testing any rule of law and any decision in any case by the sense of the community around it and whether it made sense for the community around it, and not merely by whether it was in accordance with some rule of law announced either by statute or by prior cases. 

And we saw in addition to that that among the very many possibilities open in most cases that get litigated under the existing doctrine, the Court made quite definitely, but not consistently, a choice among the doctrine possibilities in terms of something that made sense. 

We saw, on the other hand, and no less clearly, a steadfast effort, and a pretty successful one, to stay within the legal framework as it was given at the time that the case came up-first in regard to diagnosing the situation and the issue, which was always done in legal terms, terms familiar under the legal system as it stood, under the rules (29)

as they stood before the case came up, and secondly a steadfast effort to stay within the rules whether of statute or of case law as they had been received up to the time of the decision.

But we also saw that if you tried to find the rule of Pennsylvania on a given point out of the authorities alone- I repeat, out of the authorities alone-plus the standard techniques for handling the authorities, we saw that you got nowhere, because practically anything could happen, from distinguishing down to a razor-edge distinction on up to building the rule of Pennsylvania out of the "tendency of our cases; compare this, that, and the other thing," without even language used. So that it wasn't until you put together the conception of the general legal system as she stood and the doctrine framework and added to it the quest for sense that you got any safety, any real safety, as an advocate in building a case for the court. 

And we wound up with the proposition that there was a perceptible real joy in the court's performance when it hit upon, either by finding or by freshly framing, a rule which simultaneously was clear of application and made beautiful sense in application to life; and that if you had that kind of a rule to rest on you could figure that you had your case won without much trouble.

So that we set up as counsels for the advoate that to be safe he needs a sound technical case, because in most instances his adversary will have just as sound a technical case; he needs also sense and justice to his case of a sort that will satisfy the court to want to decide his way; and, best, if he can find the type of rule I have just described to rest on, then he is really safe.

When we got done with that, you see, all we got set out was: here are certain definite factual ways in which the court is plainly operating, and if we can get two things working together, to wit, a sound technical position and good sense as seen by the court, we can tell where the case

is coming out, we can tell that in advance, and we can be safe in building our argument that way. Unfortunately, however, in Pennsylvania as in most of these United States --and if there be any of the United States of which this is not true I have not yet met that state nor its law-unfortunately in these United States we do not have too many rules which fit that lovely description which gives joy to the court, the rule which is clear in application and makes beautiful sense in life, both at once. Unfortunately, most of our rules aren't like that; they are either unclear or they don't make too much sense or they make sense part of the time and not the rest of the time because they are overboard, or they make sense part of the time and not the rest of the time because they are overnarrow. In a word, most of the time when the court goes looking for a technical position to decide the case on it is going to find that the technical position is more or less at odds with the court's judgment of what is good sense. And, frequently enough, court's feeling for justice in the situation, or at least for decency-justice is a poor word, as I said last time, to use in regard to courts, and I will come back to that-unfortunately, I say, the court's sense of decency is frequently going to be vigorously at odds with what the rules seem to indicate as the appropriate legal decision, and then the court is in a jam.

No judge who has ever sat upon the bench but knows how uncomfortable he gets when that situation becomes conscious to him, because no judge worth his salt ever sat upon the bench who did not recognize two duties-one duty as to the law, and it is a clear duty, it is a vital duty; the other duty as to justice in the outcome, and it is a clear duty and it is a vital duty. And if he can't satisfy the two at once he doesn't have a comfortable night. If there are any judges here, all they have got to do is to say yes, because I won't believe them if they say no.

Now, when a judge is faced with that situation it is plain that he is in trouble, because he has got to be untrue

to at least half of what he knows to be his duty and comfort himself with being true to the other half unless in that rare and beautiful moment which sometimes occurs he finds a fresh creative answer that somehow reframes the doctrine to which he is bound into a solution that satisfies at the same time his feeling of need to stand by the law and need to get the right answer. And any judge who has ever puzzled through that one and had it come out right knows that it belongs among the beautiful moments of any man's career, and if you say no to that I won't believe you, because again I have seen it happen too often, and I have seen the sense of relief that happens when a judge gets it. 

I want to give you on that, if I may, a true tale that will illustrate exactly what I am up against and what a court is up against, as well as I have ever seen the history of a whole profession packed into a single incident. The situation has to do with a case that was decided in the District Court of Appeals for Columbia. Sitting were a conservative judge, a very liberal judge, and a moderately-minded judge all of them good craftsmen. The case had to do with a rather complex statutory situation, and the conservative-minded judge, seeing that the statutes apparently led to an outrageous conclusion, faced up to the music like a soldier, read the statutes the way he saw that they read, and wrote an opinion arriving at an outrage and said to himself, "That's the law."

The liberal-minded judge wrote a sociological essay explaining how it simply had to come out in all decency and sense the other way, and finding himself totally unable to find any legal warrant for it he strong-armed the law and voted that way.

The middle-minded judge looked at the outcome of the technical argument and his stomach turned over; he couldn't take it. He looked at the sociological argument in the light of the technical statutes and said to himself, "I cannot stand this, either." He then sat down to do the job which he thought had to be done, and he tried six sev-

And he did what I think a good many of us do when we are in that kind of a stew-he put it away for three or four weeks and tried to forget about it, at the end of which he came back and reread his statutes and, lo, he saw an answer upon the face of the statutes, if they were just handled with a slightly different angle. Once you have taken your position, once you have thought it through in terms of that position, you never can see any other position. And these people that try to get a court to reverse themselves on reargument remind me of people trying to swim up Niagara. What you get on reargument, as far as I can see, except in most extraordinary cases where a court has made a perfectly outrageous blob and requires to have its reputation saved before the world -what you get on reargument is that the court slightly modifies the ground on which it puts it so as to make you feel that you haven't been completely left out in the cold and ignored, and hands down just the same decision that it did before. On the other hand, if the words of the Act are not explicit, this Court cannot, under the guise of statutory construction, so broaden the operative scope of the Act that our construction of it amounts, in effect, to judicial legislation. The function of this Court is limited solely to interpretation.. 

Now, there is no more respectable doctrine on the statutes than that that could possibly be announced. That,

I think, is good doctrine all over the United States. It is also as great nonsense as it is respectable. There is no court in the United States that treats statutes that way regularly, and thank God that there isn't, because if statutes were regularly treated that way our courts and our law could not get along.

The true facts about statutes-I am not talking doctrine now, I am talking what happens to statutes in court, and what ought to happen to statutes in court-the true facts are that a court follows the language of the statute pretty well, and it follows the language of the statute with great care whenever the language of the statute is intelligent language, plainly adapted to the plain purpose of the statute. When the language of the statute doesn't seem to make sense the court sets about, whether it says so or not, correcting so as to produce some kind of sense. After all, a court can't apply nonsense. This never seems to occur to people who talk the normal language like this (indicating 349 Pa.). It is a fact that a court can not apply nonsense. 

It can't be done. They have to make sense of some kind in order to act under the statute, and that is their job. And furthermore, we all know that there are all kinds of statutes. There are silly statutes, there are wise statutes, there are pathetic statutes, there are dirty statutes, there are lousy statutes, there are statutes drawn in such a peculiar

way that their plain language makes nonsense, there are statutes drawn in such a way that their plain language makes part sense with holes perfectly clear that ought to be filled and aren't, and so it goes. 

Finally, there is another fact of life which never seems to occur to people who write about statutes, but occurs quite regularly in the behavior of courts that deal with statutes. Have you ever sat in a statutory drafting committee? If you have ever sat in a statutory drafting committee you have had the following experience. Nobody ever sat in one who didn't have this experience, and you don't have to sit long to have one, either. You wrestle over how to say it; people disagree; you have either included too much or you have included too little; or you have gone off a little on the wrong angle. A dispute develops; people around the table can't get together; they get mad; nobody finds a solving phrase that really handles the thing, and finally somebody throws in the language as follows: Says he, "Oh, what the hell! We can't get together on this anyhow. Why not leave it to the court?" And everybody heaves a sigh and says, "Sure, let's leave it to the court.' 

And then the controversial passage is taken out of the statute and nothing is phrased.

Now, under the language that I am talking about here, the court's job is to interpret the statute in the light of the intent of the legislature. I submit that most of the time when there is a gap in a statute the true intent of the legislature was to leave it to the court. Legislatures act in reliance upon the fact that courts are going to interpret, fill out, and work with the statutes, as they should. And why in the name of heaven shouldn't the doctrine of the courts recognize what the intention of the legislature and the knowledge of all people dealing with statutes fully understand?

I have a case here, except that it is a tax statute it would outrage me. It is the Hermann Estate. This is a guy in Pittsburgh who turned over in his will $75,000 for a

museum. I guess he gave the museum, too-yes; land, paintings, ivories, bronzes and other objects of art were given in his will, $75,000 income for the expenses of the museum, and in addition he made a trust of $25,000 more, income to himself for life. The $25,000 on his death to go to the museum, to be added to the expenses fund. The question is, is this living trust on which he has retained the use of the income taxable, or does it fall within the general notion of charitable bequests which are not taxable? And the statute says that all estates in any buildings, ground, pictures, statuary, or other works of art, and in any moneys and funds given in connection with any of the foregoing estates, passing by will from a person seized or possessed thereof, for the sole use of the public by way of free exhibition within the State of Pennsylvania, whether in trust or otherwise, shall not be subject to any collateral inheritance tax." The state is claiming the tax on the $25,000. Either the estate or the beneficiary is opposing it. The Court looks at it thus:

"We are admonished by 

Section 51 of the Statutory Construction Act of May 28, 1937, that 'When the words of a law are clear and free from all ambiguity, the letter of it is not to be disregarded under the pretext of pursuing its spirit."" Then there is another passage in the Statutory Construction Act which says, "Exemptions from taxation are to be strictly construed." 

Held, therefore, by the Court, the exemption reaches property passing by will for charitable purposes. This did not pass by will, but by living trust; therefore it is taxable. 

If it hadn't been a tax statute, and even under this tax statute it hurts me—if it hadn't been a tax statute, to take the position that the word "will" in that statute is to be construed as a restrictive word, meaning by will and by will only, when a living trust of this kind is intended to vest by death in exactly the same fashion, and under any other type of tax statute would be regarded as a disposition to take effect on death, and so taxable, so that it is a perfectly proper thing to be exempted-that strikes me as

complete nonsense. And I am perfectly clear, although I haven't got illustrations to bring you-I am perfectly clear that the other type of construction of statute can be pinned on the Supreme Court again and again and again in this same State of Pennsylvania.

Now, I did not have time when I found this case and got excited about it to chase Pennsylvania books and document that position. All I can say is this. If Pennsylvania is innocent in this regard, if you want to use an outrageous word to describe an outrageous condition, if Pennsylvania is virginal of construction of statutes in terms of purpose, then Pennsylvania is unique in these United States. That is as far as I can go. I don't believe it, I don't believe it; 

I can't believe it, in spite of their doctrine. And I want to state that your court is going to be hampered and terrifically hampered in the proper adjustment of the law as long as it goes on announcing and partly believing doctrine as remote from life as that particular line of doctrine about statutes, for it is perfectly plain in these days that statutes are providing our courts with vital new lines of policy, vital new base lines of policy, and that you can not bring a new base line of policy into a portion of the law without knitting it together with that portion of the law, by carrying the policy forward so that it joins with the other pieces and remodels those which it is necessary to remodel. If the purposes of statutes are not to become within reasonable limits new base lines for judicial thinking, our law is going to become a crazy-quilt, and in the main the courts achieve that under the guise of interpretation, what I shall call pseudo-interpretation, mouthing the language such as I have just read, finding ambiguities where there are no ambiguities, for the purpose of being able to resolve the ambiguity by putting needed meat into the statute. 

This particular case has a policy as clear as a bell in terms of encouraging charitable bequests and other transfers to take operation on death at the time by way of

relieving them from taxation, and it is the type of thing that calls for reasonable judicial development. 

As for the power to move, I want to take, if I may, one illustration, and with this I think I will close, out of the set of opinions that I dealt with before. I want to take it out of a case in which you can see what I am talking about, I think, more clearly than I could express it without an illustration. It is the Merchants Warehouse case, which is the first case in this volume, and you recall that the problem of that case was this, that there had been a difference of opinion that kept a full contract from being closed between the State liquor authority and a warehousing outfit, and the warehousing outfit brought a quasi-contractual suit for the value of services that it had performed for the State liquor authority. It didn't bring an action at law, but it proceeded under the statutes which allow certain types of claim to be decided by a board consisting of the Auditor- 

General and the State Treasurer.

Now, there are two things that I want to know about that. I don't know the background of this one. I note that it says here in a footnote from the Court, "Neither of these two officials sat at the hearing, the Auditor-General being represented by one named man, and the State Treasurer represented by another named man." There may somewhere in the statutes be a provision for such representation. It is queer, considering the fulless with which the relevant statutes are quoted in this case, that no such reference is made.

Will you please note what that implies in terms of liberal construction of statutes, in terms of sense? A board is created of two named officers, and the Court recognizes and upholds an award made by two delegates, neither of whom is either of the named officers. Very interesting in terms of, shall we say, restrictive construction of a statute, limited to its exact language. Huh? Huh? 

Huh? Limited?

Especially in view of our general views about delegation

of important quasi-judicial functions. That is number one. 

Now I go on to my next one.

There was another statute. The other statute, you recall, was a statute which provided that whenever any department, board, commission, or officer of the state government shall require legal advice concerning its conduct or operation, or when any legal difficulty or dispute arises or litigation is commenced or to be commenced in which any department, board, commission, or officer is concerned, it shall be the duty of such department, board, commission, or officer to refer the same to the Department of Justice; it shall be the duty of any department, board, commission, or officer having qualified and received legal advice from the Department of Justice regarding the official duty of such department, board, commission, or officer to follow the same.

The intent of that statute there can be no doubt about. 

Old Bill Schnader drew it, and I know old Bill Schnader and I know when he was Attorney-General what he wanted when he drew that statute. Furthermore, it is plain on its face. Old Bill Schnader is one damn good draftsman, and you don't often find when he gets something drawn that it isn't plain on its face, and this one is. 

All right. What happened in our case is that the representatives of the Auditor-General and the State Treasurer made an award, and the Attorney-General said, "Don't pay," and they didn't pay. And now a suit is brought and what does the Court do? The Court takes this language so plain upon its face and it says, "This language doesn't apply to this tribunal, which is an essentially judicial tribunal; this statutory language requiring the control of the Attorney-General applies, of course, to all acts in the executive and administrative capacities, but this is judicial. And, since it is judicial, obviously it is impossible that it has not got to be performed with the discretion, sense, and intelligence of the officers thus appointed. 99

Saying nothing of the fact that the officers appointed did not hold the hearing.

You notice the philosophy of this; the philosophy of this has to do with the nature of the judicial function. Is this not clear? So that regardless of the language of the statute, the nature of the judicial function comes to bear and results in a reading of it contrary to its apparent straight language.

In this same case, by golly, the Court goes out of its way to comment upon the fact that this statute creating this two-man board to decide these cases provides an appeal for the petitioner against the State and provides for no appeal for review by the State. I want to read that: "As further evidence of the legislative intent to permit such claims to be adjudicated it provided that if the claimant is dissatisfied with the adjudication and determination of it, he may appeal to the Court of Common Pleas of Dauphin County, subject to like proceedings in common suits in this Court. The Commonwealth may confer or withhold the right of appeal from these decisions. The legislature conferred upon the Commonwealth no such right to an appeal." And there is a prior remark to the same effect in the opinion, not quite so full, that shows that this is not an accident.

Now, here is the point I want to make. You have seen what they did to the statute. You have seen what they did to the statute because of their feeling that the judicial power was involved and that the judicial power had a nature. Surely, if there is anything which is of the nature of the judicial power it is that unless there is positive, direct prohibition, appeals in non-criminal cases should go to either side, if to either side, that both should have a whack at an appeal, and the nature of the judicial power when the legislature has said one side can appeal makes it perfectly easy to say, without a great deal more stretch, and I think with less stretch than was used in the actual decision, to say plainly, "this indicates appeal, and plainly

appeal goes for both if for either. To take a mere silence, not denying an appeal to the State, but just failing to provide one, and to say in the language of 1820 or 1880, "Whenever there is a statutory procedure any type of appeal must be express or it is implicitly excluded," this, to my mind, is just blind chasing of the tail of a dogma which should have been dead already these fifty years. 

What interests me is that they did that in the same case in which, on the basis of the same pure concept of the nature of the judicial power, they completely emasculated one phase of a clear statute. Now, I say that they were wise in what they did about that statute, I say that they were equally unwise in their failure to do it on the other branch of the case. I say that the power demonstrated in the one half of the case is enough to demonstrate the existence of the power in the teeth of the doctrine which I read before. 

Now, what I have tried to do is this. I have tried to face you with the problem which the Court faces from the standpoint of the Court, which the advocate faces from the standpoint of looking at the Court, when sense and the line of doctrine as accepted seem to get in each other's hair. I have tried to follow the Court's problem through and see a little of why they did what they did in this uncertain field, and I have tried to suggest some lines of sound advocacy in dealing with the situation. I have then turned to the proposition that the Court has considerable range, actually shown in its work, within which it can somewhat cure the situation by curing the poorer rules of law, be they statutory or be they case law, and my closing word is this: to the extent that the Court moves intelligently within that leeway which is open to it, moving not in large steps nor loosely, moving with that judicial caution, with that judicial restraint and with that judicial sense of responsibility which we can trust our supreme courts to have, to the extent that the court does so move and accomplish the kind of thing I talked about last time-do you remember that lovely passage on building the rule about the contract that

had no termination time, that the Court went at twice and finally brought out so clean-to the extent that they do that kind of thing uncertainty in our law is going to be on the decrease. The bar is going to know more where it is and the court is going to have less bad precedent to wrestle with, made by itself. For when all they do is to decide the case the way it ought to be decided without putting an equal amount of time on finding a rule that is sound for the situation, and cleanly stated, the reason that they dig out of the corner to justify deciding the case comes back and hits them and you in the eye tomorrow.

We all know our lawyers' proverb that you never can tell what peg the court is going to hang its hat on; we all know that. But when it hangs its hat on a poor peg it is a precedent, confound it. It's only good pegs that have any real soundness in being precedents. If we could only get our courts to recognize that when they are making a decision one good rule which fits is all that is needed, and that they don't have to act like lawyers and over-argue their case and argue it all over again and give six make-weights, at least three of which will not be thought through, to make trouble for the future, we would have an easier time. 

Now, one of the best men on your Court is Horace Stern, and one of his best jobs is the job from which I am going to quote a blob. I am quoting the blob to make a point. It is a nice opinion, it is well done, it is a good decision, and it lays down a good rule as far as it goes, but on one point he felt so much in the swing of the lawyer arguing his point that after he had absolutely proved where he wanted to go he threw in another reason, and the other reason is terrific, as you will see.

The problem has to do with realization on certain pledged stock, and the pledgee finding himself having difficulty collecting from the pledgor the dividends received on the stock, the pledgee transferred the stock into his own name so that he could collect the dividends direct. The pledge went into default, and after a while the pledgee bank

sold the stock. Now, it was perfectly sound, as you can see, to pass perfectly good title to a bona fide purchaser under those circumstances. There was a defaulted pledge, it was realization according to the contract. In addition to that, the bona fide purchaser had every reason to think that it was a proper realization and to take under that proper realization. After the opinion gets all done with that you get this additional ground protecting the bona fide purchaser: "In any event, defendants acquired an unimpeachable title to the stock because they were not informed as to any limitation of the bank's authority to sell it." That's fine, treating him still as a pledgee. "There is no evidence in the record," he goes on, and, Lord, I would like to strike this out of this otherwise lovely opinion, "There is no evidence in the record to establish that defendants, beyond knowing that the stock had been originally pledged by plaintiff with the banks, that he was in financial distress and that his notes had been in default, had reason to believe otherwise than that the banks which then held the stock in their own names were the full and absolute owners thereof." 

Now I've got that facing me in drawing a new investment instruments act, the suggestion that when you know the stock has been pledged, and when you know it is in default, and when you know, therefore, that it ought to be about to be realized on, you have no notice of the pledgor's rights, if he has any. You see, it is an unconsidered piece of the opinion, thrown in unnecessarily as an additional argument, whose only service is to make trouble in the future. The best of us do it, the best of us do it, law professors, judges, lawyers. But what you can see it brings the judges in the way of trouble really suggests that in your brief you might maybe try not over-arguing and throwing in extra points as well.

It has been a real pleasure to be with you again. It is, as you see, a fascinating subject for me, and I did feel when

everything looked so rosy and so pretty on the basis of last time's talk that it wasn't fair. I felt as if I had just drawn a picture of roses with no thorns, and my conscience hurt me. So I am awfully glad I got a chance to come back and show that thorns are there, too, but they don't interfere with the fact that there are roses.
