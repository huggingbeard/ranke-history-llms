PREFACE 

The text, being limited to the law of fire insurance as applied by our Court of Appeals, would seem to preclude a demand for this book sufficient to return the publisher a reasonable, if any, profit from its sales. Nevertheless, the publisher has undertaken its publication more as a contribution to the Bar than in hope of profit. 

If the book is found useful, the publisher and I will feel repaid for our labor and expense. On my part the labor was extended over so long a period, in the way of service to myself, that it entailed no burden at any time. 

The cases are grouped for collateral as well as definite application. Therefore, all cited on any point should be examined by the practitioner. 

In presenting the book I wish to express my appreciation of the courtesies I have received from the Bench and Bar where I have tried insurance cases, nearly always on the unpopular side. 

ROCKVILLE, MARYLAND. 

June, 1936.

R. E. L. SMITH.

The Contract-Its Construction and 

Interpretation As defined by the Court of Appeals a contract of fire insurance is a personal contract of indemnity against a possible loss on account of the interest of the insured in the thing mentioned in the policy. 

Heller v. Bank, 89 Md. 602 

Ins. Co. v. O'Brien, 107 Md. 341 

Rent A Car Co. v. Ins. Co., 158 Md. 169

Such contracts fundamentally are not different from other contracts. Almost invariably they are in writing, represented by policies, which by their nature are subject to numerous conditions. These conditions, as they have been construed by the Court of Appeals from time to time, have operated to erect the law into a fairly distinct system. But the well recognized rules of construction in other cases of contract have been generally adhered to, in principle in Maryland, whose courts, uninterfered with by legislation in this direction, have had an opportunity to lay down a systematic, well established, code which does justice to insurer and insured. The foundation upon which this code rests is that the court will apply generally the same rules of construction to these as to other contracts, not strictly against the insurer, but reasonably according to the ascertained intention of the parties as gathered from the whole contract when the intent does not conflict with the law. 

Jolly v. Eq. Soc., 1 H. & G. 295 

Edwards v. Ins. Co., 3 Gill 176 

Ins. Co. v. Biscoe, 7 G. & J. 293 

Ins. Co. v. Bossiere, 9 G. & J. 121 

R. Co. v. Ins. Co., 32 Md. 37 

Wineland v. Ins. Co., 53 Md. 276 

Ins. Co. v. Engle, 52 Md. 468 

M. & M. Trans. Co. v. Ins. Co., 53 Md. 448 

Ins. Co. v. M. & M. Trans. Co, 66 Md. 339 

Ins. Co. v. Weaver, 70 Md. 536 

Ins. Co. v. Hamilton, 82 Md. 88 

Ins. Co. v. Keating, 86 Md. 130 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110

Ins. Co. v. O'Brien, 107 Md. 341 

McEvoy v. Ins. Co., 110 Md. 275

Mortgage Co. v. Heft, 146 Md. 1 

Ins. Co. v. Thomas, 153 Md. 253

The purpose of the contract to afford the insured indemnity will always be kept in the mind of the court. 

Ins. Co. v. Loney, 20 Md. 20 

Ins. Co. v. Dorsey, 56 Md. 70

To effectuate the purpose of the contract the intent will not be violated by a technical adherence to its strict letter. 

The intent of the parties will be sought out. Where words of drastic effect are used and strict adherence to their technical application will strip the assured or the assurer of his reasonable rights, the court will consider all the circumstances, including the environments of the situation to ascertain the intent of the parties. Where proof of loss is required by the policy to be furnished "forthwith," that provision will be construed as meaning "with due diligence," which does not necessarily mean at the first opportunity. 

Where a policy was issued upon a steamer, her hull, apparel, machinery, boiler, engine, fixtures and appurtenances of every description, it was held that the policy comprehended only such loss or damage as resulted from actual ignition outside the furnace, the proof showing that the boiler, for damage to which the claim was made, was injured from intense internal heat arising from the negligence of the engineer in permitting it to become dry. 

Edwards v. Ins. Co., 3 Gill 176 

Ins. Co. v. DeFord, 38 Md. 382 

Rokes v. Ins. Co., 51 Md. 512 

Ins. Co. v. M. & M. Trans. Co., 66 Md. 339 

Towing Co. v. Ins. Co., 74 Md. 25 

Ins. Co. v. Baker, 94 Md. 545 

Ins. Co. v. Jean, 96 Md. 252 

McEvoy v. Ins. Co., 110 Md. 275 

Auto Ins. Co. v. Thomas, 153 Md. 253

Every clause in the contract will be given its just effect. 

None will be disregarded to reach a result which may seem justified under the hardships of the particular case. The law assumes that parties understand their contracts. Every

intelligent condition, clause and word is presumed to have been inserted by design, has some meaning and was intended to accomplish some purpose. Parties are bound by the legitimate and usual meaning of the language employed unless there are potential reasons to the contrary. 

R. R. Co. v. Ins. Co., 32 Md. 38 

Ins. Co. v. Keating, 86 Md. 130 

Miller v. Ins. Co., 127 Md. 140

New conditions will not be read into the contract to extend its scope; hence where a policy insured the cargo of a vessel against fire, loss occasioned by water on the submerging the vessel to extinguish a fire was held not included. 

M. & M. Trans. Co. v. Ins. Co., 53 Md. 448

The charter, by-laws and constitution of a mutual insurance company, and the application, if there be one, are parts of the contract and will be considered in construing it. 

Ins. Co. v. Deale, 18 Md. 26 

Webb v. Ins. Co., 63 Md. 213 

Ins. Co. v. Condon, 89 Md. 99 

Holley v. Ins. Co., C. C. Balto. Co., Nov. 2, 1933 

From the case of Towing Co. v. Ins. Co., 74 Md. 25, supra, it will be seen that the ordinary insurance policy does not cover losses from the excessive use of fire purposely maintained in the place contemplated for its use, whereby the thing to which it is applied is damaged by heat. 

While all the parts of the contract, including in the case of mutual companies the charter, constitution, by-laws and application, if one, will be considered in ascertaining its meaning and scope the usual rule is that the written parts, if any, will prevail over the print in case of competition, as that which is written must have had the special consideration of the parties, but an indorsement on the back of the policy is not a part of the contract unless made so by the language of the policy. 

Ins. Co. v. M. & M. Trans. Co., 66 Md. 339 

Ins. Co. v. Rowland, 66 Md. 236

1 Where the situation requires outside evidence to ascertain the intent of the parties resort may be had to proof of the usages of the particular trade involved to explain the meanings of expressions contained in the policy, but merely to explain and not to alter the contract. If the language of the policy is plain it will not be controlled or altered by proof of this character. 

Allegre v. Ins. Co., 6 H. & J. 408 

Allegre v. Ins. Co., 2 G. & J. 136 

Ins. Co. v. Butler, 55 Md. 233 

Rent-A-Car vs. Ins. Co., 158 Md. 169

If such evidence is offered it must be of usage at the place of the subject of the insurance. That at another place is not admissible. 

Mason & Leef v. Ins. Co., 12 G. & J. 468

In the Leef case the policy covered a new barque being built in Baltimore. Certain articles, not yet incorporated into the boat, were burnt. It was held that evidence of a custom elsewhere as to such items was not admissible to determine the intent of the parties. From this case it may be deduced that materials for a new building, not yet incorporated in it, are not covered by a policy on the building itself, but where materials are detached temporarily from an old building undergoing repairs, the intention being to reattach them, they remain as a part of the structure. 

The language of the contract will not be perverted from its ordinary meaning either to enlarge or diminish its scope, but the whole policy will be scrutinized. If one part is qualified by another the two will be read together. Where the assured has stock of his own and other goods on commission a policy covering "my stock in trade" will not be extended to include the commission goods, no qualifying language appearing in the policy. 

Ins. Co. v. Engle, 52 Md. 468

While language inaptly and inappropriately used will be given its reasonable interpretation fine distinctions will not be drawn to sustain an unmeritorious contention.

10 

Jolly v. Balto. Eq. Soc., 1 H. & G. 295 

Ins. Co. v. M. & M. Trans. Co, 66 Md. 339 

Joffe v. Ins. Co., 116 Md. 155

Ambiguous language cannot be controlled by the insurer's understanding without proof that the insured knew of such understanding. 

Ins. Co. v. Davison & Simonton, 30 Md. 91

If the language is plain and unambiguous the contract alone will be looked to in ascertaining the intent of the parties; parol evidence will not be admitted to vary it. 

Hough v. Ins. Co., 36 Md. 398 

Joffe v. Ins. Co., 116 Md. 155 

Miller v. Ins. Co., 127 Md. 140

Mortgage Co. v. Heft, 146 Md. 1 

While the language of the contract will not be extended beyond its plain meaning there may be circumstances and conditions incident to the subject of the insurance under which the meaning will be extended or restricted because of the very reason of the case, so as to reach the intent of the contracting parties.

An example is. repairs to an insured building. From the very reason of them, namely, to keep the structure in insurable and habitable condition ordinary alterations and repairs are within the contemplation of every policy, unless excepted specifically. The necessity and frequency of repairs will be measured by the condition, character and use of the building. 

Ins. Co. v. Ice Co., 36 Md. 102

If the alterations or repairs do not fall within the class above and the policy provides that in case of such without insurer's consent there shall be no liability, there can be no recovery in case of loss happening by reason of the alterations or repairs. In such case the burden is on the insured, being the wrong doer, to show his right to recover for a portion of the loss not due to the wrong doing, if he claims for such. As a general rule where the insurer claims a special exemption the burden is on him to establish the exemption, but in the above case the rule is as stated.

Howell v. Soc. for Insuring, 16 Md. 377 

Sweeting v. Ins. Co., 83 Md. 63 

Ins. Co. v. Thomas, 153 Md. 253

Where a policy prohibited the "working of carpenters" in the insured building, altering or repairing the same without permission of the insured, the prohibition will not be extended to the casual patching up of the building. It means such hazardous use of the building as is generally denominated by the term "builder's risk," which arises from placing the building in the possession of or under control of workmen for rebuilding, alteration or repair. 

Jolly v. Balto. Eq. Soc., 1 H. & G. 295 

Ins. Co. v. Ice Co., 36 Md. 102

Insurers will be held to a consistent course. It follows that if the policy prohibits a thing and yet covers a building in which, or a part of which, the prohibited thing is maintained, and the nature of the business carried on in the building requires the use of the designated article, the prohibition will be considered as waived. Oil may be kept for lubricating purposes in a machine shop notwithstanding the policy prohibits keeping oil on the premises. Machinery requires oil for lubrication. 

Carlin v. Assurance Co., 57 Md. 515

If the policy covers two or more things and is entire it cannot be separated into items; all must stand or fall together. 

Assurance Co. v. Assum., 5 Md. 165 

Ins. Co. v. Hamilton, 82 Md. 88 

Joffe v. Ins. Co., 116 Md. 155

It is obvious that the intention of the parties is that all shall comply with the terms of the contract. If the policy provides that the contract shall become void upon a certain contingency its happening will render the contract void, though the insurer shall have sustained no injury thereby. When once the contract has become void it cannot be revived by the act of the insured alone. It requires the concurrence of all parties. 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110

Binding slips, which do not include by reference terms and conditions of the policy intended to be issued, will not be construed to embrace such terms and conditions, if they, as they usually do, effect present insurance, thus becoming an executed contract. 

Ins. Co. v. Ryland & Brooks, 69 Md. 437 

Ins. Co. v. Goldstein, 119 Md. 83

If a policy prohibits anything that materially increases the risk, the increase must be in fact material or else the contract will be unaffected, and instructions to the jury that "additional risk” will forfeit the right to recover is error. 

Allen v. Ins. Co., 2 Md. 111

If the language of a policy is clear and explicit it will be given its proper effect; where it is ambiguous the insurer, being the author, cannot complain if the construction is against him. 

Ins. Co. v. Dorsey, 56 Md. 70

Policies are usually limited as to time, though some are made continuous by annual or other payments. If the contract is renewed by means of a new stipulation, renewal receipt or otherwise, in the absence of something to the contrary, the insured is entitled to assume that the premium and all the terms and conditions will continue as theretofore. 

Ins. Co. v. Floss, 67 Md. 403 

Mallette v. Assurance Co., 91 Md. 471 (See Standard Co. v. Wood, 116 Md. 575)

If the insuring corporation has failed to comply with the statute authorizing it to do business in the state its contracts of insurance will be held void. 

Stevens v. Fertilizer Co., 87 Md. 679

Unless the policy clearly indicates a contrary intention the exception from liability for loss occasioned by explosion will not be construed to include losses caused by explosions which are the direct result of the progress of a fire, but the

fire must be a conflagration and the explosion the result of its progress. The fire must be such a fire as is insured against. The word explosion is construed to mean a collapse occasioned by pressure from without by reason of a vacuum within or by pressure within by operation of a sudden major force of air. 

Ins. Co. v. Dorsey, 56 Md. 70

It is sometimes difficult to determine whether a policy creates a warranty or a mere description. Warranties are not created by construction, so if the terms of the policy are fully satisfied by interpreting the words as a description they will be held so to be. If, however, it clearly appears that the parties intended them as a warranty they will be so construed. "First floor occupied by machinery used for making barrels with privilege of storing barrels on the premises and other merchandise not more hazardous" is a description where read with a clause requiring the insured to describe the property, how occupied etc., with a further clause specifying prohibited things. 

Ins. Co. v. Kimberly, 34 Md. 224

Where the policy requires an unconditional and sole interest in the assured it means that the interest must be completely vested in the assured and not contingent or conditional; not for life only, nor in common, but of such nature that the assured will sustain the entire loss if the property should be destroyed. (This is subject to the observation that any interest the insured has in the thing is insurable, and if the contract calls for a description of that interest it must be truly stated). A successful bidder at a mortgagee's sale is not an unconditional and sole owner. 

Ins. Co. v. Keating, 86 Md. 130

Courts will not construe away reasonable conditions of a policy imposing burdens on the insured, as a provision calling for an inventory, or the disclosure of an incumbrance. 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110 

Beck v. Ins. Co., 44 Md. 95

A policy insuring rents provided that the insurer should not be liable beyond the actual value destroyed by fire, for loss occasioned by ordinance or law regulating construction or repair of buildings, or by interruption of business, manufacturing processes or otherwise. It also provided that the assured should rebuild in as short a time as the nature of the case would admit. The loss was occasioned by the great fire in Baltimore in February, 1904. The court held that the phrase "shall not be liable for loss occasioned by interruption of business, manufacturing processes or otherwise'," was broad enough to include a delay occasioned by the vast amount of debris incident to the general conflagration. 

Ins. Co. v. O'Brien, 109 Md. 100

If the words of a policy are susceptible of the interpretation given them by the insured, although by the insurer intended otherwise, they will be construed favorably to the insured. 

McEvoy v. Ins. Co., 110 Md. 275

The Code reducing pleadings in covenant and assumpsit to one requirement of form (Art. 75 sec. 4) seems to give only historic value to the distinction between actions in covenant and those in assumpsit, resulting from the effect of renewals of sealed policies by receipts or other instruments not under seal. 

See Ins. Co. v. McGowan, 16 Md. 47 

Ins. Co. v. Floss, 67 Md. 403 

Ins. Co. v. Wood, 116 Md. 575

A substantial compliance with the conditions of the policy by the assured may answer, but no compliance will not. 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110 

Ins. Co. v. Blieve, 157 Md. 426

If the effect of a strict construction will be to work an injustice and subject the fair intent of the contract to a technical superiority, the strict construction will not be adopted; as where the term "levied on or taken into possession" is used, it will not be construed to cover a taking

beneficial in its purposes and effect to both parties and which does not in effect change the control and possession. 

The phrase is construed to include some essential and appropriate step or part of a proceeding which if effected will take the property from the insured or affect his title or possession. It does not include the taking possession by a receiver where such action can have no ultimate affect upon the title or possession, but is only a device intended to meet a contingency and which will enhance the security of the insurer. 

Ins. Co. v. Baker, 94 Md. 545

Provisions in a policy against the sale and conveyance of the insured property and against the assignment of the policy without the consent of the insurer, impose a restriction against the right of alienation and will be construed with strictness. 

A clause in a policy confining liability to "direct loss or damage by fire" or lightning will not be construed to exclude loss or damage caused by fire in, or lightning striking an adjoining building. 

Ins. Co. v. Thomas, 153 Md. 253 

Loss following as result of the falling of an adjoining building, permitted to remain in bad condition for a long time after a fire, which in the nature of things would result in producing a dangerous condition will not necessarily be construed as within the contemplation of the parties to the policy.

Fire destroyed a building adjoining that of plaintiff. A wall was left standing for a long time. There was testimony that the wall was struck by lightning. It fell and damaged plaintiff's building, which was insured against direct loss by fire or lightning. Whether the proximate cause of the damage was the fire which destroyed the adjoining building was for the jury. But it was error to instruct the jury that the insurer was liable if they find that the wall would not have fallen but for the fire. 

Ins. Co. v. Thomas, 153 Md. 253

CONTRACT; WHEN COMPLETE 

11 An application for insurance neither accepted nor rejected does not constitute a contract. 

Harp v. Ins. Co., 49 Md. 307

A legal opinion expressed by an officer of the insurer is ineffectual to estop the insurer if the officer was not authorized to bind the insurer in such matters. 

A construction by an officer of the insurer after loss is inadmissible as against the insured. 

Ins. Co. v. Engle, 52 Md. 468 

McEvoy v. Ins. Co., 110 Md. 275

Where on the face of the policy there is doubt whether it was intended to cover certain goods the doubt may be resolved against the insurer on showing of its subsequent admission that it did. 

Ins. Co. v. Engle, 52 Md. 468

Leasing of property is not an alienation of it. 

Ins. Co. v. Rowland, 66 Md. 236

The vendee as well as the vendor has insurable interest in property pending consummation of a sale. 

Brewer v. Herbert, 30 Md. 301

Contract; When Complete 

A policy signed in blank in Maryland by the insurer's general agent and sent to another state to be filled up, countersigned and delivered, the insured property being located in the other state, was held to have been completed in the state of its countersignature and delivery. 

Cromwell & Sloan v. Ins. Co., 49 Md. 366

When a policy is issued for cash it must be delivered and premium paid or satisfied before it will become effective. 

The contract is consummated when and where the delivery or constructive delivery takes place. R. applied to an agent or broker in Baltimore for insurance. The agent in turn

applied to another agent in New York, who applied to an insurance company in Boston. The Boston company made the policy and sent it to the agent in New York, who sent it to Baltimore for collection of premium and delivery. It was held that the contract was a Maryland contract. If the cash premium had accompanied the application so that the signing in Boston would have completed the transaction, the placing the policy in the mails there with intent that it go unconditionally to the applicant would have amounted to a delivery and the contract would have been a Massachusetts one. The contract, made in Baltimore, was void under the statute. 

Stevens v. Fertilizer Co., 87 Md. 679

Acceptance of part of premium waives immediate payment of the residue and the contract becomes complete. 

Ins. Co. v. Eicholtz, 88 Md. 92 

Mallette v. Ins. Co., 91 Md. 471

A policy is effective from the date of its issue notwithstanding it contains an error subsequently corrected-not from the date of its correction. 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110

Where an agent issued a policy without collecting the premium, but made himself responsible to his principal, the insurance company, for the premium, thus extending credit to the insured, the policy was held effective from its issue. 

Reynolds v. Ins. Co., 107 Md. 110 

Ins. Co. v. Melman, 147 Md. 459

Warranties; Representations; Disclosure 

It is often difficult to distinguish that which is a warranty from that which is a mere representation. In effect the difference is that a warranty must be literally fulfilled; substantial compliance is sufficient in case of a mere representation. 

Ins. Co. v. Abbott, 12 Md. 348 

Allen v. Ins. Co., 2 Md. 111

Courts are not disposed to create warranties by construction, so if the terms of the policy are fully satisfied by restricting a statement to a representation it will not be considered as a warranty. If construed as a warranty it must be fully complied with. 

Ins. Co. v. Kimberly, 34 Md. 224

Language susceptible of more than one construction will be construed most favorably to the insured. It was held that the following provision of a contract did not call for the disclosure of a mortgage: "If the interest of the insured in the property be other than the entire, unconditional and sole ownership of the property for the use and benefit of the insured, it must be so represented to the company and so expressed in the written part of the policy, otherwise the policy shall be void." The court said this did not call for disclosure of a mortgage, and that if the insurer wanted to know about incumbrances it should have said so in plain terms. 

Ins. Co. v. Beck & Bolte, 43 Md. 358 

In Beck & Bolte, 44 Md. 95, the policy provided that “in all cases of applications for insurance in this company the applicant shall disclose the amount of encumbrances, if any exist" on the property. The court held that it was the duty of the insured to answer truly as to the incumbrances, saying "Where incumbrances are thus in express terms required to be disclosed, where disclosure is thus made a substantial element of the contract, no good reason exists why the courts should seek to place upon the instrument any rigid or strict construction of its terms in order to aid the assured to escape the consequences of the neglect of a plain obligation thus imposed on him by the contract, which he accepted and knowledge of which on his part is presumed from such acceptance."

The following provision of a policy was deemed sufficient to call for the disclosure of a judgment lien: "An incumbrance on the property hereby insured, whether existing at the time of the issuing of the policy or imposed subsequently thereto must be assented

to by the company, otherwise the policy shall be void." 

Bowman v. Ins. Co., 40 Md. 620 

Skinner & Son v. Houghton, 92 Md. 68

When called for the status of the property must be truly disclosed. Where a policy provided that if the interest of the insured in the property, whether as owner, trustee, consignee x x x x or otherwise, is not truly stated in the policy the policy should be void, it was held to call for any incumbrance, and failure to disclose an existing mortgage was fatal. But the existence of an undisclosed mortgage is not fatal to the contract, unless a fair construction of its terms calls for a disclosure. 

Ins. Co. v. Weaver, 70 Md. 536 (where Ins. Co. v. 

Kelly, 32 Md. 421, is distinguished).

If incumbered property takes a higher rate than unincumbered property (and disclosure of interest is required) failure to disclose a qualified interest may be fatal. 

Ins. Co. v. Weaver, 70 Md. 536

In the Weaver case, above cited, the insured covenanted that he had not omitted to state to the insurer any information material to the risk. This was held to be a warranty. 

The loss involved a piano held under a conditional sale. The insured was bound by the contract with the vendor to pay the full value of the piano in case of destruction by fire. 

See also Ledvinka v. Ins. Co., 139 Md. 434, to the effect that a conditional sale vendee of property does not have the interest and title called for by the usual insurance policy.

A policy may be void ab initio, or it may be made void by conditions broken after issuance. 

The first may be brought about by concealment of a material thing, which if disclosed, would have changed the aspect of the risk, such as increased rate of premium, or from misrepresentation, or non-disclosure if disclosure demanded, of title, interest or possession; the other by change in interest, increase of risk or other conditions violative of the policy. Again the policy may suspend while prohibited conditions obtain. The materiality of the

objectionable thing if not specifically made material by the contract is a question for the jury. 

Ins. Co. v. Coates, 14 Md. 285

If a new condition be injected into the policy, qualifying its general provisions, it will be given a fair and reasonable application and substantial compliance will suffice. A condition is substantially complied with by the adoption of precautions which, if not those specifically required by the contract, are such as reasonably tend to accomplish the same purpose and may be considered as equally efficatious. As where the contract permitted benzine to be kept on the premises in tin cans. Keeping the fluid in one can was a substantial compliance. And the introduction of a barrel of benzine in wood for the purpose of transferring it to tin was held to be within the contemplation of the contract. 

Ins. Co. v. Whiteford, 31 Md. 219

If the assured in good faith, and in the absence of inquiry, omits to state the extent and character of his interest, the policy will not be avoided. 

Ins. Co. v. Coates & Glenn, 14 Md. 285

The application of the insured becomes a warranty only upon the completion of the contract by the issuance and delivery of the policy. 

Ins. Co. v. DeFord, 38 Md. 404 .

Title of insured may be made material, or become so as a matter of law. In such case a misrepresentation with respect to it will avoid the contract, as where in a mutual company the charter provides that the premium notes shall be a lien on the land and restricts insurance to property subject to such liens. If the notes cannot become a lien because the maker is a stranger to the title, the policy is void. 

The question in such case is one of law. It goes to the consideration. But, if the insurer can insure without such limitation the materiality of the misrepresentation becomes a question of fact. 

Ins. Co. v. Deale, 18 Md. 26

The net result of the Deale case appears to be that where the charter does not prohibit contracts where the notes cannot become liens on the land if the insured has an insurable interest he may validly insure, subject to the penalty if any of false representation.

There is a wide difference between title and interest. If the policy calls for a fee simple title one of less degree will not meet its demands. The same is true if the contract calls for an unconditional title, which means a title not subject to or defeasible under any condition. Therefore, a title based upon a mere agreement, or a gift without conveyance, do not meet the requirement of a fee simple title demand. 

Wineland v. Ins. Co., 53 Md. 276

An insurer may waive the fraud of the insured if it so desires, hence fraud will make the contract only voidable. 

False swearing will have the same result. A fair mistake, although sworn to, will not avoid the policy, even if when the error comes to the attention of the insured he, by inadvertence, fails to correct it. 

Ins. Co. v. DeFord, 38 Md. 382, 404 

Ins. Co. v. Cohen, 114 Md. 130

In construing a forfeiture clause courts will look to the whole instrument. The insurer will not be permitted to successfully invoke the doctrine of forfeiture where the cause of forfeiture was secondary, as where it depended upon a condition precedent, which it was the insurer's duty to perform. The burden is on the defendant to show forfeiture by convincing proof. 

Schaeffer v. Ins. Co., 80 Md. 563 

Ins. Co. v. Schaeffer, 82 Md. 377

If the forfeiture relates to the inception of the contract it will avoid the contract ab initio. 

Insurance Co. v. Weaver, 70 Md. 536 

Turnbull v. Ins. Co., 83 Md. 312

Courts will not relieve against plain forfeitures. 

Ins. Co. v. Fid. & Cas. Co., 159 Md. 631, where the Maryland cases are assembled.

Where the policy provided it should become void if the property should become subject to a mortgage, the condition was upheld. 

Where the agent of the insurer by accident, acquiescence or design creates a situation conflicting with the terms of a warranty or representation made by the insured, the insurer will not be permitted to take advantage of it to the prejudice of the insured. 

Ins. Co. v. DeFord, 38 Md. 382 

Ins. Co. v. Gusdorf, 43 Md. 506 

Monahan v. Ins. Co., 103 Md. 145 

Ins. Co. v. Bridge Co., 116 Md. 422 

Goebel v. Ins. Co., 127 Md. 419 

Where an agent made no inquiry about incumbrances on personal property and inserted in the policy that it was fully paid for and not mortgaged and the insured accepted the policy with the false answer he was bound by the misrepresentation, both the agent and the insurer being ignorant of the incumbrance until after the loss. Reinsurance and Contribution 

Reinsurance

Reinsurance is an act by which the insurer insures its risk. It is a common right in insurance companies. It is the solution of the question of an overload in the insurer. The fourth section of Stat. 19, Geo. 2, Kilty's Rep. of Stats. 252; 

Alex. Brit. Stats. 760 prohibiting reinsurance does not apply to fire insurance. 

Real Estate Co. v. Cashow, 41 Md. 59

In reinsurance the contract is alone between the insurer and its reinsurer. Should the original insurer become insolvent, paying but a small dividend or none, the reinsurer's obligation to his insured remains in full force according to its contract with the original insurer. Though the policy of the reinsurer may be subject to the same risk and valuation, conditions and mode of settlement as the original policy, the adjustment and settlement of the original insurer fixes the liability of the reinsurer. If the reinsurer has adopted the terms of the policy of the original insurer he is bound by them in the absence of bad faith. 

Real Estate Co. v. Cashow, 41 Md. 59

Reinsurance is usually effected between insurance companies by a blanket policy or contract, which rises and falls as reinsurance is effected or ends. 

Contribution 

The contract relation between insurer and insured is sometimes qualified by a new relationship between the insurers themselves when there are two or more. Manifestly it would be unfair for the insured to recover more than the value of his property destroyed or the damage to it. Modern policies provide that the insurer shall not be liable for a greater proportion of any loss or damage than the whole amount by the particular policy insured shall bear to the whole insurance covering the property whether valid or not or whether collectible or not. Each insurer, therefore, pays 25

his proportionate part of the loss. The insured contracts to that end. The right to contribution among policies depends upon the concurrence of the policies. The several insurers must be bound with certainty and in the same sense and for the same loss. The policies must cover the identical risk. When the common advantage covered by the contribution clause becomes impossible there is no reason to give it vitality, as where one policy covers commission goods and goods held by the same insured in his own right, and another policy covers goods of the second class only. Where the amount of the first policy is insufficient to pay the loss to the commission goods there can be no contribution. If the amount of the two-way policy should exceed the loss to the goods not common to the two policies, then as between the insurers there would be contribution in proportion to the insurance applicable to the property owned in the insured of his own right. 

Ins. Co. v. Loney, 20 Md. 20

There is no right of contribution between a fire policy and a marine policy, the subject of insurance being different. 

M. & M. Transp. Co. v. Ins. Co., 53 Md. 448

Policies which on their faces are payable to a third person inure to his benefit, and if he holds other policies on the same interest in the same subject matter and against the same risk, these must contribute to the loss. 

Hough v. Ins. Co., 36 Md. 398 

Ins. Co. v. M. & M. Transp. Co., 66 Md. 339

In a suit by the insured against the insurer where concurrent insurance is shown the jury should find, under proper instructions, the proportionate amount due by the defendant company. 

Ins. Co. v. Dorsey, 56 Md. 70

It is for the insurer to require the insured to maintain contributing insurance. If the insurer does not demand it the insured is not bound to maintain it, although there was such when the policy was taken out. 

Ins. Co. v. Ice Co., 36 Md. 102

Risk - Increased Risk 

A contract of fire insurance demands the utmost good faith of all parties to it. Underwriters rely upon representations of the insured as to conditions affecting the property. They also take into important consideration the applicant's reputation for integrity and care of his property. 

Undoubtedly the ordinary man relies as confidently upon the insurance company's fairness as he does on his banker. 

Proceeding upon the presumption that the large majority of property owners are honest and devoted to protection of their property, the insurer assumes its responsibility in as much reliance upon these so-called moral securities as upon the ratio of loss to like physical situations. These mutual confidences have tended to establish a code of conduct which enters importantly into the system of fire insurance law. The insurer owes to the insured the duty not to deceive him in making or performing the contract; the insured owes a like duty to the insurer, with the duty not to materially increase the risk assumed by the insurer without the latter's consent; nor should he conceal any material matter bearing upon the risk at the inception of the contract or at any other time during its life. 

The policy usually specifically prohibits any material increase of risk, upon penalty of forfeiture. 

Ins. Co. v. Rowland, 66 Md. 236

What amounts to an increase of risk is a matter of fact to be considered in the light of common reason. In solving the question the usage of the insurer in like cases and the rates charged may be considered, not to determine per se whether the risk was increased, but to show the "usage of underwriters respecting the rates charged by them." 

Ins. Co. v. Rowland, 66 Md. 236, as explained in The Willow 

Grove Co. v. Ins. Co., 77 Md. 532

In The Willow Grove Creamery Co. case evidence that the insurance company had taken similar risks was held inadmissible. As far as that case is an authority on the 40

Every change that materially increases the risk is fatal to recovery if the loss results from the change. The doctrine of waiver is firmly fixed in the law of fire insurance. As a doctrine it is meritorious. In application it presents some difficulties and merits some criticisms. There are three distinct lines of cases in the courts of this country as to other insurance prohibitions. They are: 

1. If the other insurance is invalid it is no insurance, hence does not render void any policy otherwise valid, in the absence of a condition that the policy shall become void if there be other insurance whether valid or not. 

2. That other insurance whether legally enforceable or not, or whether voidable on its face or for extrinsic matter works a forfeiture. 

3. That the validity of the present policy depends upon whether the other insurance has been annulled and held void.

The first is the doctrine of Maryland courts. The words, "Whether valid or not" give the insurer full protection. Other insurance does not mean an unenforceable contract; nor one to be kept at the option of the insurer, but valid, enforceable insurance. If the insurer wishes his contract to become void upon uneffectual attempt to procure other insurance apt language can be used to that end and it will be given effect. A policy void by its terms is not voidable, so that it can make no difference whether the other policy is recognized as valid or not by the issuer of it. "Shall not be liable" is equivalent to void, and as a void policy cannot be turned into a voidable one by the action of the insurer, a promise to pay a loss under such a policy will be a nudum pactum. When the defense is other insurance the burden is on the defendant to show it. The policy plead on as constituting other insurance is admissible in evidence and its status directly in issue. 

Sweeting v. Ins. Co., 83 Md. 63.

Subrogation 

By subrogation is meant the right of the insurer to be substituted in place of the insured as against a third party, wrong-doer, and the right of the insurer to be substituted in place of a third party as against one insured who is himself a wrong-doer. First, as where a third person wrongfully burns the insured property under circumstances that the insurer must pay the loss; and second, where the insured himself burns his property or for other reason cannot recover and the policy carries a mortgagee clause under which the insurer must pay the mortgagee. The insurer may pay the loss and step into the position of the insured or the mortgagee as the case may be. 

Subrogation will not be allowed if it conflicts with the indemnity to which the insured is entitled under the insurance contract.

SUBROGATION 

79 pro tanto. The right of subrogation is a derivative right. 

It comes through the insured and can only be enforced in his name. If he has no right that the insurer can appropriate, the latter cannot have subrogation; so if the alleged wrong-doer pays without legal liability there can be no subrogation. If the insured releases the wrong-doer he, of course, cannot recover against an insurer with right of subrogation.
