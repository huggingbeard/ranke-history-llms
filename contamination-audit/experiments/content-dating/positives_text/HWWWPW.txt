PRACTICAL PROBLEMS IN THE PREPARATION OF WILLS 

The purpose of this pamphlet is to call attention to some of the practical problems confronting the attorney who is called upon to prepare a will. It may also offer useful hints for drafting the will and for conferring with the testator. There are many points to which the testator might very much appreciate having his attention directed to aid him in planning his estate.

INTENTION 

It should always be borne in mind that the testator's intent is to be gathered from his will.¹ (Robinson v. Martin, 200 N.Y. 159.) The attorney should spare no pains and certainly not words in order to make sure that the will clearly and unambiguously expresses the testator's intention. Otherwise construction proceedings may be necessary. (Brown v. 

Gardner, 233 N.Y. 261.) As the act of the draftsman must be imputed to the testator the attorney must strive to present the testator's intent in the will in such a manner that the court will not be called upon to say that he did not have "any clear perception at the time he fabricated the document of the variety of problems of interpretation which were being generated." (Matter of Lesser, 158 Misc. 895.)

THE ATTORNEY AND INSTRUCTIONS 

The attorney must be satisfied as to the testator's mental competency, his comprehension of the extent of his wealth, his understanding of his family situation, his remembrance of those of his relatives who are the natural objects of his bounty, and as to his freedom from undue influence.2 (Decedent Estate Law, § 10; Smith v. Keller, 205 N.Y. 39; Matter of Forsyth, 169 Misc. 1042; Matter of Eno, 118 Misc. 186.) It is advisable to interview a testator alone when taking notes for a will. See Matter of 

Voorhis (125 N.Y. 765), Matter of Price (204 A.D. 252, affd. 236 N.Y. 

656). Where instructions are received from a third person, careful inquiry should be made as to the circumstances, especially where the third person is himself a beneficiary. (Matter of Vouhans, 165 Misc. 727.) 

It is generally inadvisable to prepare a will without first having had a personal conference with the testator. See Matter of Barbineau (27

Misc. 417). In any event, except in unusual situations, the attorney should in every case have a conference with the testator alone before the will is executed to be sure that the document is the testator's will and that he fully understands it. See Matter of De Castro, (32 Misc. 193). 

Where the attorney drafting the will is himself a beneficiary he may have to explain the circumstances and where he or his family take to the exclusion of the natural objects of the testator's bounty, the will should be drawn by some other lawyer. (Matter of Putnam, 257 N. Y. 140.) 

Also the testator cannot direct the executors to employ his attorney for the estate. Administration of the estate is their personal responsibility. (Matter of Caldwell, 188 N.Y. 115.) Nor can the testator by his will control the amount of legal fees to be paid by the estate. (Matter of 

Olney, 155 App. Div. 195.)

DISINHERITING RELATIVES 

A surviving spouse cannot be deprived of statutory interests. Decedent Estate Law, § 18. With this exception, and provided his disposition does not offend against Section 17 of the Decedent Estate Law which voids charitable gifs in excess of one-half of the estate upon objection of specified relatives and does not violate any principles of law or morality, a testator may leave his property to whomsoever he will.5 (Matter of 

Hughes, 225 A.D. 29, affd. 251 N.Y. 529; Matter of Forte, 149 Misc. 327.)

In some states or countries other relatives may be specified by statute as being entitled to a certain portion of the estate. (Matter of Cruger, 36 

Misc. 477.) The testator does not need to mention in his will the relatives whom he wishes to cut off, provided the share of the estate to which such relatives would otherwise have been entitled is fully disposed of to others. (Matter of Watts, 112 Misc. 300.) Sometimes, however, it is well for him to state that he has not "made any provision for a certain relative because he is already amply provided for" (Matter of Koch, 74 App. Div. 338, affd. 

173 N.Y. 629) or if this is not the case he may state that his "omission to provide for the relative is intentional". Such a statement shows that the testator had the relative definitely in mind and deliberately decided to give him nothing. The effect of statements such as the latter in engendering ill feeling should be considered. 

EXECUTION 

A person who is a legatee or beneficiary should not be a witness to the will. (Decedent Estate Law, § 27.) The attorney himself or his employees should witness the will. The attorney and his employees can, however, testify regarding its preparation, execution and construction whether or not they are witnesses. (Civil Practice Act, § 354.)

The following is a careful procedure at the execution of a will and conforms to Decedent Estate Law 21 and 22.

The testator should place his initials or write his name on the margin of each page of the will and sign his name at the end of the will. He should then be asked what the instrument is that he has just subscribed and his reply should be that it is his will. He should next be asked whether he publishes, acknowledges and declares it to be such and whether he requests the witnesses to sign their names to the will as witnesses, and his reply should be in the affirmative. The witnesses should then sign their names to the will and write out their addresses after the attestation clause. (Decedent Estate Law, § 21; Matter of Voorhis, 125 N.Y. 765.)

One of the witnesses should then read the attestation clause aloud. The testator and all witnesses should be present during the entire ceremony. 

Two witnesses are required (Decedent Estate Law, § 21) but it is advisable to have three, especially if the testator owns real property in a state the law of which requires three witnesses. 

A testator who is illiterate or unable to write may subscribe the will by a mark, cross, or symbol, so long as he adopts this method of subscription as his own. (Matter of Mills, 171 Misc. 42; Matter of Romaniw, 163 Misc. 

481; Matter of Golicki, 116 Misc. 100.) But a seal or wafer affixed to the will should bear the testator's initials or a mark indicating his acceptance of the instrument. (Matter of Severance, 96 Misc. 387.) He may also call upon another to guide his hand or assist him in writing his name or (3) ¶ 2735. (4) ¶ 1020. (5) ¶ 243, 257. (6) 310-314, 1003, 1006. 

¶ 1006. [6]

making his mark. (Matter of Caffrey, 174 App. Div. 398; Matter of Bitzer, 124 Misc. 432.) Needless to say, this procedure should be used with caution and only in case of necessity, (Matter of Irving, 153 App. Div. 728, affd. 207 N.Y. 765; Matter of Stegman, 133 Misc. 745, affd. 227 App. Div. 

647; Jackson v. Jackson, 39 N.Y. 159) but it may be necessary where the testator is illiterate, aged, deaf, blind or very ill. (Matter of Golicki supra; Matter of McCabe, 75 Misc. 35; Matter of Mooney, 73 Misc. 315;

Matter of Beneventano, 38 Misc. 272.) 

The end of the will means the end of the language and not the paper upon which it is written. (Matter of Field, 204 N.Y. 448; Matter of 

Golden, 165 Misc. 205, affd. 253 App. Div. 919.) There is no objection to a printed form if it is correctly executed, (Matter of Field, 204 N.Y. 448; 

Matter of Johnson, 169 Misc. 215) but all too often dispositive provisions follow the signature and probate is refused, (Matter of Jarvis, 143 Misc. 

640; Matter of Crosson, 134 Misc. 154).

The testator must unmistakably communicate to the witnesses by his words or conduct that the instrument is his will and that he requests them to sign as witnesses. (Lane v. Lane, 95 N.Y. 494; Perham v. 

Cottle, 98 Misc. 48, affd. 178 App. Div. 949.) This he may do himself, (Matter of Jones, 157 Misc. 847) or by replying in the affirmative to questions asked him, (Coffin v. Coffin, 23 N.Y. 9) or by his silent permission or approval of his attorney's publication of it and request to the witnesses to sign. (Matter of Abare, 141 Misc. 143.) Publication of the will by another may, however, lead to inquiry into the testator's condition, physical and mental, at the time. (Matter of Kenney, 179 App. Div. 258; 

Matter of Holmberg, 83 Misc. 245.)

The witnesses must write their names and places of residence else they forfeit $50 to anyone interested in the property devised or bequeathed and who will sue for the same. Decedent Estate Law, § 22. 

The attestation clause though not an essential part of the will is a signed recital from which the Court may infer that the statutory requirements were complied with. (Matter of Conway, 129 Misc. 361; Matter of 

Eyett, 124 Misc. 523.)

Substantial compliance with the statute is necessary. The testator's intention is a primary rule in questions of construction after the will is probated. But it is the Legislature's intention which governs execution of the document and the question always is what did the testator do in the light of the statute. (Matter of Snyder, 154 Misc. 156.)

A will executed without the State of New York in the mode prescribed by the law of the place where it is executed or of the testator's domicile, is deemed legally executed and is of the same force and effect as if executed as prescribed by the laws of New York, provided it is in writing and subscribed by the testator. (Decedent Estate Law, § 22-a.) Under this section a holographic will written and subscribed by the decedent in a foreign country has been admitted to probate in New York as a will of real and personal property. (Matter of Fowler, 161 Misc. 204.) See Matter of 

Gifford, 279 N.Y. 470. Also a will of real or personal property executed without the state in the mode prescribed by the law of the place where executed or of the testator's domicile may be admitted to probate in New York provided also it is in writing and subscribed by the testator. (Decedent Estate Law, § 23.) And under Decedent Estate Law, § 24 the right to have a will admitted to probate and the validity of its execution or the validity or construction of any provision therein is not affected by a change of the testator's residence made since its execution. See also Sur rogates Court Act, § 144. 

It is inadvisable to make alterations, interlineations or erasures in the will before execution unless absolutely necessary. Questions may arise as to whether they were made before or after execution. (Matter of Ross, 177 App. Div. 719; Matter of Ackerman, 129 App. Div. 584) despite the [7]

presumption that in the absence of suspicious circumstances they were made before execution, (Matter of Bromley, 128 Misc. 662; Crossman v. Crossman, 95 N.Y. 145) especially if they be of a corrective or unimportant nature. (Matter of Eaton, 84 Misc. 1.) If made, there should be a signed and witnessed statement as a part of the will that they were made before execution. Of course, if made after execution they are ineffective unless the usual execution formalities have been complied with. (Matter of Ackerman, supra.)

Before execution, care should be taken to see that a revocation clause is contained in the will expressly revoking all prior wills. (Decedent 

Estate Law, § 34.) In the absence of such a revocation clause the will may be read together with an existing prior will to ascertain the testator's intention, (Matter of Cunion, 201 N.Y. 123) though a will disposing of all the testator's property may impliedly effect a revocation without a clause to such effect because inconsistent with a prior will. (Matter of Wuppermann, 164 Misc. 900.)

CODICILS 

The term "will" in the Decedent Estate Law includes a "codicil." (Decedent Estate Law, § 2.) Unlike an aftermade will, a codicil does not usually supersede the will. Its function is to reaffirm an existing will, except for the changes, alterations, qualifications, or new provisions it contains. (Bloodgood v. Lewis, 209 N.Y. 95.) It is supplementary to an existing will and is part of it. While there may be any number of codicils, they are read with the will to form a consistent whole. (Matter of Phelps, 133 

Misc. 450.) So far as there is any inconsistency between the will and the codicil and the codicil contains no express revocation of provisions in the will, these provisions will not be disturbed any more than is necessary to give effect to the new provisions. (Matter of Saks, 170 Misc. 469; 

Matter of Ithaca Trust Co., 227 N.Y. 437.)

If an attorney is asked to prepare a codicil to a will executed quite a few years before, it may be advisable for him to suggest to the testator that the will be rewritten to incorporate the substance of the proposed codicil that there may be a new set of witnesses whose present whereabouts are known. Especially may this advice be in order if the will itself were executed in some state or country other than that of the testator's present residence or the place where it will probably be probated, or in the event that one or more of the witnesses to the will are known to be dead, removed from the state, or advanced in years. See Surrogate's

Court Act, § 142, Decedent Estate Law, § 24.8 If a new will or codicil be executed a surviving spouse has a right of election under Decedent Estate Law, § 18 which he or she would not have been entitled to under the old will, if executed before September 1st, 1930.9 While the statute applies only to wills executed on or after that date, a codicil to a will executed prior thereto will make the will subject to its provisions. (Matter of Greenberg, 141 Misc. 874, affd. 236 App. Div. 733, affd. 261 N.Y. 474.)

If an attorney is called upon to make a will for a person who is in his last sickness he should consider carefully whether he should not advise a codicil to an existing will rather than a whole new will. Sometimes a person who is very feeble will be able to understand that he wishes to leave legacies to certain persons or make trusts for them, but will be incapable of undertaking a disposition of his whole estate, especially if it be a large estate or if there be complications. (Matter of Delmar, 243 

N.Y. 7.) It all depends, however, upon the facts of the particular case.

POSSIBILITY OF CONTEST 

Wherever a contest is anticipated particular care should be taken in the execution of the will. It might be advisable to have testator's physician act as a witness.

Sometimes a testator will fear a contest and will want to avoid it. 

This situation has been met in some instances by having the testator make a trust of the major part of his property (possibly revocable) the income of which may be paid to him during his lifetime, and the principal of which will be disposed of upon his death as the trust directs. This may avoid a contest, although the trust itself could be attacked in equity where fraud or other equitable grounds exist as to the effect of such trusts on the statutory interests of the surviving spouse; however, see 

Newman v. Dore, 275 N.Y. 371; Marine Midland Trust Company of Binghampton v. Stanford, 256 App. Div. 26; Bodner v. Feit et al., 247 App. 

Div. 119. With respect to Totten trusts see Matter of Glen, 247 App. 

Div. 518, affd. 272 N.Y. 530; Matter of Weinberg, 162 Misc. 867.

OLD WILLS 

It depends upon the facts of each case whether a testator should preserve his old will after a new one has been executed. If his last will should be refused probate on a contest, then the one next in order could be offered for probate, and so on down the line. This very fact may give pause to anyone contemplating a contest who does not benefit under an old will. Furthermore, old wills are often of value to show continuity of intention or to show the various changes from time to time in the testator's testamentary plans. On the other hand, beneficiaries under old wills whose interests have been reduced might seek to contest the new will should they learn of the existence of the former wills. (Matter of 

Cohen, 151 Misc. 98; Matter of Bray, 146 Misc. 415; Surrogate's Court 

Act, § 147.) If the testator, however, would prefer to die intestate rather than have the old will probated, there is no point in saving the old will. 

ORDER OF CLAUSES 

The general order of will clauses is approximately as follows:- 

Debts-funeral expenses-burial directions. 

Specific devises of real property. 

Specific bequests of tangible personalty.

Specific bequests of stock, bonds and other personalty. 

General cash legacies. Trusts. 

Miscellaneous provisions, e.g. estate, transfer and inheritance taxes payable from residue, preferences, etc. 

Residuary clause. 

Appointment guardians. 

Appointment executors. 

Appointment trustees. 

Executors' powers. 

Trustees' powers.

BURIAL DIRECTIONS, MASSES AND CEMETERY PLOTS 

Sometimes a statement should be made that a letter of instructions regarding burial will be left for the executor. In such case, or if the will [9]

itself contains directions, the will should be placed where it can readily be found in event of death.10

The law gives great weight, if not controlling force, to the wishes of the decedent expressed in his will regarding burial. (Cooney v. English, 86 Misc. 292.) Penal Law, § 2210 confirms the right to direct the manner in which the body shall be disposed of after death. With regard to disposition of testator's body by his will for the purpose of medical research, see Matter of Johnson, 169 Misc. 215.

There is some question whether a gift for the upkeep of a burial plot is to be considered a charitable trust under Personal Property Law, § 13-a, and hence to be considered in connection with the charitable gifts permissible under Decedent Estate Law, § 17, Matter of Braasch, 206 App. 

Div. 96, or a funeral expense, Matter of Voelker, 158 Misc. 97, and so not to be considered under that section. (Matter of Opdyke, 230 App. Div. 

290; Matter of Miranda, 151 Misc. 459.)

A legacy for the erection of a tombstone on the grave of another has been held of a charitable nature under both Decedent Estate Law, § 17 and Personal Property Law, § 13-a. Likewise a legacy for masses has been held to be for charitable and religious uses and not an integral part of the funeral services. (Matter of McArdle, 147 Misc. 876.) See also

Matter of McAvoy, 112 App. Div. 377. In the McArdle case it was also held that the purchase of a family plot and the erection of a headstone are part of decedent's funeral expenses to a reasonable amount (Sur. 

Court Act, § 314-3) and the excess directed by the will to be for a charitable purpose under Personal Property Law, § 13-a. See also Matter of Small man, 138 Misc. 889. The testator may judge for himself what is reasonable for a trust for masses. As religious ceremonials they are upheld as public charities and are not void for indefiniteness. (Matter of Morris, 227 N.Y. 141.)

Even though there is no provision in the will, reasonable funeral expenses are a preferred charge against the estate. (Sur. Court Act, § 216.) Included in such expenses, regardless of will provisions, are expenditures for perpetual care of the burial lot, (Matter of Delafield, 142 

Misc. 536), a mausoleum, (Matter of Myers, 140 Misc. 442) and a monument (Matter of Beach, 1 Misc. 27, modified on other grounds, 3 Misc. 

393.) But see Matter of Burroughs, 155 Misc. 237.

If the will, however, does not direct a preference for these purposes, the preference may be held to be only for a reasonable amount. (Matter of Smallman, 138 Misc. 889; Matter of Burley, 159 Misc. 560.) But see 

Matter of Meek, 113 Misc. 301.

Funeral expenses of a deceased wife are a charge against her own estate. (Matter of Van Zant, 231 App. Div. 381; Matter of Ackler, 168 

Misc. 623.)

If wills are executed in duplicate or triplicate, while one copy may be probated, it is necessary to produce all copies or account for their absence. (Roche v. Nason, 185 N.Y. 128.) The Court must be assured that the wills are identical in their terms and that there has been no revocation, for a revocation of one revokes all. (Crossman v. Crossman, 95 N.Y. 145.)

If two or more copies were in the testator's possession and one only is found upon his death it may be contended that there was a destruction with intention to revoke.¹¹ (Matter of Shields, 117 Misc. 96.) Because of these difficulties, the execution of multiple wills should not be encouraged. 

If the testator insists upon having them, as is sometimes the case, he should understand the problems involved on probate. 

COMMON DISASTER 

Common disaster is a possibility against which provision should be made in a proper case.12 Such provision is used most often in cases of husband and wife but should be considered wherever gifts are made to persons with whom the testator lives or travels. There is no presumption as to survivorship in the event of a common disaster. (Matter of 

Fowles, 222 N.Y. 222.) The presumption is rather that the deaths of persons in such a disaster occurred simultaneously. (Bierbrauer v. Moran, 244 App. Div. 87; St. John v. Andrews Institute, 191 N.Y. 254.) If a husband makes a gift to his wife and they are both killed in a common disaster, the gift passes to her estate if she survives but a single second. (Matter of Fowles, supra.) See further pages, 25 and 26.

PERSONALTY 

A will of personalty may be made if the testator is eighteen years of age or over. (Decedent Estate Law, § 15.)

Generally the law of the testator's domicile governs the validity of dispositions of personalty.13 (Dammert v. Osborn, 141 N.Y. 564.) But see Decedent Estate Law, §§ 12, 47, Personal Property Law, § 12-a; 

Mount v. Tuttle (183 N.Y. 358); Hope v. Brewer, (136 N.Y. 126).

If the testator wishes to dispose of various articles or types of personalty, tangible or intangible, specifically, adequate and proper descriptive words should be used.14 The Courts are constantly being asked to construe loose phrasings in wills. Thus, questions have arisen whether the testator meant to include jewelry as "personal effects" (Matter of Steimes, 150 Misc. 279; Matter of Mildeberger, 212 App. Div. 727, affd. 

241 N.Y. 528), automobiles as "personal effects" (Matter of Jones, 128

Misc. 244), and bonds, mortgages and bank passbooks as "personal property and effects", (Mena v. Virnard, 207 N.Y. Supp. 504.) Broad terms, like "contents" of buildings, houses or receptacles are ambiguous and lead to requests for interpretation. (Matter of Whitford, 146 Misc. 82.) See 

Matter of Strebeigh, 167 Misc. 656. If certain articles are enumerated, followed by a more general description, such as "other personal effects", the general term under the ejusdem generis doctrine is generally construed to mean articles of the same kind as those particularly described. (Matter of Thompson, 218 App. Div. 130, affd. 245 N.Y. 565; Matter of Robinson, 203 N.Y. 380.) All parts of the will are construed with relation to one another in an effort to ascertain the testator's intent. (Matter of Mildeberger, supra.)

If some articles are specifically bequeathed, the testator should know that if he does not dispose of the remaining articles of tangible personalty they will fall into the residue.¹5 (Lamb v. Lamb, 131 N.Y. 227;

Matter of Reynolds, 124 N.Y. 388.) If the residue is in trust, the articles will ordinarily be sold.

Should personalty be given to several persons, the question arises who is to have the power to make division if the will is silent. The testator may state that he will leave a memorandum addressed to the executor or other persons to serve as a guide. This memorandum, however, is effective as a suggestion only. (Matter of Atkins, 76 Misc. 386.) 

An executor cannot conclusively fix values in a division where the will was executed after May 2, 1936. (Decedent Estate Law, § 125.) 16

Where the legatee predeceases the testator the bequest lapses and falls into the residue¹ (Lamb v. Lamb, supra), unless Decedent Estate Law, § 29 applies.18 This section provides that where there is a bequest to a child or other descendant of the testator or to a brother or sister of the testator, there will be no lapse if the legatee predeceases the testator leaving issue living at the testator's death. The statute does not apply if the will expressly provides for the disposition of the bequest in the event that the legatee dies before the testator. (Matter of Claus, 153 Misc. 206.) 

If there are bequests or legacies to two or more persons the testator may wish the survivor or survivors of the legatees who may be living at his death to have the whole bequest or legacy, or he may wish the issue of a deceased legatee to have the legatee's share. The will should contain express provisions to carry out the testator's intentions.

REALTY 

A will of realty may be made only if the testator is twenty-one years of age or over. (Decedent Estate Law, § 10.) 

If any real property is situated outside of the state, the law of the state or the country where it is situated should be examined, as that law governs its disposition.19 (Clarke v. Clarke, 178 U.S. 186; Matter of 

Osborne, 151 Misc. 52.) This is important if such realty is included in a trust. The will should be executed also in accordance with the law of the place where the realty is situated in order that the will may be effective to dispose of it. Some states require three witnesses, while the 

New York law requires but two. A New York will with two witnesses might not pass title to real estate in a state the law of which requires three witnesses. Also the laws of such state should be examined as to rights of married persons in each other's realty. For example, under the 

Florida law a surviving spouse has homestead rights.20 Other states, such as California, have community property laws.21 

Decedent Estate Law, § 2922 saves from lapsing a devise to a child or other descendant of the testator or to a brother or sister of the testator should the legatee predecease the testator leaving issue surviving the testator. A lapsed devise falls into the residuary estate. (Lamb v. Lamb, 131 N.Y. 227.)

A specific devise of certain realty, such as a residence, may be advisable, else it will fall into the residue (Lamb v. Lamb, supra), which may subject it to sale.

If any legacies are to be charged on the realty the will should contain provisions to this effect, or there may be construction proceedings to ascertain the testator's intention.23 See Ely v. Megie, 219 N.Y. 112.

The testator may wish to consider whether it is wise to devise realty, such as a residence, for instance, to two or more persons, and thereby probably necessitate partition actions. See Civil Practice Act, §§ 1014, 

1018. Especially is this to be considered where infants are involved. 

Difficulties arise also where the residuary estate, which includes realty not specifically devised, is divided into a number of shares and one of these shares is in trust. (Pattison v. Cusack, 147 App. Div. 428; U. S. 

Trust Co. v. Smith Co., 141 Misc. 315.) A grant or devise to two or more persons in their own right constitutes them tenants in common unless it is specifically stated that they are to take as joint tenants.2 24 (Real Property Law, § 66.) A devise of realty to husband and wife without words specifying the interests each is to take constitutes them tenants by the entirety.25 (Miner v. Brown, et al., 133 N.Y. 308.) See also for the nature of this type of tenancy Hiles v. Fisher, (144 N.Y. 306.) An absolute devise of real property will not be cut down into a less interest unless the intention is clearly and definitely manifest. (Tillman v. Ogren, 227 N.Y. 495.)

Where realty is in a trust, taxes, ordinary repairs and interest on mortgages, in the absence of a direction to the contrary, will be paid from income, but improvements and assessments resulting in improvements will be paid from principal.26 (Stevens v. Melcher, 152 N.Y. 551; Matter of Shepard, 136 Misc. 218.)

FRACTIONAL INTEREST IN REALTY 

A testator may own, for example, a one-third interest in realty. He may make a specific provision in his will giving this interest to a devisee.

SURVIVORSHIP PROVISIONS 

Where money or property is given to several named persons or to the survivors and the testator wishes to provide that should any of such persons be dead leaving issue such issue are to take the deceased legatee's share, it should be made clear that the issue are to take such share as the deceased person would have taken if living at the time. If property is given to A, B, C and D and survivorship conditions to these legatees and their issue are desired, the wording of the will should be clear. If A should die leaving issue and B should die leaving no issue it should not be open to construction whether A's issue take a one-third share or a one-fourth share. See Hendricks v. Hendricks, (177 N.Y. 

402), Matter of Truslow, (140 N.Y. 599), Davis v. Davis, (118 N.Y. 411).

The general rule is that words of survivorship will be construed as relating to the death of the testator unless incompatible with other provisions of the will. (Matter of Wilkins, 155 Misc. 152.) It is therefore important to exercise care in defining the date at which survivorship provisions are to be determined. 42 Thus where testator bequeaths property to A and wishes to preserve the property to A's issue if A predeceases the testator, he should say, "but if A predeceases me leaving issue me surviving I give and bequeath the same to such issue". In some wills the draftsman has the clause read, "but if A predeceases me leaving issue him surviving". A may die before the testator and leave issue and the issue may predecease the testator.

Therefore the testator's death should be the determining date. In some cases it may not make any difference which way the clause is worded, but in the interests of careful draftmanship, reference should be made to the correct date.

A similar question arises where the principal of a trust is to be disposed of on the death of a life beneficiary. It is often provided that upon the death of the life beneficiary the principal shall be paid to the beneficiary's issue him surviving. The beneficiary may predecease the testator, leaving issue who may also predecease the testator. It would be better to provide that the property is to go to the beneficiary's issue him surviving, or in the event that the beneficiary should predecease the testator, to the beneficiary's issue surviving the testator.

STOCK DIVIDENDS 

Under Personal Property Law, § 17-a, all stock dividends declared on stocks in a trust created by deed after May 17, 1926, or on stocks in a trust created under a will executed after such date will be principal and go to the remaindermen unless a contrary intention is expressed in the will or deed. 67 The 1922 provisions of this section in force April 3, 1922, were to the effect that as to deeds of trust or trusts in wills thereafter executed there might be a declaration that such stock dividends were to be in whole or in part principal. Prior to 1926 the so-called rule of the Osborne case (Matter of Osborne, 209 N.Y. 450) prevailed unless a contrary intention was expressed. Under this rule, which was very complicated in its application, stock dividends and extraordinary dividends were given to life beneficiaries or to remaindermen or divided between them according to whether the dividend entrenched on the principal of the trust (that is, the book value of the stock) at the time of the creation of the trust. Generally, so far as the dividend represented corporate earnings before the trust was created it was principal, and so far as it represented corporate earnings after the trust was created it was income. (Bourne v. Bourne, 240 N.Y. 172.) The statute is not retroactive. (Matter of Hagen, 262 N.Y. 301.)

The statute specifically provides for its application only where any dividends are payable in the stock of the company or association declaring or authorizing them.

In some instances a testator has difficulty in dividing his estate. He may wish his brother to receive so much and his son and his widow so much and yet he may hesitate to name specific sums because he may not know what his estate will amount to. It is useful in such a situation to divide the estate or residue into fractions. Thus, 1/5 to the brother, 2/5 to the son and 2/5 to the widow. Then the legatecs all bear the ups and downs of the estate. He may, however, not wish his brother to receive in any event more than, say $10,000. The will should then read, "1/5 to my brother but not exceeding $10,000" and he should bequeath the excess over that sum either proportionately among the other legatees or to one or more of them as he may desire; thus

2/5 to my wife B.

Generally it is advisable for a testator who has given all his residuary estate to one named person, as to his wife, to provide for a disposition of his estate in the event that his wife should not survive him or that they should die in a common disaster. (Matter of Fowles, 222 N.Y. 222; 

St. John v. Andrews Institute, 191 N.Y. 254.) To a less extent this is advisable where more than one are named legatees, unless there are survivorship provisions as to those legatees. Whenever there is a lapse of a gift of the residuary estate or part thereof it passes as in the event of intestacy, unless the will provides for such an eventuality. (Morton v. Woodbury, 153 N.Y. 243.) The common disaster provision should ordinarily be used in the case of persons who live together or travel together, especially husband and wife.75

Sometimes a husband will leave all his property to his wife and she will not plan to make a will at all, saying that she has little or no prop- - erty. If she does make a will, she may have her own estate only in mind. 

Yet if the husband dies and she survives him, if only for a single second, she will receive all his estate (Matter of Fowles, supra) and if she has no will it will go to her distributees, which is a result neither may have foreseen or desired. She should therefor be advised to make a will to provide for such a contingency. She ought to bear in mind the possibility that the will she is making may also carry her husband's large estate. The wife may want to make an entirely different disposition of her property in the event that her husband predeceases her, as she will then be disposing also of his estate. See pages 7 and 25.

MUTUAL WILLS 

Sometimes a husband and wife or other persons closely related or associated desire to make wills at the same time containing similar provisions. If the wills are mutual and made pursuant to a contract, either may revoke while the other lives, but if one person dies having left his will unaltered, the survivor cannot change his will.79 (Morgan v. Sanborn, 225 N.Y. 454; Rastetter v. Hoenninger, 214 N.Y. 66.) He can change it as to words, but if his former will can be proved, the new legatees will take the property with a trust impressed thereon in favor of the old beneficiaries. (Wallace v. Wallace, 216 N.Y. 28.) This point should be explained and the testator should be asked whether such a result is intended. Often in such cases a husband will provide that if his wife predeceases him or he and his wife die in a common disaster (Matter of Fowles, 222 N.Y. 222; St. John v. Andrews Institute, 191

N.Y. 254) he bequeaths to A $10,000. The wife makes a similar provision. If they both die in a common disaster it would seem that both wills speak and A would get $20,000, which was probably not intended and may lead to litigation. Therefore it may be advisable, say in the wife's will, to provide for the legacy to A if her husband predeceases her but to omit the common disaster provision as to A and other legacies of a similar nature and to provide further that the legacy to A is not to become effective should a legacy to A become effective under her husband's will. Obviously this argument does not prevail as to the residuary estate. See pages 5 and 19.

The right of election is furthermore not available to a husband who has neglected or refused to provide for his wife or who has abandoned her. It is also not available to a wife who has abandoned her husband. 

The statute further provides that a husband or wife may in the lifetime of the other waive the right of election to take against a particular will of the other by instrument in writing subscribed and acknowledged, or he or she may waive the right of election against any will in an agreement made before or after marriage.

IN TERROREM CLAUSES AND CONDITIONS 

Testators frequently wish to include in their wills in terrorem clauses, that is, clauses which state that if any legatee, devisee, or beneficiary contests the will, he loses his legacy.85

It has been held that such a clause in a bequest of personal property is not enforcible unless there is a gift over to some other person or persons on the breach of the condition though the rule is otherwise with respect to a devise of realty. (Matter of Arrowsmith, 162 App. Div. 623, affd. 213 N.Y. 704.) This case also held that a general residuary clause was not a gift over. In Matter of Brush, 154 Misc. 480, affd. 247 App. 

Div. 460 the court exhaustively analyzed the cases on the subject and saw no reason for a gift over or a distinction between dispositions of real and personal property. In any event the court said the condition should not apply to a construction proceeding or be binding on a Special Guardian for infants. It is safer, however, to make the gift over on a breach of the condition. See, however, Matter of Fox, 114 Misc. 368.

Also conditions subsequent, not illegal or opposed to public policy, (Matter of Solomon, 156 Misc. 445; Matter of Andrus, 156 Misc. 268) have been said to be inoperative in default of a gift over. (Sherman v. 

Richmond Hose Co., 230 N.Y. 462.) Conditions precedent and subsequent are discussed in Matter of Lesser, 158 Misc. 895. See also Hogan v. 

Curtin, 88 N.Y. 162; Matter of Mahlstadt, 140 Misc. 245; Robinson v. 

Martin, 200 N.Y. 159; Matter of Endus, 171 Misc. 283.

The courts have sustained conditions "involving the personal habits and traits of character of a potential recipient of a testator's bounty". (Matter of Lesser, 158 Misc. 895.) There may be a condition that the beneficiary comply with certain religious observances. (Matter of Kempf, 252 App. Div. 28.)

Testamentary provisions containing conditions directed against marriage with some particular classes of persons or some specific person are upheld, (Matter of Seaman, 218 N.Y. 77) but conditions in general restraint of marriage are against public policy and void. (Matter of Liberman, 279 N.Y. 458.)

Trust Service. (85) 375.13-375.18. [ 32 ]

Where a gift over of a trust beneficiary's interest is made it may be advisable either to reconstruct the trust because the question will arise as to the measurement of the trust if the life beneficiary forfeits his rights or to provide that the payment of the principal is to be accelerated. 

An in terrorem clause is futile to prevent a surviving spouse's right of election. (Matter of Byrnes, 141 Misc. 346, affd. 235 App. Div. 782, affd. 

260 N.Y. 465.)

ESTATE, TRANSFER AND INHERITANCE TAXES 

There is no Federal Estate Tax on an estate of a resident or citizen of the United States unless the net estate exceeds $40,000. (Revenue Act 

1935, Section 201, 26 U.S.C.A. 535, Regulations 80, Articles 4, 48, 51.)

There is no exemption allowed the estate of a non-resident alien. Where death was before May 10, 1934, there is no exemption allowed the estate of a non-resident, even though a citizen of the United States. (Regulations 80, Article 48.)

There is no New York Estate Tax on a New York resident's estate where the net estate after deduction of exemptions is under $2,000. 

(Tax Law, § 249-n.) In New York there are personal exemptions. A 

$20,000 exemption is allowed for a husband or wife and a $5,000 exemption for, among others, a child, adopted child, lineal descendent, lineal ancestor, brother or sister. (Tax Law, § 249-q.) These exemptions are deducted from the first tax bracket, which is the first $150,000. (Tax 

Law, § 249-n.) 

The estates of non-residents of New York are taxed on New York realty and tangible personal property having a situs in New York. The 

Estate Tax law § 249-r attempts to tax on an apportionment basis, roughly, that proportion of the New York Estate Tax that would be paid if the decedent were a resident of New York that the New York property bears to his entire estate. But the proration provisions have been held invalid. (Matter of Lagergren, 276 N.Y. 184; Matter of Rueff, 157 

Misc. 680 affd. 249 App. Div. 617, appeal dismissed 273 N.Y. 530.) The state of a decedent's domicile only can tax his intangible personal property. 

The state of the situs can tax real estate, tangible personal property and property having a business situs. See First National Bank of Boston v. 

State of Maine, (284 U.S. 312).

Aside from questions of business situs, under Article XVI, § 3 of the 

Constitution of New York, as adopted November 8, 1938, intangible property of a non-resident is not subject to taxation in the state. Neither is trust property subject to taxation because of the domicile of the trustee in New York, however constitutional it may otherwise be to tax such trust property. (Graves v. Elliott and Curry v. McCandless, U. S. Supreme 

Court, October term, 1939.) If, however, no other state has jurisdiction to subject the trust principal to death taxes then the trust estate may be liable for New York Estate taxes. (New York Constitution, Act XVI.)

The Federal Estate Tax and the New York Estate Tax are apportioned among the legatees and devisees on an accounting proceeding, unless the will provides that the gifts are to be free of tax. (Decedent 

Estate Law, § 124.) If, therefore, the testator desires any bequests, legacies, devises or provisions in trust to be free of estate, transfer or inheritance taxes, he should make a specific provision to this effect. 

Otherwise the executor must deduct the legatees' proportion of these taxes or they must be paid by the legatees or adequate security fur nished. (Decedent Estate Law, § 124.) 

Care should be taken, however, with respect to providing that all estate, transfer and inheritance taxes are to be paid from the residuary estate. The clause may be made so broad and specific as to mean that all such taxes are to be paid from the residuary estate, even on property [ 33 ]

over which testator had a power of appointment or on transfers in trust or gifts made during his lifetime, which may have been made in contemplation of death or intended to take effect at or after death. Such a result might work great injustice and mean the wiping out of the testator's estate. Thus, suppose testator has an estate of $100,000 and has a power of appointment over $1,000,000 or has made a transfer in trust at one time of a considerable sum. His whole estate may be insufficient to pay the taxes, especially if the property transferred in trust has greatly increased in value since the transfer and the taxes are calculated on the value at the date of testator's death. The question is a matter of intention. See Farmers Loan & Trust Co. v. Winthrop (238 

N.Y. 488); Isham v. New York Association for Improving the Condition of the Poor, (78 App. Div. 396, affd. 177 N.Y. 208); In re Roger's Will, (287 N.Y. Supp. 426). As to the liability of a trustee of an inter vivos trust for a proportion of the Esate taxes, see In re Ryle's Estate, 12 

N.Y. Supp. 2d 337.

It is of importance in preparing a will to know where the decedent is domiciled, as if there is doubt and there is a danger of taxation in two or more states, (Texas v. Florida, U. S. Supreme Court, 83 Law. Ed. 549), steps may be taken to clear up the situation. See also Matter of Trowbridge, 266 N.Y. 283.

INSURANCE 

Where insurance is payable to a person's estate it is subject to Fed eral and New York estate taxes. (Revenue Act 1926, Sec. 302, g 26 

U.S.C.A. 411-g, Tax Law, § 249-r.) This is likewise true where the insurance is payable to a trustee for the purpose of meeting taxes, debts or administration expenses. (Regulations 80, Article 26.) 

Where, however, the insurance is payable to a named beneficiary, or to a trustee for named beneficiaries, and the insured has retained the legal incidents or ownership of the policies, such as the right to change the beneficiary or to surrender or cancel the policy, only the excess of the insurance over $40,000 is subject to the Federal Estate Tax. (Rev enue Act 1926, Sec. 302-g, 26 U.S.C.A. 411-g, Regulations 80, Articles 

25, 27.) If such incidents have not been reserved or have been surrendered, the insurance is not taxable whether or not it exceeds $40,000 unless possibly in the case of a surrender in contemplation of death. 

There is no Federal Estate Tax, however, on the estate of a resident of the United States unless the net estate, including this excess, itself amounts to over $40,000. (Revenue Act 1935, Sec. 201, 26 U.S.C.A. 525.)

In New York insurance payable to named beneficiaries where the legal. incidents of ownership are retained by the insured not exceeding $40,000, is not subject to tax. If the incidents of ownership have not been reserved or surrendered, the Federal rule would seem to apply. Section 

249-R-9 of the Tax Law, provides that where insurance is payable to beneficiaries other than the estate, it is taxable to the extent that it is required to be included in the gross estate under the provisions of the Federal Estate Tax law. An additional $100,000 of insurances, less the aggregate of personal exemptions, is likewise not subject to tax in New York but this additional amount is to be subtracted from the lowest tax bracket, which is the first $150,000. (Tax Law, § 249-q.) 

Sometimes the advisability of creating an insurance trust either funded or unfunded, should be considered, especially if the testator has had the insurance payable to his estate in order that he might create trusts of it in his will. By keeping the insurance out of his estate, he may avoid administration expenses and to some extent Estate taxes and the insurance proceeds, in the absence of intent to defraud creditors, will not be part of the general estate subject to debts. (Insurance Law, § 55-a.) As to the new law effective Jan. 1, 1940, see § 166. See also [ 34 ]

Personal Property Law, § 15. These results are also true where the insurance is payable to individual beneficiaries. The points made above, however, are for consideration where the testator desires his insurance to go into a trust.

In some instances testator's property is of a kind which cannot be easily or quickly liquidated, such as real estate. It may be worth while for him to consider the advisability of having a certain amount of insurance payable to his estate to provide cash to meet tax liabilities and possible debts and administration expenses. 

BUSINESS 

Gifts of a business or its assets are frequently so ambiguously worded as to necessitate litigation to ascertain the testator's meaning. Thus questions arise whether a gift includes bills receivable and money in bank, (Matter of Lowe, 206 N.Y. 671) good will, accounts receivable and money in a safe, (Matter of Whitford, 146 Misc. 82) real property, (Matter of Frey, 154 Misc. 421) and automobiles, (Matter of Jones, 128 Misc. 244). See also Matter of Johnson, 167 Misc. 588.

If a testator owns a business and wishes to have it continued in a trust or does not wish to have it liquidated or desires to avoid a quick liquidation which might result in a loss, he should give his executors or trustees specific power to continue the business.86 (Willis v. Sharp, 113 N.Y. 586; Matter of Seigrist, 146 Misc. 236.) If the representatives continue the business without authority they will not be entitled to indemnity out of the estate for liabilities incurred, but will generally be held personally responsible on their executory contracts. (Willis v. 

Sharp, supra.) Neither will they be empowered without specific authorization to use any general estate funds other than the business assets themselves in the business. (Manhattan Oil Co. v. Gill, 118 App. Div. 

17.) It may not, however, be desirable to give them this power. 

If the business is a partnership, the partnership articles should be examined to ascertain what they provide in the event of the death of a partner.87 (See Partnership Law, § 62.) The testator may authorize the executors and trustees to permit his share to remain in the business as an investment or authorize them to form a new partnership in order to continue the business or to continue it for a specific time. (Costello v. Costello, 209 N.Y. 252; Columbus Watch Co. et al. v. Hopendyl et al., 135 N.Y. 430; Matter of Gorra, 135 Misc. 93.) Sometimes a testator provides that the executors may accept a statement from the surviving partners as to the value of his interest at the date of his death. 

Some businesses are close corporations, in which case they are carried on by the executors or trustees through their stock control. (Matter of Kinreich, 137 Misc. 735.) See Matter of Auditore, (249 N.Y. 335),

Matter of Allen, (111 Misc. 93) affd. 202 App. Div. 810, modified and affd. 236 N.Y. 503. Authorizations to retain the stock and carry on the business and if necessary to serve as officers or directors would seem advisable.

Sometimes provisions are made for the sale of stock to certain persons or employees specifying the manner in which its value is to be arrived at. Employees profit sharing plans may also be created. (Matter of Allen, supra). See Personal Property Law, § 13-b exempting trusts of personal property for certain stock bonus plans, pension plans and disability or death benefit plans for the exclusive benefit of employees from the perpetuities rule.

WASTING ASSETS 

Where a wasting asset, such as a leasehold, is included in a trust, care should be taken in drawing the trust provisions. If for instance the testator has a lease for twenty-one years and it has run six years at the date of his death, then in fifteen years there will be no trust principal at all unless each year the trustee sets aside part of the rentals so that at the expiration of the lease there will be on hand a sum equal to the valuation of the lease in the estate tax proceedings.88 (Frankel v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co., 152 App. Div. 58, affd. 209 N.Y. 

553.) But the testator may not intend the lease to be amortized. He may want the whole rentals collected to go to the life beneficiary. If this is his intention it should be specifically stated, otherwise the life beneficiary may be deprived of considerable income. In some cases, amortization may be desirable, but the testator may want to give the trustee power to invade the principal in order that unforeseen contingencies may be taken care of properly. The possibility of a sale of the leasehold by the trustee should also be considered, in which event the lease may really be amortized in effect, as the beneficiary will then receive only the income on the proceeds of sale, which income may be less than the net yearly rentals. Here again the testator may desire to give the trustee power to invade the principal as a safeguard to the life beneficiary. The lease should be examined before the will is drawn. Some leases contain options to buy the property and some contain renewal privileges and power to demolish existing buildings and to erect new ones. Different leases may require different treatment. See generally Guaranty Trust Co. v. 

Halstead, (245 N.Y. 447); Matter of Elsner, (210 Ap. Div. 575).

Royalties, copyrights, oil wells and mines are wasting assets, and if a will disposes of such property the law governing the same should be carefully examined.88

FOREIGN ESTATES 

Difficult problems arise in connection with estates situated partly in different countries. The law of the testator's domicile generally governs the validity of disposition of personalty and the law of the place where realty is situated governs its disposition.89 (Bishop v. Bishop, 258 N.Y. 

216.) This means not only as to questions such as the rule against perpetuities, but as to the persons to whom a testator can leave his property by will. In some countries the law may be very restrictive. A husband may not be able to cut off his wife, and in certain circumstances various relatives. This situation may apply to a less degree in some of the states. In New York, as we have seen, there are restrictions as to cutting off a surviving spouse. 90 (Decedent Estate Law, § 18.) This situation is ameliorated in some foreign countries by the application by their courts of the law of the country of testator's citizenship. (Matter of 

Chadwick, 109 Misc. 696.) Some countries may have reciprocity provisions in their laws or there may be treaties governing the problems.

GUARDIANS 

Guardians may be appointed by will of the persons and property of infants or of both during their minority.94 (Domestic Relations Law, § 81, Sur. Court Act, § 172.) Guardians may not be appointed for any class of persons other than infants. Appointments of guardians may be made by surviving parents only (except that either parent may by will appoint the other parent guardian.95 (Domestic Relations Law, § 81, Matter of Kellogg, 187 N.Y. 355.) If an attempt is made by a father or mother to appoint some one other than the surviving parent guardian of property of infants, or if an attempt is made by a grand-parent or other person to appoint such a guardian the appointment is sometimes upheld as that of a power in trust.95 (Matter of Baechler, 121 Misc. 

691, affd. 215 App. Div. 797; Matter of Baumann, 113 Misc. 630; Matter of Scoville, 72 Misc. 310.) Such a power covers only the property passing to the infants under the will. It does not include other property the infant may own. (Matter of Dobbs, 133 Misc. 90.) A person who has adopted a child, may, however, appoint a guardian. (Matter of MacRae, 189 N.Y. 142.)

A legacy or distributive share to the extent of $300 may be paid to the infant's father, mother or some competent person with whom he resides for the infant's use and benefit. (Surrogates Court Act, § 271.)

COMMISSIONS 

Where the estate or trust exceeds $100,000 each executor or trustee, provided there are not more than three, is entitled to a separate commission. If there are more than three, then the three allowable commissions will be divided among them.100 (Sur. Court Act, § 285.)

Where the estate or trust is less than $100,000 only one commission will be allowed and the executors or trustees as the case may be will have to divide the one commission among themselves.1 100 Commissions are allowed to executors upon principal and commissions are also allowed to the trustees upon principal unless the executors and trustees are the same persons and by the wording of the will the functions of the executors and trustees are blended together, in which case but one commission to each person will be allowed, subject of course to the rule above stated regarding the size of the estate being more or less than $100,000.101 See Matter of Ziegler, (168 App. Div. 735, affd.

EXECUTORS' AND TRUSTEES' POWERS 

A fiduciary is held to the standards of diligence, prudence and reasonable care.104 King v. Talbot, (40 N.Y. 76), Matter of Clark, (257 

N.Y. 132).

The Decedent Estate Law has been amended (Section 125)105 to provide that wills executed after May 2nd, 1936, cannot (1) exonerate a fiduciary from liability for failure to exercise reasonable care, diligence, and prudence, (2) give a fiduciary power to make binding and conclusive fixation of values of any assets for purposes of distribution, allocation or otherwise, or (3) give a fiduciary power to name a successor to serve without a bond unless as the 1938 amendment provides, the successor is a corporate fiduciary or is to act jointly with a corporate fiduciary. 

The following points should be called to the testator's attention that he may provide for them if he wishes:

If the executors or trustees are not to give bonds or other security the will should so state.106 (Sur. Court Act, § 97.) 

The testator may wish to name successor executors or trustees in the event of the death, resignation or failure to qualify of those first named.

If power is to be given to executors and trustees to sell, exchange, or mortgage, partition or lease property the will should contain express authorization.107 (See Decedent Estate Law, § 13.) Leases cannot be made for more than five years without a court order unless the will specifically authorizes the same. (Real Prop. Law, § 106.)
