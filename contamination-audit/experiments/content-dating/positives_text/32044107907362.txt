FOREWORD 

T NO TIME since the outbreak of the World War in 1914 has American interest in international affairs been so strong as it is today. 

The profound conflict of national wills brought to light by the Italo- Ethiopian war, and further aggravated by the Spanish civil war, now in its critical phase, has focused the attention of the public upon present happenings and compelled speculation upon future possibilities. 

Furthermore, the destruction of moral and material values which America suffered as a consequence of her participation in the World War, and the financial depression of which that war was one of the causes, together with the possibility of a repetition of such disasters, have made the American people more sensitive to danger and therefore more determined to avoid it.

This sensitivity and determination is reflected in munitions investigations, initiation of neutrality legislation, declarations of policy and heavy increases of expenditure in armament. Amongst the citizenry it has stimulated a great extension of open forums, study groups, and other media for the discussion of matters of international concern. 

Despite this enlargement of the facilities for gathering international information the lay public is for the most part inadequately informed and therefore somewhat perplexed. The average American is more than ever aware of the responsibility he has in helping to keep America out of future conflicts. He is also alive to the undeniable fact that the 

United States, as a great nation, has a real interest in cooperating with the rest of the international community to maintain peace, but he lacks, as indeed do the citizens of all countries, that sense of direction which will enable him to fulfill his national and international responsibilities in the most effective way.

It is not and could not be the purpose of this pamphlet to give a comprehensive picture of present world conditions. Still less is it possible to examine the historical background of the conflicts of national interest which have arisen since the Peace Settlements. What follows is an attempt to sketch hastily the broad outlines of a picture the details of which must be filled in by the knowledge of the reader.

COOPERATION OR CHAOS 

by 

CHARLES DOUGLAS BOOTH "INTERDEPENDENCE OF States" 

WOULD seem that to depict the inter- I national situation must begin with a statement that the world of nations is an interdependent society. This interdependence economic, financial, political and cultural is so obvious that it requires no proof. Even at a time when doctrines of isolation and self-containment are attractive to large groups, every nation is at once the victim and the beneficiary of interdependence. 

But realization of the fact of interdependence does not prevent conflicts of national will. Indeed sometimes it provokes such conflicts. For example, the new technology demands worldwide distribution of mass production, but economic nationalism tends to prevent such distribution. It also concentrates, for a time, power to consume in the hands of a few states, giving them higher standards than others with resulting rivalry, instability and periodic depressions. 

The diversities between states as respects natural resources and technical development require intelligent cooperation to create and maintain prosperity in unity; but economic nationalism guides these diversities not toward unity but toward the temporary profit and power of a few groups and a few governments at the expense of the rest, and the natural result is economic anarchy accompanied by political hostility. 

Within the natural family, the smallest but, perhaps, the most important of all social units, and also within those larger groups, the municipality and the state, we have in a great measure solved the problem of dealing with conflicts of will by "Peaceful Change" in the atmosphere of "Collective Security.

This two-fold application of the principles of justice pro- 5

vides us with constitutional means of reconsidering and revising the law, as well as the constitutional means of upholding and enforcing it. But unfortunately man is not a consistent animal. In his normal life there are vast disparities between the standards he has attained in different spheres of activity. 

And today international morality lags far behind personal and municipal morality with the depressing result that conflicts of will between nations frequently culminate in violence to the profit of the state with the strongest armed forces. 

WORLD DIVIDED INTO TWO FACTIONS 

For the past two or three years it has been the habit of observers to divide the world into two great factions, based upon disparity of material possessions and the conflicts which arise out of attempts to correct this disparity. On the one hand we have a group of states possessing adequate territory for the expansion of their population, and adequate raw resources within their own frontiers or in their own possession. 

This group of statically contented Powers have all they wish, or at least all they need, and require Peace in which to continue their development. The United States, Great Britain, France and Russia are the great representatives of this "Have" group. It is not here necessary to examine into the circumstances or the methods which place this group in their present fortunate position-suffice it to say that some of them acquired the territory they now have by imperialistic war, and other questionable means, which they now condemn upon ethical grounds.

On the other hand there is a group of Powers that are dynamically discontented. They have not got what they need either in territory or raw resources. They have been unable to get it by "Peaceful Change" and are now preparing to take it by violence. The great representatives of this "Have Not" group are Germany, Japan and Italy. There are of course other states in the same unfortunate position. The dangers arising from their discontent do not come from the possibility of immediate aggression but rather from the probability that

they will allow themselves to be used by one or other of the dynamically discontented Great Powers in a desperate adventure to escape from their present intolerable lot. Austria and 

Bulgaria are examples of such states. 

In considering the problems arising out of the "Have" and "Have Not" conflict, it is to be noted that not only was there no satisfactory arrangement made for "Peaceful Change" to satisfy the dynamically discontented group, but, from the close of the War until today, the statically contented Powers have adopted an attitude of moral superiority, which has had the effect of increasing the discontent. 

Useful as this "Have" and "Have Not" method of explanation may be, it is an over-simplification which neglects other vital factors influencing national behavior. Followed too far it leads moreover to a pessimism not entirely justified by history, for we have examples of "Have Not" states that, by renouncing empire and adjusting their internal economies, live in prosperity and peace. We can also cite a few examples of "Have" powers displaying a flexibility of policy which permits them to live in peace with their neighbors. 

DYNAMIC DISCONTENT OF "HAVE NOTS" 

Germany's present discontent without doubt arises from a variety of causes of which the most important are: the lamentable stupidities of the Peace Settlements, based upon the assumption that the Central Powers were solely responsible for the World War; the huge and unstated burden of reparations placed upon Germany in 1919 and removed too late to avoid disastrous economic consequences; the taking of Germany's colonies; the forcible disarmament of that great nation. The reducing of Germany to a position of political inferiority and her tardy admission to the League of Nations, together with a complacent attitude of moral superiority on the part of the victorious Allies, intolerable to a proud and virile race, have contributed more than most of us realize to the present German attitude, and perhaps are among the principal causes for the peculiar and menacing form of internal government now existing in Germany.

So much for that part of Germany's troubles which has its cause outside of the state. Since 1932 we have been the witnesses of a new German policy. In the political field this new policy has not been such as would develop the fullest measure of friendly cooperation by Germany's European neighbors. Indeed some of them have characterized it as aggressive and truculent. In the economic sphere the new German policy has found its expression in the increase of manifestations of economic nationalism, such as currency controls, discriminatory barter agreements, regimentation of production as to quantity and price, accompanied by desperate efforts to achieve selfcontainment for strategic rather than purely economic ends. It is not to be expected that a continuance of these practices on the part of a Great Power like Germany could promote internal prosperity, or external good relations. 

Japan's dissatisfaction arises from slightly different causes in that her lack of extensive territory cannot be attributed directly to the Peace Settlements, but is rather a product of her late arrival upon the scene as a Great Power. For her, nevertheless, similar conditions prevail: density of population, paucity of natural resources, and reluctance of the great Colonial Powers to share with her the fruits of their own imperialistic adventures of the past along with an attitude on the part of certain states, in the matter of Japanese immigration, which has deeply wounded the pride of this sensitive oriental people. 

As Germany expressed her discontent in the West so has Japan expressed hers in the East. The imperial adventures of 

Japan in Manchuria and Northern China have kept the Soviet Government in a state of nervous anxiety as to the integrity of their Asiatic possessions and communications, whilst Japanese commercial policy in respect of textiles has given the British, and perhaps the citizens of this country, some cause for fear that markets would be entirely lost by competition arising out of great disparity of wage levels.

With regard to Italy, which, for the past fifteen years, has expressed its dynamic discontent in a series of rapid and disturbing diplomatic and military adventures, it will be re-

membered that the close of the World War left Italy victorious, but prostrate and disillusioned. Before the Italian entry into the war, and as an inducement to secure Italian help, Great Britain and France promised to Italy a very considerable share of the prospective spoils. The Secret Treaty of London (April 1915) in which part of these offers had been given legal form stipulated that, if Great Britain and France acquired Germany's African colonies, Italy would be compensated by an extension of its own African possessions, Eritrea, Somaliland and Libya. The German colonies did in fact pass to 

Great Britain, to France, and to the Union of South Africa, who proceeded to administer them as League mandates, yet Italy only received a series of minor boundary adjustments, a few shares in the Ethiopian railway and a small part of British East Africa (Jubaland) in 1924. Though some of these concessions had strategic value, none of the territory received was worth much either from the point of view of colonization or of raw resources. Italy's aspirations in regard to the Dalmation Coast of the Adriatic and the enclave in the Gulf of 

Adalia were also frustrated.

Another major factor in the Italian discontent arises from the population problem, and still another from the lack of great raw resources, such as coal, iron, copper, cotton and oil within the territorial frontiers of Italy and her possessions. 

Before the Great War, when the Italian population was increasing at the rate of about 200,000 a year, Italians migrated in large numbers to the New World, and the dollar remittances they sent back to Italy formed an important item in the invisible assets of the Italian State. Today when the Italian increase of population is nearly 500,000 a year, and the density of population has reached a figure of approximately 650 to the square mile of habitable territory, the countries which formerly received Italians in large numbers have placed drastic restrictions upon immigration. Thus Italy is, as Frank Simonds put it, "imprisoned between the walls constituted by her own economic poverty and the monopolistic policies of the selfsufficient nations." But neither the continuance of monopolis-

tic practices by the satisfied Powers, nor the badly supported plea of necessity, justified Italy's aggression in Ethiopia or her present alleged unofficial interference in the internal affairs of Spain which, within the past two years, have seriously prejudiced the cause of European peace. 

There has been much talk of the sinfulness of aggression, but little realization that history is, and must be a continual revision of existing situations. In the past such revision could only be effected by war. The League of Nations Covenant recognizes, in Article 19, that this revision must be effected by peaceful means; but there is little evidence in the history of the League from which these "Have Not" countries can derive hope that their needs will be satisfied.

In the face of these facts it seems futile to expect that the "Have Nots," victims of inequitable distribution of resources and territory, will ever be wholehearted supporters of the international community whose organ is the League of Nations whilst that organ is a supporter of the status quo which they consider to be unjust. Undue emphasis upon the "Collective Security" aspect of international justice, with little or no provision for "Peaceful Change," has brought the world in seventeen years to a point where war is in the mind of every statesman.

Indeed if the needs of the dissatisfied Powers are not taken into account, as a matter of immediate concern, and generously met, the peace of the world will continue to be threatened at short intervals for years to come.

But how can their needs be met and what is the role of 

America in the process? Any attempt to restore an approximation of justice in the international community must proceed along definite lines, both the economic and political. It cannot be conducted in an interdependent world by the old methods of individual negotiation between states because every problem involving a conflict of wills or needs between two states concerns the general interest of all states, either directly or indirectly. 10

POSSIBILITIES OF PEACEFUL CHANGE 

What are the foundations upon which we can build? Let us consider first the question of territorial redistribution, and the motives for the present demands of the "Have Nots." 

These appear to be based upon: (1) Pressure of population with consequent need of colonies to which the surplus population can migrate. Examination of this claim brings up at once several questions. (a) Have the dissatisfied Powers, Italy, Germany and Japan, used to the full the colonies they have had, or now have, as an outlet for surplus population? (b) Is there any suitable territory, they could so use, the transfer of which would not endanger the peace of the world or inflict injustice upon politically conscious indigenous populations?

The answer of history to the first question is definitely unfavorable to Italy, Germany and Japan. Italy has been in effective possession of Eritrea for fifty years and, in that time, upon 2000 square miles of highlands suitable for white habitation she has settled less than 400 colonists of which the total number of families engaged in agriculture is 84. In the whole of the Italian African possessions which it is estimated would accommodate 100,000 white inhabitants there are less than 

20,000 bona fide settlers.

Similarly Japan, with large undeveloped territory in Formosa and in Manchukuo, which she has conquered despite the Pact of Paris has had a negligible migration of settlers to these areas. As for Germany, although many German scientists have stated that the tropics are colonizable by Europeans, there were, we know, very few settlers in her African colonies prior to the Great War.

When we begin to look for suitable territory, which the "Have Not" Powers may effectively colonize, we find that Africa, the present objective of Italian and German colonial aspirations is not, and can never be, a satisfactory outlet for the surplus population of European countries. 11

Although the average density of population over great areas in Africa is meager, it will be found that such large units contain vast tracts practically uninhabitable owing to lack of water, uncultivatable soil or because they are infested by the tsetse fly, carrier of a germ, trypanosome, fatal alike to men and beasts. 

When these areas are subtracted it is notable that lands really favorable for settlement are already rather densely populated. 

Furthermore, with the advent of civilized control, epidemics are being checked, infant mortality is decreasing and the native population is increasing rapidly. For these reasons one is forced to agree with Sir Norman Angell that territorial changes in Africa would not exert any appreciable influence on the population problem and Europe must look elsewhere for an outlet for her surplus millions. 

There remain the temperate zones of North America, South America and Siberia. German or Italian migration to these areas might be arranged by international agreement, but this is a question of removing immigration restrictions rather than one of ceding territory. Whilst we are dealing with the population problem, it is to be remarked that the validity of claims for colonial expansion by Italy and Japan is somewhat vitiated by the fact that both countries have resorted to unusual methods to increase their populations. The international community cannot, and should not, attempt to control the internal policy of any state. any state. But when that state urges a claim for redistribution of territory, at the expense of others, upon the basis of an intolerable condition of population pressure, which it makes no effort to alleviate but deliberately fosters, then surely there is good ground for rebuttal. (2) The need for access to supplies of raw material and of access to markets for manufactured goods. This claim divides itself into two distinct parts: (a) Unfettered opportunity for obtaining raw materials. (b) Access to local markets free from discrimination or differential tariffs.

Of late no country has been denied raw materials, indeed all states have been anxious to dispose of their raw materials 12

at almost any price. Nevertheless, it must be admitted that a situation could arise, and has occasionally arisen, where one or two countries with something approaching monopolistic control might hold up the rest of the world. This was attempted by Great Britain, a few years ago in the case of rubber. 

It is now practiced in the shipment of tin from British dependencies upon which product there is an export duty, unless the tin is smelted in the British Empire. This problem is distinctly not one to be solved by territorial redistribution but rather by the removal of trade barriers, thus freeing the flow of goods and services.

The same answer may be given to the claim of the dissatisfied states that there is a considerable handicap in buying with a foreign currency. Under normal conditions the inconvenience arising from different national currencies present few and very minor difficulties. These difficulties at the moment have been accentuated by a slump in trade, but, for the most part are due to the growth of extreme economic nationalism. This has upset the system of exchanges by the imposition of differential tariffs so that Germany, Italy and several other states are forced to place an embargo on the withdrawal of money from their countries. This temporary and artificial difficulty is not so much due to a lack of facilities for purchase of raw materials as to the senseless economic policy of nearly all states, of which all are the victims in one way or another. 

Italy and Germany have turned their eyes toward Africa for colonies but it is not from that continent that their main food staples, wheat and meat, can be secured; nor could they secure from there the quantities of cotton, wool, coal and iron ore they need for conversion into manufactured goods. 

England has a population density, (742 per square mile) more than double that of Germany (347 per square mile) or

Italy (344 per square mile) and England imports by far the greatest part of her raw materials and foodstuffs from countries over whose fiscal policies she has no more control than has Germany or Italy.

For the greater part of these supplies she pays in a foreign 13

currency. It is in time of war only that exclusive control of colonial markets has vital importance and this depends not upon possession of colonies but upon sea power. We have therefore some ground for concluding that, although undoubted advantages as well as certain liabilities accrue to the Great Colonial Powers, there is no real basis for the claim by Italy, Germany or Japan, that colonies in Africa are necessary as a means of access to raw materials or to markets. (3) The right to equality with the present colonial Powers on the grounds of national prestige and status. 

Discussion of this claim leads us into debatable ground. It is not to be answered with an economic argument. Even if it could be proved that colonies were a burden and a disability, the willingness with which Great Britain and France bear this burden, and their extreme reluctance to inflict it upon other powers by transference of colonies to them makes all their protests seem artificial. That Portugal, Belgium and certain 

British Dominions, with small population and minor prestige, should have colonies or mandates whilst Germany and Italy are denied, is, to these Great Powers, a denial of equality difficult to tolerate. Italy nurses also the grievance that Great 

Britain and France took all the Mandates at the Peace Conference whilst she had none. To meet demands from the dissatisfied Powers for colonies upon the basis of their claim for equality of status, is a matter which presents endless thorny problems.

So far as the so-called "backward races" are concerned, cession of territory, or transference of mandate, would only be possible where the native populations gave their willing consent, or were at least so politically backward as to be completely indifferent to change of sovereignty. The time has passed when the allegiance of peoples can be changed without regard to their wishes.

The weakness of the case of the dissatisfied Powers in respect of their economic necessities, and the difficulty of satisfying their claims for equality of status, does not, however, remove the necessity of satisfying them. In the past the right 14

to possess colonies was based upon the right of conquest which except for voluntary cession or purchase was recognized as the only indisputable legal title. But those Powers which have signed and ratified the Pact of Paris have deliberately abandoned conquest as a legal basis for acquiring sovereignty. Henceforth, therefore, title can only be acquired by some form of voluntary cession. 

There is one form of disaster that the whole world wishes to avoid—namely-War. "Collective Security" with its necessary corollary of "Peaceful Change" seems to be the only way of doing it. War between the Great Powers is now threatened because of extreme disparity of assets, psychological as well as material. So far, all the important territorial changes since the 

World War, that have decreased the disparity between the "Have" and "Have Not" Powers, have been made by violent change at the expense of the weak. 

Can we continue this process, and hope thus to avoid a general war? There are some who contend that we can. At the height of the Sino-Japanese crisis it was seriously suggested, by certain Australians, that Great Britain should encourage the 

Japanese to strike in Manchuria and North China because that might induce them to forego any contemplated imperialistic adventures in Australia. In the Ethiopian affair we have had the 

Hoare-Laval Peace Plan which was nothing more than an attempt at collusion between the "Haves" and "Have Nots" with the object of despoiling the weak. 

There is still a great deal of territory belonging to the Dutch in the East Indies, the Portuguese in Africa and the Belgian Congo which might well be wrenched from its present owners if a satisfactory combination could be effected between Great Britain and France on the one side, and Germany and Italy on the other. Is not this perhaps a possible alternative to the difficult and slow method of revision and redistribution by consent within the framework of a collective system? Without any consideration of the contemptible ethics of such a proposal, history demonstrates that the consequences of such collusion in dishonor would be not the avoidance of war, but its 15

precipitation in the near future. Germany and Austria-Hungary allowed Italy to rob Turkey of Libya in 1911 in order to retain the Italian support for their coming war against the Entente Powers. When the Great War came Italy fought for the Allies, and Turkey for the Central Powers. Everything in the past shows that the policy of throwing the weak to the wolves simply increases the appetite of the wolves, and builds up burdens of hatred which at suitable moments are expressed in wars of revenge.

There is a possible alternative to this disgraceful collusion in a policy of renunciation by the "Have" Powers. If Great 

Britain and France are willing to buy immunity by renouncing

Empire, and thus to become of no more political importance than Sweden, we can perhaps have peace with honor. An 

Empire which insists on clinging to one-fourth of the land surface of the world, and putting tariff walls and immigration barriers around it cannot decide at the same time never to fight. Therefore, unless they are willing to accept unreservedly the policy of renunciation with all it implies, the "Have" Powers are driven back once again upon the policy of "Collective Security" coupled with "Peaceful Change".

NEED FOR INTERNATIONAL ORGANIZATION 

One of the conditions precedent to the inauguration of any such policy is willing cooperation by every major Power. It will never be possible, in the international community, to maintain peace and introduce an approximation of justice whilst Great Powers seek to insulate themselves against possible disaster by isolationist policies of an economic or political nature. 

The very measures which are designed to protect the isolationist power must of necessity contain provisions which, in certain circumstances, will frustrate the efforts of the organized international community to achieve "Collective Security" and thus align the nation responsible for this frustration with the disturbers of the peace.

The term "Collective Security" as applied to a policy postulates an organization and a method of action. It assumes a world gradually emerging from the blood feud of national wars 16

as a means of settlement of the conflict of national wills. It takes the almost universal adherence to the Pact of Paris in which war, as an instrument of national policy, was renounced, as sufficient ground that this assumption is justified. 

Realities, however, cannot be ignored and those who uphold the policy of "Collective Security" must therefore provide against the extraordinary case where one or more Powers resort to the primitive method of aggressive war. It is only too evident that a state which does take such action in defiance of its obligations to the Covenant of the League and the Pact of Paris cannot be made to stop by peaceful argument. Such a state must be convinced that war is an anti-social act in the only terms it can understand, namely, in terms of sanctions applied by the whole international community. 

A sanction is the legal term given to measures for securing obedience to law. Under national systems of law sanctions usually take the form of penalties for the breach of law. In the international community they may consist of measures taken to prevent a breach of law, or to check the law-breaker in the act of aggression, or, after an aggression has been committed, to compel him to submit to the law and deprive him of the fruits of his aggression. The legal basis for the application of sanctions is contained in Article 16 of the League Covenant, although other articles include in their scope consideration of acts of potential or actual aggression. 

Sanctions are of two kinds: 

a. Economic and Financial b. Military and Naval

The first consist of measures imposed in restraint of trade upon the aggressor such as embargoes upon: 

1. Loans 2. Imports 

3. Exports of arms and munitions 

4. Export of raw materials essential for war 

5. All other exports 

6. All imports and exports 

Such embargoes may be limited or complete, their degree of intensity being determined by the necessities of each particular case. 17

MILITARY, NAVAL OR AIR SANCTIONS 

But

Manifestly, economic sanctions can go no farther than complete international boycott, and these, if universally applied, might well prove effective in the great majority of cases. there always remains the possibility that the Power against which the sanctions are used may, during the course of their application, commit acts of violence which frustrate the enforcement. Thus it secures the materials necessary to continue its initial aggression. To deal with this situation the international community has only one recourse, namely, to employ overwhelming force to end the private use of force by the aggressor state. In other words, every state member of the 

League and all those outside the League who adhere to the policy of "Collective Security" should, if necessary, be ready to participate in armed police action to stop private wars. 

It does not follow that all states members of the League would be obliged to partake in military sanctions by the use of their armed forces. There has been sufficient interpretation of the relevant articles of the League Covenant to establish the principle of divided responsibility in the matter of application of sanctions, either economic or military. The proportion and extent of assistance which each state gives in the application of military sanctions to an aggressor must of necessity be determined by considerations of geography, strategic possibility, armed strength and finally of vital interest. In a certain sense the Monroe Doctrine imposes upon the United States, because of her special interest, the duty of preventing, with her armed forces, aggression in certain very definite regions, and thus of acting as an instrument of the international community in the preservation of peace by the application of sanctions. 

The practicability of sanctions as a means of enforcing peace is, without doubt, open to question, whilst the United States, Germany and Japan remain outside the League of Nations. 

Indeed recent history shows that even the nations members of the League find it extremely difficult to apply sanctions adequate to achieve the end sought without precipitating a war of which the evils might be more widespread than those caused 18

by the aggression which the sanctions are designed to arrest. 

The practical demonstration of the failure of partial sanctions in the Italo-Ethiopian war and the aggravation of international tensions that followed have seriously damaged the prestige of "Collective Security" and no doubt are in a large measure responsible for the armament race now in progress. 

ECONOMIC PEACE AND POLITICAL STABILITY 

But inadequacy of the means does not affect the desirability of the ends, "Collective Security" and "Peaceful Change.'

These are, in some sense, good in themselves toward which men aim once they understand them as such. This being granted, how then must we plan the structure of organization for peace?

It would appear that the world must recognize, by action, as well as in theory, the very intimate connection between the flow of trade, services and the migration of peoples on the one hand, and political stability on the other.

It will be useless to attempt redistribution of territory as a means of "Peaceful Change", however unfair the present distribution may seem, until, by sane and well-considered economic measures, we have eliminated the international economic anarchy which gives to frontiers their present importance. High and unscientific tariff barriers, import quotas, drastic restrictions of immigration, currency chaos, and all the drastic restrictions of immigration, currency chaos and all the with, if we are to attack political problems with any hope of successful solution.

To reverse the present tide of economic nationalism will not be an easy task even when our leaders are aware of its dangers. The illusion of self-sufficiency is attractive. The prospect of immediate profit to be derived from protective tariffs, and the ever present spectre of unemployment or reduced standards of living which might result from abandonment of such tariffs, far overshadows the more remote but none the less real advantages of a general increase in world trade. 19

We have only to look at the present condition of Europe to see how difficult it is for states to abandon the policy of economic nationalism, even when such a policy is seen to be directly responsible for political problems which threaten to engulf the whole continent in war. A good example of the direct connection between the stupidities of "economic nationalism" and the perils of political instability is the case of Austria. Before the Great War Austria-Hungary was almost an ideal economic unity, containing within its borders all the raw resources and industrial development necessary to a wellrounded national life. The Peace Settlements left Austria a very small state, with some six million people, over two million of whom were in the great city of Vienna, and nearly 94% of the area of the country being barren mountains. Immediately after the War all the newly created and greatly enlarged states erected high tariff barriers. The effect of this action was that Austria, formerly a supplier of goods and services down the Danube, was cut off from her natural markets and bankruptcy resulted. Then gravitation toward Germany resulted in the Anschluss movement and the proposed Austro-German Union of 1930. When this was prevented by some of the former Allied Powers, for political reasons, there was another collapse and now the separate existence and integrity of Austria is an extremely serious political problem. Eighteen years of negotiation has failed to produce any practical plan for the removal of trade barriers between Austria and her former customers. Even the Little Entente States, whose political aims are almost identical, have suffered intense distresses from the barriers erected between themselves. Yet yearly Economic Conferences have done little to remove these barriers. 

Similar situations exist in many other parts of the world where governments cry for revision of the territorial provisions of the Peace Settlements, and do little or nothing to remove the economic obstacles to peace between themselves and their neighbors. But the patent difficulty of securing economic sanity does not make it less desirable as an objective. 

Hopeful signs of an approach to this objective are present in the Trade Agreements program of the United States Govern- 20

ment,¹ which has resulted in the signature and ratification of some fifteen trade treaties between the United States and foreign nations. The Buenos Aires Conference of American nations held December 1-23, 1936, although not productive of great immediate or tangible results, has had moral effects which will long be felt in the improvement of commercial and political relations between the countries of the Western Hemisphere. To this may be added the effects of the Textile Conference of the International Labor Organization held in Washington during April, 1937.

The British Commonwealth of Nations, after the questionable experiences arising from the Imperial Conference at Ottawa (1932), is now,2 at the Post Coronation Imperial Con- 

2. Recent declarations of the British Government from which followed the "Raw Materials Conference" show that the British are fully alive to the necessity of examining upon an international basis the matter of freeing the movement of raw materials. 

AMERICA'S POSITION 

To say that all these problems concern America very little and that the United States can, by minding her own affairs, escape the disasters arising from a failure to solve them by the rest of the world, is to ignore the bitter lessons of history. Participation in past wars arising out of similar causes and the practical certainty of participation in future wars, should these involve Great Powers over considerable periods of time, leave only one intelligent course of action, namely, cooperation with the rest of the world to remove the causes of conflict before the incidence of war. 

1. See pp. 57 et seq. “America Must Act,” by Francis B. Sayre. 21

There are many citizens who, in good faith and with the best of intentions, would seek to insulate America from the quarrels of Europe and Asia. The Neutrality Act, adopted by Congress on April 29, 1937, is in a large measure the expression of this point of view. By this Act the provisions of the temporary legislation of August 31, 1935, which prohibited the export of arms, munitions and implements of war to belligerent countries, have been amplified and extended. The nature of these amplifications which extend also to civil strife in foreign states is a further abandonment of the classic principle of neutrality rights in order that the United States should not be exposed to the danger of participation in war to defend such rights. Further provisions of the Act call for, in certain circumstances, restrictions on the shipment of materials other than arms, munitions and implements of war, prohibition of the purchase or sale of securities of belligerent states, control of munition manufacture, prohibition of American vessels from carrying arms to belligerent states, control of foreign vessels in American ports, special restrictions upon submarines or armed merchant vessels, forbidding of American citizens from travel on vessels of belligerent states and prohibition of armament upon American merchant vessels. 

It will be noted that the provisions of this Act do not apply to American Republics engaged in war against non-American states, provided the American Republic so engaged is not cooperating with a non-American state or states in such a war. 

This is not the place to go into the history of American neutrality or to elaborate the record of a struggle between the advocates of mandatory neutrality, discretionary neutrality and isolationist policy. The text of the Act shows that it is a compromise between these several points of view, with very little influence from that considerable body of opinion in America which believes in international cooperation through the principles of "Collective Security" and "Peaceful Change”.

Having regard to the fact that the provisions of the Neutrality Act undoubtedly reflect the prevailing isolationist sentiment in the United States and that, in some measure, this senti- 22

ment is justified by a lack of sincerity and good faith on the part of some Great Powers both inside and outside the League of Nations, it remains to examine how far American citizens can go in the direction of international cooperation and what should be the nature of this cooperation. 

The American people, whilst fully conscious of their national and international responsibilities in the present crisis, should not allow their desire to keep America out of war on the one hand, or the zeal for international cooperation on the other hand, to force them into a position where their good intentions are frustrated, by legislation not sufficiently flexible to provide against future possibilities.

American membership in certain of the visible organs of international conciliation and justice may, for the moment, be inadvisable and is certainly impractical. Caution in framing legislation with external reflections is, of course, desirable, but neither of these courses of action constitutes the grounds of a positive policy directed toward the organization of international peace and world prosperity. 

The least America can do in her own interest, and should do, in fulfillment of her natural obligations as a member of the international community, is to examine carefully her own policies, with a view to altering these policies or modifying them in the interest of peace. It does little good to decry war as a barbarism and to seek means to escape from participation in war by legislation of an isolationist character. No state can cease to will war, without ceasing also to will the causes of war. Exaggerated nationalism, either political or economic, has been shown beyond doubt to be a fruitful cause of war. 

When this nationalism is manifested by unnecessarily high tariffs, or any other domestic policy which stifles the flow of goods and services, or diverts gold from its proper channels and function, then there is good cause for modification of national policy. The great majority of economists here in America agree that the present level of the United States tariff does stifle or, at least, seriously retard the flow of goods and services to the prejudice of American trade and domestic pros- 23

perity, to say nothing of its disturbing effects upon the international economy as a whole.

If this be true, then American citizens need look no further for a safe means of contributing to domestic prosperity and international peace. The Trade Agreements program of the 

United States Government is directed toward increasing trade through simultaneous reduction of tariff barriers, by the United States and all countries with whom treaties can be made upon equitable terms. Its extension will inevitably tend to generalization of lower tariffs, abandonment of economic nationalism, increase of world commerce and all the benefits in human welfare which result from these things. In the presence of a complicated and confusing European political situation it is not surprising that natural inclination and special circumstances should produce in American minds a certain aloofness, accompanied by reluctance, to commit the country to any direct political measures which might require the use of American men or resources in a quarrel between foreign states. economic cooperation, of the kind that is now being advocated, requires no political commitments, and contributes not only to peace but also to domestic welfare. It is therefore a good in itself and, when apprehended as such, should determine the will of free men to pursue it.
