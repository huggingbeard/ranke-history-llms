PREFACE 

In the preparation of this Workbook the authors have endeavored to meet the needs and challenge the abilities and interests of a wide variety of pupils. They have sought to give the teacher a highly flexible teaching aid so constructed as to be readily adjusted to local teaching conditions. 

The authors have tried to get away from older concepts of workbooks by avoiding the use of mere "busy work" and extensive testing of content. 

However, content has not been neglected. Rather it has become the means by which the pupil learns to grasp an implication, to make a comparison, to draw a conclusion, to evaluate, to establish a concept and to reach a generalization. Furthermore, the deliberate use in the Workbook of "Information, Please!", a section of the educational equipment in The American Way of Life, as an oral testing device at the conclusion of the study of most chapters, enables early, regular and complete discussion in the class. 

In addition to unit overviews, outlines and lists of general references, for each of the ten units there are listed readily available sources of statistical information; source books; useful maps and charts; pamphlets and periodicals and the sources from which such may be obtained. The authors are indebted to Dr. William E. Hartley whose Selected Films for American 

History and Problems, New York, 1940, and whose supplementary evaluations in Social Education guided them in their choice of the films listed for each unit. They are grateful also to Miss Emilie L. Haley of the Association of School Film Libraries, Inc., New York City, for her counsel in the selection of the recordings suggested for each unit. 

The wide range of activities provided for in this Workbook is divided. 

into three areas or sections: (1) Preliminary Exercises and Activities; (2) Ac tivities for Individuals and Small Groups; (3) Research Activities. The first area furnishes sufficient content and activities for a fundamental understanding of American history. This section should be used as a basic assignment for all members of the class. The second area provides for the abilities and interests of the majority of pupils while the research activities listed in the third section allow for advanced study, especially by adaptation of local community history and problems. Pupils should be guided carefully in their selection of exercises from these two sections to the end that group as well as individual interests be served. Throughout all three sections there is ample opportunity for the development of content and study techniques.

For each unit, the five (or more) tests include an organization test, a vocabulary self-quiz, an attitude indicator (for nine of the units), and two or more study-skill tests. The organization test may serve as a brief summary of the unit (especially for classes using the Morrisonian method). The vocabulary self-quiz should be used in connection with the glossary exercises in this Workbook. The opinionaires cannot be scored in terms of "right" and "wrong" answers. There will be many who would hold either side of a question to be “right." The class should study these questions in order to test their own consistency, to determine liberalism and conservatism, and to determine the "consensus" of the class. These statements may also be used as topics for discussion or debate. 

The study tests are designed to measure some of the objectives of instruction. If these are the objectives of the teacher and students, the mark on this test would evaluate the work of the teacher as well as the work of the class. Given during the study of a unit, these study tests would call the attention of the teacher and students to the essential learnings: those dealing with skills and habits.

The authors hope that these tests will not be used to give marks of "passing" and "failing," but rather that they may serve as illustrations of pupil growth or of the need for remedial instruction. The construction of keys for these tests by a class committee would be a good learning exercise. 

The summarizing activities serve as samples of pupils' organizing and summarizing abilities.

UNIT I. 

AMERICA, BLESSED BY NATURE, BECOMES THE CROSSROADS OF THE WORLD'S PEOPLES UNIT OVERVIEW 

To this land of ours, this land of liberty, men, women and children have been coming for over 300 years. These people did not come to America as conquering armies. Most of them came as families in search of freedoms denied them in their old countries. They came to find freedom of religion, freedom of speech, freedom to make their own way in life. From where have they come and where did they settle down? What thoughts, habits, skills did they bring with them?

They found a rich country. Nature has given to the American people great forests and wide prairies, high mountains and rolling hills, broad rivers and countless lakes. Hidden beneath the surface of the land are vast sources of mineral wealth. The common efforts of millions of people from practically all over the world have developed our rich resources and have made Americans the richest, as well as the freest, people in the world. 

In the course of this study, we will learn: 

I. Who are Americans? 

A. What peoples founded and strengthened America? 

B. What peoples have helped America in recent years?

C. How has America sought to reduce the flow of immigration? 

II. What has Nature given America? 

A. What are the agricultural and mineral resources of America? 

B. How have these resources affected American life?

C. How has modern science increased our resources and their use?

UNIT II. AMERICANS CHOOSE A DEMOCRATIC GOVERNMENT UNIT OVERVIEW 

This free land of ours has not always been a democracy. It has moved by gradual steps toward more and more democracy. Remember that there are several kinds of democracy. There is the right to vote and to hold office which are examples of political democracy. There is religious democracy which means absolute freedom in religious matters. There is also economic democracy, or right to choose your own job or way of making a living. In early colonial days few colonies granted all three kinds of democracy. In 

Massachusetts Bay, for example, the right to vote and hold office was given only to members of the Congregational Church. In Maryland, the law said that while any kind of Christian could vote or hold office, Jews and nonbelievers could not. With the Revolution there came an end to these religious requirements for voters. At the same time, more economic democracy was granted as property qualifications were eased. These things were done by the new states as they made their own constitutions and included in them guarantees of religious and political democracy. 

Growing out of the experience gained in colonial self-government and in the making of our states' constitutions, out of the traditional rights American colonists had brought over from England, and from the ideas of such writers as Thomas Paine and John Locke, came the demands for a better union of the American states. This demand resulted in our Constitution, the work of a group of brilliant far-seeing Americans who set up a system of government that has endured for more than 150 years and which has shaped itself to the changing needs of the American way of life. 

By amendments, by interpretation of our Constitution and by certain socalled "unwritten laws" or customs, the American people have moved steadily toward a more complete all-around democracy.

UNIT IV. AMERICA FOLLOWS THE AGRICULTURAL WAY OF LIFE UNIT OVERVIEW 

The American people became, during colonial days, a nation of middleclass farmers. Most of these farms were self-supporting because the farmers had to grow or to make almost everything they needed. But by the early 

1800's inventions were beginning to make great changes in farm life. 

For example, the cotton gin revolutionized the South which became the "Cotton Kingdom." By the latter 1800's the United States was quite clearly divided into agricultural regions, each specializing in one or two great "cash crops." Improved agricultural methods and equipment naturally stepped up farm production. Without this how could our great industrial centers have existed? On the other hand, scientific methods and mechanization spelled trouble for the farmers. Overproduction, low prices for their products, and the high cost of the goods the farmers bought became chief sources of a farm problem the American people have yet to solve completely. Meanwhile, the farmer has tried to help himself by demanding government regulation of railroads. His demand for inflated money resulted in a great political campaign in 1896 between the Republicans and the farmer-controlled Democrats. High points we will consider in this 

Unit are:

UNIT V. THE INDUSTRIAL WAY OF LIFE GAINS THE UPPER HAND UNIT OVERVIEW 

During the late 1700's a number of inventions brought about the Industrial Revolution. Production of goods by hand was changed to production of goods by machines. Factories appeared, and over the years cities grew. 

As the West was opened up, new markets and new supplies of raw materials drew the factory system westward and the American people became an industrial people.

After the War between the States, industry found there was greater efficiency and profit in large organizations than in small businesses. "Big 

Business" appeared. Trusts and holding companies grew. Soon Mr. John 

Citizen was demanding that "Big Business" be checked. He turned to the federal government which for years has been aiding American business by tariffs, by improved banking systems and by aiding the development of land and water transportation. Then, beginning with the later 1800's, the federal government set up curbs on business. 

Sturdy as our industrial growth has been, it appears to have quite definite periods of "ups and downs." Is there a satisfactory solution to this weakness?

No one seems to know, although several preventatives have been suggested. 

Moreover, the industrialization of America has created many problems. 

City life is one. Finally, huge as our city population may be, it must depend upon the farms and the farmers for the necessities of life.

UNIT VI. THE AMERICAN WORKER STRUGGLES FOR A SQUARE DEAL 

UNIT OVERVIEW AND OUTLINE 

What were the effects of the Industrial Revolution and the rise of Big 

Business upon the American worker? What led American labor to organize first into small local unions and finally into huge national organizations? 

The answer is, of course, that as industry grew, so did the number of workers. It was natural that labor would expect and demand better working conditions, higher pay, shorter working hours and many other things. 

The way for workers to achieve their ends, they learned, was by unified action. This resulted in clashes between employers and workers. At first labor was not successful in obtaining its wants, but in the early 1900's the case for labor began to gain strong supporters. Both state and federal laws have helped the workers in many ways. In some respects the biggest gains came during the 1930's. On the other hand, during these same years 

American workers were quarreling among themselves in a battle between craft unions and industrial unions.

UNIT VII. AMERICANS DEVELOP THE DEMOCRATIC WAY OF LIFE 

Early in our history as a federal union many differences of opinion among the people appeared. Most important, perhaps, were those growing out of the meanings of the Constitution and how this new "law of the land" was to be applied. Out of such questions together with other differences, arose our major political parties. While the names of these parties have shifted in a rather confusing way, over the years they have clung quite closely to the original differences as set forth in the days of Hamilton and Jefferson. 

However, our ideas of democracy have changed from time to time, particularly under the leadership of Jefferson, Jackson and Lincoln, all of whom found ways to extend the democratic way of life to more and more Americans. But their leadership was not enough. This was because the major parties sometimes got in a rut and forgot their real purpose—to serve the needs and aims of the American people. From time to time, therefore, minor or third parties have sprung up. These smaller groups seldom won an election but they did point the way for the major parties which, in the course of time, often adopted the demands of the third parties. In this way democracy is ever changing and growing, reaching toward that ideal of 

Jefferson's, "the American dream.”

Unfortunately, however, special interests or groups have tried to gain special privileges. These they have gained through political wire-pulling in federal, state and city governments. Gradually the American people have developed ways of checking the selfish interests of these groups and from time to time they have stirred themselves to rise up and demand reforms. 

Slowly they are learning that constant vigilance is necessary to guard against greed and corruption.

UNIT VIII. AMERICANS EVER SEEK A BETTER LIFE 

Democracy, Americans have believed, is something more than the right to vote or to hold office. It is more, they have learned, than the freedoms set forth in the Bill of Rights. Americans have found out that democracy, in the words of the Declaration of Independence, is also "the pursuit of Happiness." 

What does "happiness" mean to the American people? The answer is, of course, that happiness means different things to different people. We have already seen in Unit VI how the workers in their search for happiness united their forces and were successful in obtaining many of their demands. 

In this Unit we shall see how Mr. Average Citizen demanded and got our systems of free public education, extending from the kindergarten through the university, the benefits of which are shared by over 30,000,000 young Americans. There is nothing quite like this anywhere else in the world. 

But "happiness" means more than this to the American people. It means rising above older standards of living. Think how electricity has changed household tasks in millions of American homes! In countless other ways science has entered our homes and touched our daily lives. Medicine every day is making life not only possible but happier for countless sufferers. 

Such changes are only part of the story. Why are we probably the best informed people in the world? What have our architecture, art, literature and music to do with the "pursuit of Happiness"? For a thoughtful person the answers are not hard to find. Moreover, by good use of increasing leisure time, Mr. Average Citizen is coming more closely to "the American dream." But this is by no means the whole story. Here and there, over the years, there have been American men and women with the foresight and courage to dare something new. Crusaders, they might be called. Should slavery be abolished? Should women be given equal rights with men? These and other reforms are all part of the American scene.

UNIT IX. AMERICA AVOIDS ALLIANCES BUT BECOMES A LEADER IN WORLD AFFAIRS 

Was the United States able to follow the wise counsel of President Washington to take no side in Europe's conflicts? The answer is, no. Since Washington's time the American people have been drawn into the Napoleonic Wars and the First World War. What was to happen in the great conflict between democracy and dictatorship? We shall see what measures American statesmen used, over the years, in their attempts to keep us at peace with the world. Moreover, we shall also see what the American people have done since the early 1800's in an effort to bring about that age-old dream of people everywhere-lasting peace. This may seem to you to have been an idle, useless attempt, but bear in mind that weak and fumbling as first efforts may be, out of such spring new plans and new hopes. 

South of the United States are the Latin-American Republics which as a result of Napoleon's wars became independent nations. To preserve our own interest and to help our new neighbors, we set forth our policy, the Monroe Doctrine. Over the years, for better or for worse, we became "the policeman of the Caribbean" and we came to be regarded by many Latin Americans as money-minded people bent on serving our own interests. In the early 1930's, however, the United States changed its attitude toward the nations of Latin America; we developed the "Good Neighbor Policy." 

This was an attempt to bind the republics of this hemisphere together. 

At the same time that we began to expand our business interests in Latin America, a similar growth was taking place in the Far East. Hopeful of sharing in China's markets we proposed that there be no "land grabbing" in China and that trading opportunities be equal to all. However, for years Japan's determination to be supreme in the East has been growing. 

By 1940 it seemed clear that sooner or later there would be a show-down between the United States and Japan over clashing interests in the Far East.

UNIT X. AMERICA FACES THE PROBLEM OF PUTTING HER HOUSE IN ORDER 

During the 1920's the American people in general rode “far, wide and handsome." Many people believed that a new age of lasting plenty and peace had dawned. How mistaken they were they learned during the earlier 

1930's when America was in the grip of the deepest depression in history. 

Millions of people were out of work and Mr. and Mrs. Average Citizen, the industrial and office workers, suffered most of all. 

It was during these dark years that the "New Deal" swung into action. 

Apparently the majority of American voters believed the old days of "hands-off" business were done for and that it was time that government controls were extended. This the New Deal, under the leadership of 

Franklin D. Roosevelt, attempted to do, and the American people, for better or for worse, tried out a system of "planned economy." No doubt mistakes were made as "New Dealers" sought to try out their "social philosophy." On the other hand, there were great gains as the federal government developed new ways of promoting "the general welfare." 

Naturally, these things meant increased government costs and soon the American people were facing their greatest debt burden. Moreover, efforts were made to streamline our system of government to meet the challenge of increased governmental activities. 

Unhappily, the difficulties the American people were having at home during the 1930's were heightened by the growing tension abroad. New ideas of government appeared elsewhere in the world. Agents of these ideas set to work under cover in the United States, to undermine democracy. With the outbreak of the Second World War, Americans realized, more than ever before, the need for bolstering the defenses of democracy. 

One way of so doing, we believed, was to strengthen and extend the democratic way of life.
