THE NEW YORK STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS APPLICABLE TO ACTIONS IN EQUITY BASED ON LEGAL RIGHTS GEORGE A. SPIEGELBERG 

WHEN to apply the New York ten year statute of limitations¹ is a question which has resulted in endless controversy. The reasons for the existing diversity of views are almost as numerous as the results arrived at. There is no doubt that if the jurisdiction of law and equity is actually concurrent, the "legal", not the equitable, statute of limitations is applicable.2 Difficulty is presented by the necessity for correctly ascertaining when a particular litigation is within the concurrent equitable jurisdiction. Courts feel, often implicitly, that the presence of the concurrent jurisdiction of equity depends exclusively upon the nature of the right sued upon. If they find that the basic right sued upon is legal, they tend to apply a "legal" statute of limitations, even though the suit is in equity which alone is capable of granting an adequate remedy. 

It is a purpose of this article to show that the equity jurisdiction is concurrent only if the remedy at law is adequate, irrespective of the basic nature of the right, and that only where the legal remedy is adequate should the legal statute of limitations be applied. Attempt will also be made to show that error must follow if selection of the statute of limitations to be applied is made to depend upon the basic nature of the right sued upon without inquiry as to the adequacy of the legal remedy. It is submitted that the many decisions finding distinctions in precedent where none exist, result only either from lack of time correctly to analyze legislative act and judicial decision, or correctly to understand the basic problem underlying the New York law of limitations in equity.

LEGISLATIVE HISTORY 

THE first limitation statute adopted in New York is contained in Chapter 43 of the Laws of 1788. No limitation as to suits in equity appears.

Part III, Chapter 4, Title II, Article 6 of the Revised Statutes of 1830 provides an entire scheme of limitations with respect to equitable actions. Only three of those Sections are material to the matter here under discussion: "Section 49: Whenever there is a concurrent jurisdiction in the courts of common law and in courts of equity of any cause of action, the provisions of this title limiting a time for the commencement of a suit for such cause of action in a court of common law shall apply to all suits hereafter to be brought for the same cause in a Court of Chancery." 

Here, for the first time in the state, "concurrent" as opposed to the "exclusive" jurisdiction of equity received legislative recognition. Though perhaps premature, it is not inopportune to point out that the statute mentions "a concurrent jurisdiction in the 

Courts...." Courts do not create rights; they enforce them; and it would, therefore, necessarily seem to follow that the jurisdiction mentioned in the statute was a remedial jurisdiction and not a jurisdiction based either on the identity of the parties or the substance of the right involved.

Having provided for the concurrent remedial jurisdiction, the legislature then provided for remedies exclusively equitable. "Section 50: But the last Section shall not extend to suits over the subject matter of which a court of equity has peculiar

The Revisers' note states: 

"This qualification" [Section 50] "it is presumed will remove all apprehension of danger from intrenching on the powers of courts of equity."

That what has been said with respect to Section 49, i.e., that remedy and not right is there being dealt with, is made evident by the next relevant section: "Section 52: Bills for relief in case of the existence of a trust not cognizable by the courts of common law and in all cases not herein provided for shall be filed within ten years after the cause thereof shall accrue and not after."

The opening words of this Section deal with the statute applicable to the enforcement of a particular substantive right, i.e., trusts, and as to suits based on trusts, the applicable statute was to be ten years. Following this clause comes the catch-all provision with respect to which the ten year period was also applicable. This was intended to, and did, relate not merely to cases at law unprovided for by the "legal" statutes of limitation, but, in addition, it included all suits on the equity side in which there was no legal, or no adequate legal remedy. The Revisers' note to the 1849 Code of 

Procedure quoted below makes this unmistakably clear. 

Had the legislature in the Code of 1849 maintained the separate existence of those Sections dealing with the limitation of legal actions and those Sections dealing with the limitation of actions either equitable in nature, as in the case of trusts, or where the courts of equity afforded the only remedy, or the only adequate remedy, much present confusion might well have been avoided. In 

1848 and 1849, however, the Revised Statutes of 1830 were rewritten and, among other Sections, Sections 49, 51 and 52 disappeared. In their place appears a new Section intended and believed to cover completely the ground previously covered by the Sections adopted in 1830 and hereinabove set forth.

From what has been said it would also appear to be abundantly clear that the jurisdiction in equity is exclusive when equity alone affords adequate relief, but in this phase of the subject much has occurred to obscure this simple truth.

It is submitted that the sentence itself cannot be legally supported, since it does not follow that because an accounting is necessary "the right . . . must necessarily be of an equitable nature" (italics added). It is perfectly true that today, at least, the remedy of accounting is usually in equity, but the nature of the right does not determine the necessity for an accounting, and an accounting in equity may be just as necessary although the right is legal as when it is equitable. Indeed, in all cases where the law affords no adequate relief, the plaintiff may secure an accounting from the defendant in equity though the basic right is the legal right to recover damages.39 The converse is equally true, i.e., the "legal" limitation statute will be applied, if there is an adequate remedy at law even though the plaintiff is entitled to an accounting." 40 As the Appellate Division in the Potter case more correctly pointed out, the ten year statute was applied not because the recovery of profits wrongfully acquired was sought against certain director defendants, but because "as to these defendants an action at law would not be as complete or effective as the remedy in equity". CONCLUSION 

THE present uncertainty as to the appropriate statute of limitations is chiefly caused by three independent factors: 

First: The failure of the bench and bar adequately to appreciate the historical genesis and development of the New York ten year statute of limitations.

Second: The acceptance and subsequent definite but, in the earlier cases, unclear rejection by the New York courts of the doctrine that the equitable nature of derivative actions required the application of the ten year statute.

Third: The unsound argument, given some support by the language of the Potter case, that the legal statute of limitations is applicable unless the right sued upon as well as the only adequate relief is equitable.

It is submitted from what has been said, that the legislative and judicial history of the question under discussion requires that the ten year statute of limitations be applied to all actions where the law affords an inadequate remedy, whether the basic right sued upon be legal or equitable; that in determining whether such a case is presented, inquiry must first be made whether the cause presently sued upon in equity was adequately remediable at law either in the suit of the present plaintiff or by some other party who possessed the identical right now sued upon, and if the answer to this preliminary inquiry is in the affirmative, the statute to be applied is the one applicable to the legal action. 

It is further submitted that to limit the applicability of the ten year statute to those actions where an accounting is necessary is fallacious and impossible of reasonable application. 

There will still remain the difficulty of determining whether in a particular case sued upon on the equity side of the court, the legal remedy is or is not adequate, but this is a minor difficulty since precedent is abundant and if, as is likely, a novel situation presents itself, as it may be said to have in Russell v. Todd, the application of established precedents to the novel situation will afford but one more example of the genius of the common law.
