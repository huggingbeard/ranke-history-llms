The accompanying report describes the program of aid sponsored by the United States Government under Title III of the Philippine Rehabilitation Act of 1946. 

This program went far to bolster the efforts of the Philippine people in their struggle along the road to postwar recovery. Bridges, harbors, roads, airports, and public buildings were restored; schools, hospitals, and laboratories were rebuilt. The extensive rehabilitation work in the fields of education and health has contributed substantially to the successful establishment of the new Republic and gives promise of added benefits far into the future.

The aid program, carried out in close cooperation with the Philippine Government, typifies the finest in the tradition of U. S.-Philippine relations. It was enacted in recognition of the immense war devastation suffered by the Philippines and of the close and unique ties which have bound our peoples since 1898. For 48 years the 

Philippines were under the American flag--during that time the people were being prepared for self-government and complete independence. In the last war the Philippines fought side by side with the United States. Today we are 820277 iii

independent partners among the nations of the free world with continuing close ideological, economic, defense, and cultural ties.

Because of the close association of the two countries I believe that this report will be of wide interest among the people of the United States and the Philippines.

INTRODUCTION 

At 10 o'clock on the morning of July 4, 1946, before a gathering of dignitaries from every corner of the globe, an historic ceremony took place in Manila. The Stars and 

Stripes were slowly lowered from a towering staff. In their place was raised a flag new to the world--the red, white, and blue standard, with sun and three stars, of the

Republic of the Philippines. 

The governing of the Philippines by the United States, which had begun at the close of the Spanish- American War, ended with this ceremony. The United States had redeemed the pledges which it had made to the Philippine people over the years.

At the same time, the U. S. Government made a new pledge: Philippine independence would not mark the end of our association but would be the beginning of a new era of cooperation and friendship. Just as the United States had led the Philippines along the road to independence, so the United States would help the new nation establish a sure footing. To this pledge the new Philippine Government responded with its own assurances for continued close cooperation. 

Concrete evidence of American intentions was on record at the time of the ceremony, for 2 months earlier the United States Congress had passed the Philippine Rehabilitation Act. Under it, assistance amounting to approximately $620 million had been authorized for wardamage reparations and to help restore public property and services. The program was to continue until June 1950, subject to funds to be appropriated. 

Urgent Need for Rehabilitation 

The urgent need for Philippine rehabilitation was implicit in the background against which the independence ceremonies took place. Behind the cream and gold stand which held the Philippine officials and their distinguished guests stood one of the world's most completely war-ravaged cities. Manila had become, in the final days of the war, a great fortress--the fortress of an enemy 1

which would not surrender but which fought to the death street by street, building by building, room by room. 

Shells of buildings looked down on the rubble-choked streets. Light, water, and communication systems were almost entirely destroyed; only the most rudimentary services had yet been restored. The transportation system had broken down, and the people of Manila were still largely dependent upon the U.S. Army for the necessities of life. The destruction of city after city throughout the islands paralleled that of the capital. 

The entire Philippine economy had been critically damaged. Millions of dollars of worthless Japanese money had played havoc with Philippine finances. There was not enough money in the Treasury to restore essential services, to rebuild, or even to care for the hundreds of thousands made homeless by the war.

Philippine industry had come to a virtual standstill. 

The vital sugar industry had been destroyed. No sugar crop existed, and many sugar mills had been destroyed. 

The Japanese had shipped tons of machinery to Japan, along with scrap iron and raw materials. Thousands of carabao, upon which the Philippine farmer is heavily dependent for work, had been slaughtered. Railroad lines and roads had been shattered; most ships serving in the vital interisland commerce had been sunk and harbors destroyed.

Beyond this physical destruction was the damage to the people themselves. Approximately one million Filipinos had lost their lives; other thousands were injured or unaccounted for. The occupation had destroyed morale and set up vicious currents of suspicion and distrust. The revolutionary movement of the Huks, originally dedicated to resisting the Japanese, had been seized by the Communists for a hit-and-run guerrilla campaign against property owners and the Government. With murder, extortion, and intimidation they were blocking economic recovery and forcing the new government to maintain a large constabulary.

Philippine Rehabilitation Act 

It was against this background that the Philippine 

Rehabilitation Act was enacted. Action was taken after 

2

visiting groups of U.S. Congressmen and Government officials had observed conditions in the Philippines and had reported that without aid the fledgling Republic would begin life at so serious a disadvantage as to make its very survival uncertain.

The United States recognized, too, that a weak Philippines, unable to support independence, would be a threat to peace and security in the Pacific. A free and strong Philippines would be an outpost for the defense of the free world. And a free Philippines would be an inspiration to all the nations of Asia working out their own independence. Furthermore, the United States recognized that it had an obligation to help in the restoration of that part of what had been American territory which had suffered most at the hands of the enemy. 

In addition, the American people were bound to the people of the Philippines not only by reason of their close peacetime association for almost half a century but also because of their brotherhood in arms during the war. 

Philippine soldiers fought beside U.S. forces and carried on ceaseless guerrilla action long after they were withdrawn. Thousands of Filipinos showed their courage and their loyalty to the United States by helping escaped American prisoners and by risking their lives to transmit information to American authorities. Over 200 secret radio stations supplied information on enemy troop movements and naval operations. Such aid contributed to the shortening of the war and saved countless American lives. 

The Philippine Rehabilitation Act, approved April 

30, 1946, provided for (1) the establishment of a Philippine War Damage Commission to make compensation to claimants for property losses in the Philippines; (2) the transfer to the Philippine Government of American surplus war property in the Philippines with a fair value not to exceed $100 million to be used for rehabilitation purposes; and (3) the restoration of public property and essential public services, within the limitations of the funds available. It is with the last provision--Title III of the Act--that this report deals. 

Title III provided that eight established U.S. Government agencies and the Philippine War Damage Commission cooperate with the Philippine Government in a broad

program of rehabilitation. The Department of State subsequently was designated as the coordinating agency for the program.

The various agencies, and the fields to which they were assigned, are (1) Bureau of Public Roads--restoration of public roads, streets, and bridges; (2) Corps of 

Engineers--restoration of port and harbor facilities; 

(3) Philippine War Damage Commission-rebuilding of pub lic buildings, hospitals, and schools; (4) Public Health

Service--raising of health conditions and development of a public health service; (5) Maritime Commission-restoration and improvement of interisland commerce; (6) Civil 

Aeronautics Administration--establishment and initial operation of air navigation facilities; (7) Weather 

Bureau--restoration of weather service; (8) Fish and 

Wildlife Service--rehabilitation and development of fishing industry; (9) Coast and Geodetic Survey--continuation of the survey of coastal waters conducted by it in the 

Philippines prior to December 7, 1941.

In addition, each agency except the Philippine War Damage Commission was authorized to provide training for a certain number of Filipinos either through on-the-job training or through study and observation in the United States to insure a continuity of the program's objectives once the rehabilitation program itself was completed. 

The entire rehabilitation program took place within the framework of closest American-Philippine cooperation. 

Formal agreements were signed by the United States and the Philippines setting forth general objectives and working plans for each part of the program. The Philippine President appointed Philippine officials to work with the American officials in carrying out the program. Rights of way, land, and facilities were furnished by the Philippine Government, and much of the labor engaged on the various projects was provided by the Philippines. Within each area of activity the emphasis was on using Philippine workers, technicians, and professionals so as to endow the rehabilitation program with the most lasting value possible.

PUBLIC HEALTH 

Basic to the entire rehabilitation program of the Philippines was the restoration to health of a people weakened by years of wartime privation and lack of proper medical supplies and treatment. Only a strong and vigorous people could effectively embark upon the formidable tasks called for in rebuilding the country. In recognition of this fact--and of our general interest in bolstering the new Republic--the Rehabilitation Act called for a comprehensive program covering the entire field of public health, with special emphasis on the fight against some of the most serious diseases afflicting the people of the Philippines. Much of the program was designed to help the Philippine Government make a start in a longrange effort to improve overall health services. 

Postwar Health Conditions 

Health conditions just after the war in the Philippines were critical. General sanitation conditions, including the protection of water supplies and the disposal of sewage, garbage, and waste, had been reduced to levels which constituted a national hazard, especially in the larger centers of population. The incidence of malaria, tuberculosis, and venereal disease had reached levels unknown prior to the war. Over 5,000 cases of leprosy, previously segregated in leprosaria, had been dispersed throughout the general population. There was widespread evidence of beriberi. Public-health facilities, including laboratories, had suffered widespread destruction. 

U.S. Public Health Service Program 

Toward the alleviation of these conditions $5,201,846 was allocated, with another $456,004 for training. The entire program got off to a quicker start than would ordinarily have been possible because the U.S. Public 

Health Service had made an initial attack on Philippine health problems with a $1 million emergency grant in 1945. 

Health work under the rehabilitation program was also under the direction of the U.S. Public Health

Service, which worked closely with the Philippine Department of Health to carry out a joint American-Philippine program. The Assistant Surgeon General in charge of the program was made Technical Adviser to the President of the Philippines at the President's request.

Because it was obvious that the amount appropriated would not be sufficient for the building of a complete public-health structure throughout the islands, the overall objective set up by the Public Health Service was to provide a basic foundation upon which an effective public-health structure might be built in the future. Both countries agreed not to start a program that the Philippine Government would be unable to finance at the termination of American aid.

It was decided that the program should be carried on mainly by the Philippine people themselves, both to start a supply of trained personnel and to make the problem of transition easier. Out of the approximately 

1,100 employed in the program by January 1948, all but three were Filipinos.

An additional measure to facilitate the transfer of complete responsibility to the Philippine Government without disrupting the continuity of the program was the budgeting of U.S. funds over a 4-year period on a sliding scale downward during the last 2 years of operation, with the expectation that the Philippine Government would provide funds on a corresponding sliding scale upward. 

In keeping with the aim of providing a basic foundation for a health program, health education was emphasized throughout. Education for the public in health matters took its place beside treatment and the establishment of clinics and other facilities. It was recognized that lack of health knowledge on the part of the public was a major block to progress in combating many of the diseases that were prevalent in the Philippines. 

Education units staffed by qualified experts and equipped with trucks and sound equipment traveled to the most remote villages to teach principles of good health. 

Education techniques were used by each of the medical units set up to combat specific diseases and by the child-care program. Gaining the cooperation of the public

through these campaigns was a significant part of the program.

Trained Personnel 

Since the lack of trained public-health personnel was a major obstacle to the rehabilitation and long-term success of public-health services, emphasis was given to the training of qualified public-health personnel of various types. With the help of the rehabilitation program, various training centers acquired the necessary equipment and staff and gave advanced public-health training to physicians and medical students, nurses, engineers, health educators, and others. More specialized training in the control of malaria, tuberculosis, and venereal diseases was given to personnel in those fields. 

These training activities were supplemented by establishment of an urban-health field demonstration unit. 

One hundred specially selected Filipinos were sent to the United States for training in the field of public health. Ninety of these were students who attended leading 

American universities for a year's course. The other 10 were drawn from Philippine Government health programs and were sent to the United States as observers to become acquainted with the newest developments in their fields. There was particular need in the Philippines for knowledge regarding new techniques in medicine, for during the war years the Filipinos had been cut off from all such information. The 100 Filipinos who came to the United 

States included doctors, nurses, sanitation engineers, laboratory technicians, health educators--representatives from nearly every field of public health. Sanitation

In the field of sanitation the chief project was a survey of water sources followed by water-purification projects in those areas which needed them most. Over 300 chlorination systems were provided under the rehabilitation program, as well as many water-analysis stations. 

Projects dealing with industrial hygiene, immunization, food inspection, and the proper disposal of sewage, garbage, and industrial wastes were also instituted.

Nutrition Program 

The nutrition program was generally aimed at inproving health through education in proper preparation of food, balanced diet, and food values. However, the most significant part of the nutrition program was the contribution it made in providing the Philippine people with an enriched rice designed to combat beriberi, which, after tuberculosis, was responsible for more deaths in the postwar period than any other single cause. 

Artificially enriched rice is ordinary white rice to which is added a small portion of "premix," or rice coated with vitamin B complex and iron. It was tested in a pilot project on the Bataan Peninsula. The Peninsula was divided into an "experimental area" receiving enriched rice, and a "control area" in which only ordinary white rice was available. Initial supplies of premix came from the United States. Later a plant to produce premix was installed in the Philippines, and the premix was added to ordinary rice in some 26 rice mills in the experimental area.

After a period of 2 years, it was found that the rate of incidence of beriberi in the control area was over five times that in the area eating enriched rice. 

As a result of the Bataan experiment, an expanded program for the addition of enriched rice to ordinary rice sold in other endemic beriberi areas has been adopted as a national project.

Education in nutrition was also an element in the maternal-child health project, which included establishment of clinics for mothers and infants, lectures on health during pregnancy and on infant care, and training of local nurses in maternal-child care techniques. 

Communicable Disease Control Services 

A main part of the health rehabilitation program was the establishment or strengthening of communicabledisease control services. The campaign against venereal diseases included case-finding examinations of various groups, cooperation with the Manila and Philippine Departments of Health in the establishment and maintenance

of diagnostic and treatment clinics, and education of the public. A beginning was made in the control of leprosy and skin diseases through the equipping and staffing of a national skin clinic, the establishment and operation of mobile skin clinics in one-fourth of the Philippine provinces, and through education. 

The major antidisease campaign, however, was that waged against malaria. Following liberation in 1944-45 it was estimated that the incidence of malaria had risen to over 4 million attacks per year. There were many contributing causes: the movement of people to the foothills and mountains to escape the enemy; deterioration of public-health services; lack of hospital facilities; and general shortage of food and medicine during the period of enemy occupation.

Twenty-five percent of the total national labor supply was incapacitated by malaria from 5 to 10 days a year, and in many cases considerably longer. This one disease caused a loss of from 10 million to 20 million man-days annually. The toll in suffering could not be measured.

In many areas visited on the survey that preceded the antimalaria campaign, it was found that vast fields lay idle and rich forests and mineral deposits were undeveloped because the people knew that if they tried to work in these areas they would risk the hazards of malaria.

The principal objectives of the malaria-control program were to give immediate relief to malaria victims through free laboratory service and home treatment; to eradicate or control the malaria carriers by either temporary or permanent control measures; to institute a malaria-education campaign; and to get wide public cooperation in the fight against malaria.

Results of the malaria-control program were encouraging after a few years of operation. The estimated reduction in the incidence of malaria on Negros Island was 85 percent. During the entire program over 700,000 individual examinations were made on house-to-house visits and another 700,000 at dispensaries.

Another major health project was the fight against tuberculosis. Tuberculosis had long been the leading cause of death in the Philippines, responsible for 15 to 20 percent of the deaths from all causes and one of the leading causes of deaths in infants under one year of age. Studies showed that approximately 10 percent or more of the population surveyed was afflicted with tuberculosis. The problem, already enormous before the war, had been greatly aggravated because the war had not only increased all predisposing factors to the disease but also destroyed most of the means for coping with it. 

An emergency program, which had started before the Rehabilitation Act came into force, had begun the attack on tuberculosis by reactivating tuberculosis clinics, securing critically needed supplies, and constructing and equipping a tuberculosis laboratory, clinic, and training center. The primary objective under the rehabilitation program was to concentrate efforts on the long-range aspects of tuberculosis control measures. Plans called for establishment of chest clinics in a number of large cities; training classes for physicians, nurses, technicians, statisticians, and others; and dissemination of information covering TB programs to the medical profession and to the public through printed matter, motion pictures, and lectures. Among the first major projects was the construction and expansion of a National Chest 

Center to serve as administrative headquarters and as a training center for the tuberculosis control program. In the face of hopelessly inadequate hospital facilities for TB patients, it was inevitable that large numbers of infectious cases should continue to live in their homes with their families. Adequate isolation or hospitalization facilities to cope with the need for an estimated minimum of 50,000 beds (100,000 by American standards) were not available. In an effort to lessen the spread of infection in the home, the U.S. Public Health 

Service organized a Home Visiting-Nurse Service, operated by the Manila Health Department.

One of the most important activities of the tuberculosis program was the establishment of a BCG vaccine production plant. A building was erected and equipment supplied, with production of the critically needed vaccine beginning at the end of 1950. 10

In order to safeguard the advances made in health conditions in the Philippines, it was necessary to improve the quarantine system to prevent admittance of carriers of cholera, smallpox, bubonic plague, and other diseases common to many Asian countries. Substantial progress had already been made in reestablishing quarantine facilities under the emergency program. The rehabilitation program undertook to pay the salaries of certain quarantine personnel until funds could be made available by the Philippine Congress; to recondition floating equipment previously acquired from the U.S. Army; to furnish fuel and other operating expenses for quarantine boarding vessels; to supply materials and equipment for the Mariveles Quarantine Detention Station; and to provide salaries and training of personnel, together with supplies, materials, and equipment for insect and rodent control at international airports and port areas. 

REHABILITATION OF PUBLIC PROPERTY 

One of the most dramatic achievements of the rehabilitation program was the contribution it made in helping to transform charred, twisted wreckage into urgently needed public buildings and up-to-date hospitals and schools. The American contribution to this work was made under the direction of the War Damage Commission, whose major responsibility was the financing of limited claims of private individuals who had lost homes, businesses, or farms in the war. The Commission also had an allocation of $56,779,087 for the restoration of public property.

When the first contingent of American staff members of the Commission arrived at Manila on November 1, 1946, major Government buildings were in ruins. Much of the rubble had not even been cleared of mines. Hospitals and dispensaries were a shambles. Had an epidemic broken out, health facilities would have been completely inadequate. 

It was only through the constant efforts of American military forces and American and Philippine health authorities, along with the untiring efforts of the Philippine people themselves, that epidemics were averted. As it was, the existing medical facilities were heavily strained to take care of the problems at hand caused by the appalling health conditions. 11

In cities and villages, children attended classes in the open air with nothing more than a tree to shelter them. In really bad weather there could be no classes. 

Schools of all categories, including the big University of the Philippines, trade schools, high schools, central and elementary schools, and the one-room rural schools were devastated. This destruction was all the more tragic because the children and young people of the Philippines have an avid desire for education and great sacrifices had been made by Filipinos in former years to provide facilities for study.

Throughout the islands it was apparent that public buildings had suffered extensive destruction, especially at the end of the war when the buildings were used for fortifications and when the enemy realized that the Philippine people would regain their freedom. An initial survey convinced the Commission that three times the $57 million allotted to it for this purpose would be required for a complete restoration of public buildings destroyed or damaged by the enemy. So it set out to tackle only the most pressing needs.

There were many formidable problems to be faced. The unprecedented call for technical and administrative personnel for public and private rehabilitation programs had created a serious manpower shortage. There was a dearth of engineers and architects. There was a shortage of building materials in the Philippines, and it was difficult to obtain them from abroad when so many other countries were embarked upon similar programs of postwar rebuilding. There was a lack of transportation facilities and a lack of tools and equipment. Some areas were so completely destroyed that entire populations had shifted and it was necessary to find and acquire new sites for public buildings.

New public buildings were erected in the capital and the provinces to permit as rapid a return as possible to orderly government. American funds provided classroom facilities for more than 3,200,000 students, hospital beds for 3,200 patients, water for 6,700,000 people, and employment for 20,000 workmen.

In addition the Commission supplied rolling stock for the Government-owned Manila Railroad. Destruction of 12

this railroad had retarded rehabilitation from an economic standpoint. The railroad is not only the single facility for passenger travel by rail on Luzon but is also a prime freight carrier in this area, which is the source of some of the major agricultural products of the Philippines.

In order to take care of urgent health needs the Commission determined that hospitals and dispensaries should receive the first order of priority for reconstruction. Next on the priority list were waterworks and irrigation systems, followed by schools, national government buildings, provincial and municipal buildings, and similar projects. 

A scheme was also devised to insure that the work would be spread throughout the Philippines, for, while Manila had been most heavily damaged, scores of other cities were nearly as badly damaged. Some villages had been virtually razed to the ground, and considerable devastation had been suffered throughout the islands. 

In terms of actual grants of money, the school program came first, claiming 62.2 percent of available funds for the building program. National government buildings were allotted 12.5 percent of these funds; hospitals and dispensaries 8.3 percent; provincial and municipal government buildings 7.5 percent; waterworks and irrigation 

5.5 percent; and other projects 4 percent.

The school program is probably most widely known among the Philippine people, not only because of the number of buildings replaced but also because these schools were scattered throughout the Philippines, even in the most remote regions. In several instances schools were erected in such isolated areas that the men and materials for construction had to be brought in by helicopter. An innovation in the school reconstruction program was the use of prefabricated school buildings. These buildings, of the one-, two-, or three-room type, saved materials and speeded up the program considerably. 

One of the earliest allocations in the education field went to the Philippine Normal School to provide a place for the training of teachers. Numerous allocations were made for trade schools to expedite the training of 13

skilled craftsmen in a nation which was sorely in need of their services.

The allocation for the University of the Philippines-over $6 million--was the largest for any single entity. The University, with its center at Quezon City, just outside Manila, and branches in many other areas of the Republic, is an essential component of the educational system of the Philippines.

In the health field one of the first allocations. made by the Commission was for quarantine stations at Mariveles near Manila and at Cebu. The need for such stations arose from the danger of contagious diseases being brought into the islands. The old quarantine stations had been destroyed, and it was difficult to evolve a satisfactory system of control without detention facilities.

An outstanding contribution in the health field was the construction of a hospital for the care of children with tuberculosis. At the close of the war there were no adequate facilities for the care of tubercular children, although the incidence of tuberculosis in the Philippines was 10 times greater than in the United States. Quezon 

Institute, the largest treatment center in the Republic, had had a children's pavilion which was destroyed during the war. The Commission awarded $137,500 for the construction of a children's ward at the Institute. While the Quezon Institute is not a Government corporation, its rehabilitation was justified on the basis of urgency. The 

Philippine Government provided the land for the new ward. 

Because of financial limitations the Commission was not able to make many allocations for equipment. One of the few exceptions was the provision of funds for seven iron lungs during an epidemic of poliomyelitis. The respirators were placed at various points throughout the islands.

The erection of Government buildings, while not receiving first priority, was nevertheless of outstanding importance. The tremendous responsibilities of the Government in launching a new Rǝpublic, coupled with the immediate and overwhelming need for restoring order out of the chaos of war, required that it have the most 14

efficient facilities possible. As it was, the Government was forced to start operations housed in various makeshift structures, some of bamboo, and nearly all thoroughly inadequate.

Under the rehabilitation program the building occupied by the Legislature was restored. The Department of 

Finance, the Department of Agriculture and Natural Resour ces, and the Bureau of Posts were provided with adequate quarters. Many provincial and municipal Government buildings were also rebuilt. 

Like other parts of the rehabilitation program, the public-buildings program was carried on in closest cooperation with the Philippine Government. Philippine officials made the initial judgments on which buildings should be reconstructed; Philippine contracting firms did much of the reconstruction work; and a major part of the staff of engineering and administrative personnel of the Commission was Filipino.

Because of the scope of the program the War Damage Commission set up a Bureau of Public Property Claims to work closely with the Philippine Bureau of Public Works. 

The Philippine Bureau received all claims for rehabilitation and submitted those it approved to the Commission. 

The Commission, in turn, through its Bureau of Public Property Claims, was responsible for the examination, investigation, evaluation, and adjudication of all claims for loss or damage, and it exercised general control over each project until completion. At the peak of operations the Bureau employed 8 Americans and 53 Filipinos.

The Commission itself undertook no part in the actual construction work but, on the basis of bidding, allocated work to private contractors. All bidders were required to have initial approval by the Commission to obviate any doubts of a contractor's ability to carry through a project once his bid had been submitted. The Philippine 

Government arranged for top priority for the acquisition of materials and equipment in order to speed the program. 

At the close of the program in June 1950, allocations had been made for a total of 1,876 projects. Of these 

1,120 had been completed. The great majority of the unfinished projects were in the final stages of completion 15

and were finished shortly afterward under supervision of the Philippine Bureau of Public Works. 

The public property and essential public-services provisions contained in the Philippine Rehabilitation Act were adopted as a "manifestation of good will to the Filipino people." The Commission made every effort to enhance that good will through a general information program carried on by radio, press, publications, and sound trucks which carried news of the program throughout the islands.

One task of the information program was to correct a widespread misconception about the source of the funds for the rehabilitation program. The belief was current that these funds had been collected by the United States from Japan. Every effort was made to acquaint the public with the fact that all funds were provided by the United States and had been collected from American taxpayers. 

Scrolls were presented to the schools attesting to the American aid which had gone into their reconstruction, and every major building received a bronze plaque bearing the inscription: "Rebuilt with the aid of the people of the United 

States of America under the Philippine Rehabilitation Act of 1946."

TRANSPORTATION AND COMMUNICATION 

The well-being of the Philippines, like that of other island nations, is in direct ratio to the effectiveness of its lines of transportation and communication with the rest of the world. It is dependent on the outside world for many of the necessities of life, and its prosperity is founded largely on its international trade. In addition, the geography of the Philippines--there are 22 main islands and some 7,000 islands altogether--makes its interisland system of communications particularly important.

A large part of the rehabilitation program was therefore directed to the restoration of communications by land and sea and to the establishment of facilities for air transportation--a field which the Philippine Government 16

never had had the opportunity to develop. Rehabilitation programs were carried out in the fields of public roads and bridges, harbor facilities, interisland shipping, air navigation, weather stations, and coast and geodetic surveys.

Public Roads 

The Rehabilitation Act placed the public-roads program in the hands of the U.S. Bureau of Public Roads. An emergency program of the Army Corps of Engineers had done a good job of opening war-torn routes to traffic. The problem now was to replace these temporary facilities with permanent roads and bridges capable of carrying the commerce of the nation and providing the necessary arteries for travel.

An initial survey was made throughout the islands to determine the most critical needs. Then an overall plan for highway reconstruction was drawn up in cooperation with the Philippine Bureau of Public Works, which participated fully in all phases of the road-reconstruction program.

Emphasis of the rebuilding program was on replacement of roads and bridges already existing rather than on construction of new ones. In the Cagayan Valley on 

Luzon, however, five major bridges and some new mileage were built in order to open up this fertile valley for agricultural development and for resettlement from heavily populated regions. 

Actual road construction work started in April 1947.

When the program ended in 1952, 254 major bridges had been built and 239 repaired, and over 370 miles of highway had been reconstructed, mostly of bituminous concrete or Portland cement. Cost of the program was $39,384,565. 

A secondary but essential aim was to provide a base of experience upon which the Philippine Government could continue an effective highway program after American aid had ceased. Toward this end emphasis was placed upon the training of personnel. Thus, while 45 American engineers and draughtsmen and 5 administrators provided initial supervision, the public-roads rehabilitation staff 17

included some 200 Philippine engineers. All work was carried out in association with provincial engineers and governments. Local staffs designed most of the bridges, although major structures were designed by the Bureau of 

Public Roads in Washington. 

The American staff worked closely with the Philippine Bureau of Public Works in helping to establish procedures and in setting up a planning section to carry out a broad highway program. American aid also provided the 

Bureau with a modern materials-testing and control laboratory and supplied the necessary equipment and technicians to operate it.

As a further means of bringing the Philippines toward self-sufficiency in road building, American officials insisted that all actual construction work be done by the Filipinos and through private contracting firms. 

As a result of this policy the number of firms qualified to go ahead with road construction rose from three or four at the start of the program to over 30 at its end. The staffs of most of these firms had been trained largely by working with the American technicians. 

Formal training was provided through a number of short courses in highway construction and maintenance given by members of the Bureau of Public Roads at the various provincial centers. In addition, 10 Filipinos were sent to the United States for advanced training with various State Highway Departments and at the Bureau of Public Roads in Washington.

The roads program got an extra boost when, through American aid, various items of modern construction equipment not available in the Philippines were acquired--a modern asphalt plant, rollers, and concrete mixers. Some war-surplus equipment was also made available, including rollers, trucks, gravel-processing machines, motor graders, tractors, and scrapers. 

Ports and Harbors

The Philippine lifeline is the chain of ships which transport Philippine goods to the markets of the world and bring in the necessities of life. Without proper 18

ports and harbors this lifeline is imperiled and the vital interisland trade is seriously disrupted. The rehabilitation of these facilities was, therefore, a major postwar task.

The Japanese, upon the invasion of the islands, took over intact practically all of the port facilities, which were not seriously damaged. They repaired and kept in operation, however, only those ports which served their military needs. During the occupation normal maintenance of port facilities was totally neglected. The return of 

American forces and the consequent withdrawal of the occupation forces brought further widespread destruction.

The pattern of damage and deterioration at nearly all the ports was the same, varying only in magnitude. 

Postwar reconnaissance surveys revealed smashed concrete docks, broken piles, disrupted electrical and water systems, and shattered warehouses.

An emergency program of harbor reconstruction had been undertaken by the U.S. Army to meet the needs of the military situation and to permit the entrance of other urgent supplies. The rehabilitation program itself was based on a study made by the Corps of Engineers immediately after the war. Funds amounting to $17,069,104 were made available for the rebuilding of ports and harbors. 

The Rehabilitation Act designated the Corps of Engi neers to carry out the program in cooperation with the Philippine Government. It was to determine the projects to be undertaken, make necessary designs, prepare contracts and specifications, award and administer contracts for the work, and generally restore the ports and harbors to a condition at least equal to that which existed prior to the war.

To work with the Corps of Engineers, the Philippine Government created the Philippine Port Commission, a special group within its Bureau of Public Works. Throughout the project, the closest cooperation was maintained between the two agencies in all stages of port and harbor rehabilitation from planning to construction.

This coop- eration also provided training for various members of the Philippine Bureau of Public Works, who, working closely with American engineers and administrators, were able 19

later to adapt some of our techniques to further building programs.

Further training was provided for 11 Filipinos who were sent to the United States for on-the-job training in River and Harbor Districts of the Corps of Engineers. 

Seven of these trainees also did graduate work at American universities leading to M.S. degrees in civil engineering.

The bulk of the work consisted of the repair or reconstruction of wharves and piers, mostly in Manila itself. In all, 12 ports were rebuilt; surveys on 6 were completed and the data turned over to the Philippine Government. Two breakwaters were also built and several channels dredged. Detailed surveys and cost estimates were made before work was started, and construction contracts were awarded on the basis of competitive bidding. 

All work was closely supervised by officers of the Corps of Engineers. 

It has been estimated that 85 percent of all foreign import and export trade and 65 percent of the interisland traffic now move through ports rebuilt under the rehabilitation program. Cargo movements have returned to normal, and in many instances more expeditious operations are possible with the use of modern cargo-handling equipment. It is anticipated that these restored facilities will serve the shipping needs of the Philippines for many years to come. 

Coast and Geodetic Survey

Complementary to the ports and harbors restoration program was one for surveying and charting the Philippine sealanes and interior waterways. The rehabilitation program provided for extensive surveys and designated the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey to carry out the job. This agency had actually been conducting surveys in the Philippines since 1902, and under the rehabilitation program it continued its work. 

The dependence of the Philippines upon its inland waterways for so much of its commerce and transportation, and upon its sealanes for international commerce, made it 20

essential to have modern nautical charts and tidetables. 

Thus a program was planned to continue the survey of uncharted waters, to make revisions of surveys of the most important waterways, and to resurvey some of the areas covered in earlier operations.

The equipment of the Philippine Bureau of the Coast and Geodetic Survey had been totally destroyed during the war. All the vessels and small boats used in its work were lost. The reproduction plant and all survey records filed in the Manila office and at Fort Santiago were burned or otherwise destroyed.

For the coast and geodetic survey job, funds amounting to $893,466 plus $243,795 for training were allocated. 

Plans were made to replace necessary equipment and buildings; to continue the survey operation in the Philippines which had been conducted before the war; to train each year a limited number of Filipinos; and to supervise and assist in the establishment of a counterpart Philippine organization which would be qualified to carry on the functions performed by the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey. 

To accomplish this program an American advisory group consisting of three commissioned officers and five civilian technicians was maintained in the Philippines. Philippine technicians and workers participated fully in all phases of the program, from planning through operations, and in the process were given on-the-job training which permitted them to gain the needed experience to take over the program. 

Additional training was provided for 50

Filipinos who were sent to the United States for study, mostly at the Washington office of the U.S. Coast and 

Geodetic Survey and on ships it operated. 

By the program's end the general objectives had been met. A Philippine Bureau of Coast and Geodetic Survey had been established and installed in new buildings. Adequate surveying, drafting, electronic, magnetic, and other scientific equipment had been supplied, and sufficient ships and auxiliary vessels had been made available to carry out the survey work. Extensive survey operations were completed, including hydrographic and control surveys and tidal and magnetic observations. 21

Maritime Commission 

The U.S. Maritime Commission was charged under the 

Rehabilitation Act with helping the Philippines restore its domestic shipping. The Philippine interisland shipping system encompasses the main arteries of commerce and trade and provides a major means of transportation. Ninety-nine percent of the imports of the Philippines enter three principal ports, from which they are distributed throughout the rest of the islands by means of interisland shipping. Eighty-five percent of Philippine exports are first collected at coastal ports from outlying islands before being finally brought to one of four main ports handling international shipping. Thus the Philippine interisland shipping system is a vital segment of the national economic structure. 

During the war the ships of the interisland fleet were either destroyed or otherwise lost to their former owners.. It was, therefore, as part of the restoration of the economy of the Philippines that vessels of a suitable type were made available through the rehabilitation program. 

To alleviate the critical shipping shortage, the 

Maritime Commission chartered 10 coastal-type freighters ranging from 3,000 to 6,000 tons. The vessels were chartered at the rate of 10 percent per annum of the statutory sales price. The U.S. Congress has subsequently extended the original period of charter through June 1955. 

The Maritime Commission has been responsible for the training of 85 Philippine students at the U.S. Merchant Marine Academy at King's Point, N.Y. Eighteen of these are still in training. The 4-year course carries a B.S. degree, and graduates are prepared to be junior officers in the merchant marine or naval service of the 

Republic of the Philippines. 

Funds amounting to $1,047,333 were allocated for the program conducted by the Maritime Commission. 224

Civil Aeronautics Administration 

The end of the war ushered in a new age of air transport whose implications for the economic and social development of the Philippines were evident. To carry out the provisions of the Rehabilitation Act, Congress appropriated $7,408,497 for setting up facilities to help meet the needs of international air traffic. An additional $731,201 was allocated for training. 

The program was under the direction of the Civil Aeronautics Administration. Plans were based on a survey which had been undertaken by the CAA immediately after the war and on the practices and standards set up at the 

South Pacific Regional Meeting of the Provisional Inter national Civil Aviation Organization in Melbourne in 1947.

The CAA program differed from others of the rehabilitation program in one important respect: Its emphasis was not on restoring wartime damage but on setting up facilities to meet urgent new demands. The actual job of restoring the badly damaged airfields and many of the signal stations and other facilities throughout the islands was done largely by the Philippine Government and through emergency measures of the U.S. Army. 

The CAA rehabilitation program was confined to the development of four airfields for international flights and the establishment of a Philippine Civil Aeronautics 

Administration, modeled after the American CAA, capable of meeting the expanding needs of Philippine aviation, both domestic and international. 

The four fields were at Manila, Laoag, Cebu, and

Zamboanga, with the one in the capital receiving the major share of the aid. Installed at Manila were a 7,500-foot runway, a communication station, a traffic-control center and tower, Very High Frequency Omnirange, and a radio homing beacon. Housing necessary for the stations and equipment was built. At other airports the work centered mainly about the installation of communication stations and radio landing devices.

The Philippine Bureau of Aeronautics, which subsequently became the Philippine CAA, cooperated in planning and carrying out the entire program. Philippine technicians 23

and officials worked with the Americans, receiving training and instruction in modern methods of air control and operation. At the height of the rehabilitation program there were 44 Americans participating; all of these were withdrawn by June 1950, when the Philippine Government indicated it was ready to take over. 

In the CAA's training program 135 selected Philippine students were sent to the CAA's training center in Oklahoma City and other U.S. airfields. In addition 50 of the Philippine employees who had worked with the Americans in the rehabilitation program received additional training in the United States. 

Weather Bureau

The U.S. Weather Bureau worked closely with the 

Civil Aeronautics Administration throughout the rehabil itation program. One of its major duties was to restore weather reporting services both to speed up and to safeguard Philippine and international aviation. In an area that has frequent typhoons, such services were also of major importance to the public, to agriculture, and to fishing and other industries.

A postwar survey by representatives of the U.S. 

Weather Bureau and the Philippine Weather Bureau revealed a serious breakdown in weather reporting, inadequate housing and facilities, and so many defective meteorological instruments that the weather reporting that was being done was highly inaccurate. Forecasting service consisted of two public forecasts daily, a limited briefing for domestic aviation, and typhoon warnings as required. de 

The Philippine Weather Bureau had no meteorological broadcast facilities for the exchange of information required by the International Meteorological Organization (now the World Meteorological Organization) and the In ternational Civil Aviation Organization. This particular need was met initially on an emergency basis through arrangements with the U.S. Navy. 

By 1950 the Philippine Weather Bureau had 43 newly equipped surface-observation stations making a total of 24

surface-observation reports daily. Of the 43 stations, 11 were equipped for taking pilot balloon observations and 3 for radiosonde observations. There were 161 climatological and hydrological stations reporting daily temperatures and rainfall values on a monthly basis. 

Daily forecasting services consisted of two general public forecasts, complete briefing and forecasting service for both domestic and international aviation, two marine forecasts, and storm warnings and advisories. All re quirements of the International Civil Aviation Organiza tion and the World Meteorological Organization with respect to weather forecasting services had been fulfilled.

The Philippine Weather Bureau now publishes its own auxiliary weather charts and technical papers. A national meteorological broadcast including a weather broadcast for shipping is operated by the Philippine CAA under control of the Weather Bureau.

Fifty-three Philippine students were brought to United States universities for study in various phases of meteorology, and 40 employees of the Philippine

Weather Bureau received at least 3-months orientation or special technical training at Weather Bureau installations in the United States. Each of the students also received on-the-job training at various U.S. weather stations. $2,099,779 was allocated for the Weather Bureau program, with another $373,570 for training.

REHABILITATION OF FISHERIES 

In a nation whose land area consists of thousands of islands and whose water area is nearly six times its land area, it is natural that the sea should provide much of the people's food. Fish, both fresh and processed, is vital to the health and welfare of the people of the Philippines.

The Philippine fishing industry before the war, in terms of value and volume, was the second most important industry in the country. In contrast to the United States, the Philippines are almost entirely dependent upon fish as a source of animal protein. A survey made by the 

Philippine College of Agriculture just before the war 25

indicated that at that time, when fish was cheap and plentiful, the average daily consumption was 4 1/2 ounces. This was considered by dieticians to be adequate in a normal diet. 

Prior to the war, 182,000 persons, or 1 percent of the population, were engaged in commercial fishing. The yearly commercial catch averaged 800 million pounds. 

There were 283 registered fishing boats of three tons gross or more. Large-scale fishing operations, however, were mostly in the hands of Japanese, who supplied the urban areas with fish. The Filipinos were concerned mainly with small inshore operations and subsistence fishing.

With the start of the war, these generally favorable conditions changed. The decline in production and consumption of fish began early, when large fishing vessels were diverted to patrol and transport work and the remaining vessels were used principally to supply the Japanese occupation forces. Toward the end of the war, as fighting was resumed throughout the islands, most of the remaining fishing vessels were destroyed. 

During the course of the war, the Filipinos became increasingly dependent upon themselves for their fish supply. Equipped and trained for small inshore operations and subsistence fishing, and lacking the necessary gear, they could not maintain an adequate supply of fish. By the end of the war the average daily consumption of fish had decreased to 1 1/2 ounces.

The effects of an inadequate food supply could be seen in many ways. Tuberculosis had increased, physical stamina and efficiency had been greatly reduced, and diseases associated with malnutrition were prevalent. 

After liberation, only a handful of miscellaneous vessels were engaged in fishing. Few in number, poorly equipped, and lacking experienced crews, they could do little to increase the fish supply. The price of fish rose until the ordinary consumer could afford to buy only a little. Both Philippine and American leaders realized that to insure the reestablishment of the economy of the Philippines on a sound basis and to raise health 26

standards, these conditions had to be changed as rapidly as possible.

To that end the Philippine Rehabilitation Act authorized the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service to cooperate with the Government of the Philippines in rebuilding the fishing industry and in providing for better utilization of the resources. The sum of $2,870,776 was allocated for this program in the Philippines, with an additional $553,648 for training of qualified students in the United States.

A Philippine fishery program was drawn up, emphasizing research in fishery biology, oceanography, and exploratory fishing, and including many technological studies in sanitation, canning, freezing, smoking, icing, salting, and other processing of fish products. Investigations were also conducted into the possible development of deep-sea fishing for tuna. A special study of the pondfish industry was undertaken and significant progress made in its development. ^ vessel was provided for oceanographic and biological research work and for exploratory tuna fishing. The biological activities were concerned primarily with research on spawning, early life history, development, food habits, and general biology of ocean and pond fishes. 

This ship traveled almost 70,000 miles, making intensive surveys to observe oceanographic conditions during each of the two monsoon seasons characteristic of the region of the Philippines. The information gathered on these surveys provided a comprehensive knowledge of the hydrographic conditions in Philippine waters for all seasons and furnished abundant material for future research. These findings will be published and will furnish much useful knowledge of the ocean currents and the physical and chemical characteristics of the waters in the different areas of the Philippines. 

Two other vessels were used for exploratory fishing, to meet the immediate demand for the expansion of known fishing grounds and to discover new ones. These boats also experimented in fishing techniques and participated in exercises demonstrating the best ways of going after various types of fish. The technical advice, assistance, and encouragement given to the industry through these 27

demonstrations resulted in an expansion of the trawling industry. Special experiments were carried out to determine the best techniques for catching tuna and shark and the best places to find them. A beginning was also made on development of a lobster industry, with experiments on lobster fishing and marketing. One vessel and much of the oceanographic equipment used in the survey have been transferred to the Philippine Bureau of Fisheries.

The quantity and composition of the catch of commercial fish were intensively studied in the central and southern parts of the Philippines. Detailed records were made in several areas of the predominant species present at different times of the year, the quantities caught, the places where they were caught, the fishing techniques, and the prices the fish brought on the market. Records were also kept on the types of fish caught, their weight, and size. Some reports on this project were completed and a valuable collection of data is available for other studies of problems relating to fisheries. 

To meet the need for further research and development in the fishing industry--considered a necessity if the industry is to meet even current needs--the rehabilitation program provided for training in the United States of Filipinos in numerous phases of the fishery industry and science. The program was designed primarily as an "in service" type of training in which the students would receive practical training at the offices of the Fish and Wildlife Service and in the fishing industry. A few highly qualified students were given academic training in the more scientific and technical phases of fishery biology and technology.

One hundred and twenty-five students were selected on the basis of competitive examinations in one of six optional fields: fish capture, technology, oceanography, marine biology, fish culture, and home economics (fishery). At the termination of the rehabilitation program these students went to work immediately either in the Philippine Bureau of Fisheries or in the fishing industry and its associated activities. 28
