Part I INTERPRETATION AND ANALYSIS OF THE ROBINSON-PATMAN LAW 

The Robinson-Patman Anti-Discrimination Act has a far-reaching effect on modern business practices, policies, and rights. Executives of businesses engaged in any way in interstate commerce will need to be management-conscious of the applications of this Act as it affects such commerce.

Business specialists and professional men, such as lawyers and accountants employed within a business or serving as business counselors, are vitally interested in this legislation as it affects their opportunities and responsibilities. Some of the applications to the more important business specialists are here outlined: 

The Lawyer: Because it gives you a new opportunity to serve your employer or clients. The Robinson- 

Patman law applies to every seller doing an interstate business and every purchase from such a seller. 

So wide is its application that few businesses selling or buying merchandise for resale escape it. And its penalties are so drastic that no businessman can afford to neglect bringing the policies of his company into line.

The Accountant: Because it places upon every interstate business the responsibility of maintaining accurate cost records. Quantity discounts can be granted only when they represent genuine savings in production, delivery, and sales cost. When a seller grants such discounts, he must be prepared to justify them in case the Federal Trade Commission questions him

A dozen do's and don'ts must be observed in regard to such long-standing policies as quantity discounts, allowances, rebates, extension of services, brokerage fees, premiums, advertising and service allowances, and P.M.'s. Purpose of the Robinson-Patman Law

The purpose of the Robinson-Patman law, briefly stated, is to amend Section 2 of the Clayton Act so as to:

GILBERT H. MONTAGUE 

Prevent more successfully price discrimination between buyers of the same seller where such discrimination is not justified by economies of production, delivery, or sales cost.

It is alleged that in the past manufacturers have granted price concessions to large purchasers such as chain stores which were not available to those who purchased in smaller quantities, and that such price concessions were not justified on a basis of lower costs. The 

Robinson-Patman law is directed at the correction of this condition. 

How the Robinson-Patman Law Differs from Previous Anti-Discrimination Laws 

Existing anti-discrimination laws are strengthened in four ways by the Robinson-Patman law: 

1. In effect it outlaws the "rule of reason" which has been read into previous laws. Under Section 2 of the Clayton 

Act (of which the Robinson-Patman law is an amendment) the court could decide in any case whether or not the tendency to suppress competition or to create monopoly was unreasonable. Now, with certain well-defined exceptions, all discrimination is unlawful and the court or commission will be called on to decide questions of fact, not questions of reason. 

2. It makes discrimination unlawful, with certain exceptions, and this places the accused in the position of justifying his acts. The burden of proof as to the legality of discrimination is upon the accused. 

3. It defines competition in such a way as to include every link in the distribution of merchandise. Previous legislation limited the definition of competition to that between sellers engaging in a similar function-for example, one manufacturer competed with other manufacturers, one wholesaler with other wholesalers. But under the terms of the Robinson-Patman Act the meaning of competition has been extended to include not only competition between those in the same plane, but also all others engaged in

distributing goods. That is, you may not discriminate so as to injure direct competitors, the competitors of your customers, or the competitors of their customers. 

4. It makes unlawful the acceptance of discrimination, except that which is justified, so that the buyer who “knowingly receives" discrimination shares liability with the seller.

Broad Provisions of the Act Itself 1. The Act applies only to interstate commerce. Prices on merchandise sold in intrastate (within the state) commerce, however, may be used as a standard of comparison for those on merchandise sold in interstate commerce. 

2. Discounts and allowances given to one kind of a distributor must be offered on proportionately equal terms to all distributors. 

Example: A manufacturer giving a discount to Sears, Roebuck & Company must offer a proportionately equal discount to the Jones General Store. And a manufacturer must grant the same discounts and allowances, proportionately, to a wholesaler and a retailer. This is interpreted as meaning that a retailer can claim the same discount as a wholesaler if he buys in equal quantities and demands no more service. 

3. Price discriminations (allowances and discounts are a form of such discrimination) are permissible when: (a) They do not exceed the difference in the cost of manufacture, sale, or delivery. 

Example: Only when a manufacturer can produce, sell, and deliver a product for less money than a competitor can he sell it for less. (He may, however, meet a competitor's price.) This places an additional premium on more efficient production (Foremanship), selling (Salesmanship), and delivery (Traffic Management). Accountants share these opportunities because accurate and detailed cost records must be maintained.

(b) They relate to a different method of operation or dealing and do not exceed the resulting difference in the cost of manufacture, sale, or delivery.

Example: If a manufacturer can develop a more effective method of production, selling, or delivery, he can profit from that efficiency by offering lower prices. (c) They represent price changes in response to changing market conditions.

Example: A manufacturer can sell fashion goods at one price this week and reduce the price on the same goods next week without committing illegal discrimination, if fashion changes justify the difference in price. (d) They involve services or allowances which are available in equal proportions to all purchasers.

Example: See No. 2 above. 

4. Goods may not be sold at unreasonably low prices for the purpose of destroying competition or eliminating a competitor. 

5. In certain instances limits may be placed on discounts even though justified by quantity savings. The 

Federal Trade Commission can investigate quantity discounts and limit them-even though they are legitimate savings if they tend to increase monopoly advantages. 

If a few buyers can buy in quantities so much greater than any other buyers that their discounts constitute special advantages, then those discounts may be illegal. 

6. Brokerage, except to an independent broker, is illegal. In other words, brokerage claimed by the large corporate chains and by the buying organizations of the voluntary chains of independents is no longer permitted. 

7. Administration of the Robinson-Patman Act: 

(a) The burden of proof is upon the person

charged with what appears on its face to be a violation. It is sufficient for the Commission to show that there has been discrimination in price, services, or facilities furnished. The accused then must show justification. If unable to show justification, the Commission is authorized to issue an order terminating the discrimination. (b) Violations may also be prosecuted by the 

Attorney General of the United States. 

(c) Suits for injunction may also be brought by anyone in danger of irreparable loss or damage from threatened conduct in violation.

Suits may be filed in any district court of the United States in the district in which the defendant resides, or is found, or has an agent. 

8. Penalties: 

(a) Criminal prosecution in the event of conviction will result in a fine of not more than $5,000 or imprisonment for not more than one year, or both. (b) Suits for damages (under Section 4 of the 

Clayton Act) may be brought by anyone "injured in this business or property by reason of anything forbidden in the anti-trust laws." The damages sustained shall be recovered threefold, plus court costs and attorney's fees.

How This Act Applies to Common Business Practices 

1. Intrastate Business. If a company operates within a single state, the Act does not apply to it. Purely intrastate business does not come under the direct jurisdiction of the Act, but even intrastate sales may be compared

with interstate sales to determine whether or not there has been illegal discrimination. 

2. Export Sales. The Act does not apply to sales for export, but, needless to say, merchandise bought for export must be exported, not resold. 

3. Private Brands. Identical merchandise under different brand names cannot be sold at different prices under similar conditions. If investigated, sellers of merchandise thought to be similar but carrying different label designations must show real and substantial quality differences. Obviously, sellers must check their lists of merchandise and eliminate items of approximately the same quality, selling under different brand names at different prices. 

4. Trade Discounts. Trade discounts paid to wholesalers or retailers for performing a functional service for the manufacturer, thus relieving the manufacturer of a functional service cost, are prohibited. They are not mentioned in the Act, which is written so that it prohibits what is not included. 

5. Allowances. Allowances may be made provided they represent differences in costs costs of manufacturing, costs of selling, costs of delivery. 

6. Cost Accounting. Sellers coming under this Act should institute accurate cost accounting systems. When the accused appears before the Federal Trade Commission to show justification for price discrimination, his cost system may be challenged. Sellers must be assured that they can back up quoted differences by actual cost figures. (a) Cost figures must include allocated selling and delivery costs. The Act leaves open to question whether or not selling cost, inclusive of advertising, should be spread over all production. However, just what can be included

and what cannot is a question for test cases to decide. In any event accurate records must be available and then, regardless of the Commission's ruling on any particular item, accurate costs can soon be produced. (b) The Act implies that delivery cost should be based on actual cost to point of delivery, and all systems for averaging delivery costs or figuring delivery costs to a large buyer's headquarters rather than to each individual point of delivery are subject to challenge. This law may result in decentralization and relocation of industry. 

7. Deteriorated, Obsolescent, and Discontinued Goods. 

Price changes are permitted to facilitate: the disposal of bankrupt stocks, perishable goods, goods affected by seasonal obsolescence; sales of distressed merchandise under court process; or sales in good faith in discontinued lines. 

8. Brokerage. Brokerage commissions cannot be claimed by a broker affiliated with either the buyer or the seller. This does away with brokers working directly for buying or selling organizations as has been the practice in the past. 

9. Free Deals, Bonuses, Premiums, Prizes, Rewards of

All Kinds. All rewards made by seller to customer or customer's salesmen are unlawful unless they are offered on proportionately equal terms to all buyers, and are justified on a basis of the difference in the cost of selling the specified quantities which are accompanied by the rewards. 

10. Bribes. Prohibited by implication. 

11. Services and Facilities. Including advertising, missionary men, showcases, demonstrators, goods for bargain or clearance sales, all are permitted if they are offered all

customers on proportionately equal terms. The customer's sales volume will probably be used to determine proportionately equal terms. 

12. Advertising Allowances. Are permissible provided they are offered to all customers on proportionately equal terms. This includes window displays, prominent display of goods, newspaper advertising, sample distribution, radio advertising, advertising rates, demonstrations, and similar assistance. 

13. Selection of Customers. A seller may select his customers by functional service type. This permits manufacturers to: (a) Limit customers to one functional class-retailers or wholesalers, for example. (b) Disregard customer selection and sell to everyone-wholesalers, chains, independent retailers on the same basis, price varying with quantity discounts. 

14. Consumer Co-operatives. It is held that the Robinson-Patman Act will encourage consumer co-operatives because patronage dividends are exempted.

These various features and implications of the Act will become clearer as you now read the Act itself and then 

Mr. Montague's analysis.

Part THE ROBINSON-PATMAN ANTI-DISCRIMINATION ACT 

The Robinson-Patman Anti-Discrimination Act was approved by the President and immediately became effective on June 19, 1936.

Parts of the Act may be invalid on constitutional grounds, but throughout this memo it will be assumed that the Act is constitutional and enforcible-as the Federal Trade Commission and the Attorney General of the United States must officially assume, in respect of this or any other Act of Congress, until the courts have definitely held to the contrary.

The Act amends the anti-discrimination provisions of old Section 2 of the Clayton Act which has been on the statute books since 1914. “To discriminate" means, according to Webster's Dictionary, "to make a difference in treatment or favor (of one as compared with others); as, to discriminate in favor of one's friends; to discriminate against a special class." 

Four Characteristics of Price Discriminations 

To be unlawful under Section 1 of the Robinson-Patman Act, a price discrimination must have all of the four following characteristics: 

1. It must be a price discrimination in the course of interstate commerce. But in determining whether a price discrimination in interstate commerce is, or is not, unlawful, a sale by the seller in intrastate commerce may be used as the standard of comparison. If this provision of the

Act is constitutional, the Act will be a strong deterrent against price discriminations in many transactions that are in intrastate commerce and that heretofore have been regarded as wholly outside the purview (scope) of Congressional legislation. 15

2. It must be a price discrimination between purchasers of commodities of like grade and quality. In determining whether commodities are of like grade and quality, the courts perhaps may hold that differences merely in brands or in trade-names may have to be disregarded, when there are actually no differences in grade or in quality. 

3. It must be a price discrimination of commodities that are sold for use, consumption, or resale, within the United

States, or in some place under the jurisdiction of the United States. This requirement under Section 1 of the 

Act differs from the requirement under Section 3 of the Act which will later be discussed. 

4. It must be a price discrimination that has one or both of the following effects:

Either "substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly in any line of commerce"; "Or to injure, destroy, or prevent competition with any person who either grants or knowingly receives the benefit of such discrimination, or with customers of either of them."

This last "effect" was not in old Section 2 of the Clayton Act or in any other Federal statute, and prescribes a standard of legality to which business until now has been wholly unaccustomed, and opens up some interesting possibilities.

Those interesting possibilities include the question of whether or not competition has been "injured" in a case of this nature:

A manufacturer sells direct to retailers as well as to wholesalers. He will, no doubt, grant to his retail customers discounts smaller than those he grants to his wholesale customers. Suppose, then, that one of his wholesalers grants to his retail customers discounts larger than the manufacturer grants to his. As a consequence, one of the manu-

facturer's retail customers who is not competing with the wholesaler but with the wholesaler's customer is unable to meet the latter's competition. 

The question, of course, is whether or not the courts will hold that competition with the manufacturer's retail customer has been injured.

If the courts interpret the Act literally, and hold that a manufacturer is a lawbreaker because of these consequences, a manufacturer may then find himself within the zone of legal danger when he discriminates in price between his wholesaler-customers, on the one hand, and his dealer-customers, on the other, even though neither group is in competition with the other. 

If this conclusion is adopted by the courts, a manufacturer may then be obliged to weigh at his peril the possibility of unlawfulness that may arise from any competitive disadvantage which one of his own dealer-customers may suffer, if such dealer-customer encounters competition from another dealer who is neither a customer of, nor known to, the manufacturer, but is merely a dealer-customer of some wholesaler-customer of the manufacturer. "Due Allowance for Differences in Cost"

Even if a price discrimination possesses all of the four characteristics above enumerated, it will nevertheless fall short of being an unlawful price discrimination, if it is excused or exonerated under any of the following provisos: 

5. Price differentials are permissible under Section 1 of the 

Robinson-Patman Act if they "make only due allowance for differences in the cost of manufacture, sale, or delivery resulting from the differing methods or quantities in which such commodities are to such purchasers sold or delivered."

No one knows just how closely a seller must figure in determining whether his price differentials make "only due allowance" for differences in cost.

Discussing this proviso, the Federal Trade Commission in its official statement regarding the Act said (release, July 15, 1936): "This proviso is of greatest importance, for while it leaves trade and industry free from any restriction or impediment to the adoption and use of more economic processes of manufacture, methods of sale, and modes of delivery and to the translation of appropriate shares of any savings so effected up and down the stream of distribution to the original producer and to the ultimate consumer, it also strictly limits the use of quantity price differences to that sphere, since beyond it they become instruments of favor and privilege and weapons of competitive oppression."

Interpretation of Constituent Phrases 

"Certain of its constituent phrases should be further noted," continued the Federal Trade Commission. "(a)"... which make only due allowances. . . . "This phrase," the Federal Trade Commission stated, "is carried over from the present Act (i.e., old Section 2 of the Clayton Act), but as coupled with the remainder of the clause, is here extended to limit quantity differentials, as well as those on account of selling and transportation costs. It marks the zone within which differentials may be granted. "The bill neither requires nor compels the granting of discriminations or differentials of any sort. It leaves any who wish to do so entirely free to sell to all at the same price regardless of differences in cost, or to grant any differentials not in excess of such differences.

"It does not require the differential, if granted, to be the arithmetical equivalent of the difference. It is sufficient that it does not exceed it. "(b) " resulting from the differing methods or quantities in which such commodities are to such purchasers sold or delivered.... 

"This," declared the Federal Trade Commission, "limits the differences in cost which may be honored in support of price differentials to those marginal differences demonstrable as between the particular customers concerned in the discrimination." 

Prorating the Savings "It is designed, among other things," continued the 

Federal Trade Commission, "to preclude the grant of a discrimination to a particular customer equal to the whole saving in cost resulting to the seller's entire volume of business as augmented by that customer's patronage; to preclude also differentials based on allocated or imputed, as distinguished from actual, differences in cost, representing particular facilities or departments which the favored customer may not have immediately utilized, but with which the seller cannot dispense in the general conduct of his business. "It is designed, in short, to leave the test of a permissible differential upon the question: "If the more favored customer were sold in the same quantities and by the same methods of sale and delivery as the customer not so favored, how much more per unit would it actually cost the seller to do so, his other business remaining the same? "The particular words 'resulting from' and 'to such purchasers,' as here used, are deemed competent to narrow

the permitted differentials to those limits, and it seems eminently fair and just that they should be so limited. "No particular customer should be permitted distinctively to claim the benefit, nor required distinctively to bear the burden, of the immediate use or nonuse of facilities which the seller must maintain for his business generally."

Review of Quantity Discounts by the Federal Trade Commission 

"The Federal Trade Commission may," the Act provides, "after due investigation and hearing to all interested parties, fix and establish quantity limits, and revise the same as it finds necessary, as to particular commodities or classes of commodities, where it finds that available purchasers in greater quantities are so few as to render differentials on account thereof unjustly discriminatory or promotive of monopoly in any line of commerce; and the foregoing shall then not be construed to permit differentials based on differences in quantities greater than those so fixed and established."

Selection of Customers and Changes of Prices 

"Nothing herein contained," the Act provides, "shall prevent persons engaged in selling goods, wares, or merchandise in commerce from selecting their own customers in bona fide transactions and not in restraint of trade." "Nothing herein contained," the Act then provides, "shall prevent price changes from time to time where in response to changing conditions affecting the market for or the marketability of the goods concerned, such as but not limited to actual or imminent deterioration of perishable goods, obsolescence of seasonal goods, distress sales under court process, or sales in good faith in discontinuance of business in the goods concerned."

Burden of Proving Excuse or Exoneration From what has thus far been stated, it results that a price discrimination is lawful so far as Section 1 of the Act is concerned, if it lacks any of the characteristics described in (1)-(4) above, or if it is excused or exonerated by any of the provisos described in (5) above. 

Whoever is charging that a price discrimination is unlawful must, on general legal principles, assume the burden of proving that it possesses all the characteristics described in (1)-(4) above, but need not assume the burden of proving that it falls outside of the provisos described in (5) above. 

Contrariwise, whoever is defending a price discrimination as being lawful is not, according to general legal principles, obliged to assume the burden of proving that the price discrimination lacks any of the characteristics described in (1)-(4) above, but he must assume the burden of proving that it falls within one or more of the provisos described in (5) above. 

This latter rule, the Act states, shall be the rule that must govern in Federal Trade Commission proceedings to enforce Section 1 of the Act. 

Meeting Competition 

The Act then provides that "nothing herein contained shall prevent a seller rebutting the prima-facie case thus made by showing that his lower price or the furnishing of services or facilities to any purchaser or purchasers was made in good faith to meet an equally low price of a competitor, or the services or facilities furnished by a competitor."

This proviso last quoted states another ground on which may be excused or exonerated a price discrimination that would otherwise be unlawful.

A very narrow meaning was ascribed to this proviso, when the bill was pending before the House of Representatives. "This proviso," declared Congressman Utterback in the report of the House Judiciary Committee accompanying the bill, "represents a contraction of an exemption now contained in Section 2 of the Clayton Act which permits discriminations without limit where made in good faith to meet competition."

Limitations on Meeting Competition 

"It should be noted," continued Congressman Utterback, "that while the seller is permitted to meet local competition, it does not permit him to cut local prices until his competitor has first offered lower prices, and then he can go no further than to meet those prices. "If he goes further, he must do so likewise with all his other customers, or make himself liable to all of the penalties of the Act, including treble damages. "In other words, the proviso permits the seller to meet the price actually previously offered by a local competitor. "It permits him to go no further."

This thought was again developed by Congressman Utterback when the bill came before the House. "In connection with the above rule as to burden of proof," said Congressman Utterback, "it is also provided that a seller may show that his lower price was made in good faith to meet an equally low price of a competitor, or that his furnishing of services or facilities was made in good faith to meet those furnished by a competitor. "It is to be noted, however, that this does not set up the meeting of competition as an absolute bar to a charge of discrimination under the bill.

"It merely permits it to be shown in evidence. "This provision is entirely procedural. "It does not determine substantive rights, liabilities, and duties. "They are fixed in the other provisions of the bill. "It leaves it a question of fact to be determined in each case, whether the competition to be met was such as to justify the discrimination given, as one lying within the limitations laid down by the bill, and whether the way in which the competition was met lies within the latitude allowed by those limitations."

Not a Carte Blanche (Unconditional) Exemption 

"This procedural provision," continued Congressman

Utterback, "cannot be construed as a carte blanche exemption to violate the bill so long as a competitor can be shown to have violated it first, nor so long as that competition cannot be met without the use of oppressive discriminations in violation of the obvious intent of the bill. 

"To illustrate: The House committee hearings showed a discrimination of 15 cents a box granted by Colgate- Palmolive-Peet Company on sales of soap to the A. & P. chain. Upon a complaint and hearing before the Federal

Trade Commission, this proviso would permit the Colgate Company to show in rebuttal evidence, if such were the fact, an equally low price made by a local soap manufacturer in Des Moines, Iowa, to A. & P.'s retail outlets in that city; but this would not exonerate it from a discrimination granted to A. & P. everywhere, if otherwise in violation of the bill." "But the committee hearings show," continued Congressman Utterback, "a similar discount of 15 cents a case

granted by Procter & Gamble to the same chain. If this proviso were construed to permit the showing of a competing offer as an absolute bar to liability for discrimination, then it would nullify the Act entirely at the very inception of its enforcement; for in nearly every case mass buyers receive similar discriminations from competing sellers of the same product. "One violation of the law cannot be permitted to justify another. "As in any case of self-defense, while the attack against which the defense is claimed may be shown in evidence, its competency as a bar depends also upon whether it was a legal or illegal attack. "A discrimination in violation of this bill is in practical effect a commercial bribe to lure the business of the favored customer away from the competitor, and if one bribe were permitted to justify another, the bill would be futile to achieve its plainly intended purposes." 

These statements by Congressman Utterback are quoted in the Federal Trade Commission's official statement regarding the Act (release, July 15, 1936).

Brokerage So confused and so hotly debated is the brokerage provision of the Act that it will be quoted in full: "It shall be unlawful," the Act provides, "for any person engaged in commerce, in the course of such commerce, to pay or grant, or to receive or accept, anything of value as a commission, brokerage, or other compensation, or any allowance or discount in lieu thereof, except for services rendered in connection with the sale or purchase of goods, wares, or merchandise, either to the other party to such transaction or to an agent, representative, or other intermediary therein where such intermediary is

acting in fact for or in behalf, or is subject to the direct or indirect control, of any party to such transaction other than the person by whom such compensation is so granted or paid."

In a number of industries groups of retailers now pool their buying under associations, which in part depend for their operating expenses on brokerage commissions paid by manufacturers either to such associations or to brokers, jobbers, or other persons designated by such associations, these brokerage commissions being computed upon sales by such manufacturers to such associations or their members.

To these and other buying agencies, the proper interpretation of this brokerage provision is of great importance.

What Is the Meaning of the Brokerage Provision? 

Omitting the words "except for services rendered in connection with the sale or purchase of goods, wares, or merchandise," and reducing the rest of the brokerage provision to its keywords, it may be paraphrased to read: 

it shall be unlawful for (the seller) . . . to pay or grant... a commission, brokerage, or other compensation, or any allowance or discount in lieu thereof either to the other party to such transaction (i.e., the purchaser) or to an agent, representative, or other intermediary where such intermediary is acting in fact for or in behalf, or is subject to the direct or indirect control, of (the purchaser)." Upon this paraphrase, from which are omitted the words "except for services rendered in connection with the sale or purchase of goods, wares, or merchandise,” it may perhaps be contended that a seller must not pay "a commission, brokerage, or other compensation, or any

allowance or discount in lieu thereof" to "an agent, representative, or other intermediary" that is "acting in fact for or in behalf, or is subject to the direct or indirect control" of the purchaser, regardless of the amount of business such "agent, representative, or other intermediary" turns in to the seller.

Services Rendered by Associations 

Everyone who sells in industries, however, where there are associations of this character, knows that only as these associations unlock the door to a seller can a seller ever succeed in selling competitive items to these associations or their members.

From the practical and business standpoint, therefore, these associations render in fact a service that is indispensable to these sellers, in connection with their sale of goods, wares, or merchandise to these associations and their members, even though such associations and the brokers, jobbers, or other persons designated by such associations may also be "acting in fact for or in behalf, or (are) subject to the direct or indirect control" of these associations or their members.

Effect of the Exception Clause 

What, now, is the effect of injecting into the above paraphrase the words "except for services rendered in connection with the sale or purchase of goods, wares, or merchandise"?

Giving to these words their grammatical and literal meaning, it may be contended that the entire prohibition of this brokerage provision of the Act falls to the ground whenever the requirements of these words are fulfilled. 

It may forcefully be argued that in fact "services (are) rendered (to a seller by such 'agent, representative, or

of other intermediary') in connection with the sale goods, wares, or merchandise," and that the seller may lawfully pay "a commission, brokerage, or other compensation" to such "agent, representative, or other intermediary" when the latter has unlocked the door and given the seller selling access to the association and its members, and that it is immaterial that "such intermediary is (also) acting in fact for or in behalf, or is subject to the direct or indirect control" of the association and its members.

What Did Congress Intend? 

Against this interpretation, it may perhaps be contended that when Congress inserted in the Act these words "except for services rendered in connection with the sale or purchase of goods, wares, or merchandise," Congress thought and intended that these words would merely strengthen the Act as it stood before these words were inserted, and did not think or intend that these words would afford any escape from the Act by an "agent, representative, or other intermediary" who was performing two services, viz., unlocking the door which gives the seller selling access to the association and its members, and also "acting in fact for or in behalf or subject to the direct or indirect control" of the association and its members.

Statements by members of the Senate and the House in charge of this bill while it was before Congress contain some indication that these members were under some impression that these two services were mutually contradictory.

Views of Senator Logan and Congressman Utterback 

"To permit its payment or allowance ('a commission, brokerage, or other compensation'), where no such service is rendered," said Senator Logan in the report accom-

panying the bill before these words had been inserted, "where in fact if a broker, so labeled, enters the picture at all, it is one whom the buyer points out to the seller, rather than one who brings the buyer to the seller, is but to permit the corruption of this function to the purposes of competitive discrimination." "The relation existing between a broker and his client is fiduciary," said Senator Logan at a later date. "To allow a broker to collect from a client for services rendered to the adverse party is a violation of that relationship." "To permit its payment or allowance ('a commission, brokerage, or other compensation')," said Congressman

Utterback in the report accompanying the bill after these words had been inserted, "where no such service is rendered, where, in fact, if a broker, so labeled, enters the picture at all, it is one whom the buyer points out to the seller, rather than one who brings the buyer to the seller, would render the section a nullity. "The relation of the broker to his client is a fiduciary one. "To collect from a client for services rendered in the interest of a party adverse to him, is a violation of that relationship." 

Other statements by Senator Logan and Congressman

Utterback to the same effect are quoted in the Federal Trade Commission's official statement regarding the Act (release, July 15, 1936).

It will be hazardous to venture any interpretation of the brokerage provision of the Act before it has been judicially interpreted.

Services or Facilities Furnished by the Seller 

The Act provides that it shall be unlawful for any person engaged in interstate commerce to make or agree to

make any payment for the benefit of a customer in the course of interstate commerce "as compensation or in consideration for any services or facilities furnished by or through such customer in connection with the processing, handling, sale, or offering for sale of any products or commodities manufactured, sold, or offered for sale by such person, unless such payment or consideration is available on proportionally equal terms to all other customers competing in the distribution of such products or commodities."

Examples of such "services or facilities" which a customer might furnish to a seller are warehousing, special selling efforts, special displays, and advertising. 

Payment for such "services or facilities" is not forbidden.

There is no requirement that such payment shall be "only due allowance" for "such services or facilities."

The Federal Trade Commission is not empowered to fix or establish any limits on the quantity of these "services or facilities."

Beyond the requirement that such "services or facilities" must in fact be "furnished," the only requirement of Section 1 of the Act is that the "payment or consideration" paid or agreed to be paid for such "services or facilities" must be "available on proportionally equal terms to all other customers competing in the distribution of such products or commodities." 

Latitude of Payment by the Seller

Section 1 of the Act has no application to any payment by the seller for "services or facilities" under the following circumstances, viz.: 

1. When the payer is not the seller; or 

2. When the payer is not engaged in interstate commerce; or

3. When the payment made or agreed to be made is not in the course of interstate commerce; or 

4. When such payment is made or agreed to be made to or for the benefit of someone who is not a customer of the payer; or 

5. When the "services or facilities" are furnished by or through someone who is not a customer of the payer; or 

6. When such "services or facilities" are furnished in some manner other than "in connection with the processing, handling, sale, or offering for sale of any products or commodities manufactured, sold, or offered for sale" by the payer; or 

7. When such "payment or consideration" is "available on proportionally equal terms to all other customers competing in the distribution of such products or commodities."

Plainly, here is a latitude of lawfulness, in these provisions regarding payment by the seller for "services or facilities" furnished by the customer, that far exceeds that which exists in respect of price differentials under the earlier provisions of the Act. "Available" 

When is such "payment or consideration" "available”?

Obviously, it is "available” when the customer has reasonable opportunity to know that if he furnishes like "services or facilities," he can obtain payment therefor on terms that are "proportionally equal" to those received from the seller by any of the customer's competitors. 

To whom must such "payment or consideration" be "available"?

Plainly it need not be "available" to all customers, but only to such customers as are "competing in the distribution of such products or commodities" with a customer who is receiving such "payment or consideration."

"Proportionally Equal Terms" 

What is meant by "proportionally equal terms"?

One interpretation that obviously is safe was stated by Senator Logan when the bill was before the Senate, and is quoted in the Federal Trade Commission's official statement regarding the Act (release, July 15, 1936): "So long as the same advertising allowances are made proportionately on the amount of purchases, there is no prohibition in the bill against them.”

Probably there are other rates of payment which can also be shown to be "proportionally equal terms." 

Services or Facilities Furnished by the Seller 

The Act provides that it shall be unlawful for any person to discriminate in favor of one purchaser against "another purchaser or purchasers of a commodity bought for resale, with or without processing, by contracting to furnish or furnishing, or by contributing to the furnishing of, any services or facilities connected with the processing, handling, sale, or offering for sale of such commodity so purchased upon terms not accorded to all purchasers on proportionally equal terms."

Examples of such "services or facilities" which a seller might furnish to his customer are the processing of goods for the customer's account, and the supplying of equipment, advertising, and special sales service to the customer.

Here again, the furnishing of such "services or facilities" is not forbidden, and there is no requirement that the seller's charge therefor shall be "only due allowance" for such "services or facilities," and the Federal Trade Commission is not empowered to fix or establish any limits on the quantity of these "services or facilities."

The only requirement of Section 1 of the Act is that "any services or facilities" furnished to a customer must be "accorded to all purchasers on proportionally equal terms."

Latitude of Services or Facilities 

Furnished by the Seller

Section 1 of the Act has no application to the "furnishing" of any "services or facilities" under the following circumstances, viz.: 

1. When the person "furnishing" them is not the seller; or 

2. When the person to whom they are furnished is not a "purchaser" from the person "furnishing" them; or 

3. When such "services or facilities" are furnished in some manner other than "connected with the processing, handling, sale, or offering for sale of such commodities so purchased"; or 

4. When such "services or facilities" are "accorded to all purchasers on proportionally equal terms."

While the latitude of legality for "services or facilities" furnished by the seller to the purchaser is not so broad as is the latitude of lawfulness for "services or facilities" furnished by the customer, it far exceeds the latitude of lawfulness that exists in respect of price differentials under the earlier provisions of the Act. "Accorded" and "Proportionally Equal Terms"

What must a seller do in order to comply with the requirement that his terms for the "furnishing of any services or facilities" may be deemed to have been "accorded to all purchasers on proportionally equal terms"?

Obviously, such terms are "accorded," beyond possibility of dispute, when all purchasers have reasonable opportunity to know that if they desire like "services or facilities," they can obtain them on terms that are "pro-

portionally equal" to those on which such "services or facilities" are furnished by the seller to any other purchaser. 

To whom must such "terms" be "accorded"?

The Act seems to require that, as regards "services or facilities" furnished to the purchaser by the seller, the seller must take care that "proportionally equal terms" are "accorded" to all the seller's purchasers, regardless of whether they are or are not in competition with one another. 

What is meant by "proportionally equal terms"?

One interpretation that obviously is safe is the same as that for "proportionally equal terms" in respect of advertising allowances, already quoted from Senator Logan and from the Federal Trade Commission's official statement regarding the Act (release, July 15, 1936)—namely, that so long as they are "proportionately on the amount of purchases, there is no prohibition in the bill against them."

Probably there are other bases of terms, which can also be shown to be "proportionally equal terms." Discrimination-Receivers

The Act provides that it shall be unlawful "for any person engaged in commerce, in the course of such commerce, knowingly to induce or receive a discrimination in price which is prohibited by this section." 

Plainly it is the intention of Congress that all liabilities imposed upon discrimination-givers by Section 1 of the Act shall also be imposed upon discrimination-receivers who have knowingly induced or received a price discrimination which is prohibited by this section.

Incor

Litigations Now Pending 

The Act provides that litigations now pending under old Section 2 of the Clayton Act may be continued subject to certain specified conditions. 

The Borah-Van Nuys Amendment 

Section 3 of the Act-sometimes called the "Borah-Van 

Nuys Amendment"-provides that it shall be unlawful for any person engaged in interstate commerce, or in commerce between the United States and foreign countries, in the course of such commerce, "to be a party to, or assist in, any transaction of sale, or contract to sell, which discriminates to his knowledge against competitors of the purchaser, in that, any discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge is granted to the purchaser over and above any discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge available at the time of such transaction to said competitors in respect of a sale of goods of like grade, quality, and quantity."

This section overlaps in some degree the provisions of 

Section 1 of the Act. 

Unlike Section 1, however, the provisions of Section 3 

are not inserted as amendments of old Section 2 of the Clayton Act, and stand wholly apart from that or any other pre-existing law. 

Discounts and Rebates

Section 3 of the Act does not require that any "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" shall conform to the yardstick of "only due allowance" which is prescribed in Section 1 of the Act. 

Section 3 requires only that any "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" shall not be in excess of those "available at the time of such transaction"

to purchasers who are "competitors in respect of a sale of goods of like grade, quality, and quantity" with the purchasers to whom such "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" is granted.

There seems to be some uncertainty, under Section 3 of the Act, as to whether a seller is excused and exonerated from violating this section if, for the purpose of meeting competition from some other seller, he grants to a purchaser a "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" larger than those which he grants to competitors of such purchaser.

Purchasers Must Be in Competition 

Unlike a price differential under Section 1 of the Act, a "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" under Section 3 of the Act is lawful, unless the purchaser "discriminated against," and the purchaser favored are each in competition with one another. "Available" 

Undoubtedly a "discount, rebate, allowance, or advertising service charge" under Section 3 of the Act is “available" when the customer has reasonable opportunity to know that at the time these are being granted to one of his competitors he also can obtain them "in respect of a sale of goods of like grade, quality, and quantity.' 

Destructive Price-Cutting 

Section 3 of the Act provides that it shall be unlawful for any person engaged in commerce, or in commerce between the United States and foreign countries, in the course of such commerce "to sell, or contract to sell, goods in any part of the United States at prices lower than those exacted by said person elsewhere in the United States for the purpose of destroying competition, or eliminating a

competitor in such part of the United States; or, to sell, or contract to sell, goods at unreasonably low prices for the purpose of destroying competition or eliminating a competitor."

Lawful competition may well result in the elimination of a competitor and the resulting cessation of his competition.

There is a practical difficulty, therefore, in determining at just what point lawful competition changes to unlawful competition "for the purpose of destroying competition or eliminating a competitor.'

Similarly, how can anyone determine when prices are "unreasonably low"?

These provisions of Section 3 of the Act raise serious questions, both from practical and constitutional standpoints.

Penalties of the Act 

Section 3 of the Robinson-Patman Act provides that for violating this section the penalty shall be a fine of not more than $5,000 or imprisonment of not more than one year, or both.

Section 1 and Section 2 of the Robinson-Patman Act enlarge the anti-discrimination prohibitions enacted in 

1914 in old Section 2 of the Clayton Act. 

Section 4 of the Clayton Act, which now applies to new 

Section 2 of the Clayton Act as enlarged by Section 1 of the Robinson-Patman Act, provides that anyone "injured in his business or property" by reason of anything forbidden in the anti-trust laws (which now include new Section 2 of the Clayton Act as enlarged by the Robinson- 

Patman Act) may sue and recover "threefold the damages by him sustained, and the cost of suit, including a reasonable attorney's fee."

Violation of Section 1 of the Robinson-Patman Act is not punishable by fine or imprisonment, but it may result in injunction suits by the Attorney General, and in "cease and desist❞ proceedings by the Federal Trade Commission, and also in the incalculably greater financial peril of suits for threefold damages by injured customers, from whom has been withheld some price differential or some service payment to which the customer would be entitled under this Act, in which the damages generally can be easily proved and readily measured as three times the price differential or service payment withheld, plus three times any other provable injury, plus interest, plus an attorney's fee.

Co-operative Associations 

Section 4 of the Act provides that "Nothing in this Act shall prevent a co-operative association from returning to its members, producers, or consumers the whole, or any part of, the net earnings or surplus resulting from its trading operations, in proportion to their purchases or sales from, to, or through the association." 

The purpose of this section was stated by Congressman Utterback when the bill was before the House, and is quoted in the Federal Trade Commission's official statement regarding the Act (release, July 15, 1936): "Section 4 represents another provision added to the bill in the fullness of caution to protect the distribution of co-operative earnings or surplus among their members on a patronage basis. "In the dealings of co-operatives with others,” declared Congressman Utterback, "they share, of course, the protections and guaranties of the bill as to equal treatment and equal opportunity which it extends to producers, manufacturers, and merchants in trade and commerce generally.

"It leaves the members of co-operatives free to seek through co-operative endeavor the economies and savings of mass operations and assures to them, as compared with their larger corporate competitors, any real economies and savings to which those mass operations entitle them, and which they often now do not receive. "There is nothing in the last section of the bill," continued Congressman Utterback, "that distinguishes cooperatives, either favorably or unfavorably, from other agencies in the streams of production and trade, so far as concerns their dealings with others."

Future Possibilities 

The delusion is widespread that compliance with, and enforcement of, this Act will be as occasional and unimportant as were compliance with, and enforcement of, the Prohibition Act and the trade practice provisions of some of the NRA codes.

Had the Prohibition Act and the National Industrial 

Recovery Act empowered every "injured" competitor and customer to sue for an ample and readily provable measure of damages, for each violation of the Prohibition Act, and for each violation of every trade practice provision in every NRA code, there probably would have been nothing occasional or unimportant about the flood of claims and suits that would have resulted. 

In the aggregate, the possibilities of danger contained in the Robinson-Patman Act envisage consequences that are far more serious to the businessman in his daily selling and purchasing operations than any that were contained in the Prohibition Act or the National Industrial Recov ery Act.

If the Robinson-Patman Act goes as far as some say it does, it may become as odious as prohibition, and will

probably then be repealed or modified by some future Congress.

If, on the other hand, the Act means as little as some think it does, it is likely to be strengthened by some future Congress.

Finally, there is the ever-present sporting chance that public opinion and business sentiment may prove to be less tolerant of discriminatory prices and discriminatory service payments than they were of violations of the Prohibition Act and of the trade practice provisions of some of the NRA codes!

Businessmen certainly will want to weigh carefully all these possibilities of danger, in conforming their price schedules and sales practices and buying policies to the provisions of the Robinson-Patman Act.
