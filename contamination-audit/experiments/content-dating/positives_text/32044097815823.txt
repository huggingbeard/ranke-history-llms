USE AND REDEMPTION OF MERCHANDISE STAMPS LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND AND REQUEST 

House Bill No. 776, introduced at the present session of the 

General Court by Rep. Walter F. Hurlburt of Greenfield, proposes that the money value of all unredeemed trading stamps, coupons and premiums be turned over to the State Treasury annually. It would make all such stamps irredeemable on December 31st of the year following the year in which the stamps were issued. A hearing on the bill was held on February 29, 1956 by the Joint Committee on Ways and Means. 

Rep. Hurlburt intends adding to H.776, a section spelling out regulatory and enforcement provisions. According to the wording of this proposed section, the State Treasurer and Receiver General would be empowered to petition the Superior Court for an order allowing him or his agents to examine the business records of a person suspected of failing to report unredeemed stamps. 

It also provides for penalties to be imposed by the Superior Court upon violators.

On March 5, 1956, the bureau was asked by Rep. Louis H. Glaser of the House Ways and Means Committee, for information as to the annual value of such unredeemed stamps. This request was subsequently repeated by Rep. Hurlburt. 

It would be very time consuming to meet these requests if the wording of H.776 is taken at face value. The variety and number of coupons alone which are flooding Massachusetts in connection with purchases of such items as soap, coffee, and shortening is very large indeed. Since we have been given to understand that H.776, if passed, will probably eliminate the provisions relative to

coupons and premiums, this report is confined to the commonly known trading stamps issued by food retailers, gasoline stations, drug stores and various other retail outlets. 

As background for estimating the degree to which stamps are not redeemed, the report sketches briefly how stamp plans work, their frequency, the controversies they start, their constitutionality, and their use elsewhere.

EXTENT AND USE OF STAMPS 

The trading stamp company sells its

The retailer issues the stamps to his customers, usually one stamp with each 10¢ purchase. Retailers often hold "double stamp' sales to attract customers on so-called "off" days. The ordinary stamp book of 1200 stamps, when filled, represents $120 in purchases.

Some companies use larger books but the plan is the same one stamp with each 10¢ purchase.

The books, when filled in, are redeemable for standard brand merchandise at redemption centers operated by the stamp companies. 

Each completed book of 1200 stamps is worth about $3 in merchandise. 

Cash redemption is permitted, but is requested on rare occasions only, because the books are worth considerably more in merchandise.

Controversy over Use of Stamps. Trading stamps have been the subject of widespread and violent controversy from their very inception. The following brief text summarizes the most widely used arguments for and against their use. 

Pro. Good business practice everywhere includes the cash discount system. The stamp companies contend that their plan is the only practical method of giving the ultimate consumer a discount for cash. Most courts agree that "by this arrangement, the stamp or coupon operates as a discount in cash for every purchase made br

The stamp plan serves as a savings plan for many people, many of whom are not financially able to save otherwise. It also encourages these people to obtain articles for household or personal use which they would ordinarily be unable to afford. 

The trading stamp company is merely a middleman between retailer and consumer, to be compared to other legitimate middlemen such as distributors and jobbers.

The stamp plan does not add to the cost of the goods sold, because it is good advertising which increases the volume of business and pays for itself, if nothing else, by spreading overhead costs over more sales volume. The stamp companies say that a 

20% increase in sales will more than cover the 2% to 3% cost of the stamps, provided overhead costs remain the same. An executive of 1

Con. On the other hand, stamp plan opponents argue that the plan is not purely a cash discount device, because many merchants issue stamps to customers who charge their bills.

Since the net profits of retailers in the $40 billion food industry average less than 2%, it is obvious that a 2% to 3% cost of stamps cannot be absorbed by profits. CONSTITUTIONALITY. 

The issue of constitutionality has been raised by the stamp people in their opposition to H.776. The United States Supreme 

Court, in a decision previously referred to,1 quite clearly viewed regulation of the stamp business as within the police power of any state. That decision "sustained the validity of a statute imposing a license tax on merchants using trading stamps, even though the legislation might be of a prohibitive character; and this decision has been followed in other cases in that court."2

PRACTICE IN OTHER STATES 

The four states of Utah, North Dakota, Kansas and Wisconsin, encouraged by the U.S. Supreme Court decision, have taken vigorous action against the stamp companies. This action has been based on punitive taxation and confiscatory license fees. A few other states have passed milder restrictive legislation. It is interesting to note that, even in those states where legislation has been most prohibitive, Sperry & Hutchinson Co. and other stamp companies are still doing business. 

For the most part, the states have been inclined to consider the stamp business as legitimate enterprise, and have not succeeded in curbing their activities. Where restraint has been attempted, the state courts have intervened sharply. Early in 1955, the State of New Jersey brought two actions against the Sperry & Hutchinson Co. under an old escheat law. One has already been defeated, and the other is still pending.

Apparently inspired by the New Jersey action, 14 states¹ in 1955 introduced escheat bills which were worded almost identically with H.776 of Massachusetts. Every one of these bills was defeated.2

Instead, the following summary statement is presented as to the various estimates of unredeemed stamps which are available. 

The highest such estimate of 37% would bring in revenue of close to $2,000,000; however, the author of this estimate tells us that his figures are not applicable in Massachusetts.

The second highest estimate of 30% would mean about $1,500,000 in revenue; this estimate (30%) should also be scrutinized very carefully, because it was made, without substantiation, in the heat of a violent political battle in Wisconsin.

A more considered estimate is that of 15%-20%, made in a publication of the University of New Mexico Bureau of Business Research; this evaluation would result in revenue of approximately $1,000,000.

- Then we have Professor Vredenburgh's estimates of 5% for large companies and 25% for small companies. Combining the two, on the basis of volume of business done by large as against small establishments, his average is 10% and would suggest $500,000 in revenue.

Finally, we come to the fairly well established figure of 5% as representing the unredeemed portion of S&H stamps, plus the company's claim that only 11% are unredeemed. Proceeding on the general theory that small dealers redeem fewer stamps, the S&H index applied proportionately to all stamp companies in the state, would bring in about $200,000.
