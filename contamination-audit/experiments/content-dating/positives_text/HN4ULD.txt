CHAPTER I The Beginnings of Colonial Labor 

In the development of a new community three things are necessaryland, capital, and labor. Early in the Seventeenth Century, land in 

America was cheap and abundant.

Money for the development of that land was provided by gentlemen of wealth in England. Labor was scarce and high-priced. How to secure laborers was the chief problem that faced the English colonists. Probably four out of five free white laborers owned their own land and did their own work with the aid of their families. The native Indians made poor servants. From a master's viewpoint, the Indian was lazy and inefficient. He was unreliable and disobedient; his ways of doing things differed too much from the ways of the white man. Negro slaves were introduced in the colonies as early as 1619, but they played no important part in colonial labor until near the end of the Seventeenth Century.

The prosperity of the New World, then, depended upon the white laborers. The English colonists were forced to look to the mother country for their supply of labor. 

There were two forces at work, with an ocean between them, that solved the labor problem. In England, living conditions for the poor were almost unbearably hard. In 

America there was a great need for workers. How these two conditions combined to develop a system of colonial labor is the theme of our story.

Conditions in England: In Eng land, only those who had served an apprenticeship were allowed to follow a trade. All laborers not apprenticed were then left to find employment on the land. The result was a fixed class of laborers with steady work and a regular income, and great masses of people with very little work, if any, and irregular income. The land could not provide work for all. Unemployment became so severe that men without work were not allowed to go from one parish to another in search of employment. Each parish was overburdened with its own unemployed. 

It was a truly discouraging situation.

If a laborer did find work, his wages were too low to live on.

Ordinary laborers were paid eight pence a day when a pound of beef cost nearly half that much. The landlords charged high rents for the land, and this forced the farmer to pay low wages to his help; only by paying low wages, could the farmer make enough to pay his rent. 

With tears in their eyes, English laborers complained that they, with their wives and children, rose early (1)

Colonial Labor and worked hard till late at night. 

Yet the earnings of all paid for no more than a bit of brown bread and cheese to eat, and less than enough clothes to cover their nakedness. 

With a lifetime of the hardest labor, they could not save enough for their old age. Relief laws were passed by the Crown, but the well-to-do objected so strongly to the extra taxes, that few taxes were collected for the relief of the poor.

Naturally, persons who were so very poor, had no money for a voyage to America. They owned nothing but their ill-nourished bodies. 

In order to get to a new country to begin a new life, they sold their services for a term of years to an agent or ship's captain for the passage across the sea. When they landed in America, the captain sold them for more than enough to pay the expenses of the voyage. These dealers in labor thought no more of selling human beings into bondage than selling bales of cloth. Like any other goods, labor was bought cheap and sold dear. The laborer was helpless. He reasoned that it was. no worse to be a slave in America than a beggar in England. He hoped that he might become a landowner after his term of service was ended. 

The people of the upper class in England favored the transportation of the poor to the colonies. The unemployed were considered a burden upon their communities. The rich did not want to pay taxes to support the unemployed; neither did they want to lower rents or prices in order that the poor might live decently. The rich wanted to eat their cake and have it, too.

They eased their consciences by saying that crime would be lessened by sending the unemployed to the colonies, and that famine and plague would decrease. 

At this time three hundred crimes, according to English law, could be punished by death. For the lighter offenses, transportation to the colonies took the place of the death sentence. Neither imprisonment, whipping, nor branding with a hot iron had stopped stealing and petty crime.

For that reason, a law was passed which allowed such criminals to be sent "across the seas" to the colonies; their punishment was hard labor for not more than nine years and not less than five. Political rebels in Ireland, Scotland, and England were punished by sending them to America. Among these were many men of fine character, superior training, and high intelligence. 

Too often an honorable need was met by dishonorable means. The need for labor on the Virginia tobacco plantations gave rise to kidnapping. Men and women in the seaport towns of England made their living by luring the young and ignorant into their homes, where they seized the victims and put them on board ship for America. Children were led on by sweetmeats. Not only were poor children seized, but even a few of noble birth. One young

The Colonial Labor Population: 

Colonial laborers came not only from England, Ireland, and Scotland, but from Germany and France. The 

French laborers were Protestants called Huguenots, who had been driven out of France. They were usually free laborers, working for a regular wage for themselves. The 

Germans came to Pennsylvania in great numbers, lured by land agents who pictured America as a paradise, where the food was ready to drop into one's mouth. 

Most of the convict labor came from England, though some came from Germany. New England discouraged the dumping of the "scum" 

of Europe in America. The Puri tans wanted no European "weeds" in their New World garden. Even some of the wisest and most prominent men of England spoke and wrote against sending criminals to the English colonies. 

By 1625 indentured servants made up two-fifths of the population of 

Virginia. Nearly seventy-five per cent. of the people coming to the tobacco colonies (Virginia and 

Maryland) were either apprentices or servants, or boys and girls from the towns and cities of England.

Probably half of these became freemen and landholders in later years. 

In the 1630's one-sixth of the people who came to Maryland were indentured servants. In the 1680's over one-third of the immigrants to Pennsylvania were indentured servants.

CHAPTER II Free Labor 1. Introduction 

In colonial times, there was little opportunity for a free laborer to engage in any one occupation. A colonial laborer thought no more of changing his occupation than of changing his coat. Joshua Hempstead, of New London, Connecticut, was at one time or another a cabinetmaker, a coffin maker, a housebuilder, a shipwright, a shoemaker, and a tombstone engraver. A Massachusetts laborer worked for about a dollar a day from 1697 to 1711.

During that time he farmed his little plot of ground, traded in livestock, burned bricks, worked as a carpenter and painter, carried messages, and made laths from split saplings in the wintertime. There was little free labor for hire in the colonies. At least four-fifths of the free white men worked on their own land, depending upon their families for help.

The first artisans who came to 

America were not highly skilled workers. The specialized workers in England were making a living at home, and hesitated to risk their chances in a new country where the market for their work was small.

The colonial population contained a great many farmers who had little money for anything except necessities. Another thing that kept the most skilled workers at home was the fact that English merchants were slow to risk their money in the colonies; then, too, the merchants did not want to build up any business in America that would injure their own business in England. A few of the bolder ones sent skilled. workers to America to establish certain industries, but these ventures were too often disappointing.

In spite of these difficulties, political and religious refugees, adventurers and dreamers continued to come into the colonies. Among the religious refugees were the Huguenots, who brought a love of beauty and fine workmanship to this country. By 1700, in some towns, as high as one-tenth of the population consisted of free laborers who worked for shopmasters.

It was hard for the few craftsmen of the villages and towns to find work, at first, because master artisans employed young boys who wanted to learn a trade. In small shops, the masters worked with their apprentices and journeymen, but in the larger ones the masters acted as managers and salesmen. 

The relations between the owners of business and the workers were close and friendly. Among the craftsmen were three distinct classes: The apprentice who was learning a trade, (4)

A list of the founders of Newbury, Massachusetts (1635), shows some of the types of free labor that went into the making of a town. 

Among the ninety-one founders were two clergymen, eight "gentlemen," two or three merchants, one doctor, one brewer, one schoolteacher, one shipmate, one sea captain, one glovemaker, one dyer, seven or eight shoemakers, three or four tanners, one saddler, two linen weavers, two locksmiths, one cooper, two wheelwrights, and two carpenters. Only a few of the remaining ones were yeomen (small farmers); the others were servants.

In such towns as Boston and Philadelphia, there were haberdashers, hatmakers, cardmakers, clothiers, tailors, saltmakers, ditch diggers, leather dressers, bricklayers, soapboilers, glaziers, workers in brass, sailmakers, plasterers, and winemakers. In New England settlements (before 1700) most of the people belonged to the middle class; they were farmers, artisans, and fishermen, with a considerable number of servants in shops and homes. 

The industries of the colonies were largely a result of natural resources and geography. Farming could not become a main industry in New England because the soil was not very fertile, and the winters were too long and cold. Lumbering became an important industry because of the great forests and many short swift streams to furnish water power for sawmills. The long coastline, with its many harbors, and abundance of lumber, caused the people to build ships and to go. into the shipping trade. The abundance of fish in the ocean — cod, mackerel, and other fine fish turned the New Englanders to fishing.

In the middle colonies, as in New England, the people worked at many things. They had two very fine harbors the Delaware and the 

Chesapeake Bays. The soil was suited to grain crops, and the great rivers could carry the crops to the seaboard markets. The Mohawk and Hudson River valleys made the fine harbor of New York especially important as a commercial port.

Great forests provided excellent lumber for shipbuilding. Out of these forests came the fine furs that made fur trading an important industry. Under the soil lay deposits of useful minerals, and along the coast were valuable fishing grounds. 

The middle colonies seemed to have unlimited natural riches.

In the colonies south of the Chesapeake Bay, the fertile soil and fine climate encouraged the settlers to produce great staple crops such as tobacco, rice, and indigo. After the invention of the cotton gin in 1793, they produced great quantities of cotton. The London Company tried to establish various industries in 

Virginia, but, for one reason and

Poles were sent to Virginia to make pitch, tar, turpentine and potash.

Dutchmen were sent to build sawmills, Frenchmen to make wine and silk, and Italians to make wine.

Social Classes of Free Labor: A 

few of the immigrants who came to America were from high social ranks in European countries; some were connected with the nobility. A few 

English squires went to the colonies, but they were usually high officials, or owners of vast tracts of land. 

Below the large landholders were yeomen, who had been farmers and tradesmen in their native lands.

They were a law-abiding, religious class of people in search of better living conditions for themselves and their children. The more ambitious and hard-working farmers and craftsmen rose to higher social rank, and formed the very core of our American middle class.

Next to the farmers and the tradesmen were the tenants of peasant stock, who came from England or the Continent. They were inferior in position and education, and sometimes inferior in intelligence. Some had been persuaded to come to America by highly-colored stories of wealth in America. Some had come alone. Some had come with groups, following a religious leader.

At the bottom of the social scale were the servants and apprentices, who formed a rather rough element.

They fought, drank too much, swore constantly, and were all too often given to immoral habits. These rougher folk made up about onefourth of the whole society in New England in the Seventeenth Century. In the Eighteenth Century, they probably made up a much higher proportion in some of the colonies.

Single Women and Children: 

Unmarried women and children did a large amount of work in colonial homes. There is no way to separate what they did from what has been classed as the work of wives. The women in colonial days had a heavy burden to carry. Among other things, they were cooks, seamstresses, gardeners, nurses, housekeepers, and manufacturers of clothing. Outside the home, women worked in handicraft industries. In 

1666, women laborers were paid from twenty to twenty-five dollars a year. Women of good reputation were permitted to engage in many kinds of business. The most common occupation for widows was the keeping of a tavern, where "strong 

Drinck" was sold. Almost as many women as men kept taverns in Boston during the first part of the 

Eighteenth Century.

Artisans: Travelling artisans were called "itinerants" because they wandered from one town to another, or from one farm to another in search of work. On the farms, they worked up the raw materials for the farmers. For instance, a wan-

Common mechanics, like these in 1630, earned about twenty-four cents a day with board. Stonemasons and carpenters earned about forty-eight cents a day during the warm months. These wandering workers had no homes and no property-no votes and no influence in public affairs.

The shopkeepers had their own shops and made up their own materials into articles ordered by their customers. Some of these shopkeepers had to compete with "itinerants." Little money was required for the tools and materials for a shoe shop; therefore, competition in shoemaking was keen. The travelling shoemaker could carry his tools with him wherever he went. The making of iron products, on the other hand, required expensive and heavy machinery that could not be moved; consequently, competition among ironworkers amounted to little.

How the Towns Helped Indus try: It was the practice of town governments to give help to certain kinds of industry which required skilled labor. They offered sums of money, grants of land, and various other rewards to get skilled laborers to settle among them and start a needed industry. In that way, they secured things they needed with much less trouble and expense than when they imported them from

England. After the industry was well started, it was very often left to shift for itself. The town sometimes promised that they would allow no other person to engage in that industry for a certain number of years. of years. Saltmakers and ironworkers were frequently promised such protection from competition. 

Wages: Skilled workers could set their own prices, to a certain extent. There was no need for labor unions, for the shortage of skilled workers kept wages high. If a laborer could not earn high wages, he bought cheap land and went to farming. At one time in the Seventeenth Century, it was so hard to get skilled workers that a law was passed which forbade any sawyer or woodworker to farm. Another law was passed forbidding brickmakers and makers and other craftsmen to plant grain, thus indirectly forcing them to work at their trades. Massachusetts once passed a law which compelled craftsmen to work in the fields; at that time there was a shortage of farm labor. Even soldiers were required to work in the fields at harvest times, if they were needed. The position of labor depended upon the need of the community.

Colonial Labor 

By the end of the colonial period of American history, a wage-earning class was growing up in the larger towns. Some shopkeepers had more business than they could handle alone, and were forced to employ journeymen to help them. In the larger shops, the owner looked after the sales and supervised the work; in smaller shops, he worked with his apprentices and journeymen, in addition to selling and managing.

Increased wage costs were simply passed on to the persons who bought finished goods. There was no objection from the buyers, because more goods were needed than could be made. The workers, of course, coöperated with the masters to keep wages high.

It is very hard to compare colonial wages with modern wages, as there is so much difference between what a shilling (24c) would buy then and what it will buy now. The standards of living are very different, for many of our necessities had not been invented in colonial times. 

About all we can do is to compare colonial wages with those of England, to find out how much money the worker received, and to see what his money would buy. 

In comparison with English wages, colonial wages were very high. In Massachusetts in 1633, the highest wage paid to a common laborer was two shillings (48c) a day without board, or fourteeen pence (about 28c) with board, the hours beginning at sunrise and ending at sunset. In one day a colonial laborer could earn as much as an English plowman earned in one week; but the cost of living was higher in America than in England. 

If it cost twenty cents a day for food for one man, a wage of fortyeight cents would not go far toward the support of a family. 

A maximum wage was set in some of the colonies. In 1635, some men who accepted more than forty-eight cents a day for labor were fined by the Massachusetts Court. This enforcement of the wage act, however, caused laborers to remove to other colonies or to quit their trades. The law was repealed, and each separate town set its own wage rates. Naturally, the laborers drifted to the towns that paid the higher wages. 

In 1660, Connecticut set the wages of farm workers at fortyeight cents a day in summer when the days were long, and thirty-six cents a day in winter when the days were short. Carpenters' wages were set at forty cents and sixty cents a day. Farm wages varied from place to place, but ranged between sixty dollars a year in the middle colonies and one hundred dollars a year in the South, from 1765 to 1770.

These wages were paid to free labor at the same time that a German immigrant was selling his services for forty-five dollars a year, and indentured servants were selling their services at fifty-five dollars.

Free labor in the South was not so high at one hundred dollars, for it

Some free laborers were able to save in a lifetime a thousand dollars, which would buy as much in the 1700's as ten thousand dollars would buy in 1920. Labor Unions: Before the Revolutionary War, there were practically no strikes against employers. 

In 1677, the cartmen of New York

City refused to haul dirt for the city at six cents a load. In 1741, the 

New York bakers closed their shops in protest against a fixed price for a loaf of bread. These, however, were not strikes between labor and capital; they were strikes of labor against government. There were no labor unions, in the modern sense, before the Revolution. There were a few organized groups of master craftsmen. For instance, the Philadelphia house carpenters in 1724 formed a union and took a fifth of the wages of the journeymen whom they employed. The masters took the contract and looked after the work, but the journeymen did all the actual building. The journeymen paid for the privilege of working, just as members of a labor union today pay dues to the C. I. O. or to the A. F. of L. The difference is this: The colonial labor union was not directed against capital; it was simply a union of highlyskilled laborers taking advantage of workers less skilled than themselves. The modern labor union seeks to benefit all labor by demanding higher wages, shorter hours, and better working conditions.

In some of the large towns, in the 

Eighteenth Century, a few trade unions existed, but they were formed to assist needy members. Such trade unions paid set sums of money to their members who were ill or in distress, and took care of their poor. 

Because there were no banks then, the colonial trade union provided strong boxes for the savings of the members, and made loans to deserving members. Under the law, they were not allowed to tamper with wages or hours of labor, or in any way work against employers.

II. The Farmer 

A very great portion of colonial labor was done by farmers. A man with very little money could be a landholder, because there was so much free land. Such a man cleared a little land for a cornfield and a garden, built a rough hut with the aid of his neighbors, and trusted to his gun and fish net for meat for his family. Each year he cleared a little more land and raised a few more cattle, hogs, and sheep. By the end of the third or fourth year, he felt

Colonial Labor sure of food and shelter. If he had a large family, he was blessed, for each child increased the labor power of the family.

How He Cultivated the Soil: 

Farm work was very hard, because the colonial tools were heavy and crude. The Pilgrims cultivated their farms with mattocks and hoes for twelve years before they had even one plow. In all Virginia, there were only thirty plows in 1636 and only 

150 in 1650. Such plows as they had, took two strong men and from two to four oxen to pull one of them. Even with a plow, a farmer could cultivate only one acre of ground in a day, barely scratching the soil to a depth of only three inches. A man with a hoe could tend only four acres of corn, but with a pair of oxen and a rude plow he could tend thirty acres.

For some years, the settlers used the Indian way of planting and cultivating corn. To prepare the field, they first killed the trees. This was done by bruising the bark near the roots, and then burning the roots with fire. The next year, the burned roots were pulled out of the ground with a crooked stick. In this stirredup ground, the farmer made holes, four feet apart, with a stick; into each hole he put four grains of corn and two beans. When the corn was about half-grown, the earth was pulled up around each cornstalk into a hill. Aside from keeping the weeds pulled, no more cultivation was necessary. Crops of corn were planted in April, May, and June, and harvested in August, September, and October. Some ground yielded a hundred bushels to the acre, but the average was sixty or seventy. Often, after the ground had been thoroughly loosened by the growing corn, wheat, oats, and barley were planted. 

A Colonial Farm in New England 

Land was so plentiful that the farmer did nothing to preserve its fertility. It was easier to clear a new field than to fertilize the old one. In New England, farmers along the coast and rivers fertilized their soil by putting a fish in every corn hill. A thousand fish to an acre of ground increased the yield of corn threefold. 

Harvesting: Small grains were harvested in the simplest and hardest way, being cut with a sickle, threshed by flailing, then tossed against the wind to allow the chaff to blow away. On some farms horses or oxen were used to tread out the

11 grain, a method faster than the flail. It took five men with flails one hundred days to beat out three thousand bushels of grain; with four animals and four men, the same amount could be beaten out in ten days. A floor of good waxy earth, worn smooth and hard, was preferred, though grain was sometimes trodden out in the field. Such a floor was made from forty to one hundred thirty feet in diameter, with a track twelve or fourteen feet wide outside the circumference of the circle. The sheaves were laid on the track, and fences put up on either side. The horses were led by their halters around the track at a good steady trot, the four horses, equally distant from each other, acting as the four main arms of a wheel. Horses were preferred to oxen because they were faster.

Classes of Farmers: The well-to do farmer who owned up to a thousand acres of land was an employer of labor. He paid his free laborers about fifty cents a day (without board) for nine months; from November to February, when the days were short, he paid about thirty-five cents. A good mower could earn sixty cents a day. A farm laborer earning thirty-five cents a day found food prices high. He had to pay $1.44 for a bushel of corn, ten cents for a pound of cheese, two cents for a quart of beer, twelve cents for a pound of butter, six cents for a dozen eggs, and two cents for a quart of milk.

The largest group of the colonial farmers was made up of men who owned a hundred and fifty acres of land or less. Such a farmer depended upon his family for help, with a hired hand in busy times. 

They were less ambitious than the well-to-do farmers, content to live from day to day, without worry about the future. They worked hard and had little, but they enjoyed their independence. Servants employed by such farmers were treated as one of the family.

Below this group was a class of farmers who drifted with the frontier as it moved westward. They did the heaviest and most unskilled labor of all the farm workers. They lived in rude huts, wore the coarsest clothing, and looked as rough and untidy as Indians. They cleared the land, made a few improvements, sold out, and moved on to clear more land. They were roving "squatters," who paid no cash fees, but earned their land rent many times over by the hard work they did. A squatter's clearing often became a crossroads center that developed into a thriving town.

The farmer had to do many things besides raise his crops and tend his livestock. He had to be a hunter, a trapper, a shoemaker, a dresser and tanner of hides, a carpenter, a cabinetmaker, a painter, a stonemason and burner of bricks, a blacksmith, and a maker of nails and tools - in fact, a jack-of-alltrades.

Southern Farmers: In the South, there were very large estates, but there were also a great many small farms, worked without the help of slaves. By the beginning of the 

1700's, these small landowners had to compete with Negro labor. They were gradually pushed into the back country, or northward where conditions were less favorable. They grew food crops which they sold to the great planters of tobacco, indigo, and rice. Another group of laborers in the South were the tenant farmers. They were so poor that they could only rent a little land from a large landholder. About all they could hope for was food, shelter, and coarse clothing.

Most of the work done on Southern colonial plantations was done by slaves and indentured servants. 

However, some free labor was employed. The free laborer was usually paid in tobacco, rice, indigo, or deerskins, instead of money. The master laid out the work, rode over his farms, and looked after his business. 

The mistress trained the house servants, looked after the sick, supervised the housekeeping,¹ the spinning, the weaving, and the sewing for her family and her servants.

III. The Fur Traders 

One of the most important industries of colonial days was the fur trade. The skins of beaver, fox, mink, otter, and other fur-bearing animals found a ready market in the Old World. Of all these skins, beaver was the most desired. It took the place of money in trade with the Indians and among the whites themselves. English and 

Dutch traders exchanged cheap glass beads, worthless trinkets, knives, kettles, shirts, combs, hats, coats, rum, axes, guns, ammunition, hoes, cloth, and thread for furs.

V. Workers in the Forest 

Lumbering: America's great forests provided work for a great many people. A few colonists gave all their time to lumbering, but most of it was done by farmers during the winter months. The Northern colonists, especially, worked at lumbering; they were anxious to add to their small incomes from their stony farms.

At first, all the work was done by hand with axes and saws. As the demand for labor increased, some sort of machinery became necessary to speed up the work of the laborers. 

An early sawmill consisted of but one vertical saw that was pulled downward by a water wheel and pulled up again by a long limber pole. Such a mill, run by one man and a boy, could cut one thousand feet of lumber in one day. 

At the same time that Englishmen were running sawmills in New 

England by horse power and water power, the Dutch were running mills in New York with windmills, with water power, and with a combination of wind and water power.

By 1700, some of these mills were running as high as twelve saws. It was cheaper to add saws than to build more mills, for ordinary mills cost from five hundred to a thousands dollars to build. Most of these mills were located near the coast. 

By the time of the Revolution, the colonists had cut the timber so closely along the coast that they were forced to cut timber in the back country and float it down the rivers and creeks to the mills. 

Lumbering gave employment to shipbuilders, coopers, house carpenters, and cabinetmakers. The making of potash or "black salts" from wood ashes became an industry that employed a great many workers. In making potash, hot water was poured over the ashes in the ash hopper to extract the lye. The liquid lye was then set to boil in huge iron kettles until it was all evaporated, leaving only the black crystals in the kettles. These "black salts" were used in soapmaking, glassmaking, and in scouring woolen cloth.

Naval Stores: Naval stores include tar, pitch, turpentine, flax, hemp, and cordage. Flax was used in the

Colonial Labor manufacture of duck for boat sails, and hemp was used in ropemaking.

Tar, pitch, and turpentine were made from pine. These naval stores were important to the mother country as well as to our own. In the 

North, shipbuilding used up practically all the naval stores at home, but in the South there was a surplus to send to England. This industry grew by leaps and bounds. 

Between 1701 and 1718, the export to England of tar, alone, increased from 177 barrels to 82,084; more than one-third of the latter amount was sent from South Carolina. 

Shipbuilding: In every colony some ships were built, but the greater part of them were built in New 

England and the middle colonies. 

Between 1700 and 1735, New England sold ships to all the Atlantic countries. European countries could buy American ships cheaper than they could build them at home, and they were better ships, too. Labor in America was more expensive than in England, but materials were much cheaper. During the colonial period a ship could be built for thirty-four dollars a ton in America, while in England it cost fifty dollars; to build an average ship of one hundred tons, cost thirty-four hundred dollars in America and five thousand dollars in England. 

Ship carpenters received about sixty cents a day for their labor. 

Every year the New Englanders built twice as many ships as all the other colonies put together; but they were by no means the only good shipbuilders. The Dutch were great shipbuilders. New York and 

Pennsylvania turned out excellent ships. 

Shipbuilding created wealth and stimulated other industries. Ships required cut and sawed timber, nails, anchors, and other iron parts, ropes, and sails. Ropemaking gave employment not only to ropemakers, but to growers and manufacturers of hemp; sailmaking gave employment to flax growers and manufacturers. Sailors had to have food and clothing, and that gave employment to producers of food and clothing, the farmers supplying the raw materials. 

VI. Metalworkers

During the colonial period, mining never became an important industry. Only lead, copper, and iron were mined. Lead was not mined to any great extent before the 

Revolution; it was easy to get lead from the great lead mines of Missouri by means of trade with the 

Indians. Copper was not necessary to any important branch of colonial manufacturing, but iron was much needed.

The iron industry was slow to get on a paying basis. It was hard to raise money to set up the business, and the poor roads made it hard to market the manufactured iron. On the other hand, there was a demand for iron for farm tools and housekeeping utensils. The colonists had

Free Labor 

17 very little money and could not buy as many of the English-made articles of iron as they needed. It was cheaper and more convenient to buy 

American iron, and farm produce or furs could be traded for it. 

There was iron ore in practically all the colonies, and everywhere there was an abundance of wood from which to make charcoal for smelting the ore. There were two kinds of iron ore-bog ore and rock ore. The bog ore was not as good as the rock ore, but it was plentiful and easy to get. It was collected from swamps and ponds with the simplest dredging tools. It was easy to work, and made very good cast iron from which to fashion farm tools and hollowware for cooking. 

The rock ore was good for making wrought iron. It was found principally in New York, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, farther inland and in higher ground than the bog ore, which was found nearer the coast.

The first attempt to manufacture iron in America was made near 

Jamestown, Virginia, as early as 

1620. A great deal of English money was invested in it, and one hundred fifty workers were sent over from 

England to run the mine and furnace. It had barely started running when the plant and the work ers were wiped out by an Indian massacre in 1622. Nothing more was done about iron mining in Virginia for about a hundred years.

The Virginians were too busy raising tobacco to bother with iron mines; they simply traded their tobacco for English iron. 

The first ironworks in New England was set up at Lynn, Massachusetts. It brought a great deal of worry and loss to the men who undertook the business. Scarcity of money and labor made it very hard to make a profit. The manufacturers asked the Massachusetts General

Court for help. The Court admitted that the ironwork was important to the colony, but what good, said they, is cheaper iron if we have no money to buy it? An axe was not cheap at twenty-four cents, if a man had not the twenty-four cents to pay for it. 

Ironworks required both skilled and unskilled labor. An ordinary furnace employed about nine men, besides woodcutters, charcoal makers, carters, and other common laborers. The making of the charcoal alone required many laborers. It took a cord and a half of wood to make eighty bushels or one load of charcoal, and it required one hundred twenty bushels of charcoal to smelt a ton of pig iron. An ordinary furnace turned out seven or eight tons of iron a week, using from 840 to 960 bushels of charcoal every week. At the furnace a load of charcoal brought $3.50, though a few furnaces paid a dollar more.

An acre of well-wooded land would yield about sixteen hundred bushels of charcoal. Six men could make

Colonial Labor sixteen thousand bushels of charcoal in three months. 

In spite of early losses, ironworks had grown tremendously by the end of the Eighteenth Century. In 

Massachusetts, two counties operated fourteen blast furnaces and six air furnaces, twenty forges, seven rolling and slitting mills, and a number of triphammers. Besides these, there were numerous blacksmith shops and nail-making shops. 

The yearly output of the furnaces, by 1800, was from fifteen to eighteen hundred tons of ironware; the yearly output of the rolling and slitting mills was fifteen hundred tons of bar iron.

At the slitting mills, iron bars were heated white and rolled into flat plates for the making of tools and nails. For nail making, these plates were slit into long slender rods. Farmers bought these cut rods for their own use. Every farmer had his own little fireside forge, where he made nails on bad days and in long winter evenings. He exchanged his surplus nails for more iron. 

Blacksmiths made wrought iron and fashioned a great many things needed by farmers and townspeople. 

For working the iron ore the smith charged twelve cents a pound. With the help of one hand, he could work up one hundred pounds in a day. 

Some of the blacksmith shops had great triphammers run by water wheels to hammer the heated iron ore into wrought iron.

Early Factories: Related to the ironworks were other branches of the iron and steel industry. Cut and hammered nails, spades and shovels, saws and scythes, metal buttons, card teeth, wire, sheet iron for tinware, guns, cannon balls, edge tools, etc., were made in great quantities for sale. Small factories for these wares sprang up after the iron industry was well established. Leicester, Massachusetts, had a triphammer and a factory for cotton and wool cards after the Revolutionary War. 

The card factory employed fifteen or twenty men, besides the children and women who were employed in their own homes to set the wire teeth in the leather covering of the cards. Pans of card teeth were found in nearly every home. This factory turned out from twelve to fifteen thousand pairs of cards every year.

VII. Clothmakers 

The business of cloth manufacturing arose to satisfy a need. The colonists were expected to buy their cloth from the mother country, but only the well-to-do could afford it. 

The price of cloth was high, calicoes selling at fifty cents a yard. It was

Colonial Labor difficult and expensive to get cloth from the seaport towns to the country and the inland villages. In order to clothe themselves, the poorer colonists made their own cloth in their own home.

The principal raw materials used at first were flax and wool, both scarce and costly. By 1643, the 

New England colonists were making dimities, fustians, linens, and woolen cloth. The women of the middle colonies, as well as those of the Northern colonies, industriously spun their own flax and wool, and some cotton imported from Barbados and the British West Indies.

Wool from Spain was used in addition to home-grown wool before sheep raising was well established. 

England, for a long time, allowed no wool or sheep to be shipped to the colonies, because she wanted to force the colonists to buy English woolens.

By the end of the Seventeenth Century, much flax was grown in the colonies, and nearly every Northern farmhouse became a busy factory for clothmaking. The flax was first cut and laid in ponds or dewy meadows to rot; it was then washed and dried; next, the stalks were opened and the fibers separated from the stalk by pulling them through a set of coarse iron teeth fixed firmly in a board; the next step was to pull the fibers through a set of finer teeth. The tow was then separated according to fineness--the finest for fine linens, the coarsest for bags, the medium for ordinary clothing and household use. The fibers were then spun into yarn, soaked and scoured in a lye solution, and woven in a partly homemade frame. The linen was brown in color. It was bleached by soaking it in sour milk, and by numerous washings and bleachings on the grass on sunshiny days. The flaxen yarn woven with woolen yarn made a cloth called linsey-woolsey; when woven with cotton, the cloth was called dimity or fustian. 

The making of woolen cloth was equally tedious and difficult. The wool was washed, carded, and made into rolls-all by hand. The rolls were spun into thread, dyed, and woven into cloth or knitted into stockings and mittens. After the cloth was woven it had to be "fulled." The cloth was washed in warm soapsuds to shrink and clean it, and was then pounded with wooden mallets to straighten it. 

This pounding of the wet cloth was sometimes done by men with their bare feet. After the washing and pounding, the nap was sheared and the cloth steamed and pressed. After fulling mills were set up, this scouring, pounding, and pressing was done with machinery run by horse power or water power. A great deal of woolen cloth was worn without fulling or finishing of any kind. The fuller charged forty or fifty cents a yard for fulling cloth.

In one of the New England spinning schools an apparatus was set up for working and twisting the yarn ready for the looms. With the aid of this device, two boys could work and twist enough yarn to keep fifty looms at work. The school had looms for weaving, hot and cold presses for finishing the cloth, and a complete dyehouse for dyeing the cloth.

VIII. Glassmaking 

A glass factory was built at 

Jamestown, Virginia, as early as 1609. Like the ironworks, it was destroyed in the Indian massacre of 

1622. Glass was needed in Amer-

Colonial Labor ica, especially glass beads and trinkets for the Indian trade and glass bottles for home use. To import glass from England was expensive on account of shipping charges and breakage. The materials at hand for glassmaking were plentiful, but labor was expensive and hard to find. Skilled glassmakers had to be brought from foreign countries at great cost, and it was hard to keep them at their trade after they got here. Most of the glassworkers had been journeymen in European facturies, and a few had been foremen.

Many of them deserted their trade, after a time, to become landowners and farmers. If one or two key men in a group of glassmakers deserted for farming, the whole undertaking was thrown out of gear; sometimes the rest of the laborers became discouraged and quit. A master of a glass factory in New York in the 1760's became bankrupt because his laborers would not stay with him.

Apart from the difficulty of getting workers, there was the problem of getting money to build and operate glassworks. Some glasshouses cost as much as thirty thousand dollars to build. Money was scarce in the colonies, and most of the money for the glassmaking business had to come from Europe, just the same as labor. 

The two most important glassworks of colonial times were set up in the middle of the Eighteenth 

Century. One was the factory of 

Caspar Wistar, in New Jersey. 

Wistar brought glassmakers from

Holland to America with the understanding that they should teach their art to no one but himself and his son, Richard. By good management, the Wistars made a fortune. 

They made bottles and, at first, five sizes of window glass, but later they made beautiful tableware. 

The other important glass factory, and the largest one, was that of Baron Henry Stiegel. His melting house was so large that a coachand-four could turn around in it. 

Stiegel was a proud German who lived very extravagantly. He had an annual income of thirteen thousand dollars from his glasshouse, but he spent too much money in making a show. He lived in a castle with cannon mounted on it. Whenever he came home from a trip, the cannon roared a salute. When company came, the cannon called the glassworkers and ironworkers on the estate to come to the castle to serve and entertain the guests; some of workers were very fine musicians. 

When the Revolution came on, and the baron could no longer get funds from Germany, he failed in business. 

At the same time, a smaller glasshouse in Delaware made comfortable fortunes for three generations of German craftsmen.

Occupations 

Leatherworking: The manufacture of leather in colonial times was done principally in the homes. 

Every colony had an abundance of hides and plenty of oak or hemlock bark with which to tan them. Out of this leather, workers made saddles and harness. In the line of clothing they made coats, vests, breeches, skirts, aprons and stockings. A currier (a leather dresser) charged eighty cents to dress a hide, but that was not all profit, for he had to pay forty cents for a gallon of oil. Tanners bought hides at seven cents a pound and sold them at twenty-four cents. That was not such a large profit, when one considers the amount of hard work required to change a raw hide into finished leather. Paper: Paper was made from clean rags which were cut up on knives or ground between stones in a gristmill. Outside of the grinding, all the work was done by hand, making the manufacture of paper

1. Indentured Servants 

Indentured servitude was a form of slavery. Willingly, or unwillingly, a laborer sold himself into bondage for a certain number of years. Usually, an indentured servant was one who sold his services for transportation from the Old 

World to the New.

The cost of one passage across the 

Atlantic Ocean ranged from twentyfive to forty dollars. This sum had to be paid to the agent or ship's captain by the purchaser of the servant. The master who bought such servants expected from two to seven years of labor from them. The average term was about four years.

In New England it was about seven years. Those who had bound themselves to sea captains for their passage were usually advertised for sale at auction. They were sold to the highest bidder, just as any other kind of goods. Buyers of human flesh, called "soul drivers," often bought them for trading purposes. 

Sometimes these "soul drivers" bought a whole shipload of servants and drove them like cattle through the country until the last one was sold.

The earliest indentured servants came from Great Britain, consisting of English, Welsh, Irish, and Scotch immigrants. The Germans began to come in great numbers after 1730. 

High-pressure advertisement of the 

New World secured a great many free laborers as well as indentured laborers. Regular agents called "crimps" were employed by companies to persuade persons to go out to the colonies. Agents who went through Scotland to gather up emigants were called "drums.” Kidnappers were called "spirits," because they "spirited away" young and unwilling laborers. Even after laws were passed against such methods, as many as ten thousand persons annually were "spirited away" to be shipped to the colonies. The illegal trade went on because it was highly profitable. 

On the other hand, emigrants sometimes tricked the shipmasters in order to get food, clothing, and transportation. After they had received such benefits, they claimed to have been taken on board the ship against their own free will. 

They then demanded freedom from their contracts with the captains.

This led to the passage of a law to allow no one to board a ship bound for the colonies, unless he registered himself as willing to go. 

The contract between the master and the servant called for the payment of the passage money, "Meat, Drink, Apparel and Lodging, with (27)

Colonial Labor other necessaries, during the said 

Term." At the end of the time of service (indenture), the servant was to be paid "freedom dues." These dues consisted of a year's supply of corn, fifty acres of land, tools for clearing the land, weapons and ammunition, and two suits of clothes. However, the amount of the dues varied with times and communities. In New England, less liberal rewards were promised. On account of the "freedom dues," an indentured servant was paid less for his work than a free laborer. 

In some respects, indentured servants fared worse than black slaves. The slaves were assured of food, clothing, and shelter as long as they lived. Indentured servants were often worked hard for a few years and abandoned with broken health. An old or sick servant had no one to take care of him after his term of service was over. He was cast aside like an old shoe.

The very nature of such a system of labor caused the masters to exact all they could from these servants during their term of indenture. The master profited by working them hard and by spending as little as possible for their support. The term of service ended at the end of seven years or less. All masters were not so heartless as to exact the most labor for the least comfort provided, but the system tended to encourage that practice.

Hardships of the Voyage: Severe hardships from overcrowding, lack of proper food, and unsanitary living conditions made the trip across the ocean one of great misery and distress. Disease and death lurked in every crowded emigrant ship.

Many of the passengers died on the way, and others arrived in America too sick and weak to care what happened. The loss of emigrants at sea was always large. The passengers were packed like "herring in a box," with scarcely room. enough to lie down. Rotten meat and bad beer, and small portions at that, caused disease and suffering. Besides those buried at sea, a great many immigrants were buried ashore. In three months 253 immigrants were buried by five sea captains. One ship landed only 21 passengers out of a passenger-list of 341. 

Naturally, when men were hungry and desperate, there was thieving and quarreling. Hard-hearted captains delighted in punishing such offenders by unusual and inhuman methods of torture. One poor wretch who stole a bottle of rum was strung up to the yardarms of the ship with a two-hundred-pound weight tied to each foot. Only the outraged cries of "shame! shame!" from the other passengers persuaded the captain to stop the fiendish procedure.

II. Apprentices 

One class of indentured servants were apprentices. In Pennsylvania and the Southern colonies, there was no difference between the indentured servant and the apprentice, except that of age. Apprentices were usually young boys "bound" for seven or eight years to learn a trade. There is a New England record of a blacksmith's apprentice being bound for nine years at a price of twelve pounds sterling. At the end of his service, he was to receive two suits of clothes and five shillings. Apprentices signed agreements not to loiter in taverns or alehouses, go to plays, play unlawful games, or carry on love affairs.

Both freemen and indentured servants could be apprenticed to some artisan or farmer for five, six, or even eight years. Even Negro slaves could be apprenticed. Thus the English system of learning a trade was passed into the American system of labor. Masters had little money with which to hire labor, but they could give apprentices food, clothing, shelter, a little education, and training in a trade. The apprentice system was more than a labor system. It was a way of giving poor boys and girls an education, a trade, and training in morals. 

Labor was simply exchanged for technical training. The shop was the colonial trade school; the farm was the colonial agricultural school. 

Curious provisions for apprentices add interest to the old contracts between master and apprentice. A New England cooper's apprentice was to be set free at the end of seven years, at the age of twenty-one. In the meantime, the master must teach the apprentice the cooper's trade and "to write and read English, and to cast accounts."

At the same time, the cooper must do his best to get the boy's "scurf head" cured. At the end of the apprenticeship, the boy was to receive two suits of clothes, a musket, sword and bandoliers, and twenty shillings.

Girls could pass through an apprenticeship in domestic service.

They were taught knitting, sewing, spinning, "housewifery," and "such like exercises" as were thought “fitting and becoming" to their sex. At the close of their terms, they were given two sets of clothing, one for 

Sunday, one for weekdays. That meant, among other things, "two pairs of hose and shoes, two hoods or hats, or such headgear as may be comely and convenient, with all necessary linen." One master gave a girl a cow and one set of garments.

On the other hand, there were those who gave their apprentices such excellent training that they were able to make fortunes for themselves after gaining their freedom. 

An interesting example of the master-apprentice relationship can be seen in the experience of Benjamin Franklin. When he was about twelve years old, his father took him around to different craftsmen of Boston in order that he might choose a trade. Benjamin wanted to be a sailor, and none of the trades interested him, particularly. Finally, his father apprenticed him to a relative to learn the cutler's art. The relative demanded a hundred dollars as a premium for training the boy. The sum could not be raised, and Benjamin went back to his father's shop to make candles. A little later he was apprenticed to his brother, James, a Boston printer. 

The contract called for nine years of service. During this time, Benjamin was to keep his brother's trade secrets, obey all lawful commands, and work diligently.

After some years of quarreling with his harsh and domineering brother, young Franklin left Boston (1723) and made his way to Philadelphia. His apprenticeship had ended at the age of seventeen instead of twenty-one, as the indenture required. However, Benjamin had learned to use his mind, to print, to write, and to match his wits with colonial officials who did not believe in a free press.

In later years, Franklin was employed by a printer named Keimer. 

He soon discovered that Keimer was using him to train the indentured servants in the shop. He knew that when the servants were trained, he would be out of a job. For that reason, Franklin quit Keimer and went into business for himself. 

III. "Free-willers" and "Redemptioners” "Free-willers" were known to some colonists as persons convicted of

Misled into believing that they could immediately find work upon arriving in America, they usually had a sad awakening when they got here. Without money they could not go in search of work; they were forced to sell themselves in order to pay for their passage before they were allowed to land. Generally, they were put into the hands of agents who classified them roughly, according to their qualifications, and sold them to planters or other employers. 

Other "free-willers" were known as "redemptioners." They bargained with captains or merchants for a chance to find an employer in the colonies who would pay for their transportation. Some came to America expecting friends or relatives to pay the expenses of the trip. If no one came to pay for him, the laborer had no choice but to sell himself for a number of years to anyone who would buy him. Old folk were often "redeemed" by their children. The young folk served for both themselves and their parents. "Redemptioners" were so called because they "redeemed" their passage money by serving the one who paid the shipmaster. 

Some of our nation's leaders sprang from families founded by "redemptioners." Charles Thomson, the clerk of the Continental Congress during the Revolution, was a "redemptioner," as also was George Taylor. 

Taylor was an Irish lad who ran away from home with only a shilling in his pocket. He went off to

It was his job to shovel charcoal and ore into the furnace. His education and pleasant manners attracted his employer, who asked him if he could wield a pen better than a shovel. The master put the boy in responsible positions and trained him to know the business thoroughly. After the master died, Taylor stayed on. Eventually, he married the widow and became the proprietor of the ironworks. He accumulated a fortune, and had a distinguished political career Pennsylvania. 

Such stories as Taylor's could be duplicated over and over. Young adventuresome boys heard glowing tales of the opportunities in the 

New World, and sold themselves into servitude in order to reach this land of promise. Through hardship and suffering, they worked for their freedom. Their gifts of mind and body were of great value to their adopted country.

IV. Convicts 

Servitude for crime is an old, old law. In the book of Exodus it is written that if a thief "have nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft." 

As early as 1516, Sir Thomas More, of England, wrote that labor should be substituted for the death penalty in the punishment of theft.

The English monarch, Edward VI 

(1547-53) passed a law providing that anyone who had been idle for three days should be branded with the letter "V" (vagabond), for idleness was thought to be the mother of crime. A vagabond might become the slave of the man who took him up; he could be forced to work by beating, chaining, or other methods equally harsh. While this law was never strictly enforced, it does show what the English thought about labor before the colonization of America. By the Seventeenth 

Century, these severe ideas about servants had been somewhat softened, but they remained hard enough. 

Thousands of convicts were shipped off to America because they chose to be sold into servitude rather than to be imprisoned, or to suffer something worse at home. 

They hoped to make a new start in a new country, after their time of service was out. Many of these convicts were sent to America purposely to relieve overcrowding in English prisons, and to rid the mother country of undesirable characters.

A large number of these convicts were of the one-offense type, and were really desirable laborers. The woman who stole a piece of meat for her hungry children was not necessarily undesirable as a laborer. 

She was subject to the death penalty for her crime, because the meat had been worth more than a shilling. In cases of this kind, English judges found it easier to send the criminals to the colonies than to condemn them to death. A great many of the convict laborers were those who had been imprisoned for debt; they were not hardened criminals. On the whole, the colonies profited by receiving the criminals, for there were more good than bad ones.

Nevertheless, many of these immigrants were habitual criminals of the worst type. A few colonies protested against receiving them, especially New England. Strict laws. were passed to prevent their com-

Colonial Labor ing; consequently, few criminals went to New England. A considerable number of the convicts returned to England, if they survived their terms of service. Some of them committed crimes, and were sent to 

America a second time.

After a convict had served his time, he was free to sell himself as a servant, get a job as a free laborer, become an apprentice, or go to the frontier, where he could farm, hunt, or trap for fur. Far too many of the freed convicts became wandering robbers, highwaymen, murderers, counterfeiters, or horse thieves.

Some of them had social diseases, jail fever, or unclean habits; they were crude, profane, and generally ignorant. Some of the more cunning ones proved very troublesome.

The terms of service for convicts were usually longer than those for law-abiding laborers. These “King's prisoners," or "transports," served from seven years to a lifetime. 

Over forty thousand of them were sent to Virginia, Pennsylvania, and 

Maryland between 1717 and 1775.

In hard times in England, the struggling poor would commit small thefts in order to be sent to the colonies. All "transports" were shipped on vessels which were paid by the English government at the rate of fifteen to twenty-five dollars per convict.

The supply of criminals and political offenders for servants was considerably less than that of "freewillers" or "redemptioners." However, enough convicts were received in the colonies to create a social problem. As late as 1753, Benjamin Franklin warned Pennsylvania against German criminals.

He said that shipowners were sweeping the German jails to make up their quota of passengers.

There was a difference of opinion about the transportation of European convicts to America. Some thought it a wise and humane thing to do. Some said that in the New

World there would be fewer temptations to go wrong; besides, the colonists needed the labor. Some said that the criminals would be sorry for their past lives, and become industrious and respectable in new surroundings. Still others wanted to give the convicts free land. After they were well established, they could pay an annual quit-rent of 112 pounds of hemp for each one hundred acres of land. By this scheme the English Navy could be supplied with hemp. The convicts would marry young and have families, thereby increasing the demand for England's goods. This plan, however, was never carried out. 

On the other hand, Sir Francis

Bacon was against the transportation of convicts to the colonies. “It is a shameful and unblessed thing to take the scum of people and wicked condemned men, to be the people with whom you plant," he wrote. Bacon thought the convicts would continue to be lazy and wicked, and would dishonor and

The feeling against England became so strong that she was called "Undutiful Mother." Convicts endangered life and property and lowered private and public morals, the colonists said. Benjamin Franklin wrote: "Their emptying their jails into our settlements, is an insult and contempt, the cruellest, that ever one people offered to another." Home Supply of Convict Labor: 

Convict labor was supplied not only from Europe, but from sources at home, as well. Criminals within the colonies were punished by being sold into bondage. They often worked with Negro slaves at the hardest and lowliest tasks. Criminals in towns were often sold into the country, as a means of removing them from the temptations of the town. Another reason for selling the labor of the criminals was that there were few jails in the colonies. Even the few were unfit for use and were poorly constructed. Again, by selling the labor of the criminals, the towns saved the expense of taking care of the offenders while serving out their sentences. The prisons were such miserable places that prisoners usually preferred to be sold into temporary servitude. 

A Pennsylvania county court in the early Eighteenth Century sentenced a man, who stole forty shillings' worth of deerskins, to eight years of bound labor. The owner of the deerskins was to have three times their value, and the costs of the trial were paid from the sale of the convict. Men who forged papers or made counterfeit money were sold for not more than seven years, if they could not pay their fines. 

Colonial laws against stealing were modelled after the laws laid down in the Bible in the book of Exodus. 

Imprisonment for debt was very common in the colonies. Some debtors voluntarily sold themselves to a friendly creditor, before court action could be brought against them. If a debtor had children, he often sold one of his children in order that he himself might be released from prison.

CHAPTER IV 

Indian Slavery

Slavery Among the Indians: 

When the whites came to America,

Indian slavery was already well established. The Indians enslaved their captives taken in warfare.

They traded their slaves for wampum, hatchets, knives, articles of copper, or whatever they might desire. The Indians were notorious gamblers. When all other possessions had been lost, the Indian would stake his own body. If he lost, he became the slave of the winner for a year or two, perhaps a lifetime depending upon how high were the stakes for which he had played. Indian outcasts or criminals were punished by enslavement. 

In times of famine, Indian parents sometimes sold their children in order to obtain food.

Opposition to Slavery in Eng land: The African slave traffic was introduced in England by Captain

Elizabeth called the enslavement of the Africans "detestable," an action that "would call down the vengeance of Heaven." Captain Hawkins promised the Queen that he would take no more slaves, but he broke his promise. The slave traffic was continued in England. 

From the very beginning, there were those in England who opposed slavery. In 1685, a brave preacher, Morgan Godwyn, went before King

James II and preached a sermon against the inhuman slave trade. 

Godwyn was the first Englishman seriously to undertake the work of abolishing slavery. With his own eyes, he had seen the sufferings of Indian and Negro slaves in the island of Barbados. To Godwyn, the idea of selling a man into bondage, simply because his skin was red or black, was outrageous. He wrote a pamphlet called The Negroes' and 

Indians' Advocate. In it he described in vivid and convincing terms the sad plight of the slaves. 

Gradually, others took up the cause. They preached and they pleaded. They called the slave traders demons and robbers, unworthy to be called Christians. Anthony Benezet: The work of 

John Woolman was supplemented by that of Anthony Benezet. Bene zet was a well-educated man of London, who had served an apprenticeship in one of London's largest mercantile houses. His three brothers went into business in Philadelphia and made comfortable fortunes. 

Anthony could have done the same, but he felt that it was more important to do good than to make money. 

For that reason he became a school-

Benezet reasoned that his young pupils had to live with slavery; therefore, they should be taught how to deal with it. He devoted his life to the cause of the Negroes. He wrote articles for the newspapers and wrote and distributed pamphlets against slavery. His writings were read by the most prominent men of the time. George Washington read them, and Patrick Henry was definitely influenced against slavery by them. Gradually, Benezet built up a wide correspondence with antislavery workers and sympathizers in different parts of the world. He never overlooked the smallest opportunity to serve the Africans.

Through the efforts of George Whitefield, the Countess of Huntington had established a college for poor orphans near Savannah, Georgia.

Benezet was so distressed by the sight of a religious institution owning slaves that he wrote to the Countess and told her all about it. The Countess replied that she wanted no slaves on the college plantations and would put an end to such a practice. As prominent Methodist and a follower of John Wesley, she wanted to have nothing to do with slavery. 

In 1783, after the Revolution was over, the slave trade showed some signs of reviving. Anthony was so alarmed and grieved that he wrote a letter to the wife of King George

With the letter he sent a great number of pamphlets describing the horrible condition of the enslaved Africans in the English colonies. He begged the Queen to be God's instrument to relieve the sufferings of a persecuted people. 

While seeking to make easier the lot of the Negroes in different parts of the world, Benezet never neglected the Negro slaves in Pennsylvania. He established a school for

Negro school.

Equal Rights for All Men: At the same time the Quakers were preaching emancipation for the slaves, political leaders were demanding equal rights for all men. The colonies were trying to free themselves from the power of the British 

Crown. The antislavery movement became a part of the greater movement for political liberty. American

Colonial Labor statesmen recognized the fact that they ought not to demand liberty from England and at the same time deny liberty to the Negro. 

The American patriot, James Otis, contended that by nature all men were born free. "Is it right," he asked, "to enslave a man because he is black? Will short curl'd hair, like wool, instead of Christian hair help the argument? Can any logical inference in favour of slavery, be drawn from a flat nose?"

Other Groups Support the Antislavery Movement: Other religious groups joined the Quakers in the antislavery movement. Especially forceful were the Methodist preachers who travelled all through the colonies preaching against slavery

As time passed, however, the churches split on that very question. 

The Northern churches stood for freedom for the Negroes; the Southern churches stood for slavery. 

Antislavery Movement in New

England: In New England, during the Seventeenth Century, there seemed to be no general public feeling against slavery. Here and there an individual might be found who suffered a twinge of conscience over the enslavement of the Indian or the Negro. Roger Williams and 

John Eliot, and other prominent

Puritan divines sought to make the lot of the slaves easier. They instructed the slaves in the Christian religion, but they did not stress the point of giving him his freedom. 

However, it was Rhode Island, founded by Roger Williams, that passed the first law in America for the emancipation of slaves.

The first positive statement against slave holding was printed in Boston in 1700. Judge Samuel Sewall, who thought it wrong to rate Indians and Negroes with cattle and sheep, wrote a famous pamphlet called The 

Selling of Joseph. It was widely distributed, and influenced the antislavery movement throughout the colonies. 

Then followed the first debate on the slavery question. Sewall's pamphlet was answered by John 

Saffin. Sewall said, "All men are the Sons of Adam... and have equal Right unto Liberty." He pointed out that the Golden Rule ought to apply to men with colored skins just the same as to men with white skins. Do unto others as you would have them do unto you, "for this is the Law and the Prophets," 

Sewall wrote.

Saffin replied that the Indians and Negroes had been elected by God to occupy lowly stations in life. "God had set different Orders and Degrees of men in the World, both in Church and Common Weal." Saffin cited the book of Leviticus and the writings of Saint Paul to prove his point. 

Colonial New England was not convinced by Sewall's pamphlet. To most of the Puritans, the Indian and Negro slaves were but the heritage of God's elect; and the Puritans never doubted that they were the elect. Even Cotton Mather con-

Mather and most of his fellow Puritans were strong for the religious instruction of slaves, but the idea of emancipation never occurred to them. Not until the stormy pre

Massachusetts took the lead in 

Eighteenth Century antislavery legislation. In 1787, a bill was introduced in her House of Representatives "to prevent the unwarrantable and unnatural custom of enslaving mankind." A few years later, the town of Salem instructed her representatives to prevent any importation of Negroes into the state of Massachusetts. In 1776, a resolution was introduced, declaring that slavery was a direct violation of natural rights. The next year a bill to free the slaves was drafted. 

Antislavery Movement in the 

South: In the South, Arthur Lee was a leader in the antislavery movement. His argument against slavery covered four points: Slavery tended to suppress all improvements in the arts and sciences; it degraded the minds of men already free; it endangered the community, because it kept alive the smoldering discontent of the slaves; it shocked humanity and violated the teachings of Christ. 

At Darien, Georgia, resolutions against slavery were adopted on 

January 12, 1775. In spite of the need for slave labor on the plantations, the people of Darien resolved that slavery was rooted in injustice and cruelty, and was highly dangerous to the liberty of free men. As early as 1739, the inhabitants of

Darien had petitioned Governor Oglethorpe to allow no slaves to be brought into Georgia. The Georgians felt that liberty was as dear to the Negro as to themselves. They firmly believed that God would surely punish them, if they denied the Negro his freedom. 

Some of the Southern colonies passed laws against the importation of slaves, because they were afraid of the Negroes. They wished to keep the slaves they had, but wanted no new ones brought in. More slaves would only make a difficult problem even more difficult. Southerners were beginning to think that the slave trade was not good for business. A resolution was passed in 

Prince George County, Virginia, declaring that the slave trade kept freemen from settling in Virginia; that it prevented "manufacturers and other useful emigrants amongst us," and increased the debt of the colony to England. 

Introduction of Slavery a Grievance Against the King: When the colonists came to frame their complaints against King George III, they listed the introduction of slavery as one of them. Thomas Jefferson submitted the several grievances of the colonists to the Virginia Convention of 1774. He wrote that the

Colonial Labor dearest desire of the colonies was the abolition of domestic slavery, which had been "unhappily introduced in their infant state." Before the colonists could free the slaves, he said, they must stop bringing more slaves from Africa. The King had defeated every effort of the colonists to stop the slave trade.

Consequently, the colonists were "deeply wounded," because the

King preferred the immediate profits of the slave trade to the "lasting interests of the American states." 

The Virginia Convention refused to accept Jefferson's list of grievances because of that one concerning slavery. Jefferson was not discouraged; he listed it again in the first draft of the Declaration of 

Independence. King George had waged "cruel war against human nature itself"; he had violated the life and liberty of innocent persons in another hemisphere; he had caused unhappy captives to suffer and die while being carried to a strange country. These accusations of Jefferson's against King George were again struck out by the Southern members of Congress. 

Thomas Paine: Thomas Paine, who came to America while the colonists were trying to gain their freedom from England, was an ardent believer in the natural rights of man. He believed that Negroes had a perfect right to liberty, because they had never done anything to forfeit that right. Paine said that the slaves should be set free and the slaveholders punished. Certainly children of slaves should be freed, for they were completely innocent. 

First American Antislavery Society: The first antislavery society in America was formed by the people of Pennsylvania in 1775, eight years before the first antislavery society in England. Doctor Benjamin Rush and James Pemberton, a prominent Quaker, organized the society. Persons of different religious denominations made up its membership. It had scarcely begun its work when the Revolution broke out. The war, of course, checked its activity. After the war was over, the Pennsylvania society again took up its work. Its professed object was to promote "the abolition of slavery and the relief of free Negroes unlawfully held in bondage."

The society became so popular that it was made larger in 1787. Several new members were admitted, and 

Benjamin Franklin was made president of the society. 

The Antislavery Movement After the Revolution: From this time on, the question of slavery became more and more a topic of debate and a cause of bitter feeling. By 1787, most of the Northern states had passed laws providing for the future emancipation of the slaves. In the 

South, those who believed that slaves should be free were outnumbered by those who believed that slavery was a necessity. Among the

The Antislavery Movement 59 Southern states, Virginia was strongest for emancipation, but the antislavery group in that state was not a majority. However, men of character and influence belonged to that group, and young men were being added to it every year. George 

Washington, Virginia's First Citizen, favored gradual emancipation, and directed that all his slaves should be set free after his wife's death. In Maryland and North

Carolina, there were few who wished to free the slaves. South Carolina, North Carolina, and Georgia continued to bring in slaves. Georgia, begun so bravely without slaves, was now a strong slave state.

In the North there were comparatively few Negroes. They were not necessary to the economic life of that region. The economic life of the South would have been disorganized and completely upset if slavery had been discontinued. It is easy to see why the antislavery movement grew steadily in the 

North, and why it was almost smothered out in the South.

After the Revolution, the seeds of the antislavery movement, sown in the colonial period, sprang into life and began to bear fruit. This movement eventually helped to set the 

North against the South in a long and bloody struggle.

CHAPTER VII The Social Standing of Colonial Laborers 

The indentured servant was one who signed a contract to work for a certain number of years to pay a debt. Legally, he was not a slave.

Socially, he was considered little above a slave. Actually, he came under the protection of the law. 

The Penns, the Calverts, and others were glad to have indentured servants come to America to develop their lands and industries. In the 

Seventeenth Century, over half the immigrants that came to the middle colonies were "bound" servants.

The law protected them in life and limb, and gave them the right to appeal to the courts for justice.

Every servant possessed the legal right to sue his master for breaking a contract. If the master lost the case, he had to free the servant, reduce his term of service, or pay him a set sum of money at the end of his contract. William Penn declared in 

1683 that no indentured servant should be kept over his time, and that he should receive a decent reward at the end of his term.

In 1715, Maryland passed an act which declared that a master could be fined by the court, if he mistreated his servants. He must pay a penalty if he failed to provide "meat, drink, lodging, and clothing"; if he denied them necessary time for sleep; or if he beat them or gave them more than ten lashes for any one offense.

In case a servant was fined for some offense, his master was expected to pay the fine. The servant then had to work longer for his freedom. Bound servants had to pay heavier fines than did freemen or slaves. If they missed a day of labor, it was added from three- to fivefold to their term of service.

The Virginia colony passed a law which required every freed servant to carry with him a certificate showing that he had been freed; otherwise, he might be imprisoned. A servant could not marry without the consent of his master. Bound servants were not counted as citizens or voters.

No indentured servant could learn a trade or run a business without the consent of his master. Servant labor was property that could be transferred from one master to another. Governors could buy a man's servant for military service.

The labor of servants was leased, bequeathed by wills, and sold with the plantation to which the servants belonged. Their time could also be sold, willed, or hired, apart from the plantation. One servant conducted dancing schools in Williamsburg and Yorktown, and turned the fees over to his master. In the middle (60)

Puritans could not tolerate idleness or the waste of money. All inhabitants of a town, young or old, had to be engaged in an honest calling, profitable both to "themselves and the publick." Idlers were sentenced to the workhouse, where they were given ten lashes on the bare back and put to hard labor. Children of paupers were apprenticed to some responsible person in the neighborhood. Older persons, unable to support themselves, were apprenticed by the Overseers of the Poor. 

Runaways: White servants resented their state of slavery. They became restless and discontented with their situations. Every week, colonial newspapers contained advertisements of runaway servants. 

As early as 1612 returned servants in England reported Virginia as a vile place for servants. In that same year English judges were sentencing criminals to terms of labor in Virginia.

George Washington was the master of many Negro slaves, but he bought white indentured servants for use as mechanics and artisans.

He owned thousands of acres of land. A carpenter and a brickmaker would have been valuable to him for the building and repair of his houses and outbuildings.

By the end of the Seventeenth 

Century, an American labor system had been worked out. Laws had been passed for the protection of both master and servant. For each

Colonial Labor day's absence, the runaway was fined five days of extra time. Added to this was a money fine for damages to the master. Usually, this fine had to be worked out, which lengthened the term of service still more. For a fine of four or six pounds sterling, an extra year of service was not unusual. For an equal fine, runaway women had to serve twice as long. If a servant ran away a few times and was captured, his period of service might be lengthened to a lifetime.

Runaways were picked up and lodged in jail. They were then advertised in the newspapers. When the master came to claim his servant, he paid the jailer's bill, presented it to the Court, and collected it from the runaway. If a woman happened to be a pretty hard character, her master would not claim her. One jailer advertised that if such a woman were not claimed, he would be ruined by the expense of keeping her.

A servant was sentenced to the workhouse if he refused to work or serve extra time. This usually brought him around to returning to his master in a more dutiful frame of mind. The condition of the jails and workhouses in those days was so bad that, in contrast, life with the master seemed good.

Sometimes runaways travelled in companies. They wore the discarded clothing of their masters, and traded clothes around, in order to add confusion to the printed descriptions of themselves. Runaways played the parts of master and servant, mistress and maid, husband and wife in their attempts to get away. 

Women dressed in men's clothes and men dressed in women's clothes. 

One runaway was described as wearing buckskin breeches, short wide trousers, two pairs of white hose, and a well-worn broadcloth coat with metal buttons. Another wore a red greatcoat, a brown jacket, leather breeches with brass buttons and strings at the knees, good gray yarn stockings, a speckled shirt, good shoes, a hat with spots of paint on it, a light brown wig, a woolen cap, and a red silk handkerchief. 

Michael Berry, a runaway carpenter, was a "lusty well-set Fellow, full fac'd, no Hair, but wears a 

White Cap or Wig." Michael went away wearing a blue cloth coat, short homespun linsey-woolsey waistcoat, an osnabrig shirt, leather breeches, gray yarn stockings, old shoes with steel buckles, and "a good fine Hat." He carried with him a new handsaw with his master's initials "TS" (Thomas Sugar) stamped in several places on the handle. 

Some runaways were lucky enough to gain their freedom, but more often such servants were caught.

There were so many spying eyes to watch for runaways that it was almost hopeless to make an attempt to get away. If runaways were caught, they were sure to be flogged, in addition to having their terms of

Convicts were noted for trying to get away from their masters. 

Worthless runaway servants were advertised by their masters to warn other masters against them. "Six cents reward" meant that the master was glad to get rid of the servant and would not pay for the expense of his arrest or imprisonment. One master described in rhyme such a servant : He has always been a vexatious lad,

The rhyming lines went on to state that the servant had taken three quarter dollars “to pay his freight," and that he was a liar and too stubborn to weep when whipped. 

Captured runaways sometimes had their hair and eyebrows shaved off. Sometimes they were made to wear iron collars with the master's initials engraved thereon. These collars were very heavy and uncomfortable, having from two to four rivets in them. More than two rivets in the collar made it impossible for the servant to lie down to sleep. Such collars could be removed only by the aid of a blacksmith. In 

New Orleans, as late as the 1830's,

Negro slaves wore these collars as a punishment for stealing. Brutal masters treated their servants brutally. 

One of the reasons why indentured servitude disappeared was the inability of the masters to keep their servants.

Many of these servants, of course, ran away because their masters abused them. When the time of the indenture had nearly run out, the master sometimes goaded the servant into running away, for he stood to gain in two ways; if the servant ran away and was not captured, the master escaped paying the "freedom dues." If the servant were taken, his term of service was lengthened.

Certain laws were enforced against the master, however. If a servant were really mistreated and dealt with unjustly, he might be taken away from his master and sold to another who would treat him more kindly. The law upheld the rights of the servant against starvation, overwork, and harsh treatment.

There were some instances, of course, where the servant was afraid to complain.

The maid had been with them for two years and a half, and she had not risen twenty times before her mistress had been up to call her; many times the mistress had the fire lighted before the maid got out of bed. Indentured Servants in the South:

In the South, indentured servants ranked next to slaves, socially. They were never recognized by the upper classes in a social way. Even freemen who were ordinary laborers looked down upon bound servants. 

These servants had little freedom even to associate with each other. 

They could not leave home without the master's consent; they could not go into taverns; they could not buy or sell anything, for that would be a temptation to steal the master's goods.

In fact, colonial planters preferred convicts as laborers, because they had to work seven years, whereas ordinary indentured servants had to work only five. In the Eighteenth 

Century, the Southerners came to look upon all indentured servants as disorderly persons, thieves, and rascals a lazy, impudent lot hardly worth their salt. As time passed, the incoming servants became less desirable as a class. On the whole, these servants were poor laborers. It became almost impossible to sell Irish 

Roman Catholics in America, because they were feared and disliked by the Protestants. 

In Maryland, in the earliest days, great tracts of land were held by proprietors who had been given their land on the condition that they would take to America five men for each two thousand acres of land.

In addition, they were required to pay an annual quit-rent of four hundred pounds of wheat. Servants and tenants, therefore, had to work hard to enable the proprietor to pay his rent, make a living, and secure a profit. The owners of these land grants held their own courts and dealt out "justice" to their tenants and servants. This made it very hard for a servant to win a decision in his favor, regardless of how much injustice he had suffered.

In the South, workers often gave up their trades to become tenant farmers. As travelling artisans, they could not make a living. Plantations were too far apart. They were paid in country produce, which they could not carry with them, and for which they could find no good market. It was impssible to trade or sell their produce for enough to make a comfortable living.

Even children suffered from the injustice of the labor system. On the plantations in the tobacco colonies, they were often shamefully abused. Too many masters refused to release them when their time had been worked out. Too often the colonial officials worked for the interest of the masters, rather than for the servants. It must be said, however, that masters were sometimes punished for unusual cruelties to their young servants.

The Plight of Free Laborers: Ten ant farmers, travelling artisans, and wage earners lived a hard life. Unable to vote, they had no voice in the government. To save money enough to buy a farm or shop, was a slow and discouraging undertaking, even for an ambitious laborer.

Many of these workers were shiftless, lazy, and worthless. These roaming farmers fished and hunted to supplement their scanty store of grains and vegetables for the table. 

Often they were degraded in both soul and body; the best of them were as unkempt and miserable in appearance as the wild Indians of

Colonial Labor the forest. The tenant farmers were tied to the soil. It was very hard for them to rise in the world. They rarely saw any money. Their tools were so crude that they could not hope to produce enough surplus products to exchange for land of their own. Competition with slave labor in the Eighteenth Century made their lot even harder. Few of them were able to rise above the class known as "poor white trash."

Social Position of Laborers in the 

North: As early as 1723, the color line was drawn in Boston. In that year, the town meeting voted an act to restrain Indians, Negroes, and mulattoes. If such persons received visits from slaves, they were to be fined, whipped, or imprisoned. They dared not sell cake or strong drinks, nor possess guns and ammunition.

If they received property stolen by slaves they could be fined, whipped, or banished from the colony. They must not loiter on the streets nor gather in groups. If fires broke out, they must remain indoors. If an

Indian, Negro, or mulatto assaulted anyone, he was sent out of the colony. In court, his testimony was not accepted, unless supported by that of a white man. As the number of freed Negroes increased, it became more and more difficult to detect runaways. This problem plagued the Southerners, their agents, and all keepers of the law until the 

War between the States.

Indentured servants in Massachusetts could not be released from their contracts or indentures until they had given satisfaction to their masters, even though they had been unjustly treated. In the eyes of the 

Puritans, neglect of duty by a servant was inexcusable, regardless of the unfair practices of the master. 

In law, the masters were supposed to make a distinction between slaves and indentured servants. Slaves could be sold out of the colony, but bound servants could not. Captains caught transporting runaway servants were fined about two hundred and fifty dollars. 

In New England once a servant had been released from his indenture, he was free to take his rightful place in society as a freeman. His former social position was not held against him. If he had the ability and opportunity, he could rise to a respectable and influential position.
