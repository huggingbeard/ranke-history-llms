Similarly, there is a movement from the North to more southern sections, having a milder and more intermediate climate, where the rigors of winter interfere less with outside movements and where nature reduces the fuel problem by more perfectly warming the office and factory for suitable working conditions. 

Virginia meets both these conditions to an admirable degree. She is in this intermediate, southern position, with no very large centers of population, yet with many comfortable, active, well-situated towns of small to medium size. She is just at the southern edge of the great industrial and population areas of the North. She has a large variety of

resources in her own domain and ready access to outside supplies of many kinds through her remarkable transportation facilities. 

Eight continental lines of railroad, reaching out to the North, West and South, serve the State. Two-thirds of the area of the State is in trunk-line freight territory. Most of her towns are a comfortable overnight's ride to the leading metropolitan cities as far North as Boston, as far West as Cincinnati, and as far South as Atlanta.

For water transportation, the State has the Port of Hampton Roads, at the focus of these several lines of railroad. This deep-water port, with a tremendous harbor and dock and storage facilities, is touched by ship lines that range up and down the coast and to all important foreign ports. The deep indentation of rivers and bays, with deep water, affords a remarkable opportunity for combining deep-water transportation with interior rail transport.

Coal for fuel, supplemented by a possible 800,000 horsepower of water power, with well-developed electric power lines, are all within her own bounds. In addition, high tension electric lines from other States insure a stable and abundant supply of power.

The State is well watered by streams and springs. Ninety-eight per cent of the population of the State is American born. Living conditions are relatively lower in cost than in the larger industrial and population centers. Land values are low and local transportation conditions have not become complicated. 

In elevation and situation, one may choose mountain or valley or seashore. Coupled with all of these facilities is a wealth of historical sentiment and beautiful associations that add, not only to the pleasant. associations of business, but even more to living. 

Good roads link up all parts of the State. A progressive, economical

GENERAL DESCRIPTION OF THE STATE 

The State reaches from tide water, across two ranges of mountains, to touch the Cumberland plateau. In elevation, it rises from the broad, low Coastal Plain to the crest of the Blue Ridge and the Alleghenies, more than 4,000 feet high at their maximum. The western tip of 

Virginia, on the south line, is 450 miles from the Atlantic Ocean, and lies west of Detroit.

Bordering the sea is the Coastal Plain, generally under 100 feet in elevation, which reaches inland to the falls line, which runs nearly due. north and south on the line of Washington and Richmond. The old terraces of the coastal region support the great trucking areas, and at the south is the peanut-growing region.

CLIMATE 

The winters are short and relatively mild, and the summers are seldom subject to extremes of heat, while the nights have the saving grace of coolness.

The mean latitude of 37 to 38 degrees determines for the State its intermediate climate, sometimes characterized as "South of North and North of South." It has the length and rigor of neither the winters of the North nor the summers of the far South. For example, the mean temperature of 58 degrees in the latitude of Richmond has been found to be almost exactly the same temperature found by scientific study to be most conducive to maximum human effort in industrial and office operations. Nor are the swings of temperature extreme and long continued.

The coastal regions, tempered by the large bodies of water, are most uniform. The variations are largest in the western half of the State, where the elevations are greatest and where the land is farthest removed from the tempering effects of large bodies of water. 

From violent storms, wind, hail and sleet, the State is comparatively free. The average total snowfall per year ranges from about eleven inches in Tidewater to 18 inches for middle and Piedmont Virginia to 22 inches for the Great Valley and Allegheny region.

The humidity, which largely determines the sensible temperatures, is moderate. The mean ranges from about 60 per cent, in midsummer, to above 70 per cent, in midwinter.

Sunshine is a little larger in amount in the Tidewater than in the Valley and Mountain sections, ranging from 61 to 54 per cent. For all sections, it ranges from 62 per cent, in midsummer, to 48 per cent, in midwinter.

WATER SUPPLY 

Virginia is known as a well-watered State. As shown by the map, it has numerous streams, ranging from small to fairly large flow, and these traverse the State in a way to give a very even distribution of both their volume and fall.

The rather even rainfall throughout the year imparts to these streams a comparatively even volume of flow. At the same time, the topography provides numerous areas for storage. 

Their waters are comparatively free of undesirable dissolved matter. 

West of the Blue Ridge are many large springs of crystal-clear, cool, mountain water that break forth from the ground with volume of flow up to millions of gallons per day. There are also thermal or warm springs. The flow of the main streams is gauged by the State Stream

TRANSPORTATION 

The best idea of the transportation position of Virginia is to be secured by looking at a map of the eastern United States, showing the main trunk lines, the main highway thoroughfares and the chief population centers and shipping points. 

Virginia has eleven lines of railroad, of which eight are continental lines, stretching north, west and south, focusing on or through this State. 

The internal mileage is 4,660, or a little more than one for each ten square miles of area. It is true that some sections, such as the area between the York and the Potomac, are not yet served by rail. But hard-surfaced roads are rapidly bringing these areas into close communication with other parts of the State and the outside world. They have splendid, deep-water frontage. Most of the State is in the trunk line freight territory.

POPULATION 

From an industrial viewpoint, the outstanding facts are that the population of Virginia is thoroughly American born, is alert and highly intelligent, and is not disturbed by labor organizations and propaganda. 

Only 1.4 per cent of the population was foreign born in 1920, with perhaps 3 per cent foreign born in cities, and this has not changed materially. The colored population is a little less than one-third of the total.

POWER AND FUEL 

In these modern times, the large use of power is a chief test of industrial possibilities. Virginia has a good supply of actual and potential power.

Rates for electrical power are comparatively low. The prevailing rates for power purposes are as low as 1 cent and less per kilowatt hour for large consumers and up to 4.21 cents for a minimum of 1,250 kilowatt hours per month. 

For domestic purposes, the rates are from 10 cents down to 1.5 cents per kilowatt hour, according to load and uses. 

The potential power and fuel resources of the State are indicated by the fact that there are large reserves of water power and of coal. Coal occurs in several localities. The chief deposits now being mined are in the southwestern part of the State. The first coal mined in the United 

States was from the Triassic basin, just back of Richmond. The coal of the State is chiefly a medium bituminous coal, but there is some anthracite. Estimates place the total quantity of minable coal in the 

State at 25,000,000,000 tons, or enough to supply the entire United States for fifty years. The annual production is about 14,000,000 tons. 

Large quantities of coal are shipped from Hampton Roads. The total dumpage there in 1928 was 7,760,000 tons. Some of this, of course, comes from adjacent states. Hampton Roads is an important port for the receipt and storage of raw and fuel oil. Its storage capacity amounts to nearly 3,000,000 barrels.

The State has developed power in plants of 100 horsepower or more capacity used in manufacture, of about 500,000 horsepower, of which, in 1929, there was developed from water 145,000 horsepower.

RAW MATERIALS 

The prime materials of industry are power, labor and raw materials. 

The first two have already been touched upon. With raw materials, Virginia is well blessed in both the agricultural and the mineral fields. 

A more definite idea on these points may be secured from the tables of agricultural and mineral products on pages 23 and 22. 

In the agricultural field, Virginia produces in quantity, tobacco of several types, peanuts, cotton, wheat, corn, potatoes, sweet potatoes, cowpeas, apples and some peaches; hogs, beef, dairy products, poultry and eggs.

More than half of the State is in forests. The Coastal Plain and eastern Piedmont are largely occupied with pine and some cedar, with white cedar and cypress in the Dismal Swamp region. Mixed through the conifers and dominating all the upper Piedmont, the mountains and the Valley sections are many species of hardwood. Solid virgin stands are very scarce, but the aggregate of good lumber cut is large. 

Species of pine, especially the Loblolly, dominate the timber areas of the Coastal Plain and the eastern Piedmont. These and some species of broad leaf trees are very rapid-growing here to sizes for paper pulp and poles, and afford the possibility of a continuous supply of material for mills, at gross returns that compete favorably with agricultural crops on many classes of land. The long growing season in this latitude permits the production of the raw material, cellulose, in trees, as well as in 

Page Eighteen

cultivated crops at a very rapid rate. A state wide survey of available wood wastes has just been completed. 

Of mineral products, the State yields coal, salt, gypsum, iron, zinc, lead, manganese, routile, limestone, marble, valuable clays, bauxite, shale, quartz, soapstone and building stones, and many other materials in lesser supply.

Great cotton and tobacco areas lie just to the South, and the supply of mineral products is greatly augmented in the nearby states. 

CAPITAL AND BANKING 

The banking resources of the State have grown amazingly in recent years. At Richmond, is the Federal Reserve Bank for this district. The deposits in Virginia banks in March, 1930, were $491,502,000. Of this, 58.8 per cent is in National banks and 41.2 per cent is in State Banks. 

The total number of National banks is 156, with resources of $318,239,- 000; of State banks, 306, with resources of $277,335,000. There are 

90 Building and Loan Associations, with resources of $58,879,000, and 22 Industrial Loan Associations, with resources of $7,743,560. State banks are supervised through the Banking and Insurance division of the State Corporation Commission.

CONCLUSIONS 

These few figures and statements will, perhaps, serve to exhibit the basic extent and diversity of resources, equipment, and state organization that make Virginia worthy of consideration for certain types of industrial development. These figures are manifestly introductory, and anyone seeking a location will wish to secure much more detailed information. Some of this is in State files, some in local files and often the inquirer will be obliged to make extended personal investigations. An industrial survey of the State is being made under the auspices of the Virginia Polytechnic Institute at Blacksburg. This will afford an additional source of information. In so far as practicable, the facilities of the State will be made available in assembling those facts that come within the survey of the State. The facts as to the industrial and other facilities of the State must speak for themselves. That they do speak with a commanding voice is indicated by the rate at which new industries are coming to the State.
