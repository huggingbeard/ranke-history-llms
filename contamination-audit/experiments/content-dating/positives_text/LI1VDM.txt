THE REPORT IN BRIEF 

The vast majority of American people accept the fact that adequate health care is a basic human need which must be met in an effective manner. 

Hospital care, which amounts to $500 million annually in Pennsylvania, is the largest single portion of the health care bill which must be met. 

The self-supporting segments of the community, which represents approximately 88 percent of the population, can, at the present time, provide for their own hospital expenses through community non-profit prepayment plans, health and hospitalization insurance, or from their own financial resources. 

There are at all times, however, segments of the population in every community who do not have the financial resources to provide the necessary protection against the expense of hospitalization; these segments of the popu lation are: 

1. 2. 

The "Needy" - these are persons who are receiving assistance from public funds for their basic living needs of food, clothing, and shelter; 

The "Medically-needy" - these are persons who have sufficient financial resources to meet their basic living costs, but who do not have sufficient financial resources to meet the cost of hospital care.

The voluntary hospitals in Pennsylvania have always provided, as a voluntary community function, hospital care to patients irrespective of the patient's ability to pay for the services rendered. 

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, recognizing the role which the voluntary hospitals play in maintaining the public's health at a high level have

since 1863 made appropriations to hospitals for care rendered to "needy" and "medically-needy" patients. For many years Pennsylvania led all states in grants to hospitals.

In recent years, the Federal Government has provided, by amendment to the Social Security Act, grants for medical assistance to "needy" persons to states with approved plans. The various amendments provide for 

Federal reimbursement of medical care expenditures, including hospital care, to states in accordance with a formula outlined in the Social Security Act. The medical care reimbursement provisions cover the public assistance categories of old age assistance, aid for the blind, aid to dependent children, aid to permanently and totally disabled and, most recently, medical assistance for the aged.

Forty-seven (47) of the fifty-four (54) states and territories have taken advantage of the Social Security Act and provide hospitalization as a medical benefit under their public assistance plan. 

Forty-two (42) of these states purchase patient care from the hospitals as opposed to making a grant to the hospitals to offset the cost of the service rendered to public assistance recipients. 

Pennsylvania no longer leads the states in financial support for hospital care to public assistance recipients. Until 1961, Pennsylvania was one of seven states and territories which did not provide for hospitalization of public assistance recipients in its State plan. These states and territories were 

Alaska, Arizona, Delaware, Georgia, Guam, Iowa, and Pennsylvania. 

1961, Pennsylvania included recipients of old age assistance in the State plan, together with medical assistance for the aged. At the present time recipients of assistance for blind, dependent children, together with the permanently During ii

In addition to the programs adopted in 1961, the State has 10 Stateowned general hospitals where public assistance recipients and "medicallyneedy" persons in need of hospital care may receive services. However, seven of these hospitals are concentrated in the northeastern section of Pennsylvania and are available to only a small portion of the State's population. 

The voluntary hospitals in Pennsylvania render approximately 

2,835, 600 days of inpatient hospital care to 185, 491 "needy" and "medicallyneedy" persons annually. The cost of the care rendered in 1961 amounted to an estimated $55, 363, 392.

The current State-aid program of financial aid to 188 voluntary hospitals amounts to approximately $16 million. 

The voluntary hospitals in Pennsylvania are left with approximately $39, 363, 392 of costs for hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" patients which must be met from other sources. 

Some of the major sources are: philanthropy, local government appropriations, part payments from patients, mark-up over cost of services rendered to patients who are not covered by a prepayment plan, and revenue producing activities. 

During recent years a number of the hospitals have not obtained sufficient funds from the sources previously mentioned and were required to deplete capital funds and obtain short-term loans to meet the cost of care rendered to "needy" and "medically-needy" persons.

By comparison, our neighboring states of Maryland, Ohio, New York, and Massachusetts reimburse the hospitals on the basis of cost for services iii

The hospitals' cost per patient day is determined by a formula and the hospitals are paid the approved per diem rate.

The result is that the hospitals in these states bear a very small part of the cost of care rendered to "needy" and "medically-needy" patients. 

The administration of the hospital care programs in the neighboring states vary. In three states a governmental agency determines the hospitals' per diem reimbursement cost, and in Maryland it is determined by a nonprofit corporation. In all four states the local welfare districts or boards determine eligibility for hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons.

CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

It is the League's conclusion that the present method of making appropriations to voluntary hospitals should be discontinued. In its place a sound, orderly and efficient program should be developed based on the policy of purchasing needed health services for "needy" and "medically-needyꞌꞌ persons from the hospitals, clinics, and other suppliers which contract to provide the services.

In considering any program it is important to note that the need for inpatient hospital care at public expense might be lessened if full advantage can be taken of developments in medical care such as home care and outpatient care. 

The State's program, therefore, should be developed in such a way that additional service benefits can be included as the field of medical art is developed, so that expensive inpatient facilities are used only when necessary. 

It is also the League's conclusion that the basic responsibility for financing hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons is an obligation which should be shared by the entire population.

Government should not finance hospital care of "needy" and "medicallyneedy" persons without providing procedures and effective mechanisms to prevent unnecessary expenditure of public funds and to prevent the cost of the purchase of care program becoming an economic burden on the public. 

Therefore, a program of purchasing hospital care for "needy" and "medicallyneedy" persons will require the development of adequate procedures of determining eligibility. It will be necessary for hospitals, as well as government, to provide the means whereby cost increases can be held to a minimum.

Effects of the Proposed Program 

The proposed program provides that the Commonwealth and local government change their basic policy of appropriating funds to aid hospitals in financing the hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons, to a policy of purchasing hospital care for those persons which the government determines are eligible for hospital care at public expense. 

The proposed program, if adopted, would relieve voluntary hospitals of a large financial burden, releasing hospital funds for needed plant maintenance, new equipment, and improved health services. 

It recognizes the need of an agency which would concern itself with the overall problems which arise from necessary inter-relationship of government and hospitals.

The recommended use of the existing private or voluntary organizations in providing group coverage to public assistance recipients would maintain the voluntary approach, it would utilize the knowledge and experience of people in the specialized field of hospitalization insurance and prepayment plans.

The League's program, as outlined, would require additional expenditures of approximately $19.8 million by the Commonwealth and $4.4 million by the counties. The League recognizes adoption of the recommendations must be considered in light of available tax resources together with other needs of the Commonwealth. The program has been designed so that the overall program could be accomplished step-by-step if necessary. xvi

Adoption of the League's recommendations would bring an orderly, efficient and effective method of financing hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons and would provide a sound basis for the establishment of good administrative practices. xvii

CHAPTER VOLUME AND COST OF PATIENT CARE 

Chapter 1 analyzed the number and types of hospitals in Pennsylvania and demonstrated the significant part played by the voluntary hospitals in the provision of hospital facilities. 

The Chapter that follows discusses the volume of patient care in terms of admissions and days of patient care and the cost for providing that care. Before proceeding with the discussion of volume and cost of patient care, however, a description of terms seems advisable. 

As shown in the previous Chapter, hospitals fall into four main groups when classified by the type of ownership or control. The balance of this report will deal only with three of these groups; State and local government hospitals, voluntary hospitals and to a limited extent, proprietary hospitals. Federal hospitals have been eliminated from the study at the suggestion of the Governor's Hospital Study Commission. 

Each of the three main groups are broken down into sub-groups. 

Hospitals are classified into one of the sub-groups depending upon the major type of service rendered and the average length-of-stay. 

The sub-groups, according to service are mental, tuberculosis, general and special hospitals. 

If the average length-of-stay for all patients in the hospital is under 30 days the hospital may be further classified as a short-term hospital. If the average length-of-stay for all patients is over 30 days, the hospital may be classified as a long-term hospital. - 5 -

Because of the many opinions regarding the items of expense that constitutes "operating cost", it is necessary that the Governor's Hospital Study Commission clearly understand how the term "operating cost" is used and obtained for the purposes of this report. 

Due to the numerous bookkeeping methods and the various opinions as to the items of expense that should be included when computing operating costs, it was necessary to use the "reimbursable cost" as computed by the State-aided hospitals and as audited by the Department of the Auditor General. 

This method of determining "cost" is used by the majority of the voluntary hospitals in Pennsylvania.

The total operating expenses of each department in the hospital is recorded on the financial report of State-aided hospitals. From these expenses "Deductible Items" are deleted to arrive at the "reimbursable expense". The reimbursable expense is then apportioned by department to the various patient classifications of nursery, private, semi-private, ward, and two classes of outpatient service. The total "reimbursable expense" for each of the patient services is then divided by the total number of patient days or, in the case of outpatients, by the total number of visits to produce the per patient day costs or per visit costs. 

The "Deductible Items" referred to include all income which is deleted from operating expenses, also non-patient service expenses which pertain to other than patient or hospital personnel maintenance. 

The Commonwealth permits hospitals to charge the costs of equipment replacement to operating expense on a "depreciation basis". Depreciation on buildings is not recognized by the Commonwealth as a reimbursable expense. [I 1 1 - 6 -

Interest on mortgages is allowed for that portion of the cost of a structure actually devoted to hospital use, when approved by the Auditor 

General. 

All cost estimates in this report are based on the State-wide average per patient day costs or per visit costs for all State-aided hospitals. It is not the purpose of this study area of the Governor's Hospital Study Commission to analyze the causes for the differences in cost among hospitals or to make an analysis of the factors which make up cost. These matters are the subject of another area of study.

The figures on admissions and length-of-stay referred to in the balance of this Chapter were obtained for twelve-month periods in 1960-61. 

The same twelve months were not used in each case since the information was not readily available.

As shown in Chapter 2, reimbursable hospital operating costs for providing patient care in Pennsylvania amounted to approximately $494 million during a twelve-month period in 1960-61. This Chapter will be devoted to a discussion of the manner in which the hospitals in Pennsylvania finance their operating costs, the various sources from which the funds are obtained and the extent to which the operating revenues meet operating costs. 

Voluntary Hospitals 

Voluntary hospitals derive their operating income from four main sources; revenues from patients, philanthropy, governmental appropriations, and miscellaneous operating revenue. 

Patient revenues include money received from patients who have been billed directly for the services, money received from prepayment plans on behalf of patients, money received from insurance companies who have made payment directly to the hospital, and other third party purchasers of care, such as workmen's compensation, welfare agencies, etc. Patient revenues include both inpatient and outpatient department revenues.

Philanthropy includes donations, grants from community chests, united funds, and endowment income. 

Governmental appropriations includes money received from the 

Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, county, city, borough or township for maintenance of hospitals which provide hospital care for indigent persons.

Miscellaneous operating revenue includes all other revenue received by the hospital from hospital operations which cannot be placed in the above categories; such as sale of materials and supplies, tuition fees, recovery of bad debts, etc. - 18 -

Financial Operating Results 

An analysis of 173 voluntary general hospitals' financial reports filed with the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania by the State-aided hospitals demonstrates that when cash income is compared to cash operating expenses, 30 percent of the hospitals experienced cash deficits amounting to $3 million during the fiscal year of 1960-61. These deficits were financed from reserves, transfers from reserve funds, borrowing and capital funds. 

In addition to the cash operating deficits incurred by 30 percent of the State-aided hospitals, funds were diverted from maintenance of buildings, - 22 -

equipment replacement, capital needs for buildings and additional equipment, and improved health services in order to meet the difference between patient revenues and operating costs. 

With cost of operating the hospitals increasing each year and appropriations from government sources and philanthropy continuing to play a smaller part in the overall financial structure, the difference between patient revenues and operating costs will become larger. It can be expected that a greater number of the voluntary hospitals will experience cash deficits in the coming years unless some relief is provided. 

Hospitals cannot continue to use funds which are required to maintain the hospital structures and are needed to provide continued improvement of the health services to meet deficits which are resulting mainly from the "free" care rendered by hospitals to "needy" and "medically-needy" patients.

PART II MEDICAL CARE PROGRAMS FOR NEEDY AND MEDICALLY-NEEDY PERSONS CHAPTER I PENNSYLVANIA MEDICAL CARE LEGISLATION 

It has been pointed out in Part I of this study that the voluntary hospitals play a most important part in the Pennsylvania hospital system. 

It has also been shown that 30 percent of the voluntary hospitals had operating deficits in 1961 and that, with operating costs increasing, the problem of deficits will continue to become greater. 

Hospital care rendered at the expense of the hospital to "needy" and "medically-needy" persons is the major cause for the difference between patient revenues and operating cost. This part of the study is devoted to a discussion of the various aspects of medical care for indigent and medicallyindigent persons with particular emphasis on hospital care for persons in this economic class.

In order to obtain the necessary background for Pennsylvania's program in the area of medical care for needy persons, it is necessary to understand the Federal program of medical assistance to needy persons which is administered by the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare under terms of the Social Security Act (as amended). 

This program consists of various grants-in-aid distributed to the States with approved plans in order to encourage them to provide adequate medical assistance to needy persons. Currently, Federal grants are available - 27 -

to the States for medical care of old-age recipients, needy aged not receiving old-age assistance, dependent children, crippled children, the blind, and persons permanently and totally disabled.* Except for crippled children, the grants are open-end, the amount of Federal expenditure depending upon the total expenditures of the States. 

In the Federal legislation, hospital care is specifically denoted in only a few categories, although the terms "medical care" and "medical assistance" are used in the broadest sense to permit each State to use Federal funds for a wide variety of medical services, including inpatient and outpatient hospital care. 

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has enacted legislation providing a variety of medical services for these classes of indigent persons, and, in addition, to recipients of general assistance. 

But Pennsylvania utilizes Federal grants for hospital care only for recipients of old-age assistance, needy aged not receiving old-age assistance and crippled children. Hospital care for dependent children, persons totally and permanently disabled, blind persons, other classes of indigents is financed entirely with non-Federal funds in State-owned or State-aided hospitals, or through participation by local governmental jurisdictions.

CHAPTER VOLUME AND COST OF SERVICE RENDERED TO NEEDY AND MEDICALLY-NEEDY PERSONS 

One of the major reasons patient revenues do not meet the cost of operating the hospitals is that hospitals render a certain amount of care to persons whose economic circumstances are such that they cannot pay their hospital bills or can only pay a portion of their bills. 

It has been shown that a part of the difference between patient revenues and operating cost is met by government appropriations, philanthropy and miscellaneous sources; that in the voluntary hospitals there is no specific revenue source from which the deficit between patient revenues and operating costs can be met. Inasmuch as the cost of free service rendered in government-owned hospitals is met by tax funds, this portion of the study will be devoted to a discussion of "free" patient care as it relates to voluntary hospitals, to determine the volume and cost of free care rendered by the voluntary hospitals and the sources hospitals use to finance the cost. 

Before proceeding to present this analysis it is necessary that we understand clearly the terms, which are used by hospital people and those persons connected with the hospital system, when referring to services rendered to persons whose financial circumstances prevent their paying the full amount of their hospital bill. 

Patients are usually classified into one of three categories: (1) paying patient, (2) part-pay patient, (3) free patient. 

---

The paying patients are those persons who are able to pay for required care through personal or family resources or through third party payments such as a member of a prepayment plan. 

The part-pay patients are those persons who, because of their financial circumstances, can only afford to pay a portion of the hospital bill.

The free patients are those persons who, because of their financial circumstances, cannot pay any portion of the hospital bill.

Free patients and part-pay patients are usually classified into two groups: (1) "needy", sometimes referred to as indigent, (2) "medicallyneedy" sometimes referred to as medically-indigent. 

The "needy" are persons who receive public assistance or whose resources are insufficient to cover a reasonable standard of living.

The "medically-needy" are persons whose economic resources. exceeds the minimum for a reasonable standard of living but who cannot afford the added costs of illness. 

It is estimated that admissions of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons in voluntary hospitals, during a 12-month period in 1960-61, represented 12 percent of the total admissions, or 185, 491.

It is also estimated that these 185, 491 "needy" and "medically-needy" admissions represented 21 percent of the total patient days, or 2, 835, 600 patient days, and $55, 363, 392, or 15 percent of the total operating costs of the voluntary hospitals.

The reason for "needy" and "medically-needy" persons accounting for a larger portion of patient days than they do admissions is that they stay in the hospital longer than patients whose hospital bill is paid for. CHAPTER FINANCING MEDICAL CARE OF NEEDY AND MEDICALLY-NEEDY PATIENTS 

In the previous Chapter the volume and cost of hospital inpatient services rendered to the "needy" and "medically-needy" was shown. In this 

Chapter the present State-aid program to voluntary hospitals, the method of determining if a patient is eligible for "free" or "part-pay" care, and the manner in which the cost of care for "needy" and "medically-needy" persons is presently financed will be explained. 

State-Aid Program 

The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has always recognized that the voluntary hospitals help maintain the public health at a high level by providing necessary care to patients irrespective of their ability to pay for the care rendered.

In 1863, the General Assembly began to make appropriations to voluntary hospitals to help defray the costs of operation. From time to time changes have been made in the wording of the appropriation acts as to the amount of appropriation and the method of payment, but the intent of the legislature has not changed.

Act 84-A passed by the 1961 sessions of the State legislature appropriated to the Department of Public Welfare for the maintenance of certain hospitals the sum of $17, 303, 125 for the fiscal period of June 1, 1961 to June 30, 1962 (13 months). The appropriation was to be paid to the hospitals - 52 -

designated in the bill in the amounts specified for each hospital for the purpose of the maintenance of such hospitals, in accordance with the rules and regulations issued by the Department of Public Welfare and the Department of the Auditor General, as prescribed by law, at the rate of $10 per diem, but not to exceed the regularly established ward rate, nursery rate, or pediatric rate or any lesser rate contracted between the hospital and patient for the medical and surgical services rendered to and maintenance of persons treated in the hospital who are entitled to free service. Such rates were to include only room, board, and general bed care but not to exceed rates charged other classes of patients or the actual cost to the hospital of such services and maintenance whichever is less. All moneys received on account of the care of the patient has to be reported as a credit against the gross earnings of free and part-pay service chargeable against the appropriation. 188 hospitals together with the amount of appropriation is listed. (A list of the State-aided hospitals together with a list of the non-State-aided voluntary hospitals appears in Exhibits XXIV and XXV.)

The Department of Public Welfare has two important responsibilities in connection with the program of aid to voluntary hospitals: (1) To prepare annual estimates of the needs of each institution for submission to the Governor and the General Assembly as a basis for the annual maximum appropriation to each hospital. (2) To establish reasonable administrative procedures in accordance with the law by which payments are made to each institution under its appropriation. - 53 -

The General Assembly does not attempt to guarantee the financial solvency of any individual hospital, or to reimburse a hospital for a stated portion of its net deficit from operations. 

To be included as a State-aided hospital, the hospital must be a voluntary nonprofit hospital, non-sectarian, and willing to comply with the regulations covering payments, inspection, and financial reporting and auditing.

Current legislation provides that three factors determine the reimbursement for each hospital: (1) Amount of free service to inpatients (2) Unit cost of the service (3) Established rates for approved service The regulations require "... the Credit Department of each hospital to determine which patients are entitled to free or part-free service after investigation of the social and financial background of each case. A uniform credit card is prescribed which must be completed on each such case, and must show reasons why, in the judgment of the credit officer, the patient should be free or part-free. These records are subject to inspection and final approval by Commonwealth officials." 1/ The hospital is not reimbursed for service to a patient who was classified as full pay, but whose account later proves to be uncollectable in full.

The Commonwealth does not provide any formulae of ability to pay, each case is considered on its merits. 

1/ "Instruction Bulletin" published by the Department of Welfare and the 

Department of the Auditor General, June 1951. 

- 54 -

The Act of the General Assembly also provides that a hospital cannot be reimbursed at a rate greater than the per diem "cost" of the service. In addition, since the appropriation Act provides for "maintenance" of hospitals, the Commonwealth prescribes that cost data may include only operating costs, and must exclude all capital expenditures. 

The average per diem operating costs of ward patients in voluntary hospitals is $23.37. The present Commonwealth appropriation Act would pay basically $10.00 for each day of free care provided, within the limits of the total appropriation, or an average of 42.8 percent of the average operating cost per patient day. PART IV A PROGRAM FOR PENNSYLVANIA CHAPTER 1 CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

Some people find it financially impossible to pay for their hospital care either from their own financial resources, through the purchase of insurance, or as a member of a community prepayment plan; they are: "'The Needy" - those persons who receive public assistance or whose resources are insufficient to cover a reasonable standard of living. "The Medically-needy" - those persons whose economic resources are sufficient for a minimum standard of living but who cannot afford the added costs of illness. 

In Pennsylvania, it has always been the responsibility of the voluntary hospitals to provide hospital care to "needy" and "medically-needy" persons with limited financial assistance from government and philanthropic sources. 

In 1961 the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania broke the traditional pattern of making appropriations to hospitals which care for "needy" and "medically-needy" persons by adopting the purchase of care concept for hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons over 65 years of age under the MAA and OAA programs. In its 65 and over program, its crippled children and vocational rehabilitation program, the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania purchases care from the hospital on the basis of cost for the benefits covered. 

The present program of purchase of care for the 65 and over age bracket is a step in the proper direction, but it will not solve the financial problem of the "needy" and "medically-needy" under 65. - 79 -

In considering any program for the uncovered "needy" and "medicallyneedy" it is important to note that the need for inpatient hospital care at public expense might be lessened if full advantage can be taken of developments in medical care such as home care and outpatient care. 

The framework of the State's program, therefore, should be established in such a way that additional service benefits can be included as the field of medical art is developed. If this were done, expensive inpatient care facilities would be used only when necessary. Also it would not be necessary to hospitalize a patient because the less expensive benefits (outpatient services, etc.) are not available or are not covered by the State program. 

It is the League's conclusion that the present method of making appropriations to voluntary hospitals should be discontinued. In its place a sound, orderly and efficient program should be developed based on the policy of purchasing needed health services for "needy" and "medically-needy" persons from the hospitals, clinics and other suppliers which contract to provide the services.

In addition to the foregoing reasons for discarding the present system, the League finds that the present system produces the following undesirable effects: 

1. The present program of State-aid to hospitals, based on a rate of $10.00 for each day of approved free care for persons under 65 years of age, meets only 42. 8 percent of the State-wide average cost of a ward patient in those hospitals which receive State-aid. Because of the difference in ward costs among the State-aided hospitals, the per diem rate pays as much as 87 percent of the operating cost in some hospitals and as little as 22 percent in others. - 80 -

2. 

The present method of State-aid has had the effect of placing the burden of the major cost of hospital expense for providing hospital care to the "needy" and "medically-needy" persons on a small part of the State's population; those persons who provide philanthropic funds and those hospitalized persons who are not covered by a prepayment plan but who pay hospital charges which includes a portion of the cost of providing care to "needy" and "medicallyneedy" persons. 

3. State-aid in 1960-61 paid only 29 percent of the cost of services rendered to "needy" and "medically-needy" persons leaving the voluntary hospitals with approximately $39.5 million of costs to be financed from other resources. 

4. The expense of providing hospital care to the "needy" and "medically-needy" has been increasing due to mounting operating costs and the increase in the number of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons utilizing the voluntary hospital facilities. At the same time revenue from governmental and philanthropic sources have been decreasing when related to the cost of providing hospital care to the "needy"! and "medically-needy" persons. These factors have had the effect of placing a financial strain on many of the voluntary hospitals, requiring the voluntary hospitals to borrow funds, to divert funds. from capital needs, and to charge paying patients an amount above the actual costs for the services rendered. 

5. The present method of financing hospital care for persons considered to be "needy" and "medicallyneedy" is inadequate and does not lend itself to sound fiscal and administrative controls or procedures.

It is our conclusion that the basic responsibility for financing hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons is an obligation which should be shared by the entire population, and that the most effective means of spreading the financial responsibility is by having State and local - 81 -

government responsible for purchasing hospital care for the "needy" and "medically-needy" persons of all age groups including those under 65 years of age. 

State and local government should not finance hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons without providing procedures and effective mechanisms to prevent unnecessary expenditure of public funds and to prevent the cost of the program becoming an economic burden on the public. 

A change in the basic approach of financing hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons to a purchase of hospital service benefits will require the development of procedures to safeguard the Commonwealth and the counties against unnecessary use of hospital facilities. Adequate procedures for determining eligibility will be needed so that only those persons who are in need of financial assistance are certified as eligible for hospital care at public expense.

If hospitals are to be reimbursed on the basis of the cost for the services rendered, it will be necessary for hospitals as well as State and local government, to be concerned about cost increases. Efforts must be made to keep cost increases at a minimum and to determine those areas of hospital administration and service where new techniques might be employed to reduce costs.

CHAPTER THE EFFECTS OF THE PROPOSED PROGRAM 

The proposed program recognizes that some people are not financially able to pay for the hospital care they need and that it should be the communities' and not the hospitals' responsibility to seek the ways and means of financing that care.

It provides, therefore, that the government be responsible for hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons to the extent of their financial need at the time of hospitalization by placing the full fiscal responsibility on the Commonwealth for providing hospital care for public assistance recipients, and the responsibility on local government to share in the cost of hospital care for those persons who are determined to need financial aid at the time of hospitalization.

The proposed program provides that the Commonwealth and local government change their basic policy of appropriating funds to aid hospitals in financing of "free care", to a policy of purchasing the care from the hospitals for those persons which government determines are eligible for hospital care at public expense. 

The proposed program, if effected, would put the Commonwealth in a position to take advantage of any Federal reimbursement that may be obtained from any future expansion of Federal legislation without undue delay and loss in reimbursement.

The uniform standards of determining eligibility, by local public assistance offices, as recommended, would place this responsibility in the - 107 -

hands of people experienced in determining financial need. The adoption of uniform standards may not reduce the number of free admissions, however, proper application of uniform standards, together with enforcement of relative. responsibility and collection procedures for that portion of the cost which is determined to be the responsibility of the patients, will reduce the overall cost of the program.

The proposed program, if effected, would relieve the hospitals of a large financial burden, releasing hospital funds for needed plant maintenance, new equipment, and improved health services. 

It recognizes the need of an agency which would concern itself with the overall problems which arise from necessary inter-relationship of government and hospitals. It also recognizes the need for such an agency to be as far removed from government control as possible and still maintain the authority needed to obtain the results desired by the public, hospitals and government.

The recommended use of the existing private or voluntary organizations in providing group coverage to public assistance recipients would maintain the voluntary approach, it would utilize the knowledge and experience of people in the specialized field of hospitalization insurance and prepayment plans, and serve as an example of a cooperative solution to serve the "needy" and "medically-needy" persons.

Adoption of the League's recommendations would bring an orderly, efficient and effective method of financing hospital care of "needy" and "medically-needy" persons and would provide a sound basis for the establishment of good administrative practices. - 108 -

EXHIBIT XXIX SCOPE AND METHODOLOGY OF STUDY 

This study contains a general review of Pennsylvania hospitals by type of ownership together with statistical data on: (1) Volume of inpatient and outpatient care rendered; 

Estimates of cost of care rendered; (2) (3) Sources of hospital revenue and the extent that each of the sources play in financing patient care.

Several types of hospitals were excluded from the study for the reason that: the cost reflects both medical care and educational services which cannot be separated; that the educational services rendered is the primary function; that the final recommendations do not affect the hospital; or that data on the hospital was not available because of its size.

Detailed data on bed complement, admissions, patient days, volume of free and part-pay care, outpatient visits, cost of inpatient and outpatient visits, revenues and expenses, in State-aided hospitals was obtained from the financial reports filed by these hospitals with the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. 3. 4. 5. 6. 7. 

The data on the volume of free care we consider to be accurate because it had been audited by the Auditor General's Office and was the basis for payment to the hospitals by the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania. Information on costs contained on these financial reports are not accurate as they apply to the detailed breakdown of costs by patient category, that is private, semi-private, ward and outpatient, because few hospitals maintain cost accounting systems. 

However, since in this report the per patient day costs are not used on an individual hospital basis and are averaged to obtain overall State-wide estimates of the cost of patient care, we feel that the costs used are sufficiently accurate for the purposes of this report. 

Data on State-owned hospitals were obtained from published reports, letters of inquiry and personal interview with various offices of the Department of Public Welfare, the Department of Health and the Department of Revenue. In some instances costs cannot be compared to voluntary hospitals because of the different methods of accounting and items of cost which are included or excluded when computing operating costs. It is our opinion that the data available and supplied by the various departments of the Commonwealth is sufficiently accurate for the purposes of this report.
