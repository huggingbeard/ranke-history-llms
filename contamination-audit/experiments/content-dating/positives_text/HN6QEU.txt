CHAPTER II  TWEN 

'WENTY years had passed since the heydey of placer mining in Idaho, but a veteran of those early days, dropping into the Miner's Luck any time after the lights were lighted, could easily have imagined himself back in Bannack or Florence at its best or worst, according to his point of view. In the clamorous crowd gathered in front of the long bar, and among the less noisy but more dangerous men at the card tables, he would have recognized every type of the adventurous tribe that had made those earlier camps uncomfortable places for timid men. But he could not have remained long without becoming aware of a difference. Under all the noise and reckless swagger he would have sensed a certain curious. repression. Before long, it would be driven home to the least observant that this was due to the presence of one man, a man with inhuman, fishlike eyes, who sat alone at one of the tables, his slow, lifeless gaze photographing every movement in the crowded room. 

In the first few months of its existence Roaring 

Canyon had vied with the wildest gold camps of an earlier day. Filled with disappointed miners, many of them bold and desperate men, who had arrived to find the limited ground all staked, the little camp became an inferno in which the expectation of life was measured by the speed with which a man could scatter lead. The time came when those who had a stake in the place, revolting against a condition in which neither life nor property was safe, determined to

clean up the camp. They selected for the purpose a man notorious for his ice-cold nerve and uncanny skill with the six-gun. Sure-death Holbeck proved to be the man they wanted. With a nervous system proof against agitation, cold-blooded as a fish, swift in action as a striking snake, he was a most effective killing machine, and he had a record that justified his nickname. 

They called him "town marshal," and gave him a free hand. Before a month had passed, he had established a reign of terror. Two swaggering fire-eaters, leaders of the lawless element, overconfident in their skill, found they were a full half second too slow, and thereafter ceased to trouble the community. 

After this object lesson no roistering bully, drunk or sober, was bold enough to challenge the cold-faced marshal.

This did not mean that it was a blue-law town. 

An offense against life or property drew swift justice from Judge Colt when the perpetrator was unwise enough to linger-otherwise the town was very much wide open.

It was only eight o'clock in the evening, but by far the largest part of Roaring Canyon's population was already housed within the walls of the dingy, squalid saloon which flaunted the name of Miner's 

Luck. Sure-death Holbeck, frigid and unapproachable as an iceberg, occupied his usual seat. From the card tables came the rattle of chips with now and then a profane exclamation from a disappointed player. At the bar, men clamored for the drinks that were slow in coming. Blue-shirted men in high boots, cowboys in checkered shirts and gay handkerchiefs, pushed and jostled, and whether frowning or laughing, swearing with equal fluency.

From somewhere out on the street the distant sound of music floated in through open door and windows and caught the attention of one and then

another. Gradually the noisy hubub in the saloon died down until the room was oddly still. The notes that came to them, weirdly plaintive, were those of "Home, Sweet Home," and soon every man in the motley gathering forgot his occupation in the effort to catch every note. Reckless eyes grew sober, and hard faces took on a look of wistful sadness as the familiar strains grew louder, played by a man who knew how to make music pull on heartstrings until they ached. When the last note died away, more than one man with the outward appearance of a savage drew breath with a sound that was like a sob. "That's that cowboy, Denny Bourne," said a blackwhiskered giant reaching hurriedly for his glass, possibly to hide the moisture in his eyes. "He sure can make that contraption talk, but I wish he wouldn't play them kind o' things."

As if in answer to the wish, the music began again, but this time it was rollicking air which besought the landlord to fill the flowing bowl. At the end of the first bar the player came into view. His appearance was so out of keeping with the music that had affected them, that the reaction of the motley crowd was instantaneous. A chorus of shouts greeted him as he staggered into the room. 

Denny's hat was on the back of his head. A lock of hair straggled over his forehead and covered one of his eyes. An abrasion on his cheek was still bleeding. The dirt on his clothes could not all have been gathered in one fall. As he advanced into the room, he continued to play, never striking a false note, but his feet seemed very undecided about carrying him to the bar. Behind him came English, white-faced and stern, striding with the exaggerated deliberation of a man who was losing his battle with John Barleycorn.

Way was made for them by the now laughing crowd. A dozen voices commanded that they be

served with drinks. Denny, having finally brought up against the bar, braced himself and responded gravely: "Gen'lemen, I thank you for My Lord Duke. For me I'm a man o' temp'rance. I don' drink after the liquor wets the roots o' my hair. He'll drink one for him an' one for me, an' I'll play his funeral march."

The drinks were taken to the slow notes of a dirge, but the moment they were finished, Denny switched into a lively jig. In a moment a dozen men were heeling and toeing it on the floor. The girls, who had been hovering about the men at the tables, suddenly found themselves in demand, and soon the floor was filled with rollicking, clumsy, but very energetic dancers.

It was Kate, and her slender body was a perfect shield for the worst scoundrel unhanged! Suddenly Kate's voice rang out, clear and unafraid. "Shoot, Denny! Don't mind me!"

She made a violent effort to throw herself down on her horse's neck, but Salter held her with a firm grip.

"Drop your guns on the ground, Bourne. If you don't, I'll kill her first and you afterward." 

Denny heard the order in a sort of stupor. His eyes were strained on the two horses. If only one would move and give him the ghost of a target! "Drop 'em-you've only one second!" 

Denny opened his hand and let his gun drop, took the other one from its holster and threw it on the ground. He was stunned by the complete failure of his plans and the sight of Kate's danger, and was hardly aware of the tread of a horse coming up behind him. "You there!" called Salter, "whoever you are, put a gun against his back and search him, then tie him up!" 

Denny felt a gun pressed against his spine, and a hand passed rapidly over his clothing in search of a hidden gun. Salter, assured that his enemy was helpless, holstered his gun and, coming from behind 

Kate, began to move toward him. Watching Salter slowly approaching, Denny passed through a moment of maddening, blinding rage, when he would willingly have bartered his soul for a gun and one cartridge. The hard pressure of steel against his spine was gone, but he hardly realized it, then sanity returned as he felt the butt of a gun pushed against his clenched fist, and over his shoulder heard Texas' slow drawl. "He's your meat, pardner-git the swine!" 

There was a scurry of hoofs as Texas jumped his horse out of range. Salter stopped short and for a second seemed bewildered by the unaccountable change in affairs. Covering him with Texas' gun, Denny moved his horse aside until Kate was longer in line, then he spoke, and there was a deadly calmness in his tone. "Salter," he said slowly, "I'm going to give you

Kate said afterward that it seemed as if that quarter of a minute of dead silence would never end. When it did, she saw no movement before two spurts of flame flashed in the moonlight like darting snakes' tongues, and the air was rent by two explosions which were not a tenth of a second apart. For a brief instant both men sat upright in their saddles, then Salter's body collapsed like a punctured balloon, and he rolled to the ground. 

Denny's thoughts flew to Kate. Turning quickly, he saw that Texas was already at her side helping her from the saddle. Until he spurred his horse to move toward them, he had forgotten the battered hulk of Salter's rider at the end of his rope. Feeling the drag, he leaped from the saddle and ran to the group by the trees. "Are you all right, Kate?" he asked anxiously. "Right as can be, only I seem to have lost my legs," Kate replied. Unable to stand, she had seated herself on the ground. "How did Salter come to get you?" "It was all your fault," said Kate. "If you had told me what you knew, instead of treating me like a dummy, things might have been different. I watched Brady," she went on, her voice changing to a note of sadness. "He went to a cabin back in the mountains and told the whole thing to Salter. 

I wasn't going to be left out entirely, so I followed Salter and his men. I was a little too careless, and he trapped me." "Did he treat you rough?" "Not more than he had to—he was quite decent." "What was he meaning to do with you?"

"I don't know, except that he intended to use me in some way to draw you into a trap." "And he darn near did it," Denny commented grimly. "If it hadn't been for Texas, we'd both been in a bad way." "I seen yuh grab that pinto hoss," Texas explained, "an' I figgered yuh'd be wantin' help when yuh caught up with Salter." "It's another one I owe, old man," said Denny. "Mebbe I'll get a chance to pay some time. Kate, if you can walk now, I'd like to take a look at 

Salter. I want to prove something.' 

Kate was still a little unsteady on her feet, but she managed to move over to where Salter lay face upward in the moonlight. His hat had fallen off, and the still, white face, softened in death, gave her a strange feeling that she had seen it many times. 

Denny was kneeling beside the body searching the pockets of the slicker it wore. In an inside pocket he found what he was looking for. 

Carefully he drew forth a black skullcap with a fringe of gray hair attached to the edge, and a pair of steel-rimmed spectacles. With a swift motion he lifted the head and covered it with the cap, then put the spectacles over the eyes. "My God! Brady!" Kate gasped. 

Denny looked up at her with a quizzical smile, then reading in her face the pain the revelation brought to her, he spoke quickly and reassuringly. "Not your Brady, though, Kate-not the Brady who was kind to you, and protected you. Yesterday the real Brady caught me snooping round his private entrance in the alley, and didn't shoot me.
