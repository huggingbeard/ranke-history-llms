FOREWORD 

It is apparent to everyone that in both bad and so-called good times the problem of unemployment is one of the most important of our age. It has produced much talk, but little has been done to solve it in a direct or specific way. 

The importance of this matter cannot be exaggerated. If our economic and social problems are not solved-and I postulate that our present Social Security Act does not solve them*-democracy and our existence as a free people; yes, and even the capitalistic system itself, will become a thing of the past. There is no use to stand idly by and talk. Action must be had if we are to avert the imminent danger.

The reader would do well to ask himself the following questions:

Would a return to our so-called business normal, (if such a return were possible), solve our present unemployment problem? Would it relieve the plight of the blind, the hopelessly crippled and the aged? Could and should more be accomplished to insure a minimum of unemployment and a greater security for both those temporarily unemployed and those unable to work even in the best of times?

THE BASIC PRINCIPLES ON WHICH THE PLAN WOULD OPERATE 

As the principles of the plan herein outlined are sound, just, and in harmony with our American System of doing business, the author feels confident, that, once understood, few will oppose their enactment into law.

The plan is based on sound business principles-that of investing in and receiving an annuity. It cannot be termed a pension plan. 

On the contrary, its operation would enable us to do away with all pensions, should we so desire. 

By doing away with all pensions, we would certainly be economizing to a marked degree in the operation of our present economic life. In fact by abolishing all pensions-federal, state, industrial, etc., and the present Social Security Act, which no longer would be required if the proposed plan were enacted into law and which now levies a tax of from one per cent to 6 per cent of the laborer's wage and a like tax on the employer; together with the abolition of the present sales tax, which also would no longer be required for the purpose for which it was levied (relief purposes), would far more than compensate for

INVESTMENTS AND ANNUITIES 

The federal government is the logical and proper agency to perform this beneficial work. 

The best way to bring about its accomplishment would be to create an opportunity for the unemployables to invest with the government and receive an annuity from such an investment.. All able bodied citizens should be granted the same privilege. Their investment, however, should be a form of insurance against unemployment.

OBTAINING MONEY TO INVEST 

No doubt a pertinent question has by now entered the mind of the reader. How can the unemployables obtain money to invest? A little reflection will provide the answer. Few indeed are the unemployable citizens who do not have highly interested relatives and friends. At present these relatives and friends are faced with the responsibility and necessity of taking care of those who are unable to work. To many it is a very realistic burden. They would certainly have an incentive to invest in the financial wel-

fare of those who are a continual drain upon them. Such a procedure would relieve their own pocket books. The same incentive would induce the various agencies now caring for certain unemployables in a meager way to invest for the welfare of their charges. In the few remaining cases of unemployables, the government could arrange credit; the benefitted individual to repay in monthly installments the principle and interest of his loan. The banks could be empowered to make such loans, the government guaranteeing them against loss. This would give the banks and other loaning corporations an opportunity to put at least ten or twelve billion dollars of idle money at work earning a fair interest. That in turn would make it possible to put ten or twelve million idle men at work producing and distributing commodities to those who now have little or no buying power.

Even if all the unemployables should have to borrow for their investment, they would certainly have the ability to repay. The fair sized annuity they would receive would make this possible. Even a measure of self-denial the first year or two would be well worth the effort, as they would receive the annuity for the rest of their lives.

The foregoing being true, the necessity for action by our government becomes very apparent, and especially to those who would answer the previous questions in the way the author thinks the majority will answer them.

DIVIDE THE CITIZENS INTO TWO GROUPS 

For the purpose of this plan the citizens are divided into two groups:

FIRST: The physically fit hereinafter called the Employables.

SECOND: The physically unfit hereinafter called the Unemployables.

The employables will receive their annuities only when temporarily out of employment.

The Unemployables will receive their annuities each month.

THE PLAN IN DETAIL 

Ask Congress to enact a law requiring the Federal government to create a fund to be known as the United States Unemployment and Annuity Fund. This fund to be established and continuously renewed in one of the following manners: 

FIRST METHOD: Eligible unemployables wish ing to participate in the Unemployment and Annuity Fund must invest with the Federal Government ten times the amount of the annuity they may choose. (See the amount they may choose later on.) Eligible employables wishing to participate must invest $500 with the government. 

A 12% general transaction tax will be levied on all transactions in the United States including transactions in stocks, bonds and the sale of services. The money raised by this general tax will

make up the Unemployment and Annuity Fund.

SECOND METHOD: The Investment features are identical with the first method. Instead of employing a general tax on all of the transactions, the government will be required to issue trade certificates equal to the amount that the annuitants have invested with the government. 

The government will declare the trade certificate legal tender.

A two per cent transaction tax will be levied upon all transactions in which Trade Certificates are used, including transactions in stocks and bonds and the sale of services. The tax will be collected through the purchase and use of revenue stamps to be affixed upon the reverse side of the Certificates. The money thus raised by the tax will make up the Unemployment and Annuity 

Fund. 

No doubt the reader will notice the apparent discrepancy in the per cent of the tax used in the two methods of financing the fund. Therefore, a word of explanation is desirable. 

If a 2% tax on the turnover of onefourth of the nation's business is sufficient to finance the fund with the trade certificate method (and it undoubtedly will be ample as can and will be demonstrated further on) it would seem that one-half of one per cent would be sufficient to finance the fund when all of the

nation's business is taxed on its turnover as it would be in the first method. 

However, the required rapid turnover of the trade certificate dollar would not only speed up business immensely, but it would net considerable more money in the same length of time than would the slower turnover of the usual currency. 

Therefore, in order to be fairly safe, the author has decided it will require a levy of one and one-half per cent transaction tax on the total turnover instead of one-half of one per cent.

Under the second method of financing the fund, the annuitants will receive their annuities in Trade Certificates whenever that is necessary to keep sufficient certificates in circulation to pay for administration and to furnish all annuitants their maximum annuities.

Regardless of the method of financing, the following will hold true:

After deducting sufficient to pay all expenses of administering the fund, the balance will be pro-rated up to the maximum, as outlined below.

Should there be a balance after paying all expenses and the maximum amount of all annuities, such balance will go toward reducing the national debt.

The seller will be required to pay the tax,

except in cases of forced sales, or foreclosures, in which case the buyer will be required to pay it.

QUALIFICATIONS FOR THE EMPLOYABLE INVESTORS 

A. Any citizen between the ages of 18 and 60 can apply for an annuity.

B. He must be free from repeated major criminal convictions.

C. He must not be confined in a State or Federal Institution.

D. His total net income shall not exceed $2,000 per annum.

E. He must invest $500 with the govenment. 

F. He must furnish the government with a sworn financial statement, detailing the income from his estate and from his labor. Any increase of his combined income above $2,000 per annum must be immediately reported, or penalties will attach.

When out of work fifteen days or more, through no fault of his own, he can apply for an annuity. This will be granted him on a pro-ratio basis after all expenses of administering the Unemployment and Annuity Fund have first been deducted. In computing the employable's annuity and his pro-ratio, the sum of $150 will be used as his maximum for the first thirty days; $100 for the second thirty days, $75 for the third

thirty days, and $50 for the fourth thirty days.

At the end of the four-month period, and from then on until he finds employment, his pro-rated annuity shall be computed on a maximum of $30 per month.

The foregoing pro-ratios are predicated on the assumption that the employable has not participated in the Unemployment and Annuity Fund during the past eight months, and also that eight months have elapsed since he invested with the government. If these conditions are not met, his pro-ratio will be computed on a maximum of $100 for the first thirty day period, $75 for the second, $50 for the third, and $40 for the fourth; then $30 per month until he finds employment. Should he fail to seek employment or refuse employment at a fair wage of the kind to which he is accustomed, his annuity shall stop and his investment be returned to him. Investors in this class can withdraw their investment any time they so desire.

HEADS OF FAMILIES IN THE EMPLOYABLE GROUP 

In addition to investing $500 with the government as insurance against unemployment, any citizen who can meet the requirements of the employable group, and who is the head of a family, and whose net income from his estate and labor does not exceed $2,000 per annum, can supplement his yearly income by investing $300

with the government and receiving a $30 monthly annuity. However, never can his yearly income be augmented beyond $2,000 by reason of his investment.

QUALIFICATIONS FOR UNEMPLOYABLES 

To qualify as an annuitant in the Unemployable group, the investor must be a citizen of the United States, must be free from repeated major criminal convictions, must not be confined in a

State or Federal Institution, must have a net income from his estate of less than $900 per annum, and must fall in one of the following groups: 

A. Sixty years of age or over. 

B. Orphans and similar dependent children under 18 years of age who are in the care of a legal guardian.

C. A widow with two or more children under 18 years of age. Remarriage automatically voids the participating widow's annuity, and her investment will be returned. 

D. At least 50% disability due to permanent physical defects. Should disability prove to be non-permanent, the participant's annuity will be discontinued and his investment returned. Disability, as well as its percentage and permanency, shall be determined as occasion demands by a committee of three physicians and three laymen.

An unemployable who disposes of his estate

after this plan is enacted into law must wait five years after such disposal before he becomes eligible to receive an annuity.

Unemployables will be required to furnish the government with a sworn financial statement upon making application for an annuity. Sworn monthly statements, showing how they have spent their monthly income, must be made by unemployable annuitants. Should they fall heir to an estate, that fact must be reported within ten days, or penalties will attach.

INVESTMENTS AND ANNUITIES IN THE UNEMPLOYABLE GROUP 

As living expenses vary in different sections of the nation, and as each unemployable may differ in what he feels he needs and can legitimately spend under this plan each month, each unemployable investor will be permitted to choose any sum up to and including $75 as his monhly annuity. Never at any time can he receive more than $75 a month.

The $75 per month maximum might well be advantageously increased to $100. per month. In fact if Congress believes that in certain large centers $75 per month is not sufficient to supply the annuitants with all of the actual necessities of life: food, clothing, shelter, and medical and

dental care and a small surplus for amusement and a few comforts it should increase the maximum to $100.

In order to become a beneficiary the un employable must invest with the government ten times the amount of the maximum he chooses. 

Example: An annuitant choosing a $50 maxi mum as his monthly annuity would therefore invest $500.

The annuitant may lower or increase his maximum from year to year as he sees fit, never, however, exceeding the $75 maximum, unless 

Congress changed the maximum to $100.

An unemployable annuitant, having a net income from his estate, can supplement it through his annuity only up to the $75 maximum. 

Never can his income, from his estate, from his annuity, or from the two sources combined, exceed $75 per month, unless Congress changes the maximum to $100.

An unemployable annuitant may withdraw his investment any time he desires, thus stopping his annuity; provided he has not violated any of the regulations of the proposed law; and provided further he has not obligated himself to the government, or to a lending corporation the government has backed. In case he is so obligated, the government will withhold from the investment of the withdrawing annuitant an amount sufficient to cover such indebtedness. 

At the time of death the annuitant's invest-

ment will go to his estate, and shall be used for paying any debts he may have contracted and for burial expenses. Any balance shall go to his heirs.

REGULATIONS GOVERNING THE UNEMPLOYABLE GROUP 

A. They must agree to give up all gainful occupations.

B. They will be required to pay all debts. 

C. They will not be permitted to aid in supporting any able-bodied person in idleness.

D. They must agree to spend each month their total net income whether derived wholly or in part from their annuities, except that part used in building up a reserve fund to take care of such expenses as come at irregular intervals such as travel, doctors and dentists fees, etc.

E. They may build up a reserve fund, not to exceed $200. 

(Trade certificates if that method be used to finance the Unemployment and 

Annuity Fund - cannot be used to build up a reserve, as all Trade Certificates must be kept in circulation in order to earn the annuities. In order to build up a reserve fund, the annuitant may exchange his certificates for the usual U. S. currency, by placing an extra stamp on

each certificate.)

When and if the reserve fund reaches $200 and the annuitant is then unable to spend his annuity each month as indicated in the proposed law, it is evident the annuitant has chosen a maximum annuity beyond his capacity to legitimately consume; therefore he should at once notify the government, and ask the government to let him choose an annuity more in proportion to his needs.

If at any time the reserve fund is not sufficient to meet the demands of the annuitant's needs for irregular expenses, he may contract to pay such balance in monthly installments. When all the annuitant's obligations have been paid, or proper arrangements made for their monthly payment, he may again begin to build up his reserve fund wih any surplus he may have. 

F. Gifts to relatives or friends in the form of trade certificates, (if the second method of financing the plan is used), money or commodities, or all three combined shall not exceed 15% of the beneficiary's total monthly income. Gifts to churches and charities are exempt from this limitation.

G. No part of the annuitant's income shall be used in repeatedly getting drunk, or participating in drunken brawls, or in gambling; nor shall it be used in trading for gain or in speculating or investing for profit.

The sworn-to monthly statements of the unemployable annuitants will furnish the government workers with the figures necessary to judge whether or not the annuitants are spending their annuities in accordance with the proposed law. A hurried glance at the statements will be sufficient to arouse suspicion and place the government on its guard against an offender.

PENALTIES 

Congress, when enacting a law to put into operation the fundamental principles of the plan outlined above, should provide proper penalties for the violation of its provisions and requirements. Penalties for not complying with, or for circumventing the law, might be as follows:

A. Reducing the annuitant's income. 

B. Voiding the annuity and returning the investment. 

C. Voiding the annuity and retaining the investment.

D. Classifying as a misdemeanor the making of false reports, etc., with severe penalties for such offense.

TRADE CERTIFICATES & REGULATIONS GOVERNING THEIR USE 

(If the second method of financing the 

Fund is used, the following regulations

will govern; if the first method is the one employed, all mention of Trade Certificates should be disregarded.)

The law will require the Federal Government to issue Trade Certificates to the amount that the annuitants have invested with the government. Such Trade Certificates will be in denominations of 50c, $1, $2, $5, $10, and $20. On the back of these certificates will be spaces for affixing proper revenue stamps, which can be purchased at any United States postoffice. The revenue stamps shall be in denominations of 1c, 2c, 4c, 10c, 20c, and 40c.

Under the operation of this plan financed by the second method the government shall de clare the Trade Certificates legal tender. The law shall require everyone to accept them whenever offered in any transaction, and in paying for services, and in paying debts. The certificates must be kept in circulation, therefore, they cannot be banked; nor can they be given in change unless the holder places an extra stamp on them, or unless the receiver does not object, or unless certificates of a larger denomination were used in the transaction. 

If and when taxes or other public debts are paid with the certificates, (such might become necessary in certain instances), the organization or the taxing unit receiving them shall take them to the postoffice and receive in return the usual U. S. Currency. This becomes necessary so that the government can keep track of the number of

certificates in circulation, and can issue new ones in their place, thus sustaining the proper earning power.

The person or firm, receiving a certificate shall, the same day, affix the proper revenue stamp on its reverse side, placing the date and his own or his firm's initials on the stamp with pen and ink. Should he hold it longer than five days, the process will be repeated, and so on every fifth day until all the spaces for the stamps have been filled. It is to the holder's advantage, therefore, to use his certificate to make a purchase or liquidate a debt before the fifth day. 

Trade Cerificates shall be put into circulation by the government in paying annuitants their annuities, and, when necessary, in paying government employees part or all of their salaries. In order that these two classes shall not be penalized by having to put the certificates in circulation, they will not be required to affix the first revenue stamp. Instead, they will merely date and initial the first space at the time they pass the certificate on, the one receiving it affixing his stamp over the initials. Government employees and annuitants must pass the certificates on within a period of thirty days. However, when government employes and annuitants receive the certificates in the regular channels of trade, they shall be required to use the stamps as do other citizens and pass them on within five days.

The person holding the certificate when the

last space for stamps is filled shall take it to the postoffice where he will receive a new one in exchange. As he has already affixed a revenue stamp on the old certificate, all he will be required to do is date and initial the first space on the back of the new one, and then pass it on within five days.

EXAMPLES SHOWING HOW THE TWO PER CENT TRANSACTION TAX ON TRADE CERTIFICATES WILL EARN THE ANNUITIES 

The two per cent transaction tax would be effective in obtaining sufficient revenue to give the maximum annuity to each investor. Take as an example the $750 investment - the largest in the unemployable group. It would earn the $75 plus if it only turned over once every five days. 

If $750 of trade certificates turns over once every five days, it would turn over six times every month. Six times $750 equals $4500 worth of business done and two per cent of $4500 turnover equals $90 the amount of the annuity plus $15. 

The employables investment would also earn his annuity. In estimating this it is necessary to strike an average of unemployment. A safe average for the group, even in the worst of times under such a plan as this would be employment at least eight months each year.

As the $500 investment would earn at least $60 per month, in twelve months it would earn $720. Four months of enforced idleness would call for $375 in benefits. The difference will be ample to take care of those temporarily unemployed for a longer time than the estimated average. Those employables out of work longer than the average, even if the average is longer than estimated, would be receiving only half of the amount their investment would be earning. 

It is evident that their investments would be ample to care for them. 

As the majority of the Trade Certificates will turn over much faster than once every five days, the earnings would be considerably more than enough to pay both maximum annuities and overhead expense. That would be true in the case of both classes of investors. Therefore, the government would be freed from paying interest on some fifteen to twenty-five billion dollars, which, in itself would be a great help, hastening the time when the nation would be free of debt. 

ESTIMATE OF THE TOTAL THAT WOULD BE INVESTED 

It is very difficult, in fact impossible, to make an accurate estimation of the amount that would be invested. However, a fair estimate would be that between twelve and fifteen million unemployables would take advantage of such legislation; and between thirty and forty million

employables who would invest five hundred dollars each. We would, therefore, place the total investment at between fifteen and twentyfive billion dollars. The forced rapid spending of the annuities by the unemployables, together with the forced rapid turnover of the Trade Certificates representing the combined investment, would speed up business the like of which no country has ever before experienced. 

ESTIMATE OF WHAT PORTION OF THE NATION'S BUSINESS WOULD BE TAXED UNDER THE SECOND METHOD OF FINANCING THE FUND 

This estimate is also difficult to make, but an estimate of one-fourth of the nation's business is somewhere near correct. The required rapid turnover of the trade certificate does not alter the per cent of business transacted by the nation; but it does increase the nation's business very rapidly. That increase of business would be new business and would represent the amount of prosperity that would result by reason of the adoption of the proposed plan. A 2% transaction. tax on one-fourth of the total business done by an individual or firm would be a very small tax. It would be a tax that capitalists and big interests should be anxious to pay, both from the standpoint of their own prosperity as well as from the humanitarian principle of easing the life of the unemployables.

THE PROPOSED TAX TO FINANCE THE PLAN AND A FEW WORDS ON TAXES IN GENERAL 

Where is the money coming from to finance the plan to buy the revenue stamps? To some people that question seems unanswerable. Where is the money coming from to purchase a postage stamp with which to mail a letter? If you haven't the three cents, and can't get it, your letter goes unmailed; but that does not do away with the postal service, nor argue that the postal service is a poor governmental agency.

From where does the money come with which you pay your present sales tax, income tax, property tax: If you are without money, income, and property you are fairly well exempt from paying a tax of any kind. Suppose you have property, but are without money when tax day comes. You usually manage to rustle the necessary amount. The, same would hold true of a transaction tax. There would be no difference. 

If you have neither property nor service for sale you will have no occasion to buy revenue samps under the proposed plan. Those who do have commodities and services for sale will find a ready market. As about three-fourths of the individual's business will be transacted by the usual currency and credit you will experience little trouble in obtaining the small amount necessary for stamps. 

Authorities agree that a transaction tax,

wherein the seller is required to pay the tax, is a scientific and fair tax. Those affected by such a tax would be paying in proportion to the turnover of the dollar while manipulating money or wealth in attempting to accumulate more. Such a tax comes out of the profits on sales. As transactions are ordinarily made for profit, the more the turnover, the more able is the businessman to pay. In the case of those selling services, the larger the salary, the more able they are to pay. Practically all agree that we should pay taxes in proportion to our prosperity and ability to pay. A transaction tax is automatically a tax of that kind. NO TAX COULD BE FAIRER.

The proceeds of a transaction tax, used as described in this plan, would return to the taxpayer in added profits more than the amount he pays. New business that the system would develop would make this possible, and business in general would be speeded up to a marked degree. 

The tax any people can pay depends largely on what becomes of their tax money. If it goes to pay interest on vast sums to those who do not turn it into the channels of productivity, or if government is wasteful and a medium for graft, or if government expenditures fail to strengthen the nation's productive powers, or if, in the final analysis, the taxpayer fails to get a good percentage of it back; then a small tax becomes a real burden.

On the other hand, if the tax money finds its way back to the people in paying for essential

services and in promoting production of new wealth; the people can afford heavy taxes. 

Oliver Wendell Holmes once said: "Taxes are a privilege and not a burden."

In most cases that is true. It would be doubly true of a transaction tax used as here proposed.

All taxes should be so arranged that it would be impossible, or at least difficult, to pass them on. A property tax, an income tax, and a transaction tax levied against the seller, are examples of taxes so arranged. Present day sales taxes are automatically passed on.

Some people seem to think that if the proposed law required the seller to pay the tax, he would add it to the price of the commodity. He certainly would if he thought he could get the added price; but competition would, then as now, regulate prices and prevent him from adding the tax. Competition, supply and demand, alone regulate prices; they cannot be greatly affected by the desire of the merchant to add any certain amount to the price of his article. The seller at the present time, with the present sales tax paid by the buyer, asks all he thinks he can obtain.

It is self-evident that the passing on of the tax becomes automatic when the buyer is required. to pay it. That requirement frees the seller from paying the tax. That certainly would not be the case if the law required the seller to pay the tax instead of the buyer.

When the poor become poorer and are no

longer able to pay the accumulated, passed-on taxes, they are forced to pass them back in the only way they can-in sickness and in inability to longer care for themselves. The rebound taxes bring with them tax coupons, as it were. 

When passed-on taxes are passed back, it becomes the duty of society to take up the burden which has greatly increased since society passed the tax on.

Having to take up the relief burden and handle the charities is the heritage of those who passed the taxes on. In fact, this is one of the reasons for our present-day plight. Not only that, the body politic has suffered immensely and the poor have not only lost their health and morale but they have lost their sense of honesty, truthfulness, and integrity; and in case of some of the younger people they have turned to crime in order to exist.

IN SUPPORT OF THE PLAN 

Those of us who have had jobs right along and who, regardless of considerable shrinkage in any securities we may possess, have continued to enjoy three square meals per day, know little of the hell the unemployment situation has brought to millions of our good citizens. Nor do some realize how near to a real revolution our country came. That a revolution was averted by the creation of the CCC camps, the P. W. A. and the W. P. A. many of us admit. These

measures were not designed to permanently solve the unemployment situation. 

To do away with this emergency set-up today would leave us in the same sorry plight of the early depression years. The present relief program, piling up the national debt, must go on until some technique of government is adopted that will furnish practically all those now unemployed with jobs at a fair living wage. 

The mal-adjustment of social and economic conditions constitute the major trouble with the world today. This mal-adjustment is the cause of all present day wars, declared or undeclared. 

The dictatorships of fascism and communism are but attempts to solve it. Americans as a class prefer to shun these two isms; yet they may be driven to one or the other in order to exist.

Capitalism and democracy governed by liberalism and desire on the part of capitalists to help the masses, (the number of such capitalists is increasing), will save us from communism and fascism, and I may also add socialism. CAUSE OF UNEMPLOYMENT 

There is but one direct cause or immediate cause--withdrawal of immense sums of money from circulation and hoarding it.

CAUSES OF DEPRESSIONS 

The causes are too many and too complex to treat here. One of the main causes-wild speculation-deserves special mention. WHAT IS NEEDED TO PUT PEOPLE TO WORK 

A technique of government that will place sufficient money in the hands of the unemployed and unemployables each and every month to meet their daily needs and legitimate desires. Contrast this with the two methods that have been tried and found wanting: (a) Government loans to Big Business and reduction of its taxes. This "Trickle Down 

System" of the late Andrew W. Mellon is the 

Republican approach. It is doubtful if Big 

Business needs or wants loans at the present.

One cannot blame industry for not wishing to produce until there is an outlet for the goods already on merchants' shelves and in warehouses. Industry does not care to produce that which cannot be sold. (b) Pump priming a shot in the arm!

This is the Democratic approach. It saved us temporarily and was all that our government could do under the circumstances. 

Large scale spending must be put on a permanent basis if we wish our economic machine to function satisfactorily.* Whether it be governmental or private, or both; extensive and repeated spending is absolutely necessary. Under present conditions it has to be altogether too 

*Reference: "An Economic Program for America," by seven Harvard and Tufts economists.

much governmental spending. However, governmental spending must continue until some technique of government is adopted that will give to the unemployed and the unemployables consumer, purchasing power each and every month. 

The sensible approach therefore, is for the government to help the unemployed and the unemployables to help themselves by permitting them to invest and receive annuities. 

Senator Wm. E. Borah has recently said: "There is greater danger in the vast army of unemployed-in men, women and children living in constant sight of the poverty line, in mal-formed and rickety children-than in any fleet of battleships which any nation or group of nations may choose to send against us.

The danger is here in all its hideous ugliness eating away at the moral fiber of our people. These are the things that make for communism and facism: which wage war against every democracy. If democracy cannot find a cure for the disease, it is useless to talk of alliances and preparedness." 

Senator Borah is right and I hope he and his colleagues will give earnest attention to the remedy that has now been found to cure the dangerous malady. I sincerely hope that Senator

Borah and his colleagues will perform the operation that will cure the disease.

When Mr. Marriner S. Eccles, Chairman of the Federal Reserve Board, appeared before the Senate Committee investigating the problem of unemployment in the spring of 1938, he said: "The most important thing at the moment to lift us out of this depression is to sustain consumer buying power." 

Had Mr. Eccles said CREATE as well as "sustain" he would have added much to the force of his statement. 

Continuing, Mr. Eccles said: "A billion dollars of increased spending, if it worked quickly enough, would act as a necessary stimulus and stop the recession."

The plan herein proposed would furnish more than a billion dollars of increased spending, not only once, but once each month; thus creating the consumer buying power as well as the "sustaining" power of which Mr. Eccles spoke. 

John B. Kennedy, speaking before the Knife and Fork Club in Salt Lake City in early 1938, had this to say: "America has passed through a revolution in which the majority of the American people made clear that they will not live in economic insecurity across the border line from poverty. No

matter what arises that is a fact; and now it remains for us to find a technique of government which will produce this economic security without fostering idleness, which will bring economic safety to the majority without denying economic liberty to the minority."

The technique of government outlined in this thesis meets all the requirements suggested by Mr. Kennedy. It would increase the rapid and scientific flow of dollars simultaneously with an increase in the rapid flow of goods; a condition absolutely necessary to prosperity. It would furnish our economic system with a muchneeded balance wheel.

In a scholarly book entitled: "In Defense of 

Capitalism" written for business men by James 

H. R. Cromwell and Hugo E. Czerwonky, the authors have this to say: "A solution to this grave crisis will not be found by waiting for some miracle to be performed by the Constitution, nor by harking back to the good old days and the invincible spirit of our pioneer forefathers, nor by rabid and unthinking condemnation of the policies of our government. Unless the capitalists and business men of America get done with vain hopes and idle talk, and turn their minds to a comprehension of how our capitalistic system operates today, they

and they alone will be responsible for the death of individualism." 

And again from these same economists: "Business recessions and general unemployment are caused by lack of purchasing power which, in turn, springs from neglect to scientifically increase the flow of dollars with the flow of goods."

There is much to think about in these two quotations, much that is vital to our best interests and to the course of civilizaation.

The economic struggle of the masses has always exerted a devastating effect upon civili zation. Witness the French and Russian revolu tions and the present-day rise of fascism, nazism and communism.

In an article of October, 1938, Mr. Walter 

Lipman said: "It is always true and always has been all through history, that the arrival of masses without sustenance will, in the end, destroy civilization."

A few years ago the masses in Germany arrived without sustenance. The barbaric treatment of Jews and Catholics in that unhappy country today is the direct result. Fear and mental anguish engendered by privation and a lack of security have blunted the power of the

Germans to discriminate. It has warped their sense of justice. They even go so far as to punish a whole race for the acts of an individual, and compel the innocent to pay for the destruction perpetrated by others. 

We ourselves have experienced the arrival of the masses without sustenance. Had it not been for the timely intervention of our government, furnishing relief and temporary jobs for millions thrown out of work and in dire distress, we would have realized even more keenly the serious consequences to us and our civilization. 

Few will deny the serious effects it has had in making us poorer in the cardinal virtues. What may yet happen unless the proper remedy is applied can only be conjectured. 

To grope along with out present temporary set-up and with our present uncertainty and insecurity, when conditions could be easily and quickly remedied, is a poor commentary on our intelligence; or else it speaks volumes for our stubborn desire to hang on to the old order, regardless of consequences. Could we but realize the physical and mental suffering of the unemployed and unemployables, (not entirely unlike that of Jews and Catholics in present-day 

Germany) I feel sure we would urge our government to adopt a technique that would remedy widespread unemployment and care for the unemployed and unemployables; and that too, in a way which would be a real credit to our nation.

President Roosevelt, in his annual message to Congress January 4th, 1939, said: "The first duty of our statesmanship today is to bring capital and man power together. "Dictatorships do this by main force.

By using main force they apparently succeed for the moment. However we abhor their methods, we are compelled to admit that they have obtained substantial utilization of all their material and human resources. Like it or not, they have solved, for the time at least, the problem of idle men and idle capital. Can we compete with them by boldly seeking methods of putting idle men and idle capital together and, at the same time, remain within our American way of life, within the bill of rights, and within the bounds of what is, from our point of view, civilization?"

In answering his own question in the affirmative the President stressed the necessity of putting capital to work. Therein lies the rub. Dictators coerce, constitutional governments plead; but the fact remains that capital goes to work willingly only under one condition-when it can show a profit.

The author would answer the President's question with even a more emphatic YES. He, too, would stress the necessity of getting both

labor and capital to work. If it will make a continuous market for the goods that Industry produces by placing in the hands of the jobless and the unemployables every month sufficient money to care for their daily needs, then there will be no question of what capital will do. 

DIFFERENT METHODS OF FINANCING THE PLAN 

The reader will recall that two methods of financing the Unemployment and Annuity Fund were offered. While the general transaction tax plan is better in some ways than the Trade Certificate plan, the author fears that its proposed enactment into law would meet with more resistance than would the trade certificate plan, owing to the fact that it would tax more of the turnover on the sale of stocks and bonds. In this opinion he may be mistaken.

The fact that both the people and the government would benefit greatly by the adoption of the plan, no matter which way it might be financed, would compensate for the unemployables receiving fair-sized annuities in proportion of their investment. However, similar large profits are not entirely unknown to financiers. Why shouldn't our government begin to manipulate money in the interest of ALL, on the basis of the good it will accomplish? If this plan was adopted, none could charge the unemployables with obtaining something for nothing. 

Please consider thoughtfully the two ways

of supplying and renewing the Unemployment and Annuity Fund. Either can be used in financing the plan. The author is quite undecided which method is the better. In making comparisons, he finds advantages and disadvantages in each. Congress can decide that matter better than he.

ADVANTAGES OF FINANCING THE PLAN WITH TRADE CERTIFICATES 

1. The annuitan's investment, as represented by the taxable turnover of the trade certificates issued against it, would furnish his annuity and more, after paying all expenses. 

2. The rapid required turnover of the annuitant's dollar would speed up business immensely in comparison with the general transaction tax method of financing. 

3. The transaction tax on the trade certificate would be levied on about one-fourth of the nation's business. This would please many. 

4. The great masses of our citizens would not have to make monthly or quarterly reports; only unemployable annuitants would be required to report. Relief from making such reports would mean much to the majority. (Possibly no report would be necessary with a general transaction tax. Perhaps a method of collecting could be found without such reports.) 

5. The expense of operation would be little in comparison to the other method, or any other

method. The cost of printing the trade certificates and revenue stamps, together with the hiring of an extra clerk or two in the larger postoffices, would be small in comparison to the clerical help needed to collect a general transaction tax, and to look after the monthly or quarterly reports. However, the expense of administering the general transaction tax plan would be but a fraction of the cost of our present social-economic set-up. 

ADVANTAGES TO THE GOVERNMENT 

The trade certificate method of financing would be much more advantageous to the government than would the general transaction tax; as the revenue from the fund after paying all overhead and the maximum annuities would probably be much greater. This surplus, when applied to reducing the nation's indebtedness, would in a reasonable length of time get the government out of debt. Following that, it would serve to lower general taxes for the support of government. The government could employ to good advantage the money invested by the annuitants. 

A large part of such trust fund could be used in retiring U. S. bonds, and in avoiding the necessity of issuing new tax-exempt bonds. The government would then collct more taxes than at present from the holders of such tax-exempt bonds. This would be a valuable feature and one the government should welcome.

ADVANTAGES OF THE PLAN IN GENERAL 

Under the plan as outlined, all classes of citizens as well as the government would benefit. 

All classes would contribute towards its operation. The author grants that under the trade certificate method of financing, the various classes of citizens would not contribute strictly in proportion to their wealth; rather, they would contribute more or less in proportion to the benefit they received.

It is obvious that under the trade certificate plan those rendering services and those engaged in retail trade would contribute the most toward the Unemployment and Annuity Fund. All business would be contributing some. It is also obvious that these two classes would benefit in proportion to the amount they paid, hence there would be little cause for complaint. It is also self-evident that the more the people would pay under this plan, so long as the tax remained the same, the more they would be prospering. 

Most anyone would be glad to pay 12% for new business, or for a raise in salary. The adoption of this plan would vastly increase new business and insure a fair raise in salaries. 

The principle of the plan has been tried in a limited way, using a two per cent transaction tax. Its trial speeded up business very materially with all those who participated. Practically all who paid the tax were highly pleased with the experiment and would like to see the plan put into operation by the Federal Government.

The operation of this plan is also possible by the State or by a corporation. If the Federal 

Government does not soon adopt this or a similar plan, the various states would do well to do so; but the advantages and benefits would be correspondingly less. 

LITTLE OPPOSITION TO THE PLAN 

After a thorough study and analysis of the proposed plan, those who would oppose such beneficial legislation must surely have other reasons than wanting 'to escape the payment of such a small tax. Especially as it will greatly reduce other taxes. The author cannot believe that anyone has a desire to perpetuate recurrent depressions nor can he believe that anyone wishes to keep the unfortunate unemployables on the verge of starvation. Even those who have a passion for power-those who desire to control industry, prices, production and pay-cannot logically oppose such a plan. If they have no other reason, and the author can think of none, such people must have extremely small soulssouls so small that the combined group would have ample room to dance the big apple on the point of a cambric needle.

CLOSING REMARKS 

This plan has much to commend it. It would benefit all and injure none. It is a "payas-you-go" proposition; that is, nothing is paid out that has not been taken in.

It would greatly benefit our government by aiding materially in defraying the nation's indebtedness and balancing the budget. 

1. By making available a vast sum of money, interest free. 

2. By using the surplus in retiring the national debt. 

3. By receiving considerable more revenue from 'those now holding taxexempt bonds.

As no fixed amount would be guaranteed the annuitants, there would be no risk of running the government into debt. 

The reader will note that no one would be compelled to apply for an annuity. It would be purely a matter of choice. No wage earner, no matter what his age or physical condition, would be compelled to give up work and retire. 

The plan's adoption would obviate the necessity for pensions, whether old age, state, federal, or industrial. It would relieve individuals, organizations and the states of the care of the permanently crippled and blind. It would be cheap insurance against unemployment, and cheap insurance against the hazards of loss of eye-sight, permanent crippling, and infirmity due to age. 

It would relieve industry of the care of workers permanently disabled to the extent of fifty per cent or more. This would be a real saving to industry. The adoption of this plan would further help industry, and all those who employ labor by freeing them from the penalizing effects

of the present social-security law.

Under the present Social Security Act the more men the employer puts to work, the more money he himself is required to pay. This tends to curtail employment. Then, too, the much larger tax levied against the wage earner under the Social Security Act than 'would be under the proposed plan, (1% gradually increased to 

6% as against 12 per cent with no increase) is a distinct disadvantage to the laborer. 

The gradual piling up of an immense reserve fund as is being done under the Social Security Act, seems to the writer to be poor economics, as it gradually lessens the consumer's purchasing power. Hoarded money is of no use. The faster money circulates and especially among the consuming masses the more useful it becomes. 

The proposed plan would do away with the dread of economic insecurity for the crippled, blind and aged. It would do away with the overwhelming relief problem we now face. It has been estimated that about 80% of crime is due to bad economic conditions. Certainly the operation of this plan would alleviate that condition. It would actually save billions of dollars annually, now used in crime detection and prevention and for the erection and upkeep of penal institutions. 

Idleness in the able-bodied breeds crime. If the plan did no more than return the millions of our now idle able bodied citizens to work, where they would again earn a livelihood for their families and produce new wealth for the nation, it would

be worthy of adoption. 

No doubt the reader can think of other things this plan would correct and some things it would foster, such as encouraging business to act on a cash basis, and fostering truthfulness, honesty and integrity.

Some skeptical readers, who perhaps have not yet fully grasped the principles of the plan; and who therefore, should reread it, will be thinking that the writer is too optimistic. All such will at least have to admit there could be but little poverty in our land if all the unemployed and the unemployables were getting a fair sized annuity each and every month, month after month, and year after year.

Our President and the Congress are chosen by us to make beneficial laws for the welfare of all citizens. Such a law as herein proposed would certainly do more good to the great masses of our citizens than would any other one measure that might be adopted. The passage of such a law would rejuvenate and revitalize our underprivileged citizens, and make the United States a most happy and forward-looking nation. 

Therefore the author appeals to you, Mr.

President and the Congress, to enact such a law at the earliest possible moment without waiting to be urged or petitioned. At least eighty per cent of our citizens would be grateful to you for such action and the other twenty per cent certanly can have no valid reason for objecting to such a law. 

J. W. AIRD, M. D.
