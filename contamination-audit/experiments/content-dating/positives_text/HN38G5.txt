TO THE STUDENT 

THIS workbook will give you a chance for self-expression as well as guide you in (1) thinking out the answers for yourself, (2) watching current periodicals for topics allied to your daily lessons, and (3) observing that all the world about you is a lesson in geography. 

The study of economic geography is valuable to everyone, because it shows that the everyday life of man is directly influenced by world-wide economic and political conditions. Such conditions are constantly being influenced and changed. Therefore, newspapers and current periodicals can be used to great advantage to add interest and prove the practicability of this subject. You will find some special pages in this book which may be used for mounting clippings of current events. If you need more pages, school paper may be used and inserted at the proper places. The selection of these clippings affords an opportunity to develop a discriminating sense of that which has permanent value as contrasted with that which has value for the moment only. No article should be pasted on the current events pages unless it has value for the entire year. The time to begin your collection of clippings is early in the course, mounting them in the workbook under the subject to which they are related. If you wait until you study the subject or country, it will be hard to find good clippings at just that particular time. 

A comparison of the news gathered by the different students will add much to the up-to-the-minute interest of the class at discussion time. 

Maps: The modified equal-area outline maps are a special feature of this book. 

These maps will help you work out for yourself some of the important relationships that exist between people, places, and resources. The maps are free of the usual map detail, so the completed map should give you a clear picture of the country, its resources, and its industries. The relationship between these will then be apparent. 

Arrows pointing outward may be used to carry the names of the exports; inward, the names of imports. Only facts of unusual interest or world importance should be placed on the maps. Too many local details become confusing. The use of a red and a blue pencil will add much to the appearance of the map. 

Shade the water areas or the water's edge in blue and the division lines between countries in red. Use a fine pen or a hard, black pencil for all writing and lettering. 

Graphs: The many graphs in this workbook offer opportunities to study the subject more thoroughly in a very practical way. 

The graphs also help you to see relationships much better and more easily.

There are many different types of graphs, but for this study the more simple types will better serve us. Examples of the bar, the line, the rectangle, and the circle graph are given on pages v and vi. Some material is best presented by the use of one type of graph, other material lends itself better to another type. You are to select the type of graph to be used in each case.

In the second part of the book, the study of foreign lands, your class might divide into groups, with each group responsible for a special country. Each group can prepare the bulletin board for the time that you are studying their particular country or subject. 

Many of you will have access to actual goods brought from these countries and should be

This graph (sometimes called the "pie" graph) serves the same purpose as the rectangle graph. The circle must be divided into equal parts. A regular school protractor may be used to find the amount of space to plot for each section. The protractor divides a circle into 360 degrees and, therefore, to find the number of degrees to allow for each section, simply multiply the percentage by 3.6.
