REPORT TO SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

INTRODUCTION 

1. The most important development in the administrative supervision of the judicial system occurred on April 25, 1967, with the adoption by the Justices of the Supreme Judicial Court of 

Rule 3:16 Judicial Conference. 

2. Among the recommendations of the Judicial Survey Commission in 1956 was authorization for the organization by the 

Justices of a judicial conference. St. 1956, c. 707, §2, added to

G. L. c. 211, §3, a new §3 F, which provides for the holding of judicial conferences "for the consideration of matters relating to judicial business, the improvement of the judicial system and the administration of justice." The purposes and prospects of the 

Massachusetts Judicial Conference are discussed in paragraphs 

24-31 of this report. 

3. The reporting year, ending June 30, 1967, has been marked by increased public interest in the problems of the courts. Two excellent series of articles have appeared in the Boston newspapers, one a study of the District Court system, the other a national study of the work of the trial courts. However, there remains widespread misunderstanding of and indifference to the needs of the judicial system. 

4. With continuing emphasis both in the appellate and trial courts on criminal law, judges and lawyers have been called upon to devote increased time and energy to a branch of the law which a few years ago was considered by many an unattractive portion of the work of the profession. The publication of The Challenge of 

Crime in a Free Society, the debates over Free Press and Fair Trial, and the alarming increase in criminal activity have all focused public attention upon the function of the judicial system in the preservation of public safety and of human rights. 

5. Since one of the duties of the Office of the Executive Secretary is to inform the public of the needs of the judiciary, the increased awareness of the people of the Commonwealth of the importance of the courts is an encouraging sign. Even though the average citizen rarely enters a court house as a party, witness, juror, or spectator, he is a daily observer in his newspaper or on his radio or television screen of some aspect of the performance of the courts.

10. Many factors are involved in the movement of population to the suburbs. Important among the causes is the development of improved highways and of rapid transit by private automobiles. 

Routes 128 and 495 have played a large part in the growth of business and residential areas in communities spreading constantly further from Boston. The district courts in these communities are particularly affected by the increasing case loads which accompany population expansion.

DISTRICT COURTS 

11. In spite of the fact that most of the full-time judges of the district courts are faithfully discharging their duties which include not only the hours spent in the trial and disposition of civil and criminal cases in their own courts, in taking assignments in other courts, in appellate division work, and in administrative labors, the impression is still prevalent that district court judges spend very little time at work. 

12. To a layman this impression, wrong as it is in so many cases, means that we have more than enough judges and, therefore, there is no need for more trial judges, even in the superior court. Unfortunately the misconception is so strong that many persons with considerable knowledge of the operation of the courts are ready to accept the conclusion and are unable to be persuaded that the premise is largely false and that the conclusion does not follow. 

13. In order to improve the efficiency of the operation of the district court system and to correct the public's notion of its character more progress must be made in completing the reorganization of the district courts. A system, or rather lack of one, developed in the nineteenth century to satisfy the needs of that period is inadequate to meet the conditions and demands of the remaining decades of the twentieth century. 

14. The studies and recommendations of the commissions which have dealt with the structure of the district courts are a good beginning but they are largely out of date. The regular case load, civil and criminal, of the district courts has increased about 82% in the last ten years. 

15. The trial and disposition of cases transferred from the superior court, and in several courts the provision of six man jury misdemeanor sessions, have imposed additional heavy burdens upon the judges and the offices of the clerks. Due largely to shifts

16. Since any major change in the organization of the district courts will meet with strong resistance, it is essential that earlier studies of the system be brought up to date and projections of the future needs of the courts be prepared. 

17. With this material a plan covering at least ten years should be developed that would provide step by step for the needed facilities and for the personnel to operate the courts efficiently. 

18. Without such planning the patchwork process of new court house construction and of personnel changes will continue. 

The system will remain an anachronism. 

19. The district courts employ more people, serve more people, and cost more to operate than any other court system in the Commonwealth. It is difficult to understand why the public does not demand a more efficient organization. 

20. Since the changes to bring about reorganization require legislative action, it is easy to blame the legislators for the inadequacies of the courts. The legislators, however, are not entirely responsible for the fact that each year they are swamped with bills which look for individual rather than general treatment of court problems. 

21. Typical of such bills are those which seek to change a small court from part-time to full-time, to increase a clerk's salary, or to add an additional assistant or court officer. Many of these bills have merit but the Legislature should not be asked to spend its time on dozens of such measures while the more important general problems remain unsolved.

PROBATE COURTS 

22. Last year's report noted the disparity between the population served and case load handled by a judge in the largest fulltime probate court and one in the smallest. No satisfactory method of equalizing the work has been devised. 

23. The nearest approach to a solution is the assignment of judges to larger courts as they are needed. There is no provision at present to equalize the salary for full-time judges so assigned. 

This should be corrected in fairness to the judges in the smaller courts. It would also tend to raise their level of compensation to that of a full-time district court judge.

CIVIL PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE 

32.

Experience with the changes in pre-trial discovery prac tice effected by Supreme Judicial Court Rule 3:15 on Depositions and Discovery has failed to produce the calamities foretold by the opponents of the rule. The rule has furnished to lawyers in cases which merit the use of oral depositions a satisfactory means of preparation of their cases for settlement or trial. 

33. By June 30, 1967, the rule had been in effect for a year and three months and had gained general acceptance by trial law yers. Section 7 on Physical and Mental Examination of Persons had furnished to the legal and medical professions considerable relief from the difficulties encountered by both groups in making expert medical opinion available without the inconveniences attendant upon holding doctors for court appearances. 

34. Unfortunately statistics are not available to answer many interesting questions. How often have the various discovery procedures been used? By the plaintiff? By the defendant? In what types of cases? In cases involving how much probable recovery?

How often has the use of the discovery procedures contributed to the settlement of cases? To more effective trial of cases? How

expensive has the procedure turned out to be? What improvements in the rule are desirable? Has the rule to some extent lightened the work load of the Superior Court? 

35. In the absence of statistical information the answers might be obtained by a survey of experience and opinion of a representative sample of trial judges and trial lawyers. By way of suggestion to an author on trial practice, an article on Oral Discovery-Two Years After would be good reading in the spring of 

36. A side effect of the adoption of Rule 3:15 has been the increased interest of lawyers in the whole problem of judicial rule making and its place in the law making process. Does it mark a turning point in Massachusetts in the acceptance of greater responsibility by the courts for the improvement of legal procedure? 

37. One of the purposes of the Keeton-O'Connell automobile insurance plan was to provide the accident victim with early compensation payments. This purpose underlies the provisions of St. 

1967, c. 259, amending G. L. c. 231 by adding §§140B and 140C. 

Advance payments are permitted without affecting the question of liability, are inadmissible in evidence, and are credited on the plaintiff's judgment. No settlement or partial payment made under a liability insurance policy of a claim against the insured shall be construed as an admission of liability with respect to a claim arising from the same event. 

38. The new law has not been in effect for a long enough period in order to prove its effectiveness. It does remove an obstacle from the path of the accident victim in receiving early compensation. Whether tactics of attrition will frustrate the purpose of the legislation remains to be seen. 

39. The trial of personal injury cases is frequently hampered by the death of an attending or examining physician. St. 1967, c. 265, amending G. L. c. 233 by adding §79H, makes the medical report, including expressions of medical opinion, admissible in evidence, at the discretion of the trial judge. 

40. Other developments include provision for the appointment of an official stenographer to record proceedings before an auditor or master. St. 1967, c. 138. A defendant may accept a judge's addition to a verdict so that a verdict shall not be set aside on the ground that the damages are inadequate. St. 1967, c. 139.

41. Most of the important changes in criminal practice and procedure took place after the end of the reporting year and will be the subject of comment in the next report of this office.

Time-lag as measured by the clerks of court and reported at page 14 necessarily differs from the above figures, since it is determined by a mathematical average of all cases from the civil trial lists which were reached and held for trial and disposed of (by trial or settlement) in a three-month period ending June 30, 1967. 55. From 1960 on the pressure of the increasing criminal business of the superior court has been an important factor in reducing the judicial manpower available for the civil lists. At the start of the period criminal cases were kept reasonably current by diverting judges and civil sessions for criminal work. 

56. In many of the larger counties the courts now have been unable to dispose of enough criminal cases to equal the intake of indictments and misdemeanor appeals from the district courts. 

The prospect of the dismissal of criminal cases involving serious criminal charges because of delay in the courts should be realized by the public and by their representatives. Prosecutors and court officials are well aware of the prospect and are powerless to correct the situation without the necessary personnel, funds and facilities. 

57. Studies of the problem in other jurisdictions with the use of modern methods of analysis show that the criminal process must be viewed from start to finish, that is, from enforcement to the courts to the correctional mechanism. The courts are a link and a central one in the criminal process but delay between arrest and trial may be greater in earlier stages than between indictment and trial. It was the conclusion of the District of Columbia study, for example, that the process there could be accelerated at its slowest point by calling an additional grand jury. 

58. Systems analysis is a forbidding term and doubtless is foreign to most judges and lawyers as applied to the system of criminal justice. The system is complex and involves all of the agencies engaged in criminal investigation and enforcement, in adjudication, and in correction. Without the data concerning all phases of the process the best of electronic equipment cannot analyze the system.

DATA PROCESSING 

71. If the clerks of the various courts had competition for their work, they would be out of business. The courts with few exceptions have not been equipped with the technical improvements that are commonplace in the competitive world of business. 

Too many are using the same methods of administration that prevailed before the turn of the century.

P.D. 166

72. Some part of the blame, though perhaps a small part of it, rests with the judges and the personnel of their courts who feel that the old ways are necessarily the best ways. The enlightenment will only come when the overburdened courts realize that they are unable to do the day's work in a day's time by use of the old methods. At that point the courts must seek to improve their business methods. 

73. Few courts are sufficiently large to justify the use of electronic data processing equipment but there are none so small that they could not benefit from the use of improved business methods and equipment.

THE JUDICIARY 

74. The selection and tenure of judges continue to be subjects of discussion in many states. The present trend in the states where judges have been selected in partisan elections for a term of years is toward executive appointment from a commission list for a term with the question of the judge's continuance in office thereafter submitted to the voters. 

75. In Massachusetts the power of appointment of judges rests solely in the Governor. His appointments are by and with the advice and consent of the Executive Council and judges hold their offices during good behavior. The commission plan would limit the constitutional authority of the Governor. The last two 

Governors have voluntarily sought the advice of responsible representatives of the bar in the determination of the qualifications of aspirants for judicial office. 

76. The operation of the Massachusetts system for the selection of judges is highly satisfactory. The question remains, however, as to whether there is need for other than existing methods for dealing with the problem of judicial incapacity. A proposed rule to establish a commission to deal with complaints of such incapacity has been submitted to the Supreme Judicial Court by the Massachusetts and Boston Bar Associations. 

77. Efforts have not yet succeeded in procuring the passage of general legislation that would permit the recall of retired judges for temporary service. A limited recall provision is contained in St. 1967, c. 620, §6. This is the temporary act that established procedures for the hearing and determination in the Superior Court of petitions of persons alleging illegal confinement at Bridgewater state hospital and at other state mental institutions.

P.D. 19 

78. A bill for the recall of retired judges of the Supreme 

Judicial and Superior Courts has been approved by the Judicial Conference. This is an inexpensive method of increasing the manpower of the courts since the additional cost is the difference between the pension rate of the retired judge and the salary rate of the office. Among the many advantages of recall is its flexibility which permits the immediate use of experienced judges to meet temporary needs without the delay and expense involved in adding new judicial offices. 

79. Recall has proved to be very useful in many other jurisdictions and would strengthen the judiciary in Massachusetts. 

The bill deserves prompt and favorable action by the Legislature. 

JUDICIAL SALARIES 

80. Although several salary adjustment bills were filed in 

1967, no general revision of judicial salaries occurred. In previous reports the inequities in the present salary structure have been discussed. The most apparent of these, the level of compensation for the justices of the Supreme Judicial Court in relation to that in the other courts, should be corrected. 

81. A careful study of the judicial salary structure, such as those which resulted in the Griswold Commission Report in 1962 and in the Healey Commission Report in 1963, is essential for the purpose of appraising the changes which have taken place both in Massachusetts and in the other large states since those studies were undertaken. Without such an appraisal salary adjustments will perpetuate, and even tend to increase, existing inequities.

By Resolves 1967, c. 82, the life of the commission was extended with the provision that its final report be filed on or before December 25, 1968, and the membership of the commission was increased.

BARNSTABLE COUNTY 

86. The bill that would provide for the construction and equipping of a court house for the Barnstable district court, for alterations to the present court house, and for other county improvements and would authorize borrowing up to $1,970,000 has been enacted and approved. St. 1967, c. 771. 

87. The bill providing for the construction and equipping of a court house for the Provincetown court and for borrowing up to $900,000 has been enacted and approved. St. 1967, c. 657. The bill calls for the location to be in the town of Orleans. The court now sits in Provincetown and Harwich. 

88. The construction of the new building will not only furnish lower Cape Cod with a modern facility but will eliminate the inadequacy and inefficiency of the two existing court premises. 

BERKSHIRE COUNTY 

89. The gravest need for a new court house exists in the City of North Adams. Both in location with its lack of off-street parking and in its cramped and otherwise inadequate quarters on the second floor of an old building, the district court is poorly housed and should be relocated in a new building. 

90. Choice of a site and plans for the building should be considered with the possibility of a central court to serve the present districts covered by the Williamstown, North Adams, and Adam courts. Details of a plan to provide for a single court for the communities of northwestern Massachusetts must await the availability of adequate and convenient facilities.

93. The revival of the central court house project is first a matter of local concern and apparently greater effort must be expended by the local bar and court officials in order to force action. 

However, the county's responsibility for the provision of "suitable court houses" is a delegated responsibility of the Commonwealth. Failure to provide adequate facilities for the sessions of the Superior Court is a matter of general concern.

96. The district courts at Fall River and New Bedford, each located in the business district and without proper space and facilities, inside and out, are victims of the delay. 

DUKES COUNTY

SUPERIOR COURT 

127. The Superior Court has been forced to meet its problems without an increase in the number of its judges and, for the most part, without the improvement in its facilities that would aid its performance. In 1968 the court will have four more associate justices. St. 1967, c. 850.

But even their best efforts have not been enough to prevent further delay in reaching cases for trial as noted earlier in this report. 

129. The disposition of important civil and criminal cases requires a high order of judicial ability. The work cannot be performed satisfactorily on a production line basis. It is unfair to a superior court judge to expect of him the highest level of performance if he is hurried from one case to another without the time to study his pending cases and to keep abreast of developments in the law.

THE MUNICIPAL COURT OF THE CITY OF BOSTON 

148. The court business, both civil and criminal, has increased substantially. 155. The Massachusetts Defenders Committee continues to provide counsel in order to meet the standards of the Gault decision. Frequent multiple sessions of the court have created a problem of conflicting engagements of the defender. CONCLUSION 

156. The work of the office of the Executive Secretary has increased substantially with the additional duties connected with the activities of the Massachusetts Judicial Conference. Our performance will improve if we are authorized to enlarge our staff. 

Further study and research in the administrative problems of the courts and the development of trained professional personnel are essential to the future effectiveness of the supervision of the court system by the Justices of the Supreme Judicial Court. 

157. The Executive Secretary continues to answer requests from the Governor's office for advice upon bills affecting the administration of the courts. This service during a long and active legislative session requires prompt action upon, as well as careful analysis of, a large number of bills. It is hoped that this activity is helpful to the Governor in the discharge of his responsibility for speedy action upon the bills which are laid before him. 

158. The office also supplies information on court matters to members of the Legislature for their assistance in the study of proposed legislation relating to the judicial department. Much remains to be accomplished in the way of reducing the numbers of bills which are filed every year relating to a single court position. It would help the legislators if the administrative officers of the various courts could devise some plan for study and consolidation of such proposals before they reach the filing stage. 

159. Requests are often received from judges and court administrators in other states for information on our system. This office in turn has had occasion to make similar inquiries of others. 

This exchange of information, the examination of court adminis-

There appears to be increasing understanding by each court of the problems of the other courts and of the need for a united effort to improve the quality of the performance of the whole judicial system.

 FOREWORD TO APPENDICES 

The court cost tables, state and county, in Appendix I, show the resumption of substantial increases in both gross and net expenditures. The former were higher by about $3,188,000; the latter, by about $2,082,000. Increased county receipts credited to the courts as they appear in the county figures in Appendix I account for the lower net increase. 

The cost reporting methods in this report have been used consistently in earlier reports. They show accurately the expenditures and receipts by the Commonwealth and the counties in the operation of the courts.

The large capital costs of building new court houses and of capital improvements to existing court houses are reflected in the figures only to the extent of interest charges on bonded debt. 

In all counties except Suffolk very large sums collected by the courts in fines which are paid by statute to the cities and towns do not appear as receipts since they do not enter into county accounting. A fully consolidated system of cost accounting which would include these court receipts would reduce very substantially the amount of the net cost of court operations. 

In the large cities the volume of receipts, mainly from traffic tickets and complaints, may well exceed the gross cost of operating the court. This is the case in the Boston Municipal Court where receipts of $2,008,218.45 exceeded expenditures of $1,382,- 

428.51 by $625,789.94.

No credit in Appendix I is given to the courts for collections under the Uniform Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act, since these too are not part of the county accounts. 

While it cannot be asserted that the courts are self-supporting, it can safely be stated that each inhabitant of the Commonwealth is responsible for closer to $2 year than to $4 a year as his share of the cost of operating the courts.

 APPENDIX II REPORT OF THE STATISTICS OF THE WORK ACCOMPLISHED BY THE VARIOUS COURTS 

In the ten years of reports of total civil and criminal entries in the various courts, the total entries have increased 94%. For 

1958 total entries were 567,691; in 1967, 1,103,084.

These figures show that the work load of the judicial department has almost doubled in a ten year period. Case entries bear a fairly constant relationship to cases tried. Thus, the total entry figures demonstrate that judges, clerks, probation officers, prosecutors, and all the other officers of the courts are handling almost twice the case load of ten years ago.

The gross cost increase in the same period is from $17,312,000 in 1958 to $30,148,000 in 1967, a rise of 74%. 

Since a large part of the cost increase is attributable to salary increases, and since the total number of judges has risen very little, it is clear that the judicial department as a whole is badly understaffed.

The population increase, about ten per cent in this period, does not explain the vastly greater increase in case entries.

Superior Court, 152 law, 90 equity, and 11 Workmen's Compensation. The remainder came from the Land Court, 2; from the Probate Courts, 25; from the District and Boston Municipal Courts, 14. The volume of cases from the single justice session remained the same as in the previous year; from the District and Boston 

Municipal Courts the number doubled; from other sources the numbers decreased. Last year's total was 331. All cases were decided during the year.

On the average more time elapsed between the entry of cases and the decisions than in the previous year. The average time was a little less than six months as compared with about four months in the year before. These figures do not measure the performance of the Court in its decision process which commences at the time of argument or submission on briefs. 

Of that time almost 74% was from entry to consultation, longer than the previous year's 70%. A rough average of the time from argument or submission on briefs to decision was somewhat less than two and one-half months.

In the earlier years of the reports no problem of delay existed. As the problem began to emerge, the solution lay in the assignment of superior court judges from civil to criminal sessions.

 APPENDIX III THE JUDICIAL SYSTEM A. THE SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

The chief justice and six associate justices. 

Final appellate jurisdiction in all cases. 

Original jurisdiction-mainly petitions for extraordinary writs-single justice.

Advisory opinions at the request of either branch of the Legislature or the Governor or the Executive Council. 

General superintendence of all courts of inferior jurisdiction.

B. THE SUPERIOR COURT 

The chief justice and forty-five associate justices. 

General trial jurisdiction, jury and non-jury. Circuit system.

C. THE PROBATE COURTS 

The chief judge, twenty-three judges, two part-time judges, and one special judge.

Jurisdiction of probate of wills, administration of estates, appointment of guardians and conservators, divorce and annulment of marriages, separate maintenance, adoption and change of name. Equity jurisdiction except as otherwise provided by statute. County system except judges subject to assignment by chief judge.

D. THE LAND COURT 

The judge and two associate judges. 

Jurisdiction in land titles, registrations and uses.

E. THE DISTRICT COURTS, THE BOSTON MUNICIPAL COURT, THE BOSTON JUVENILE COURT 

The chief justice, sixty-one associate justices, eighteen part-time justices, and eighty-one special justices. 

Jurisdiction of lesser criminal cases and of civil actions at law and of juvenile cases. Non-jury, except where six-man jury sessions being tried experimentally in certain county seats. 

Five-judge appellate divisions.

The chief justice, eight associate justices, and five special justices of the Municipal Court of the City of Boston. 

The justice and two special justices of the Boston Juvenile Court.
