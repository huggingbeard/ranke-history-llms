INTRODUCTION 

A person's responsibility to pay for damage he causes has been recognized in most civilized societies. The mechanics of establishing this responsibility may involve a law suit in which the person injured, the plaintiff, must show that the defendant was negligent because he failed to perform a duty, and that this failure caused injury to the plaintiff or his property. This concept of "civil liability" was developed prior to the advent of the motor vehicle and, with some adjustment, has been broadened to cover damages resulting from its operation.

Under legal doctrines called "contributory negligence" and "comparative negligence, " however, the plaintiff may have his compensation reduced or completely denied, notwithstanding the negligence of the defendant, when his own actions or wrongdoing also contributed to the accident and resulting injuries. Even if he avoids these problems, the plaintiff may be unable to recover for any injuries which he could have reasonably avoided by taking proper care. This legal doctrine is referred to as "avoidable consequences. 11 In recent automobile accident cases, defendants have attempted to avoid civil liability by introducing evidence that the plaintiff was not wearing an available safety belt. This defense, hereinafter referred to as the seat belt defense, is predicated mainly on two theories: 

(1) The non-use of a seat belt caused the plaintiff's injuries and therefore the defendant should not be liable; and (2) The defendant should not be liable for injuries that could have been avoided by the use of a seat belt.

These cases present issues found in other civil liability cases. 

Was the plaintiff negligent in failing to wear a seat belt? Assuming the plaintiff's negligence in failing to wear a seat belt, was this negligence the proximate cause of the plaintiff's injuries? If negligence and proximate cause are established, will the plaintiff's negligence bar all recovery or merely bar recovery for those injuries which were caused by the non-use of a seat belt? Finally, if the plaintiff's negligence did not cause the injuries, will recovery still be denied for those injuries which could have been avoided by the use of a seat belt?

This Commentary examines these issues by reviewing court decisions on the non-use of automobile safety belts in civil litigation. It updates and expands a discussion of these issues in a 1972 Commentary entitled "Laws Requiring Seat Belts."1

CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE 

Contributory negligence is negligence which contributes to the accident, having a causal connection with it, and but for which the accident would not have happened. The doctrine precludes recovery by one injured in a motor vehicle accident in an action based upon the defendant's negligence, where there is any fault on the part of the injured person, even though small in comparison with that of the defendant, if the negligence of the injured person contributed proximately to the occurrence of the accident and the defendant did not have the last clear chance to avoid the accident.2/

In many of the seat belt cases, defendants have alleged that the non-use of a seat belt constitutes contributory negligence. If the plaintiff is contributorily negligent in failing to wear a seat belt, then he is barred from recovering from the defendant. 

Before discussing the issue of contributory negligence as it relates to the seat belt defense, however, it is necessary to understand the elements of a negligence cause of action because contributory negligence, in general, is determined and governed by the same tests and rules as the negligence cause of action. To prove negligence, a person must establish: (1) a duty to a particular class of individuals; (2) a breach of that duty; and (3) that the breach was the "proximate cause" or "legal cause" of the accident and resulting injuries.3/ 

Duty to Wear Seat Belts. 

The Reasonable Man Standard 

The law requires a person to conform to a certain standard of conduct for the protection of others. It is the standard of conduct to which the reasonable man of ordinary prudence under like circumstances would conform. The duty is to refrain from conduct which would create an unreasonable risk of personal injury or property damage.4/ This duty is owed only to those persons who are foreseeably endangered by the conduct and only with respect to those risks whose likelihood made the conduct unreasonably dangerous.5/ Duty is measured by the scope of the risk which negligent conduct foreseeably entails. Whether or not the defendant should - 2 -

have foreseen the risk to the plaintiff and should have known it was unreasonable is determined by whether or not a reasonable man would have foreseen it and would have known it was unreasonable.1/ 

While the duty required in a negligence cause of action defines a standard of conduct for the protection of "others, most seat belt cases involve conduct which presents a risk to the "actor" himself. This is a contributory negligence issue.

Contributory negligence is conduct on the part of the plaintiff, contributing as a legal cause to the harm he has suffered, which falls below the standard to which he is required to conform for his own protection. Nevertheless, contributory negligence still involves the issue of whether the plaintiff conformed to the duty of care of the reasonable man of ordinary prudence under like circumstances.

Whether the reasonable man standard of care requires the use of a seat belt, without a state or local law requiring it, depends upon whether a failure to do so would create an unreasonable risk. If such a standard does exist, a breach of it will constitute contributory negligence.

Only a few cases have suggested that there is an absolute duty to wear seat belts.9, In Bentzler v. Braun, the case most often cited for the proposition that a duty does exist, the court ruled there was a duty based on the common law standard of ordinary care, independent of any statutory mandate to use available seat belts.10/

Some courts have commented in dictum that a duty to wear seat belts may arise under certain circumstances, such as where a particularly dangerous trip is undertaken, but in light of all the factors noted above the exercise of reasonable care does not require the use of seat belts under all circumstances. In 

Remington v. Arndt, a Connecticut court held that proof of the defendant's narrow allegation that the passenger did not fasten his seat belt would not establish negligence and that the defendant's allegations did not spell out an omission to act which would bar the plaintiff's action. The court did say the duty may arise, however, should the operator warn the passenger during the course of a journey to fasten his seat belt because the brakes or steering mechanism had suddenly become unreliable.11/ - 3 - 

---

Two years later, another Connecticut court, relying on the Remington decision, stated that when there were no unusual circumstances, such as hazardous conditions or a known incompetent or unsafe driver, ordinary care did not require a passenger to fasten a seat belt and thus the non-use of a seat 12/ belt was not negligence.- However, these decisions probably will be limited to their narrow holdings for two reasons. 

First, the courts' statements that a duty may arise under certain circumstances were dictum and neither court found those curcumstances present in its holding. Secondly, these decisions were handed down when Connecticut was a contributory negligence state and a more recent court held that the non-use of a seat belt was not negligence under Connecticut's comparative negligence statute (see discussion on comparative negligence, infra).13/

The vast majority of courts have held that there is no 14/ common law duty to wear an available seat belt.. Absent a duty to wear a seat belt, the defendant will not be able to bar a plaintiff's recovery on the basis of contributory negligence. 

Many courts have refused to impose a duty based on the common law standard of ordinary care because of the conflicting evidence about the value of seat belts.. 15/ Courts point out that there is some evidence that seat belts may cause or aggravate certain injuries,16/ that some people are afraid to wear seat belts because they fear being trapped in a wrecked or submerged car, 17/ and statistics indicate a very low level of 18/ usage.

This fact that only a minority of people use seat belts is a relevant factor in determing the standard of care of the reasonable man because the standard is a community standard and evidence of the usual customary conduct of others under similar circumstances is normally admissible in deter- 19/ mining the conduct of the reasonable man. 

In McCord v. Green, the plaintiff was the passenger in a car involved in an intersection collision. Since the plaintiff was not wearing a seat belt, the trial court instructed the jury on contributory negligence. The appellate court held that this instruction was error because the court doubted statements that seat belts had never aggravated injuries for example, 

where the car has been driven off a bridge or where the car had caught fire. The court felt that in intersection collisions seat belts can cause damage that otherwise would not have occurred - 4 -

had the victim been thrown free. 

In addition statistics refuted the idea that the usual practice of drivers was to wear seat belts.20/ 

Some courts have been influenced by the traditional tort theory that there is no duty to anticipate the negligence of others. In Nash v. Kamrath, the plaintiff was a passenger in a car struck by the defendant. The defendant sought to introduce evidence that the plaintiff's injury was caused by his failure to wear a seat belt. The appellate court said this was irrelevant because there was no duty to fasten a seat belt. The court said a person has the right to assume that others on the highway won't be negligent. The plaintiff should not be required to "truss himself up in every known safety apparatus before proceeding on the highway. "21

Statutory Duty to Wear Belts 

Several courts have examined the issue of whether failure to wear an available seat belt constitutes negligence per se. 

Generally, one is held to the standard of a reasonable man in determing whether conduct creates an unreasonable risk of harm.. 22/ 

When the legislature enacts a statute, however, it dictates a standard of conduct to which all must conform. A violation of such a statute is negligent per se. 

23/ That is, the statute determines the standard of care required and a breach of that statute conclusively establishes negligence.24/

Many state statutes require the installation of seat belts in passenger automobiles manufactured after a certain date. These laws do not expressly require the use of such belts, however. 25/ 

Defendants have claimed that these installation statutes implicitly create a duty to use seat belts, and that the breach of that duty constitutes contributory negligence as a matter of law, thus barring the plaintiff's recovery. However, courts have rejected this argument because the legislative enactments do not impose an express duty to wear the belts. Since there is not an express statutory use requirement, the failure to wear a seat belt does not violate the installation statute and the non-use of a seat belt is not negligence per se. 26/ - 5 · 

---

Proximate Cause 

Assuming that the plaintiff failed to conform to the standard of care of a reasonable man under like circumstances, does it necessarily follow that the plaintiff's negligence will bar his recovery? The answer is no, and the reason is the doctrine of proximate cause. 

Proximate cause requires the existence of some reasonable connection between the negligent conduct and the injury suffered.27/

All attempts to devise a universal formula to determine when such a reasonable connection does in fact exist have been fruitless.28/ Proximate cause is the sum total of all those considerations of policy, reason and justice which compel some limitation on a person's legal responsibility for the consequences of his act.29/

In establishing proximate cause in automobile accident cases, it is essential to distinguish between negligence contributing to the accident and negligence contributing to the damage sustained. Contributory negligence requires negligence which contributes to the accident, having a causal connection with it. An act or omission that merely increases or adds to the extent of the loss or injury occasioned by another's negligence is not such contributory negligence as will defeat a recovery.30/

Even though the reasonable man standard of care may require an actor to wear a seat belt, the non-use of a seat belt will not bar a plaintiff's recovery unless the defendant can also prove a causal connection between the failure to use seat belts and the resulting accident. It has been held that a failure to use a seat belt is not contributory negligence such as would bar all recovery because the failure to wear a belt did not contribute in any way to the causation of the accident.31/

This has been particularly true where the injured plaintiff was a passenger with no physical control over the vehicle's operation.327

The requirement that the causal connection relate to the accident, as opposed to relating merely to the injury, is not an absolute doctrine. There have been inroads in other areas of the law imposing civil liability where the negligence merely exacerbated the injuries rather than causing the injury.33/ - 6-

The prevailing view, however, seems to be that while the non-use of a seat belt may aggravate a plaintiff's injuries and thus be relevant to the mitigation of damages issue (see the discussion under MITIGATION OF DAMAGES, infra), the non-use of a seat belt may not be the basis for a contributory negligence defense unless the defendant can show a proximate cause between the plaintiff's negligence and the accident. 

In Spier v. Barker, the plaintiff driver was thrown from her car and pinned beneath her automobile immediately after being struck by a tractor-trailer driven by the defendant. At trial, the defendant called an expert witness who, after viewing photographs of the vehicles, stated that had the plaintiff been wearing a seat belt, she would not have been ejected from her automobile, and that had she not been ejected, she probably would not have been seriously injured. One of the issues on appeal was whether the non-use of an available seat belt constituted contributory negligence. The court rejected this defense because the non-use of the seat belt was not the proximate cause of the accident.34/

The requirement that an act or ommission must be the proximate cause of the accident for contributory negligence to be established will be a difficult burden in the seat belt cases. While the non-use of a seat belt may aggravate injuries received in an automobile collision, rarely have the courts been faced with situations in which the failure to use a seat belt caused the accident. And, in fact, of the seat belt cases examined, no court has found the requisite causal connection necessary to allow the defense of contributory negligence. 

MITIGATION OF DAMAGES 

The issue of the use or non-use of seat belts has had a greater impact on the matter of damages than on the question of liability.35/ This section of the Commentary reviews court decisions that have examined the non-use of seat belts as it relates to damages. 

Avoidable Consequences

In terms of the damage question, the seat belt problem does not fit easily into any standard legal classifications. 

The doctrine of avoidable consequences appears most nearly relevant to the problem. Under this doctrine, recovery is - 7

denied for any damages which could have been avoided by reasonable conduct on the part of the injured party. 36/ A person injured by the negligence of another has a duty to use reasonable care to obtain treatment for his injuries to mitigate his damages, for example. The doctrine is closely related to contributory negligence except that avoidable consequences usually involve unreasonable conduct after the injury while contributory negligence usually refers to conduct which occurs before and contributes to the injury.37/ 

Unfortunately, the failure to fasten a seat belt always occurs prior to the injury, and thus the seat belt problem is not clearly covered by the doctrine of avoidable consequences. 

Nevertheless, some courts treat a failure to use seat belts as an avoidable consequence problem, holding that a failure to wear available seat belts which results in an aggravation of injuries may result in a reduction of recoverable damages. 38/ 

Distinguishing the Damage and Liability Questions 

In the previous discussion on contributory negligence and the causal connection required to make out such a defense, it is as noted that a 1974 New York case, Spier v. Barker, refused to allow the introduction of the non-use of a seat belt to establish contributory negligence.39/ This same court, however, held that the trial court could properly submit the seat belt issue to the jury if the use of the evidence was limited to the issue of damages. In justifying the application of the avoidable consequences theory to the seat belt defense, the court stated that the seat belt affords the automobile occupant an unusual and ordinarily unavailable means by which he or she may minimize his or her damages prior to the accident.40/

Decisions in Illinois courts illustrate the importance of distinguishing between the non-use of seat belts in establish ing liability and non-use as it related to the mitigation of damages. Mount v. McClellan was one of the first Illinois cases to distinguish the two uses of seat belt evidence at trial. In 

Mount, the court allowed the jury to consider evidence that the plaintiff's car was equipped with seat belts but the court stated that the evidence should be limited to the damage issue of the case and should not be considered by the trier of facts in determining the liability issue.41/ - 8 -

Subsequent Illinois courts followed the lead of Mount in rejecting the use of seat belt evidence to establish contributory negligence but allowing it for the purpose of mitigating 42/ damages. Appellate courts have overturned jury verdicts where it appeared that the jury may have confused the issues of 43/ contributory negligence and mitigation of damages.

A federal court applying Virginia law made this distinction to preclude the application of a Virginia statute providing that failure to use seat belts "shall not be deemed to be negligence.

The court ruled that the statute prohibited use of seat belt evidence on the question of negligence but did not preclude the use of such evidence in the mitigation of damages. 45/

Statutory provisions like the Virginia law are discussed in greater detail under a separate heading later in the Commentary. 44/ 

Causal Connection 

To establish a proper foundation for the mitigation of damages, courts generally require evidence establishing a causal relationship between the failure to wear seat belts and the injuries sustained.46/ In the Spier case, supra, the court said that seat belt evidence should not be submitted to the jury unless the defendant can demonstrate, by competent evidence, a causal connection between the plaintiff's non-use of an available seat belt and the injuries and damages sustained.47/

Courts in Illinois have reached the same conclusion. In Hale v. Cravens, the court held that "an instruction as to the existence or nonexistence, use or non-use of a seat belt would be proper with reference to damages only when the record establishes by competent evidence that the damages might have been mitigated if a seat belt had been in existence and used.48/

Courts have held that there must be evidence that the vehicle was equipped with seat belts,49 

49/ there must be proof that the plaintiff was not wearing a seat belt at the time of the acci- 50/ dent, and there must be evidence that the plaintiff's damages might have been mitigated by the use of seat belts.51/

In 1975, an Illinois court in Eichorn v. Olson established a two-fold test for determining whether to instruct the jury on the seat belt issue. The evidence must (1) show that the plaintiff failed to wear an available seat belt, and (2) establish a causal connection between the injuries and damages sustained.52/ - 9 -

The plaintiff in Eichorn drove his automobile into the rear of the defendant's vehicle, which had run out of gas on the highway. The defendant's employee had been pushing the car on the highway. At trial, the plaintiff testified he was not wearing a seat belt but that he could not remember whether his car was equipped with seat belts. The trial court refused to instruct the jury on the non-use of seat belts in the mitigation of damages. The appellate court held that an instruction on the seat belt issue would have been improper since the defendant showed only that the plaintiff failed to buckle-up. 53/ 

While the seat belt evidence was not admissible in Eichorn, a later court in Dudanas v. Plate allowed such evidence using the 

Eichorn test. 547

In Dudanas, the plaintiff was suing for injuries sustained by her daughter in a collision between the automobile driven by the defendant and a vehicle driven by the plaintiff. Because of the collision, the daughter, who was not wearing an available seat belt, was thrown forward into the windshield. At trial, the plaintiff called an expert witness who stated that the minor would have hit the windshield even if she had been wearing a seat belt. On cross-examination the witness stated that the top of the head would have impacted the windshield and that there might have been a slight reduction in the speed at which she hit the windshield. The defendant presented no witness in the seat belt issue. The trial court instructed the jury on the non-use of seat belts and the mitigation of damages. The appellate court stated that the issue was whether the testimony of plaintiff's seat belt expert constituted competent evidence of a causal connection between non-use of available seat belts and the injuries and damages sustained. While the court noted that none of the previous 

Illinois cases had decided what constituted competent evidence, the court held that the evidence here satisfied the two-fold test set forth in Eichorn. Since the expert witness testified that the top of the daughter's head, rather than her face, would have struck the windshield had she worn a seat belt, the court felt that the jury reasonably could have concluded that it should not award damages for the facial injuries.55/

The Dudanas case also points out the importance of expert testimony in seat belt cases. Expert testimony is generally required to establish the extent to which injuries were aggravated by the failure to use seat belts.56/ - 10 -

While one court has not allowed a seat belt instruction where there was no medical evidence upon which to apportion damages, later courts have not required medical evidence if other types of expert testimony have been given.. 57/ For example, one court has held that the testimony of a professor of mechanical and aerospace engineering who had been employed in the field of accident reconstruction was sufficient.58/ In the Dudanas case, the testimony of a consultant on automotive safety provided sufficient evidence from which the jury could apportion damages. However, in one case, the testimony of the investigating police officer was not sufficient expert testimony upon which to establish the necessary causal connection.59/ 

Even in jurisdictions that allow an apportionment of damages it is recognized that such an apportionment is a difficult and highly speculative task, and the defendant has a heavy burden of proof regarding the effect of the failure to use 607 the belts on the injury. 

Several courts have refused to apportion damages on the basis of speculation in the absence of such evidence.61/

The plaintiff has recovered for all his injuries in several cases where the injuries were not separable.62/ 

One court has refused to allow the jury to apportion damages in a situation where the passenger was killed in the accident. 

The court felt that it would be inappropriate to allow the jury to speculate on dividing damages in such a case. 

63/ Another court has refused to allow seat belt evidence in a case involving death because it felt to do so would create extremely difficult problems of measuring damages.64/

Yet recent courts, even where the suit has been brought on behalf of a party killed in the accident, 65/ have allowed a jury to apportion damages despite the objection that apportionment is too speculative.66/

One court has commented that the argument against apportionment underestimates the abilities of those trained in accident reconstruction and that the argument fails to recognize other areas of the law in which apportionment is allowed.67/ 

Comparative Negligence 

An increasing number of jurisdictions have adopted the concept of comparative negligence to ameliorate the harsh, all-or-nothing rule of contributory negligence.68/ This part of - 11. 

---

the Commentary will examine how the emergence of the doctrine of comparative negligence has affected the availability of the seat belt defense in civil litigation. 

Comparative negligence is a fault concept that apportions liability for damages in proportion to the contribution of each person causing the injury or damages. More simply, if several parties are responsible for an accident, then the liability for damages is divided among the parties according to the fault attributable to each. The doctrine abrogates the common law concept of contributory negligence and substitutes apportionment by fault.69/

For the seat belt defense, the critical issue in comparative negligence is whether apportionment is made by comparing only the negligence which contributed to the accident itself, or also the negligence which contributed only to the injuries. This determination is critical because non-use of a seat belt rarely causes the accident itself.

In the seat belt cases decided under the contributory negligence standard, one often cited basis for rejecting the seat belt defense was that the failure to wear the belt did not cause the accident. Some courts were also reluctant to apply the avoidable consequence theory in mitigating damages because of the time factor. The doctrine of avoidable consequences traditionally involves unreasonable conduct after the injury, but the failure to fasten a seat belt is conduct which occurs prior to the injury. Comparative negligence may obviate both of these problems for the seat belt defense, or it may not, depending on how it is interpreted by the courts. ..71/

The Restatement (Second) of Torts comments that damages may be apportioned between the defendant and plaintiff "where the antecedent negligence of the plaintiff is found not to contribute in any way to the original accident or injury, but to be a substantial contributing factor in increasing the harm which ensues. 

Should this view be adopted, non-use of seat belts could constitute fault in a comparative negligence jurisdiction, resulting in an apportionment of damages. The effect of this kind of comparative negligence is to merge the doctrine of avoidable consequences into the liability question. 

On the other hand, the comparative negligence analysis may be limited, just as the contributory negligence analysis generally 

12

has been, to only that negligence which contributed in some way to the accident itself, rather than just affecting the resulting injuries. Under this approach, the avoidable consequences doctrine is not merged into the liability question, but remains a separate issue.

The doctrine of comparative negligence is usually created by statute, although in some states it has been adopted by judi- 72/ cial decision. Whether the comparative negligence analysis will or will not include the avoidable consequences type of fault depends primarily upon the language of the statute adopting comparative negligence.

For example, the Uniform Comparative Fault Act, as approved by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws in 1977, definitely and specifically includes the avoidable conanalysis ype of fault within the comparative negligence The Uniform Act provides that, [A] ny contributory fault chargeable to the claimant diminishes proportionately the amount awarded as compensatory damages for an injury attributable to the claimant's contributory fault, but does not bar recovery. 74/ The Act defines the term "fault" to specifically include, "unrea- 75/ sonable failure to avoid an injury or to mitigate damages.

The Comments to the Uniform Act, under the subheading, Conduct Covered, specify that, "The doctrine of avoidable consequences is expressly included in the coverage. 176/ Under the subheading, Causation, the Comments provide the following explanation, using non-use of a seat belt as an example: "Injury attributable to the claimant's contributory fault" refers to the requirement of a causal relation for the particular damage. Thus, negligent failure to fasten a seat belt would diminish recovery only for damages in which the lack of a seat-belt restraint played a part, and not, for example, to the damage to the car. A similar rule applies to a defendant's fault; a physician, for example, negligently setting a broken arm, is not liable for other injuries received in an automobile accident.77/ - 13

This makes it clear not only that the comparative fault analysis will consider fault which contributed to injuries although not necessarily to the accident itself, but also that damages will be apportioned, where possible, by directly connecting particular injuries to their causal element.

No state has adopted the Uniform Act, but some states have adopted a form of comparative negligence which may include the avoidable consequences type of negligence in their concept of "fault." The Arkansas statute, for example, defines "fault" as follows: 

The word "fault" as used in the Act includes any act, omission, conduct, risk assumed, breach of warranty or breach of any legal duty which is a proximate cause of any damages sustained by any 78/ party.

The Maine statute contains a similar definition: 

Fault means negligence, breach of statutory duty or other act or omission which gives rise to a liability in tort or would, apart from this section, give rise to the defense of contributory negligence.79/ 

The New York statute refers to the "culpable conduct" of the claimant, and it is clear that "culpable conduct" encompasses more than just contributory negligence since contributory negligence is specifically included in the term. The law provides as follows: 

In any action to recover damages for personal injury, injury to property, or wrongful death, the culpable conduct attributable to the claimant or to the decedent, including contributory negligence or assumption of risk, shall not bar recovery, but the amount of damages otherwise recoverable shall be diminished in the proportion which the culpable conduct attributable to the claimant or decedent bears to the culpable conduct which caused the damages.80/ 

The Rhode Island statute does not use the term "fault." provides as follows: It 

14

[T]he fact that the person injured may not have been in the exercise of due care shall not bar a recovery, but damages shall be diminished by the finder of fact in proportion to the amount of negligence attributable to the person injured 81 All of these statutes use language which could be interpreted as including the avoidable consequences kind of fault in the concept of comparative negligence. Unfortunately there have been no cases which have effectively interpreted the laws on this point. 

The one recent seat belt case we have located in these states is inconclusive.

In Harlan v. Curbo, the Supreme Court of Arkansas addressed the seat belt issue briefly in a comparative negligence case, but it was not the principal issue in the case, and the decision may create more questions than it answers in regard to the seat belt 82/ defense.

The court ruled that it was error for the trial court to instruct the jury that on the question of the plaintiff's negligence it could take into consideration the fact that seat belts were available for the plaintiff's use. The trial court had already given a standard jury instruction regarding the duty of all persons involved in the occurrence to use ordinary care for their own safety. The supreme court held that the additional jury instruction relating to seat belts not only was an unnecessary duplication but also singled out a particular fact for undue empha- But these are the only reasons cited by the court for ruling that the seat belt instruction constituted error. sis.

The trial court had allowed evidence that the seat belts were available and were not in use. The supreme court did not find this to constitute error; it is not clear from the decision that the court even considered the question. Apparently, it would have been proper for the jury to consider the plaintiff's failure to use seat belts under the general instruction relating to the duty to use ordinary care for one's own safety. Nor does the decision specify that expert testimony linking the failure to use the seat belts with the plaintiff's injuries would be required. Again, that issue is not specifically addressed by the decision.

To further complicate the case, the trial court had ruled, as a matter of law, that one of the plaintiffs, a passenger in the vehicle, was not guilty of any negligence. The trial court did not make such a ruling as to the other plaintiff, the driver - 15 

---

of the vehicle, however. Thus, the jury was free to find the driver-plaintiff negligent, and the failure to wear an available seat belt could constitute an element of such negligence. 

But the jury was not free to make such a finding as to the passenger-plaintiff, although there was evidence that neither the driver nor the passenger wore the available belts. The supreme court did not find this distinction to constitute error. 

Does this suggest that a driver has a greater duty to wear available belt for his own protection than does a passenger?

Most of the states which have adopted comparative negligence have used the term "contributory negligence" in their statutes.

The laws typically provide that contributory negligence on the part of the plaintiff shall not bar recovery, but any damages allowed must be diminished in proportion to the plaintiff's negligence. In states with laws of this type, it is likely that the courts will simply apply all the old precendent relating to contributory negligence. The laws will be seen as not changing. the substance of contributory negligence, but only its effect.83/ 

Acts or omissions on the part of the plaintiff which formerly constituted contributory negligence will now result in an apportionment of damages rather than a bar to any recovery. But acts or omissions which formerly would not have constituted contributory negligence, such as the avoidable consequences type of negligence, will still not constitute contributory negligence. 

Connecticut is a good example. The comparative negligence statute provides as follows:

In causes of action based on negligence, contributory negligence shall not bar recovery in an action by any person or his legal representative to recover damages resulting from injury to persons or damage to property, if such negligence was not greater than the combined negligence of the person or persons against whom recovery is sought, but any damages allowed shall be diminished in the proportion of the percentage of negligence attributable to the person recovering. 84/ A superior court in Connecticut has ruled in a seat belt case that this statute does not purport to alter the prior law as to proximate cause.85/

The well established rule of causation in Connecticut is that contributory negligence must be an act or omission which contributes to the happening of the event. An 

16 -

act or omission that merely increases or adds to the extent of the loss or injury is not contributory negligence. The court ruled that the failure to use a seat belt in this case could not, as a matter of law, have contributed to the happening of the accident. Thus no diminution of damages was allowed on the basis of the seat belt defense.86/

A federal court applying Mississippi law has reached the same conclusion. Since there was no causal connection shown between the negligence, the failure to use seat belts, and the injuries to the plaintiff, no dimunition of damages was required 87/ under the comparative negligence law.

The Texas Court of Appeals reached a similar conclusion on the causation issue, although the case did not involve the seat belt defense.88/

The Texas comparative negligence statute is similar in substance to the Connecticut law.89/ The defendant in the case asserted that under the comparative negligence law the plaintiff's negligence need not be a proximate cause of the occurrence in order to require a diminution of damages. The court, noting that it must consider the condition of the law at the time the statute was enacted, ruled that the words "contributory negligence" in the statute mean such negligence as was a proximate cause of the occurrence in question. contributed to the injuries.92/ 90/ Wisconsin, on the other hand, has a comparative negligence statute similar in relevant ways to the Connecticut and Texas statutes.91/

But the Wisconsin Supreme Court has ruled in Bentzler v. Braun that the question is not whether the negligence contributed to the cause of the accident, but rather whether it It is up to the jury to decide, as in any other case of negligence, whether the failure to use an available seat belt was a substantial factor in producing the injury. The court indicated that there must be evidence before the jury to show a causal relationship between the injuries sustained and the failure to use seat belts. Where there is such evidence, a jury could conclude that an occupant of an automobile was negligent in failing to use the seat belt. Such negligence on the part of the plaintiff would result in a diminution of damages recoverable by the plaintiff under the Wisconsin comparative negligence statute.

The Wisconsin decision is not based upon an analysis of the comparative negligence law. Rather it relies upon legal doctrines relating to causation. The Wisconsin court might have reached the 

17

the same conclusion even if Wisconsin did not have a comparative negligence statute. On the other hand, since the comparative negligence statute ameliorates the harsh consequences of the contributory negligence rule, it may have made it easier for the court to rule that there is a duty to wear the belts, and that the breach of the duty can constitute negligence if it contributed to the injury, even if it did not cause the accident itself. 

The Supreme Court of the State of Washington has not been persuaded by that viewpoint, however. In Amend v. Bell, the defendants pointed out that the Washington comparative negligence law avoids the harsh consequences of contributory negligence which would bar all recovery. 

Because of this, the defendants urged, prior case law on the seat belt defense had been abrogated. The court rejected the argument, noting that while the results of contributory negligence and comparative negligence are much different, both are still premised upon negligence. In the one case the negligence bars recovery, while in the other the negligence is compared and potentially reduces damages. But in either case there is no negligence without breach of a legally imposed duty. 

The court declined to impose a duty on all persons riding in vehicles to use available seat belts. Such a duty would require the plaintiff to anticipate the defendant's negligence in causing the accident. The plaintiff should have a duty to fasten his seat belt prior to an accident only if it can be said that the plaintiff should have anticipated that the defendant would negligently cause the accident.

DIRECT NEGLIGENCE 

While most of the seat belt cases have involved the issue of whether a defendant can reduce or avoid liability to a plaintiff who has failed to wear a seat belt, one case has examined the issue of whether the non-use of a seat belt may form the basis for a direct negligence cause of action against the defendant.

In Quinius v. Estrada, vehicle A negligently collided with vehicle B, causing the unbelted driver of vehicle B to lose control, whereupon vehicle B collided with vehicle C. It was argued that had the driver in vehicle B been wearing her available seat belt, the second collision would not have occurred, and that the driver of vehicle B was, therefore, under a duty to the driver of vehicle C to wear the belt. The court held that there was no duty to wear seat belts because it was not reasonably foreseeable that - 18 

---

a failure to use a seat belt would result in a failure to regain control after a collision negligently caused by another. The court refused to recognize the crash prevention potential of a driver's lap belt, noting that the wearing of seat belts has relevance only to the safety of the person wearing the belt. court took judicial notice of this fact as a matter of general knowledge.94/

Accident investigations have uncovered other instances where the driver was ejected from his seat in a collision resulting in a second collision and further injuries to others. 95/ 

PRODUCT LIABILITY CASES 

The concept of product liability or strict liability in tort has rapidly emerged as a means of imposing civil liability in addition to the theory of negligence.

In a 1963 case, the strict liability doctrine was defined as follows: 

A manufacturer is strictly liable in tort when an article he places on the market, knowing that it is to be used without inspection for defects, proves to have a defect that causes injury to a human being. 96/

To impose civil liability based on strict or product liability the injured plaintiff does not have to prove that the manufacturer was negligent but he must prove that the manufacturer was at fault.97/ 

That is, the plaintiff must show that the product was defective, and he must show that the manufacturers produced a product in a con- 98/ dition justifying liability for the ensuing injury.

Product liability relieves the plaintiff from problems of proof inherent in pursuing negligence.99/ However a manufacturer is not an insurer. 100/

A manufacturer is not liable for every occurrence involving its product but only for the injuries attributable to the defects of the product. 101/ Is contributory negligence a defense to an action based on product liability? The prevailing view is that ordinary contributory negligence is not a defense to an action based on strict 

19

liability.102/ 

Contributory negligence will be a defense, however, if the negligence consists of voluntarily and unreasonably proceeding to encounter a known danger.103/ This defense is commonly referred to as assumption of the risk. 104/ While ordinary contributory negligence is not a defense, the unintended, unforeseeable misuse of a product will constitute a 105/ valid defense to a strict liability action. 

That is, if the product is put to a use by the consumer that is not reasonably foreseeable, then strict liability will not be imposed. 106/ 

The issue of the availability of the seat belt defense in product liability cases has only recently begun to be examined. 

In Horn v. General Motors Corp., the plaintiff brought a products liability suit against an automobile manufacturer for injuries 107/ received due to a defective horn cap. 

While the injuries were incurred in an accident that was not the fault of the manufacturer, the plaintiff sought recovery for injuries sustained when, as a result of the horn cap coming off, sharp prongs beneath the cap scarred her face.

While the manufacturer conceded that contributory negligence was not a defense to an action based on strict liability, it still attempted to introduce evidence of the non-use of an available seat belt, alleging: (1) that by not wearing a seat belt the plaintiff assumed the risk of her injuries; (2) that the non-use of a seat belt was the proximate cause of the injuries; and (3) that the plaintiff's automobile was misused. 

The court rejected the defense of assumption of the risk because there was no evidence that the plaintiff was aware of the defect or that she knew her failure to use a seat belt would expose her to the risk of injury from the prongs beneath the horn cap. The court said that at best the evidence indicated some negligence of a general nature on the plaintiff's part in not 108 using the seat belt.

The court also rejected the defendant's claim that the seat belt evidence would tend to show that the plaintiff's failure to use a seat belt was the proximate cause of her injuries. The court said that this was nothing more than a restatement of the inadmissible defense that the plaintiff's negligence in not using a seat belt contributed to or was a contributing cause of the accident.109/ - 20 - 

---

Finally, the court rejected the defendant's third argument that the product the plaintiff's automobile was misused. 

While the court recognized that strict liability should not be imposed if the product is put to a use not reasonably foreseeable, the court said that the driving of an automobile without using a 110/ seat belt is an entirely foreseeable use of the vehicle.. 

Three other courts have refused to allow the defense of contributory negligence based on the non-use of an available seat belt because contributory negligence is no defense to a 111/ cause of action based on strict liability. 

Two of these courts, though, have stated that seat belt evidence is admissible in the mitigation of damages if the defendant can establish by competent evidence a causal connection between the alleged negligence and the injuries sustained.112/

It is too early to speculate on the impact that the comparative negligence doctrine will have on the availability of the seat belt defense in product liability cases. was the causative factor of the The development of the product liability doctrine in cases other than those dealing with seat belts may alter the view of courts on the issue of whether a person has a duty to wear seat belts. In a 1968 product liability case not concerned with the use of seat belts, a court held that a manufacturer could be liable for damages caused by a defective design even though the defect did not cause the original accident. The court stated that there was "no rational basis for limiting recovery to situations where the defect accident, [since] the accident and the resulting injury so-called 'second collision' of the passenger with the interior part of the automobile, all are foreseeable. 113/ Based on this case, commentators have argued that if manufacturers must anticipate or "foresee" collisions, then automobile occupants may also be under a duty to foresee the same occurrence, and thus be required to fasten a safety belt. 

114/ However none of the seat belt cases examined have adopted this view.

the 

One other factor affecting the seat belt defense must be considered. As we have already noted, one is generally held to the standard of a reasonable man in determining whether certain conduct creates an unreasonable risk of harm. 

When the legislature enacts a statute, however, it dictates a standard of conduct 115/ - 21 -

to which all must conform. A violation of such a statute is negligent conduct.116/

The Uniform Vehicle Code now includes a requirement that. 117/ drivers and passengers use available lap and shoulder belts. 

The Code also includes a section specifying the effect of non-use in civil litigation. It provides that failure to use any belt in violation of the requirements "shall not diminish recovery for damages" in any civil suit.118/

Without this "non-use" provision, the Code requirements for use, if enacted by a state, would constitute a legislative determination of the standard of conduct required for one's own protection. Failure to conform to that standard could constitute negligence as a matter of law. The "non-use" provision removes that impact from the law. But the "non-use" subsection goes beyond that. It precludes any diminution of damages for failure to wear a seat belt, even if the court concludes that the person had a duty apart from the statute to wear the belt. The "non-use" provision completely obviates the seat belt defense under all circumstances.

No state has a law generally requiring all vehicle occupants to use available seat belts. Five states, however, do require the use of belts by certain occupants of specific kinds of vehicles: California Massachusetts 

New York Tennessee

Rhode Island 

The California law provides that it is unlawful for any driver or passenger in a driver training vehicle to operate or ride in such vehicle while it is being used for driver training without using an available seat belt in the proper manner.. 

119/ The Massachusetts and New York laws make it illegal for the driver of a school bus to operate without using an available seat belt.120/

The Rhode 

Island law applies to every person driving a bus (including school bus), trolley or authorized emergency vehicle. The law requires that such a driver, "use and have his body anchored by" an available seat belt.121

The Tennessee law requires the parent or legal guardian of a resident child under the age of four years to be responsible, when transporting the child in a motor vehicle owned by the parent or guardian, to either place the child in the arms of an older person riding as a passenger, or place the child in a proper child restraint system. 122/ - 22

Of the five laws discussed above, only the Tennessee law includes a "non-use" provision. It specifies that in no event will a failure to wear a child restraint system be considered contributory negligence, and evidence of any such failure is 123/ inadmissible in the trial of any civil action.

The use requirements in the California, Massachusetts, New York and Rhode Island laws probably represent a legislative determination of the required standard of care. Failure to comply with that standard could constitute negligence as a matter of law. 

In addition to the Tennessee provision already discussed, five states have "non-use" provisions which, to varying extents, would obviate the seat belt defense. Except for Tennessee, these states do not have any statutory requirements to use seat belts. In each case the "non-use" provision is part of a requirement that certain vehicles merely be equipped with seat belts. The five states are: Iowa Maine Minnesota Tennessee Virginia 

The Iowa law provides in relevant part as follows:

The fact of use, or nonuse, of seat belts by a person shall not be admissible or material as evidence in civil actions brought for damages.124/ 

This law would completely preclude the seat belt defense since evidence of non-use, or or even of use, is inadmissible.

The Maine law provides in relevant part as follows: 

In any accident involving an automobile, the nonuse of seat belts by the driver of or passengers in the automobile shall not be admissible in evidence in any trial25Civil or criminal, arising out of such accident.

Note that the law applies only to the non-use of seat belts in an "automobile." Like the Iowa law, it makes evidence of non-use inadmissible. 

The Minnesota law provides in relevant part as follows:

Proof of the use or failure to use seat belts, or proof of the installation or failure of installation of seat belts shall not be admissible in evidence in any litigation involving personal injuries or property damage resulting from the use or operation of any motor vehicle. 126/ The effect is much like the Iowa law. Evidence is inadmissible on the matter of seat belts.

In addition to the Tennessee law already discussed which applies to the child restraint requirement, Tennessee has the following provision: 

Provided that in no event shall failure to wear seat belts be considered as contributory negligence, nor shall such failure to wear said seat belt be considered in mitigation of damages on the trial of any civil action.1277

This section does not make evidence of seat belt use inadmissible, but does effectively obviate the seat belt defense. 

The Virginia law provides in relevant part as follows: 

Failure to use such safety lap belts or a combination of lap belts and shoulder straps or harnesses after installation shall not be deemed to be negligence.128/

A failure to wear seat belts under this law, although not negligence, might be considered in mitigation of damages. A federal court interpreting this law reached just that conclusion.129/ 

Thus, the Virginia law does not completely obviate the seat belt defense.

SUMMARY 

1. Most of the cases involving the non-use of automobile safety belts in civil litigation have examined the issue of whether an injured plaintiff who failed to use his seat belt may be barred from recovery under the contributory negligence doctrine. The primary issue in these cases is whether there is a duty to wear seat belts for one's own protection. 

2. A few courts have held that there is a duty to wear a seat belt based on the common law standard of the reasonable man. 

A few other courts have stated that a duty may exist under certain limited circumstances. But most of the decisions have held that in the absence of a statute requiring use there is no duty to wear an available seat belt. Many courts point to the statistics showing low level of seat belt usage to support their conclusion. Absent a duty to wear seat belts, the failure to wear them will not constitute contributory negligence. 

3. Even when there is a duty to wear seat belts, the defense of contributory negligence will not be allowed unless it is shown that the non-use of the belt was a cause of the accident. This requirement will preclude the defense of contributory negligence in most cases because, while the failure to wear seat belts may aggravate injuries, rarely has such non-use been the cause of the accident. No court has allowed the seat belt defense to completely bar the plaintiff's recovery under the doctrine of contributory negligence. 

4. Although courts have held that failure to wear seat belts does not constitute contributory negligence which will bar all recovery, non-use of seat belts may result in a reduction or mitigation of damages under the theory of avoidable consequences. 

For the non-use of seat belts to result in the mitigation of damages, the defendant must establish by competent evidence a causal connection between the plaintiff's failure to use an available seat belt and his resulting injuries. This usually requires the introduction of expert testimony. Courts generally refuse to apportion damages on the basis of speculation. 

5. Some courts do not allow the failure to use seat belts to be considered under the doctrine of avoidable consequences because the failure to buckle-up always occurs before the accident happens. The doctrine of avoidable consequences has traditionally been limited to the injured person's conduct after the accident. - 25- 

---

These courts are unwilling to mitigate the plaintiff's damages on the grounds that he failed to anticipate the negligence of the defendant. 

6. An increasing number of jurisdictions have adopted comparative negligence in place of contributory negligence.

Comparative negligence apportions damages on the basis of relative fault instead of completely barring recovery by a negligent plaintiff. Whether the non-use of a seat belt will result in an apportionment of damages under the theory of comparative negligence depends on the type of comparative negligence adopted in a particular jurisdiction. If a jurisdiction adopts a concept of comparative negligence which incorporates avoidable consequences, as does the Uniform Comparative Fault Act, then the seat belt defense may result in an apportionment of damages. But most of the states' comparative negligence laws do not seem to have this effect. Although there have been few cases interpreting this aspect of the comparative negligence laws, the language of the most of the laws suggests that only such fault as contributed to the accident itself will constitute comparative negligence, requiring an apportionment of damages. Fault which exacerbates injuries but does not contribute to the accident itself, such as failure to wear seat belts, probably will not constitute comparative negligence. 

7. The difficulty of using the seat belt defense is compounded in product liability cases because contributory negligence is not a defense to an action based on strict liability. The impact of comparative negligence on product liability cases remains to be seen. Future cases may turn on the issue of whether the failure to wear a seat belt constituted a misuse of the manufacturer's product, which, if established, would be a valid defense. 

8. Statutory provisions can have an enormous impact on the seat belt defense. Laws requiring the use of seat belts establish a standard of care to which all must conform. Failure to meet this standard would be negligence, as a matter of law. Such laws establishing a duty to wear seat belts would be a substantial assist for the seat belt defense since most of the cases have found that such a duty does not now exist. On the other hand, statutes which specify that evidence of non-use of seat belts is inadmissible, or that non-use does not constitute negligence or cannot be used to mitigate damages completely obviate the seat belt defense. Although no state has a law generally imposing a duty on all vehicle occupants to use belts, several states have laws specifying the effect of non-use in civil litigation. - 26
