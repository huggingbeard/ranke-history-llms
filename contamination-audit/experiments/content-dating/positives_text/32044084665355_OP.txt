Father Haas emphasized a number of positive and constructive tasks in which, without danger to their separate interests and ideals, Jews, Catholics, and Protestants might immediately unite. It was characteristic of Catholic participation in the Seminar as a whole that the first spokesman for the Church at once addressed himself to those acute social ills that demand the full cooperation of all citizens if they are to be tackled in a permanent and fundamental way. "We place a high premium on human life by spending millions for public health and by applauding the splendid cooperation and even the heroism of the medical fraternity. Yet we hold life cheap by doing next to nothing in an organized way to rid ourselves of the twin scourges of humanity—unemployment and militarism. ... "I single out the two problems-unemployment and militarism—not because there are not others calling urgently for organized action-permanent poverty, bad housing, drunkenness, racketeering, crime, and vice-but for the reason that these two are now uppermost in the public mind, and one of them threatens the very existence of the human family."

The relevancy of these considerations to the subject and purpose of the seminar soon became evident as the speaker proceeded to point out that the combat of both unemployment and militarism requires planning on a national and, in some of their aspects, international scale. But "planning, both national and international, assumes abandonment of some attitudes and acceptance of others." How is this change in attitudes to be effected? "The success of a plan or its failure can be easily predicted: if there is mutual trust, it will achieve its purpose; if suspicion, it will come to nothing. 

The experience of local communities is sadly illuminating. How often projects for better city government, housing, recreation, and zoning, languish because men of influence refuse to work with others of like capacity but of different faith and worship. . . . ”

Rabbi Simon saw hope in a movement for religious understanding that was so deeply rooted in our national history, matured so slowly, and saw its revival in a time so much in need of an ethical ferment. "Its importance," he said, "will lie in the creation of a constant stream of (6)

public opinion by virtue of which the few who are broad and bold and socially minded will impress their spiritual power upon the community. "The import of this Conference lies in the fact that it is one of study. 'Prejudice' is to receive an academic and a critical investigation. We aim to bring into the open our suspicions and misinterpretations, examine them objectively, and submit them to the light of critical and unbiased inquiry. We have come here in religious groups for a psychological study of the origin and behavior of bias, of the reasons for our social maladjustments. With all our progress in sociology, this phase has not found its place in the college curricula." 

He desired "a clinic" for the dissection of our emotional and our mental distortions in community living. He further emphasized-and in this later speakers supported him—the importance of having such self-examination take place in an atmosphere of friendly mutual enlightenment between members of different religious groups. 

Rabbi Simon expressed his conviction that religious prejudices are the most bitter of all prejudices and the most stubborn of eradication, because religion is the most intimate of emotional experiences, and because prejudices in religious life have become almost sacrosanct. "Biases are never so harmful as when they have found sectarian wings. Then, bat-like, they seek the cover of darkness.”

THE BACKGROUND OF PRESENT INTER-GROUP RELATIONS 

Before we can understand such a social phenomenon as religious intolerance, we must endeavor to see it in relation to its setting, both in contemporary life and in historical origins. 

Anti-Semitism in Germany

One comparison made in several round tables, which likewise suggested a connection between religious bigotry and race attitudes, was that between anti-Semitism in the United States and in Central Europe-specifically in Germany where, during the days of the Seminar's sessions, it was still doubtful whether the strongly anti-Semitic National Socialist Party (Hitlerism) was to dominate national politics in the near future. While there had for long been an anti-Semitic party represented in the Reichstag, and many restrictions against Jewish citizens survived in the statutes and administrative traditions of most of the German states, the movement had lost much of its aggressiveness, it was reported, in the early years of the present century, until it was revived as part of a militant nationalist and racialist movement during and after the War. While this movement represents, in part, a not unwholesome renascence of folkways, comparable with similar developments in other nations during times of crisis, it soon assumed, under the guidance of bitter and unprincipled politicians, the character of a campaign of vituperation in which (7)

the Jews were made the scapegoats for every ill that had befallen the nation. 

One of the speakers referred to the part played in this incitement of mob anger by the large-scale immigration of Jews from Eastern Europe, partly due to forced evacuation of occupied territory. Another element, it was found, was the success with which unscrupulous leaders had persuaded uninformed public opinion that the blame for inflation rested upon Jewish business groups. 

It was thus seen that here also a seemingly religious conflict had other strong elements-racial and economic. 

The growing concern of the seminar in the membership of which Jews represented less than one quarter-with the situation in Germany was focused, at the final meeting, in a short speech from the floor by the General Secretary of the Federal Council of Churches. The Rev. Samuel McCrea 

Cavert said: "One matter arose in one of the sectional discussions today. which has not officially to do with the scope of this Conference since it concerns a situation outside our own borders. It is a matter, however, which deeply affects may possibly tragically affect one of the groups that constitute this Conference. I refer to the situation in Germany, more particularly to that aspect of the Hitlerite movement which just at this moment is threatening such discrimination, even persecution, for the Jewish people. "It is nothing less than incredible that in a highly civilized nation there should be an attempt today, a serious proposał put forward by any group, to disfranchise the Jewish citizens of that nation-Jewish citizens who constitute less than one per cent of the total population, Jews who, for the most part, have lived in Germany for centuries and have made a major contribution to its life. "In what is taking place in Germany today, or may take place, we have an appalling illustration of the lengths to which the spirit of ill-will and misunderstanding and exclusiveness, if not checked, can go. I have wanted this opportunity to express, as a Christian, a word of profound sympathy with our Jewish brethren in what is happening or may happen to their brothers across the sea; to let them know that what so deeply affects them affects us, too."

The Growth of Tolerance 

To Professor Carlton J. H. Hayes, one of the co-Chairmen of the 

National Conference, fell the task of introducing another aspect of that larger perspective without which many present manifestations of religious intolerance and misunderstanding in the United States must seem incomprehensible and perverse. The occasion seemed to warrant and require a more than extemporaneous inquiry into the connection of the present problems in group relations with earlier problems of a like nature since the days of the First President. He reminded his hearers that the idea of "live and let live" is a relatively new factor in human history. (8)

"Intolerance appears to the historian to be about as old as the human race, and to have been pretty constantly displayed by clan, city, and empire, by prince and populace, and by every faith from primitive animism to modern nationalism."

None of the religious groups represented at the Seminar, he showed, was historically entirely without fault. As an extenuating circumstance, he pointed out that in former days it was widely believed that religious and political unity must go together, that it was the duty of priests and princes to be intolerant of religious difference and dissent. "There is a curious notion among some Protestants," he proceeded, "that, with the advent of Protestant Christianity in the sixteenth century, the old and universal belief in state religion and religious intolerance rapidly declined and the modern experimentation with separation of church and state and with religious liberty immediately began. . . . The actual historical facts are all to the contrary. They show that the advent of Protestant Christianity was accompanied by an unprecedented outburst of religious intolerance and cruelty, and that in this outburst, while Catholics were forward, Protestants were not at all backward. Catholic leaders felt that they were defending traditional 

Christian civilization against anarchical forces of rebellion and greed. 

Protestant leaders felt quite as sincerely that they were restoring the pure Gospel and safeguarding it against superstition and corruption." 

While Protestants claimed without justification to have been the authors of modern religious liberty, Catholics could not explain away the intolerance of their medieval and sixteenth-century co-religionists; nor should Jews forget the more ancient phases of their history for which the Hebrew Scriptures and the earliest years of Christianity furnish abundant proof of intolerance. 

The explanation for enormous progress in modern times, he thought, must be sought in the growing power of minorities and the pressure of political needs. 1 

How could such remnants of outdated attitudes toward those of other faiths be removed? The speaker showed that two theoretically possible ways are obviously not open: Americans could not all be converted to the same faith-whether it be that of one of the existing major groups or one to be introduced from without. Nor could their separate creeds be whittled down to a "lowest common denominator" without loss of the most cherished and essential religious convictions. The only practicable way is a sincere respect for the beliefs of others: "It may be a hard way, admitting of no short-cuts, but it is the way to genuine religious liberty. For, the test of genuine religious liberty is not the admission to political and social equality with ourselves of only such persons 1 The complete papers by Prof. Hayes and Prof. Greene, dealing with historical backgrounds, are available through the National Conference office. (9)

as believe about the same as we do or are indifferent to religion. The supreme and final test is whether we admit to such equality persons who believe quite differently from ourselves and are zealous in the practice of their religion." 

One difficulty that must not be overlooked, he continued, is that in American life there is always a Protestant majority, while Catholics and Jews are in a minority. This lack of balance produces attitudes that make for friction: the majority, considering itself largely responsible for existing religious freedom, "is apt to be irritated by importunities of minorities and to suspect that their eagerness is prompted by sinister and subversive motives." 

The minorities, experiencing numerous discriminations, are "likely to impute to the majority a large measure of hypocrisy or a considerable lack of the sense of humor." It was inevitable, in the circumstances, that the major share of responsibility for religious liberty in the United States must fall upon the Protestants. This task, he explained, was no longer in the main related to the influence of an emancipated elite upon the legislatures, but to mass education.

Religious Liberty in America 

Professor Evarts B. Greene presented in greater detail "the American record in relation to religious liberty." His concern was that of showing the present situation in its historical development and to indicate the steps by which religious liberty, once a distant ideal, was gradually assuming reality. 

In this way, many who had come to the Seminar perturbed by problems that seemed to be deeply imbedded in human nature found these "problems" transmuted into "tasks" for a purposeful, progressive program of enlightenment. Dr. Greene traced the connection of the long survival on American soil of the doctrine cujus regio, ejus religio to the political and economic system in which the colonies originated and from which they but slowly emancipated themselves. It was the English pattern of land ownership and political jurisdiction which made it impossible for the colonists to conceive the practical possibilities of separating church and state. This revolutionary idea first grew and secured a foothold in small and uninfluential communities, particularly in Rhode Island. It was not, however, religious idealism but business interest that gradually introduced a practical kind of liberalism. Old regulations as to residence, to Sunday observance, to participation in public affairs, fell into disuse long before there was a definite change in policy on strictly logical or ethical grounds.

It was mass migration and immigration, and the fact that often religious differences cut across political issues, which from the middle of the eighteenth century on increased religious fraternity. 

Then came the repercussions of the rationalistic thought of eighteenthcentury Europe. Religious liberalism was not a major issue in the (10)

revolutionary struggle, but it influenced it in many ways. At first, cultural intolerance often went together with Whig politics; there was even exploitation of religious prejudice in attacks upon the British Parliament. But again practical considerations provided a stronger motivation for tolerance than did idealistic policies. There was progress toward legal equality, at any rate among Protestant Christians, even though discrimination in some form was almost everywhere continued. 

It is not possible here to follow the slow progress of enactments aiming at separation of church and state in the early days of the Republic or to reproduce the speaker's explanations for the lag between the modernization of the state constitutions and the adoption by Congress of the First Amendment to the Federal Constitution, in 1791, prohibiting the passage of any federal act "respecting an establishment of religion or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." 1 "It is difficult to sum up in a few simple phrases the status of religious liberty in our early republican tradition; but in broad outline it may be stated somewhat as follows: So far as federal authority was concerned, it reflects chiefly two influences. First, there is the element of practical politics-the impossibility, in a political union with diverse traditions, of taking any other course than the one of neutrality to rival systems. . . . We must also, however, recognize an important contributing factor in the intellectual liberalism of Franklin, John Adams, Jefferson, Washington, and many of their associates." 

The speaker recognized that the subject of religious tolerance is not limited to the relations between State and Church, by calling attention to the issues raised by those citizens and would-be citizens who refuse to render military service on the ground that to do so would violate their religious convictions. The recent Supreme Court decision on a case involving this issue, he held, could not be considered final since four distinguished members of the Court, the Chief Justice and Justices Holmes, Brandeis, and Stone, had registered their dissent from it. In his concluding words, the speaker emphasized how gradual had been the passing of the tradition of community or state responsibility in matters of religion. "In this development, as I see it, the logic of facts-condition and not theory-has played the more important role." Among the modern survivals of the age-old problem of Church and 

State, he considered none more difficult or of more critical importance than those which relate to education. 

Lessons from the Past

The last paper in this group was one read by Dr. Cyrus Adler, President of the American Jewish Committee, and of the Jewish Theological Seminary. 

1 A reprint of Professor Greene's paper can be obtained from the office of the National Conference. (11)

The lesson from history which he particularly wished to impress upon his hearers in connection with the questions that were to come before them in later sessions was that "in matters of conscience numbers do not count. "It was a recognition of this fact that has marked our Republic off from the older countries of Europe from which our population is drawn. The beginning of the Republic meant the end of any kind of established Church in America, and hence the end of any domination or of even an especially favored position by a religious majority. So far as matters of religion are concerned, there are no majorities or minorities." 

He expressed a similar distaste for the frequent use of the word "group" in the discussion of inter-faith relations: "It indicates a sort of segregation, especially when connected with the word 'minority,' to which my own conscious thinking and my unconscious self in no way correspond. I am a citizen of the United States, I am one of the 

American people, and I belong to the synagogue; but I do not feel myself a member of a 'group' nor of a 'minority group.' Except in the matter of my form of religious worship, I do not feel myself in any way marked off from my fellow-Americans; and, indeed, I feel a greater kinship with those who have some form of religion other than my own than with anyone who has no religion at all."

He recalled to the memory of his hearers that Jews had no fault to find with the slow beginnings of religious liberty on the American continent. The 

British colonies in North America had breathed the air of religious freedom long before Jews could enjoy religious liberty or the full rights of citizenship in their European mother countries-with the possible exception of Holland. 

The British Parliament itself had authorized the naturalization of "foreign Protestants and others"-defined in the body of the Act as Jews-in 1740, more than a hundred years before similar rights were fully accorded to Jews in Great Britain itself.

Dr. Adler invited inquiry as to whether the teaching of Washington in this matter had become firmly embedded in American life. "Within the last ten or twelve years, the American people, or at least part of them, have greatly deviated from this noble ideal. . . . It behooves us or rather, it behooves even more those who are not present-to engage in something more than lip service to the memory of Washington." 

Another matter to which Dr. Adler directed attention was that Church membership no longer meant what it did in the days when issues of religious liberty were most eagerly debated. "Church statistics, which may be favorable enough, do not tell the entire story; for the Church is often best supported by its most conspicuous absentees —and I admit that this is true in some cases of the synagogue as well. Formal adhesion to a religious body no longer means that the tenets and practices of the body enter into the every-day life of the communicant or member." (12)

In spite of this difference, compared with former times, there seemed to be no lessening of bigotry and illiberalism-"not only on the part of ignorant people but also on the part of people who have public responsibility and are more or less in a position where one might expect them to be disciples of Washington rather than of Hitler." He felt sure that a strengthening of religious faith, whether Christian or Jewish, would predispose people to recognize the Brotherhood of Man. 

The need for an increased awareness to the historical factors in contemporary group relations was stressed repeatedly in the course of the group discussions. It was one desideratum upon which all seemed to be agreed. 

It bridged the contrast in outlook between those who regard religious intolerance as essentially an outcome of differences in belief and those who regard it as largely a mask for more ignoble differences. Even though creedal controversies may not affect many people today, it was seen that indirectly, by having given rise to group antagonisms in the past, they contribute not a little to the survival of at least latent tensions today which lend themselves to exploitation from selfish motives. Mr. C. E. Silcox, of the Institute of Social and 

Religious Research, illustrated this. "Every Christian here feels keenly the great injustice and the persecutions of various kinds which Jews have suffered at the hands of Christians. Nevertheless, we cannot understand the history of the rise of the Christian Church without becoming sensitized to the fact that the difficulties between Jews and Christians go back, not wholly but in part, to the first century and were developed and enlarged from that time on. The early persecutions of Judaism later lived on in the memories of both Jews and Christians. "Similarly, Americans with a strain of Huguenot blood, whose ancestors at the time of the revocation of the Edict of Nantes were forced to leave France may know the explanation for those events, but they cannot forget them. Many of the attitudes of fear which Protestants have toward the 

Church of Rome today are based in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. 

Until you understand that background, you are not going to relieve the various pressures and suspicions. "Until we understood these historical backgrounds, we cannot make much headway in the future. I would propose, therefore, that each group endeavor to explain for itself the history of the other groups." (13)

CHAPTER TWO SOME OBSTACLES TO MUTUAL UNDERSTANDING 

A large variety of examples of obstacles to understanding were submitted, giving rise to some confusion between symptoms of misunderstanding and the real causes of prejudice.

Experiences were mentioned that could not, with any amount of imagination, be laid at the door of religious intolerance; yet it was difficult for chairmen to rule them out because it was one of the purposes of the Seminar to disentangle the mixed motives and social attitudes that complicate, if they do not create, almost every problem in group relations. We have already seen, in the previous chapter, that in historical retrospect even those larger conflicts and adjustments that most clearly had their setting in religious controversy had elements of practical politics and, occasionally, of economic interest. 

Today, with much less popular interest in theological differences, and decreasing influence of creedal disputations on public affairs, it would be almost impossible to pick out cases of mutual antagonism, of discrimination, or of defamation between Americans of different faiths that can be wholly attributed to attitudes arising from differences of religious belief. Nevertheless, that such attitudes are part of the situation, and often the central factors, soon became evident as the descriptions of such situations passed before the members of the Seminar. Again and again it was pointed out in the course of the discussion that a particular tension between groups in America that are identified with a religious faith nevertheless derives from non-religious causes and is wholly divorced from religious dogma in its manifestations. Yet, when these manifestations were further examined, it was often found that a difference in beliefs entered into them at some point. To say, for example, that in many communities Catholicism is suspect because a majority of Catholics are foreign-born and the children of foreign-born, does not necessarily exclude a basically religious motive; for, antipathy toward the alien may be due precisely to the notion that he does not fit into a mode of life dominated by a Puritan Protestant tradition. This notion rests, it was pointed out repeatedly, in part on prejudice-since in fact the presence in the community of a large group of Catholics may in no way interfere with the pursuit of its accustomed ways by the Protestant majority-but in part it may be the result of experience: it may have been found that there is an irreducible minimum of resistance on the part of the minority, activated by religious principles, to a complete adjustment of the group to the established social pattern. (14)

I. 

SYMPTOMS OF GROUP INTOLERANCE 

There were three major categories of discrimination-economic (or vocational), social, and political; and in addition to these, or cutting across them, two other categories not related to one particular aspect of life; personal annoyance and more or less systematic defamation.

Before presenting the illustrations advanced by members under these various headings, it should be recorded that the Conference itself was fully aware of the limitations in the nature of the discussion of causes and remedies. 

Most of the illustrations were presented with insufficient detail to permit adequate analysis. In a considerable proportion of the cases, the Conference or the round table did not even agree with the speaker in his interpretation of the example given as a symptom of religious intolerance or, in fact, of any sort of prejudice. The cases are given here not as objective evidence but as illustrations, rather, of the very subjective response of people to an invitation to give their ideas of what constitutes expressions of antagonism between religious groups motivated by prejudice.1 Thus, the very disagreement and confusion accompanying a simple attempt to secure a picture of the situation will be seen as an outstanding factor in the whole problem. This is a first step toward the understanding which the Conference leaders tried to produce by the method of their procedure. 

a. Vocational Discrimination 

It was not necessary for members of the round tables to describe at great length the experiences which Jews and Catholics particularly often suffer as members of their respective groups when desiring to take advantage of American opportunities of livelihood on terms of equality with others. Almost every "case" adduced could have been multiplied from the personal knowledge of those present. Moreover, the different forms of vocational and other economic discrimination illustrated by these examples have become familiar through previous discussion in the relevant literature. For example: 

A Protestant minister in Washington discovered that members of his church were discriminated against in a section of the U. S. Navy Yard where the foremen were Catholics and gave preference to members of their own group. 

1 The editor suspects that many of the illustrations given are not even correct renderings of ascertainable facts; but of course it would have been impossible to check up on them for the purpose of the present publication, and for the most part the intelligent reader will have no difficulty in discerning the emotional coloring of statements made. Often correct observations have explanations, obvious to the social student though not to the speaker, that take them out of the category which they were intended to illustrate. (15)

A Department Store was said to refuse employment to any persons known as Jews, in any capacity.

Unitarians, it was reported, find it difficult to secure work in southern and middle-western communities with a history of conservative opposition to their creed.

Members of a social club refused to do business with a Catholic member although they had associated with him for thirty years. 

Not only in private but also sometimes in public employment, under civil service rules, Jews feel excluded from equality of opportunity by a requirement that applications be accompanied by photographs.

An interesting correlation was discovered between opportunities to exercise professions and training for them.

A Jewish complaint was that members of his group cannot reach positions of academic distinction in many New England colleges. 

A Protestant had difficulty in securing a teaching position because he had for twelve years taught at a Catholic school. 

In certain states, Catholic teachers have difficulty in securing appointments in public schools. 

The quota regulations imposed by certain medical schools upon Jewish applicants were explained with the difficulties experienced in securing appointments for Jewish graduates in hospital staffs in the region or state. 

As regards regulations of admission to colleges on grounds of religious affiliation, a general distinction was made between denominational schools where such discrimination, though by no means universal, is expected, and other institutions that do not openly admit sectarian leanings. 

A Jewish Ph.D. who applied for appointment as instructor in chemistry in his home-town college was refused on the ground that "this is a small college, and there must be close contact between students and teachers"-the assumption being that such close contact between Christian students and a Jewish teacher was either impossible or undesirable. 

It was stated that discriminations against Jewish applicants for employment often were found in Jewish firms; and that the reasons given were of a practical nature, such as the difficulty of meeting absences on Jewish holidays, temperamental maladjustment between Jewish and Gentile employees, excessive ambition and aggressiveness on the part of Jewish workers. 

It was said that at one eastern university with a predominantly Jewish body of students, non-Jewish members of the faculty had little chance of promotion. "The universities may want to be liberal and give degrees without discrimination," said a Catholic leader, "but often they cannot place their Jewish and Catholic graduates in the smaller colleges and schools; and that makes them timid about admissions."

A middle-western state university justified the refusal of a Jewish applicant for a teaching position, qualified for the job, on the ground that he would be "a foreign element in a homogeneous group." The head of this university, in giving this explanation to the applicant, denied that there was any discrimination, but refused to enter into a correspondence on the matter with an interested Jewish organization. (16)

In one state, Catholic lawyers, even when highly esteemed in the profession, and although a member of that Church was not long ago unanimously elected president of the Bar Association, are rarely appointed to the Bench. 

It was reported that, with an increasing discrimination against Jews in medical schools, a number of Jewish students each year leave this country to study medicine in Scotland and other European countries. An increasing entrance of Jewish students from eastern colleges in southern medical colleges in recent years, it was said, had been followed by the adoption of quota restrictions on the part of these institutions. 

At another session, Mrs. Estelle M. Sternberger, Executive Secretary of the National Council of Jewish Women, reported on a recent study of discrimination in admissions to medical schools: "In defense of their practices it has been stated that since the Jews represent only three or four per cent of the population of the United States, physicians ought not to be graduated in numbers excessively beyond that population percentage, particularly since it was claimed that non-Jewish patients will not avail themselves of the services of Jewish physicians. "In my opinion, the question can hardly be analyzed or defended from a logical basis, since it is essentially a question of inter-group prejudice and inter-group conflict. We have not yet arrived at the day when we are ready to accept a man for his intrinsic worth and to allow him to go ahead in the world as far as he can utilize the opportunities that should be open to all." 

Discriminations made by employment agencies were seen as somewhat different from those made directly by employers, since usually they do not rest upon direct experience and often do not represent the expressed wishes of the employer-client. In some cases, these agencies increase difficulties for members of religious minorities by asking their employer-clients to state their religious preferences. On the other hand, the suggestion was made that by ascertaining such preferences and acting upon them employment agencies avoid much unnecessary effort and disappointment for work-seekers, conditions being what they are. 

b. Social Discriminations 

Typical of many similar cases was the account of a Jewish citizen in the national capital who, on visiting a large new apartment house with a view to renting an apartment, found in the business office a sign to the effect that the management "caters only to Gentile applicants."

Women's clubs even those of a democratic and civic character, it was reported from New England, while they do not discriminate against minority groups in their membership, "show prejudice so far as representation on their boards and in the appointment of executive offiicers is concerned. Although there is sufficient material among Jews and Catholics, you will find that the majority of the executives are Protestants." (17)

Jewish women, it was said at a round table, feel keenly the slight in not being offered the honor of being elected to important offices in a large national women's organization to which they have contributed more than their proportional share financially and in every phase of work. 

A woman with a German-Jewish name, after having bought a ticket for a cruise on a first-rate liner, found that she was not permitted to sit at the table she had chosen in the dining saloon, though several places at it were vacant. The steamship company explained that the whole accommodation of the first class had originally been chartered by a club which was thought to be entitled to preferential treatment, even though some tickets were sold to the general public. This explanation did not satisfy the passenger who felt herself discriminated against as a Jewess. 

A Jewish resident felt obliged to sell his house and move into another neighborhood because of increasing tension between his children and those of neighboring families. This originated with a childish quarrel in which adults took part by taking sides against "you darned Jews." 

These and other experiences were later scrutinized as to the extent to which the exercise of preference in social contacts contains elements of religious intolerance or of social prejudice. 

c. Political Discrimination 

Although much of the discussion had for its background a special awareness to the historical political issues between different religious groups, there were relatively few specific charges of such intolerance. Or perhaps it would be more correct to say that the outstanding difficulties faced by religious minorities in the political life of the nation were too well known to require detailed illustration. Moreover, they were felt to be more related to the processes by which public opinion is influenced than to obvious inequalities in the enjoyment of civic rights and hence came up more prominently in discussions of defamation as a means of reaping group advantages from the dissemination of intolerance.

Among specific complaints made, however, the following may be mentioned:

The present evidences of anti-prohibition sentiment in Southern political circles was held to indicate that not prohibition but religious intolerance was the main reason for turning the Southern Democratic vote against the Democratic candidate for the Presidency in 1928. 

Considerable uneasiness was expressed over the beginnings of a "Hitlerite" movement in the United States. Although representatives of that movement had publicly declared that it was fostered in emigrant German circles exclusively in relation to the politics of the fatherland, it was feared that hospitality to such a movement in America could not but re-awaken unfortunate divisions and inflame unfortunate prejudices here. (18)

Specific illustrations given of difficulties experienced by religious minorities in relation to the Federal Government referred to questions concerning army chaplaincy during the War. But there was no charge of prejudice. The introduction of Jewish chaplains, it was said, was a novelty for which no precedents existed in the regulations governing appointment or uniform. 

d. Personal Annoyance 

A Catholic speaker first made the suggestion that there are many annoyances suffered by members of minority groups which are too isolated or too insignificant to be regarded as discriminations but which nevertheless, though experienced by individuals, have their causes in social situations and lead to social tension. 

It is annoying to meet with misunderstanding of the beliefs and ways of one's group; and even more so to be labelled as an individual with the assumed characteristics of the group to which one belongs-"Jews are neurotic"; "Catholics never do anything without first asking the priest"; and so on. 

Members of religious minorities are annoyed by reading untruths about their groups or hearing rumors concerning it based upon misunderstanding or prejudice—even though they may not as individuals be obviously harmed by such expressions. If loyal members of Church or Synagogue they especially resent misstatements concerning the nature of their faith or the position of their religious organization, which they are often powerless, as individuals, to combat. "Personal annoyance results from lies told, caricatures presented, abusive language used in conversation-especially when you hear what you are not meant to hear-and from reading certain kinds of cheap, bigoted newspapers.' 

Anonymous letters, some, but not all, obviously written by pathological cases, are frequently received by Catholic leaders, attacking them and their Church. A score of abusive letters were received by the National Conference officers in connection with the Washington Seminar: several attacked the 

Jews, while the larger number denounced Catholics. Two abused "liberals" and "modernists" among the Protestants. e. Defamation

This personal annoyance as a separate category is closely related, then, to the larger evils of defamation as an expression of religious intolerance. 

Under this heading quite a number of examples were given to illustrate the point that, whether they lead to actual discriminations against individuals or not, the spread of derogatory misinformation about groups has an exceedingly mischievous effect upon social and political life. 

In several round tables a recent article in the Army and Navy Register, an unofficial organ published in Washington, was discussed, which had branded Jews as a disloyal group. The article had been answered immediately by a 

Jewish veterans' organization and by public officials, who drew attention to (19)

the excellent war record of American Jews. But it was felt that charges of this sort are never completely cancelled in their influence, no matter how effective the answer.

Two illustrations given had to do with Jewish industrial enterprise. In both cases the defamatory nature was said to lie in the generalization of charges against a large and varied religious and racial group; in one of them investigation had proved the charges made unfounded even in limited application:

A good many of the old mills in New England have had to close down in recent years; and some of the concerns have moved South for a variety of purely economic reasons. In some cases, the old mill plants had been closed for a number of years when enterprising individuals from New York bought them up.

Since there had been much unemployment, and wages never had been high in the cotton industry of New England, these newcomers were able to take advantage of the plight of the disorganized labor market and began operations on an exceedingly low wage scale. But local leaders of public opinion chose to deal with this situation on racial rather than industrial lines: "The talk went around, and I heard it at clerical meetings in Fall River and New Bedford, that it is Jews who are coming to buy up these mills and who pay these miserable wages. I have investigated the matter a little, and so has Miss (member of an old New England family) who published some of her results in the Boston papers. She makes no mention anywhere of the fact that Jews are predominant among those who are buying up these mills and paying these low wages. Yet, the impression is going around that it is 

Jewish people, with Jewish capital, who have come there to force down labor conditions."

A town in West Virginia reported that a Chamber of Commerce had subsidized capitalists from New York (business men who happened to be Jews) urging them to open a shirt factory in order to increase the industry in that town. Wages were reported as very low. A feeling against "those Jews" arose and many people failed to implicate either the unemployment crisis or the city fathers (not Jewish) who lured the clothing manufacturers with actual subvention. "The Jewish community in Southern California," another member reported, "is made uneasy by reason of the tremendous amount of ill-will directed against Jewish producers of motion pictures. Of course, it is true that many motion pictures are not quite desirable; but people fail to discriminate between the individual producers of these films and the industry as a whole, which is what it is because of certain factors in American interests, in the American mind."

As to illustrations of defamatory rumors and their spread, many speakers referred to the recently published book, "The Shadow of the Pope" by Michael Williams. Others referred to the continuing circulation, especially in the more remote parts of the country, of old and frequently refuted verbal traditional stories about Catholic practices, the life of the Catholic clergy, and the like.

PREDISPOSING CONDITIONS 

To recognize symptoms of religious intolerance and to classify them under convenient categories is the first stage toward understanding the nature of the evil. But it does not suffice for its diagnosis. The physician must know not only where it hurts but also what are the occasions on which the hurt is felt or the situations that bring on the symptoms. The illustrations that have been listed often exhibit no more than an eruption through which an unwholesome state of mind becomes apparent. They give fleeting glimpses of misunderstandings, emotions, attitudes that are the real causes of tension and friction between groups. But tension and friction do not necessarily result from them, just as the presence of a disease germ does not necessarily produce an inflammation and a fever. The acute stage of a disease, which makes it recognizable, occurs when certain conditions make the normally more or less harmless activity of a germ virulent, and intensify its power of reproduction. 

What, then, are the conditions under which a latent intolerance or misunderstanding or antagonism between religious groups in America is liable to manifest itself in painful intensity? This question was asked, in varying form, in several of the round tables and, as a further preliminary to diagnosis, provided a number of interesting clues. It was found, from the experience of the members, that certain types of occurrence or occasion have the effect of reviving and making manifest a latent conflict: for example, where one of the groups attempts to impress its convictions upon the community in forms that do violence to the convictions of other groups. Such occasions arise in legislative campaigns, and again, in aggressive methods of propagating the faith. They also arise, and perhaps as frequently, from the opposite tendency: the withdrawal of a group from participation in common ventures, its determined self-isolation, its exhibition of either fear or a sense of superiority in response to proposals that might tend to bridge differences, its hypersensitiveness to insult, even when unintended. 

Further considered, these phenomena seemed to indicate an always latent and in given situations active conflict between two opposing forces in American community life: the tendency toward uniformity and the tendency to protect separateness in order to insure group survival. Thus an injudicious missionary zeal was seen to have a twofold disruptive influence: in the group upon which it exercises itself it produces an often violent reaction; in the missionary group itself it surrounds itself with those concomitant attitudes of contempt and depreciation by which faith in the rightness of its cause is strengthened. The word "injudicious" has here been used because the conference was conscious that not all missionary activity produces these effects; and, indeed, a not altogether successful effort was made to distinguish by definition desirable from undesirable forms of the effort to share the values of one's religion- (21)

applying to the one the term of missionary enterprise, and to the other that of proselytism. 

Group identity and loyalty were seen to be threatened not so much, however, by direct methods of conversion through persuasion, as by indirect methods; hence the Seminar paid particular attention to two ways in which adherence to a religious faith is undermined: intermarriage between members of religious groups, and school policies and methods by which children are indoctrinated with a creed different from that of their parents. Intermarriage

It is noteworthy that only in relatively recent times has intermarriage assumed an important rôle in inter-group relations. It required that intensification of social contact and of the desire for unification which came with modern conditions of life to make intermarriage between members of different faiths other than a relatively rare marginal occurrence. The older literature of religious conflict has few references to this subject compared with the extensive study that has been devoted to it of late. Today, members of both minorities and majorities recognize its importance: "As a minister of a New England parish, I know that the bringing together of a Protestant and a Catholic in matrimony produces difficult problems; after a period, there usually comes a separation because of the difference in religion. Especially where there are children, this becomes a vital issue." "I have a sister in an art school where she has Catholics for close friends," said an officer of a Protestant organization. "My family is apprehensive not so much from a concern for my sister's religion as from fear that this relation may lead to marriage with a Catholic, and from what we know of the Catholic attitude toward intermarriage.' 

"Fear of intermarriage," said a Catholic leader, "produces a situation that leads to vocational and social discriminations and to personal annoyance; in itself it is not necessarily a cause of friction between groups.” 

A Protestant minister betrayed his own emotional reaction toward this problem when he said: "Marriage lies at the foundation of human society. 

How can we overcome the unfairness to Protestants in the Catholic attitude toward intermarriage?"

In one round table, a Protestant minister pointedly asked what the attitude of a Jewish rabbi would be if his daughter proposed to marry the son of an Episcopal bishop. To this, two contrasting answers were given: one rabbi declared that there would be no opposition if the two young people recognized a common denominator of religious values; another referred to the rapid disintegration of Judaism in America as a cause of serious concern that could not but influence his personal attitude in such a hypothetical case: "My three daughters all have Gentile friends; but I should hate to see one of them marry a Christian and thus contribute toward the extinction of the race." (22)

Seeming exclusiveness and "pride" were seen, then, as not necessarily expressive of an antagonistic attitude but as resulting from apprehensions for which there is a good deal of factual justification. The danger to mutual understanding, it was found, does not so much lie in social restrictions that spring from this motive as in their misinterpretation. 

Teaching of Religion in Public Schools 

In the same way, a Catholic spokesman admitted: "There is considerable prejudice against Catholics growing out of their maintenance of a separate school system." Catholics and Jews resent being regarded as aliens and outsiders, discussion in one round table brought out; but so far as the former are concerned, more especially, this irritation arises more from a popular misunderstanding of the reasons why they maintain an expensive system of parochial schools than from the separation of the children in itself. Although the great majority of Catholic and Jewish children attend the public schools of America, it was stated, this does not necessarily prevent friction. Even when in the major policies of a school system there is nothing objectionable to the religious minorities, nevertheless occasions are apt to arise which place children from these groups in a difficult position. Thus Jewish members referred to many situations when such children are compelled either to take part in ceremonies that are contrary to the religious teaching they receive in their homes or else to make themselves conspicuous to their classmates as being different. Of this further examples will be given in the next chapter. 

Missionary Methods 

Concerning missionary propaganda, a distinction was attempted between methods of lure, coercion, deception, to persuade persons to forsake the faith of their fathers, on the one hand, and intellectual methods of conversion by a reasonable exposition of differences in belief. However, it was found that this distinction cannot always clearly be made. Not only is there a difference in the receptivity of adults and of children, so that what is fair persuasion in the one case becomes "kidnapping" in the case of children, according to one Jewish editor; but among adults also there are differences of mental caliber, and what among intellectual equals may be an admissible form of persuasion too often becomes a form of uneducational indoctrination when applied with all the methods of emotional conditioning to the simple and the ignorant. 

Type of Community 

Two questions relating to the present subject-the conditions that predispose to tension between religious groups-were left unanswered, or rather were answered by different speakers in opposite ways. One of them is: Does the relatively greater propinquity of life in small towns predispose to better mutual understanding between groups than does the opportunity of religious (23)

and racial groups in larger communities to provide a self-sufficient group life for themselves? The view of most observers seemed to be that in the small town religious differences are more acute; and the reason, as one speaker explained it, is that people are more closely attached to their church which is more exclusively the center of their social life than it is in the big city. 

Another explanation given was that often the smaller town, with fewer resources of adult education, is more ignorant, so that every sort of rumor and suspicion takes a firmer root there. On the other hand, the head of a nationwide Jewish organization at one of the general meetings gave it as his experience that economic discriminations against Jews flourish most in the large metropolitan centers. While no specific explanation was given for this statement, it may here be suggested that in the great cities competition is, perhaps, more severe, and vocational appointments are more apt to be made in accordance with general principles and prejudices than from personal knowledge of the applicants.

Isolation The other question, incompletely answered, was: Does group solidarity, so far as Catholics and Jews in predominantly Protestant communities are concerned, make for greater friction or for greater respect and mutual understanding between the adherents of the different faiths? On the one hand, it was urged that the sense of difference that expresses itself in such friction is a measure of religious loyalty; that when all avoidable misunderstandings and clashes have been removed, there will still remain an essential conflict between the groups which is wholesome and preferable to a lessening of group distinctions. On the other hand, it was felt that friction results more from lack of acquaintance than from difference. In so far as Protestants, Catholics, and Jews can agree to limit their separateness to those things which are essential to their respective faiths and to bridge those mutual exclusions that have arisen historically from conditions inimical to harmony, they will overcome misunderstanding without loss of separate group loyalties. In one of the round tables, a rabbi attempted to demonstrate that anti-Jewish discrimination tends to grow in proportion to the degree of concentration. 

Wherever Jews provide a completely rounded social life for themselves, with educational institutions of their own, in Europe as well as in America, prejudice against them increases. This statement and others similar to it were not accepted without reservations. Many believed that an intense group loyalty on the basis of a deep religious conviction makes for mutual liking; that the chief bearers of prejudices in every community are persons without religious faith and without standing in their own religious community; that religious tolerance means respect for all who sincerely adhere to one of the established confessions, but does not include respect for those who take their religious affiliation lightly. This assumption will be examined further. (24)

3. CAUSES OF GROUP INTOLERANCE 

Two sessions in which selected spokesmen for the three groups and for the scientific disciplines relevant to the subjects on the agenda threshed out difficult questions of interpretation and policy, with the whole conference membership as audience, proved the Seminar's most interesting contribution to conference procedure. The first of these "U-table" discussions endeavored to "find and define the main problems and issues." 

Has religious intolerance any significance at all in the present day, apart from national, class, and racial prejudices? was one of the questions that had been raised. Some of the members seemed to feel that the religious element was brought out by some circles simply to disguise much less respectable emotions of self-interest and bigotry. "As between Protestants and Catholics," a Jewish member felt, "it may be largely a religious issue; but I am doubtful even as to that. It is a question of the Anglo-Saxon myth against the Latin, Celtic, and Slavic races. As far as the Jew is concerned, it is very definitely a racial problem. . . . I noticed a considerable over-emphasis of religious issues over and against social and economic issues; all possible factors in the fight for freedom of conscience and religion cannot obscure the facts of economic and social discrimination." 

Since this feeling was shared by others, some attention was paid to the social patterns within which ostensible religious antagonisms are often found. 

Cultural characteristics which distinguish one group from another were recognized as more potent causes of mutual dislike than differences in creed. 

But often the "social distance" between such groups produces a mutual ignorance of interests, tastes, and beliefs which makes it a good soil for the growth of misunderstanding and misrepresentation of their respective faiths. A 

Catholic speaker, therefore, found much support when he insisted that "ignorance" should be listed as a separate cause of prejudice. 

Social distance also makes for fear-fear, for example, of the centralized organization of the Catholic Church and its political power which tends to be greatly exaggerated. Members of the Seminar did not need to be reminded. that subversive designs of a different order have often been insinuated also into campaigns against Jewish candidates for office and against Jews generally. 

These insinuations are directed with equal force against the part which Jews are asserted to play in communism and in the organization of a capitalistic international super-government. Rumors of "conspiracies," whether of the 

Catholic Church or of the "Elders of Zion," have played a conspicuous part in every organized movement against Catholics and Jews. 

In this connection, some attention was paid to the process by which fear and dislike of foreignness (xenophobia) combine with other psychological factors in the creation of stereotypes that tend to fill out and to perpetuate (25)

erroneous views as to the character of other groups. Such stereotypes flourish most where the other group is least known through personal contact. The individual member of such a group is supposed to conform to the mental picture of it and so is exposed to misunderstanding and discrimination. "Prejudice," said a psychologist, "rests upon an acceptance of certain 'pictures in people's heads'; these 'stencils,' largely words, really, serve to characterize groups which are thought of as units. And so Mr. Isaacson to many people is not an individual of education first and secondarily a Jew, but a Jew first and only incidentally something else. "When you start that way in dealing with people, it seems to me, you mark yourself as prejudiced. When such a picture in the head, such a notion, or whatever you want to call it, applied to a group as a term of classification, is used as a category into which you drop the individual first before judging him as an individual, then you have prejudice. This is something entirely different from antipathy, from likes and dislikes of people, which are natural and without which social life could not go on." 

Another speaker further tried to clarify this distinction by saying: "Prejudice in the bad sense is not honest difference of opinion or liking, due to sincere conviction or to natural differences of taste that are consistent with mutual friendliness and respect; but it is an unfriendly emotional reaction, due to misinformation, narrow group consciousness, personal selfishness, or a sincere belief which works out in this emotional way." 

The tendency to rationalize prejudice until it resembles a creditable exercise of judgment was further explored. But a stereotype is recognizable by several features: for one thing, it is always unfavorable to the individual who is classified before he has been heard; it jumps over contrary facts which the prejudiced person has ample opportunity to know; it usually has been latent unconsciously for a long time before it is revealed by some incident. It was pointed out that classifications and unconscious characterizations in accordance with a stereotype occur between all sorts of people. Americans who form such pictures of other peoples and of distinct groups in their own country are themselves victims of this mental trait abroad: we do not think of a particularly boisterous person as an American-but Europeans often do. 

Frequent exposure to prejudice and discrimination produces an attitude of mind which itself sometimes becomes an additional cause of dislike; hypersensitiveness which predisposes a person to see or expect insult or to form a stereotype of his own concerning the group in the community from which he or his own people have experienced insult in the past. 

While, thus, other elements loomed larger in the situation than creedal difference as a cause of prejudice, there was testimony to the fact that, especially in orthodox and conservative groups with a strong emotional attachment to their religious institutions, religious difference often is the central point in a defensive attitude that includes a wider cultural area. Especially where (26)

such a religious group is a small minority, it tends to exaggerate the part played by religious beliefs in social matters and thus succeeds in creating a stronger cohesion in its own membership.

Not only religious leaders, but parents and teachers likewise are apt to fortify existing prejudices in a needless extension of the desire for exclusion to relationships where it becomes a cause of group antagonism and disruption. "The younger generation," said a college teacher of religion, "would rid itself of many racial and religious prejudices if it were left to itself. But the existing attitudes of home and faculty prevent this." 

Thus the discussion attempted to find the Golden Mean in cultural loyalties, escaping both chauvinism at the one extreme and indifference at the other. The cultural drives of self-preservation and perpetuation abiding in every normal group express themselves in strong methods of conditioning the young. Hence the question: is not a certain amount of social conflict a price that must be paid? Can these conflicts between culture groups be made constructive and creative, rather than be allowed to take the forms of outrages or unsocial isolation? b. Prejudice as a Tool of Policy

The creation of prejudice, the appeal to prejudice, and the perpetuation of prejudice-all have their recognized techniques and their trained practitioners. Compared with the efficiency with which methods of indoctrination are applied in the deliberate effort to stir up antagonisms in the defense of the in-group, the undesigned by-products of prejudicial teaching in home, school, and church seemed to many of the members insignificant. This judgment was based on the contention, however, that both religious and secular education contains many elements of conditioning for intolerance that are not accidental but part of a deliberate policy. It was hinted that, with the large advances made in pedagogy, interpretations of history, of literature, or of the Bible that keep alive old group prejudices could have been eliminated long ago were it not for an actual desire to hand on from generation to generation traditional attitudes of loyalty with their full concomitant of hatreds. What is needed here, it was contended, is a more wide-spread recognition, among teachers, parents, and religious leaders, of the pernicious social consequences of such practices and a larger awareness of the value of humanitarian ideals that are supressed in the process.

The question was asked whether the continued Jewish teaching that their group is "God's Chosen People" does not contribute as much to the mutual estrangement and the growth of prejudice as any Christian teaching about the Jews. In answer it was said that Jewish teaching no longer emphasizesand in many institutions no longer mentions-this thesis, or endeavors to (27)

explain it on historical grounds with the peculiar position of the Jews in ancient times, or even psychologically as a concomitant of the ethical and spiritual missionary rôle which Jews assumed and tried to keep alive throughout the ages.

While this was seen as a task which it would take a long time to accomplish, a more obvious and immediate challenge was seen in the uses of prejudicial propaganda for purely selfish ends that do not even have the excuse of a mistaken zeal for group preservation. The unscrupulous politician who attempts to rise to power through a campaign of libel, the professional lecturer who holds his public by a clever manipulation of latent prejudices in the minds of his hearers, the organization that thrives on libellous appeals to them these are enemies that can be effectively combated by a courageous exposure of their purposes and methods. Many examples were given to show how largely this profit-motive has affected the practices of often seemingly respectable organizations. 

Even some forms of proselyting were seen as insincere and prejudice-raising efforts with a profit motive. Unfair methods may be used to build up the membership of a religious institution in competition with other institutions. 

Cases of Protestant missions in Catholic and Jewish neighborhoods were charged by some as often having no basis in sincere religious conviction but as betraying in the methods used a lack of respect for faiths honestly held and lack of sensitiveness to ethical principles. Indeed, in one round table, proselyting generally was given a definition of extreme severity (a definition which itself may possibly be held to contain elements of prejudice): “It is the use of unfair methods to lure, coerce, deceive, largely directed against children; to cause them to forsake the faith to which they belong and to embrace a new faith."

The very fact that many leaders in religious movements refuse to associate themselves with efforts such as those of the National Conference of Jews and Christians was held by some to indicate that these people at least indirectly favor the use of prejudice as an instrument of policy. More direct evidences of this attitude were seen in letters received on this and similar occasions in which the writers endorse the view succinctly expressed by one of them who roundly declared that "it is the business of the Church to convert 

Jews not to dine them." (28)

CHAPTER THREE SOME SPECIAL FACTORS IN GROUP RELATIONS 

On the third day of the Seminar, the members distributed themselves over nine section meetings each of which took up one specific area of contact or one specific potential approach to the combat of religious intolerance in America. These group meetings, some of them very largely attended, had no uniform method of procedure: some of them explored rather thoroughly the problems brought before them, others proceeded almost immediately to an evaluation of various proposals made to make one instrument of public opinion contributive to greater harmony between members of different faiths. I.

CHURCH SCHOOLS 

The question proposed by the leader, Dean Henry H. Meyer, of Boston 

University School of Religious Education, was: "Has the church school any part in causing children to become prejudiced against other religious groups?" 

This soon resolved itself into the further question: "Is religious prejudice ever a product of religious education, or is it a product of environment?" In a group predisposed to think well of religious education in its general results, obviously the answer was: children usually come to the church school with prejudices already well formed; religious instruction lessens rather than increases them. 

Members of all three faiths seemed on the whole united in this belief. 

On the other hand, the existence of private parochial schools was considered a cause of prejudice, in that those who attend them are thus marked off from the children in the public schools. This sense of strangeness often is accentuated, according to further testimony, by the desire of teachers in the religious schools to emphasize in the minds of their pupils the sense of difference. It was felt by some that the existence, side by side, of public and parochial schools need not produce tension or even a pronounced sense of difference if steps were taken to bring the children together on appropriate occasions or, at any rate, to make them conscious of their common stake in relation to the community and the nation. 

In so far as it was admitted that the teaching in religious schools (Sunday as well as weekday) does contribute at times to mutual prejudices between children of different faiths, a distinction was made, both in force and in nature, between the influences of the curriculum and of the teachers. Jewish members challenged the large part played by the Crucifixion story in the teaching of children. They felt that, even in the hands of tolerant teachers, the typical bible lesson on this subject could hardly fail to impress Christian children with the thought that "Jews must be rejected since they rejected (29)

Jesus." The interpretation given to the bible story was considered by some of the rabbis present as essentially untrue, since the Jewish recognition of Jesus as a great teacher and prophet was in no way different from that of a large section of Protestant Christianity and, in a sense, was appreciation rather than rejection. As a race or a nation or members of a religious organization, the 

Jews never had rejected Jesus.

One of the round tables considered this matter important enough to adopt formally the following statement: "We feel that one of the constructive ways in which to overcome Christian unfriendliness toward Jews is to modify the manner in which the story of the Crucifixion is taught in many Christian Sunday schools. Recognizing that this story is an integral part of Christian instruction, we urge that those who have charge of educational work in the various bodies be persuaded to encourage the teachers to use a presentation of the Crucifixion in which the emphasis will be somewhat changed. "It is our conviction that it would be helpful to stress, first of all, the fact that the Crucifixion is an ancient event; that though some Jews assisted 

Rome in accomplishing the downfall of Jesus, there were at the very time. 

Jews who loved Him and who accompanied Him on the Via Dolorosa; that many of the followers of His teaching then were Jews; that Jews were the first Christians; and, above all, that there is no place in modern religious life for antipathies between people because of ancient differences. What should be emphasized is the teaching cardinal to both Judaism and Christianity and urged by Jesus himself: "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself.""

At one of the round tables, a speaker made a distinction between (1.) a broadening of religious teaching until the doctrines of a Church have been watered down to the point where all can accept it, and (2.) a courageous attitude of study and reverence toward the reverences of other faiths. The difficulty, he said, does not lie in a tenacious clinging to accepted tenets but in a disinclination to face new facts. A firm conviction that one's own faith is the right one need not make one arrogant in one's attitude toward other faiths.

Again, the objection was made that so long as any Church claims to have complete and final authority, tolerance between religious groups is not possible. 

The difference of opinion lay in the definition of tolerance. It was, for some of the members at least, resolved in later sessions of the Conference, not by further theoretical argument, but by a common search for practical constructive methods of reaching a larger unity between members of different faiths. "Evidently," said one of the speakers, "we do offend one another. And unless we talk frankly about it, we can never get over this situation. For instance, I have learned here for the first time that I have been offending the Jews. I have talked a good many times about Jesus having been killed by the 

Jews of His time. He was [by some Jews]. But I have learned that many (30)

Jews are sensitive about this and are offended because we Protestants talk so much about it. Of course, we orthodox Protestants tell one another that we are constantly crucifying Christ anew. We accuse one another of doing it. We must in the same direct way explain ourselves to members of other faiths."

Whether a frank exchange, such as that taking place at this Conference, would dissolve prejudices and eventually so affect all religious education as to free it from the blemishes that now attach to it, was discussed at considerable length. In the end the view seemed to prevail that it is desirable for the leaders, both nationally and in each community, occasionally to undergo this wholesome experience which would make them more aware than even the most broad-minded of them had been before of the barnacles of prejudice that attach themselves to every strong religious conviction in its sincere application to the problems of daily life. It was considered doubtful, on the other hand, whether such cleansing experience could at this stage be introduced with good effect into the general processes of religious education. Progress toward mutual understanding normally comes not from stressing deep-seated differences of belief but from an honest effort to strengthen existing common ties. If the teaching of the Sunday schools is to contribute largely to greater tolerance between various groups in American life, it should deal increasingly with these ethical problems on the solution of which members of all Churches can cooperate, each inspired by the doctrinal teachings of his own faith. The importance of working together in constructive projects in the interests of social justice, the elimination of poverty, public welfare, world peace, and other major concerns of the community as a whole, were repeatedly emphasized. A given illustration of this was The Baltimore Federation of Christian and Jewish Youth. (Details can be obtained from the National Conference office).

How such cooperation can best be effected will further be considered in the next chapter. 

2. PUBLIC SCHOOLS 

The part played by the public school in overcoming religious intolerance was, as we have already seen, discussed from the start as one of the most important factors in the whole situation. Traditionally, the American public school is looked upon as the strongest unifying element in our national life. 

It has been extolled time and again as the one institution that knows no class, race, or nationality but treats all alike and teaches the vast majority of American children respect for the fundamental rights of man. In practice, however, this rôle has not been filled completely by the American school system at any time; and it does not fill it now. (31)

In the section meeting, the existence of such intolerance was confirmed by reference to the results of attitude tests in the public schools of Washington and other cities. There was the familiar tendency to link up few individual experiences with current unfavorable rumors about members of other faiths, and to interpret the former in the light of the latter. As a contributive factor, found in some communities, the organization of public school teachers in religious groups was mentioned. Examples were given of the effect of such stimulated group-consciousness upon discrimination in the appointment of teachers.

Teachers who attended this session seemed to be agreed that both the attitudes of public authorities toward the public school system and the attitudes of the teachers expressed in their dealing with non-religious subjects are stronger factors than the controversy concerning Bible teaching in the schools. 

In some places the school system is essentially still a Protestant school system: celebration of the Christian holidays is taken as a matter of course; "good character" in the appointment of teachers is interpreted as involving membership in a Protestant Church; the textbooks used and extra-curricular activities promoted are expressive of a single cultural outlook, that of the Protestant majority; while there may be little open hostility toward Jews or Catholics, the teaching is imbued with an exclusive nativism that creates suspicion for everything that does not fit into the local tradition. It is natural for children brought up in such schools to believe that Jews are unpatriotic, and that Catholics are trying to set up a Papal government in the United States. One school principal gave unconsciously a typical illustration of this attitude by saying that she tried to make her school "a social reflection of the emotional tone of the community: the child is taught to sing Christmas carols, not as a 

Christian practice but as a community expression of seasonal religious interest." 

Evidently, the attitude of religious minorities was not understood much better by others. Several teachers stated emphatically that Jews have exaggerated the contributions of the public school teacher to religious sectarianism at 

Christmas and Easter. "At Christmas time, the tree and the spirit of giving are stressed—not the Nativity story." That Jewish parents object to having their children interested in Christianity in this indirect way, they did not, apparently, appreciate; nor that the school as a public institution, by celebrating these holidays, does practically acknowledge a state religion. On the other hand, there was general agreement that reading portions of the New Testament, even without comment, and recitals of the Lord's Prayer, in public schools, are practices which, while adding little to religious education, do constitute a legitimate grievance for Jewish parents eager to bring up their children in their own faith.

The question came up whether the teaching of history is not as full of (32)

possible occasions for offending the sensitiveness of minority groups as any definitely religious teaching may be. Too often, it was pointed out, history as taught in our schools either leaves out or misrepresents the cultural, religious factors that have played so large a part in the formation of present group divisions and in creating the fears and suspicions that still survive. But then again, would not the bringing out of these historical influences on contemporary life increase friction between groups among which there already is tension? Should not, in the teaching of children, at any rate, those phases be emphasized, rather, that stress the common stake in nation and community? 

There was no unanimity on such questions as these which, with the limitations of time, could not, of course, be fully explored. Some felt that exactly at this point education in the religious school could helpfully supplement the teaching in the public school.

Another outcome of the discussion was agreement that in the teaching of all the social sciences, in the public schools as well as in religious schools, there could be greater emphasis on the rôle played by tolerance and cooperation in human affairs. It was thought that there was often too much one-sided concern with conflict, as though it were the only principle of social advance. 

Significantly it was at the section meeting of representatives of women's organizations that the most far-reaching suggestion in this connection was made, namely that the method of teaching in general requires modification. 

Children should be encouraged, it was said, to subject all the materials taught them to critical scrutiny in their own minds instead of accepting without intellectual effort, other than that of memory, the interpretations given by the teacher. 

At one of the general sessions, the question was raised whether it would be practicable for the public schools presumably high schools - to go even further and to introduce courses for the analysis of prejudice, "to warn pupils of their own weaknesses in that respect and how insidiously prejudices can creep in, and to build up a mental attitude that will ward them off." Various actual experiments made with that aim in view were reported upon, which will be further dealth with below. (See p. 65 et seq.) A Protestant editor of long experience suggested that, perhaps, the need for such courses was even greater in the training of teachers. "You must educate the teachers before can educate the children." you [Details regarding the experiment in high school assembly programs interpreting the contributions of various cultural groups to American civilization, can be obtained from the National Conference office. Rachael Davis DuBois has been at work for several years gathering materials and successfully employing them.] (33)

3. WOMEN'S ORGANIZATIONS 

The section meeting on this subject was introduced with an address by 

Mrs. Estelle M. Sternberger, Executive Secretary of the National Council of Jewish Women, who pointed out that women had a special stake in the concern of the Seminar, both as the chief sufferers from intolerance in every form and as particularly sensitive to the sufferings of others. To rid the world of religious intolerance was part of the task upon which modern women had embarked when they undertook the struggle for human equality. She proposed that women's organizations should inquire in what specific ways that intolerance affects women, and in what specific ways they could, as organizations, combat it. Thus, for example, associations of Jewish women often had before them complaints from mothers of young women who are excluded from women's colleges because of their Jewish faith. Admission of Jewish students on a quota basis also extended to private schools. A study had shown that in some instances such schools admit no Jewish students because the parents who send their daughters to these schools do not want them to meet socially girls from the Jewish group. No such explanation could be claimed, however, to explain the limitation of admission of Jewish students to medical schools which she had had an opportunity to study. (See p. 17.)

The question was raised whether women's organizations had a distinctive contribution to make on questions such as these. It was suggested that, perhaps, women had got more into the habit, through other civic and charitable endeavors, to work together with those of other faiths. But significantly the time-absorbing effort in these directions by women leaders was given as an explanation, on behalf of Catholic women, why often it was difficult for them to take part in new, suggested activities. 

Another suggestion was to the effect that women, in their organizational meetings, might spend more time to examine their own prejudices as a helpful preliminary to efforts for dealing with those of other groups. 

While the discussion largely duplicated that of other round tables and is, therefore included under other topics in the present report, many of the women present at the section meeting showed awareness to the fact that it is primarily in the area of social relations that women are carrying on old prejudices, and that it is in that area that they can make their largest specific contribution toward greater tolerance. In their active concern with selfeducation, women's clubs have an opportunity, it was said, to break down intellectual barriers, to face the facts involved in the group antagonisms of American life "with simple intellectual honesty and sincerity." At one of the other round tables it was a woman member who said: "Let us hope that one of the things that will come out of this Seminar will be that women's clubs will study concretely the problems of inter-religious group relations." (34)

4. THE COMMUNITY SEMINAR 

Since it was a review, in a sense, of the major program of the National Conference of Jews and Christians itself, the section meeting on the community seminar had several distinct advantages over those previously reported. 

Most of those who took part had personally participated in one or more of such educational efforts; the philosophical basis for these was understood and more or less agreed upon; there was thus at the same time less need for a fundamental analysis and a larger fund of experience with constructive methods to draw upon. Rabbi Ferdinand Isserman, of St. Louis, presided.

After several of these seminars, sponsored by the National Conference, and related enterprises had been reported upon and discussed, the question was asked whether the method is applicable in the smaller communities-so often (as has been seen above) hotbeds of prejudice and of active defamatory propaganda. It was felt by some that the closer association of people with the Church, as compared with that in larger cities, was both an asset and a liability from the point of view of group relations. Meetings of rabbis, priests, and Protestant ministers are apt to be perfunctory affairs at which polite remarks are exchanged; but the combination of neighborliness with social distance does not encourage that common attack on community problems which is more feasible in other circumstances. There is often a deplorable lack of local leadership for efforts to bring members of the different religious groups together, and more depends on the personality of the local leaders. 

A further difficulty, in the small community, was how to select the members of a community seminar. Here, for instance, was a small town. in the state of New York which in the last two years had had an influx of Jews. Should the interested citizen (in this case a member of the American 

Legion) work through his own organization, should he try to secure the. cooperation of other organizations, or should membership be open to anyone in the community? The answers given by other members indicated that in their opinion the method adopted as to organization and membership must be influenced by the local situation. In most cases, such as that referred to, it was probably better to have religious tolerance discussed quite frankly in relation to the situation that produced the interest-by a small selected group meeting informally in each others' homes. Too much organization might defeat the purpose. A mass meeting would almost certainly be a mistake. 

One criticism made of seminars in general was that sometimes they were too "high-brow❞—that is, that the problems are discussed either on the basis 1 Persons desiring to know what the experience has been in communities working in this field of human relations should write to the National Conference. (35)

of illustrations or factual data that have no local significance or in terms too abstract to permit of ready application to the local interests. The statement was made that the prejudices of majorities in the three groups were not affected; that the leaders understand each other and enter into a spirit of fellowship, but that when the seminar is over, "nothing is done" to improve the local situation. Hence, the need was felt for a more active follow-up program of seminars in which leading individuals "clear the air." Exactly what such a program might be was not, however, fully developed. In other sessions also this question came up; and a division of opinion appeared between those who thought the aim of the Conference could best be achieved by a wider spread of its characteristic method, that is by the holding of many more local seminars with the aim of reaching larger sections of the community, and those who felt that this type of approach is suitable only for selected groups of intellectual leaders in the three religious groups and would be ineffective, even harmful, if opened up to large numbers of uninformed persons of much lower mentality. "It is no indictment of the seminar method," one speaker said, "that the masses are not reached. It only means that we have yet to find a technique for reaching the masses."

A more critical opinion of method was voiced by a leading Protestant layman: "I have a feeling that the direct attack, when organized as these seminars are organized, with thoughtful and dispassionate representatives of all three groups pursuing an established technique, and under the guidance to a certain extent the moderation-of chairmen who are familiar with the discussion of problems of this sort, is helpful. But I have also a conviction that if we were to scatter to our respective homes and, without the kind of preparation which there is for this sort of a seminar, would gather promiscuously out of our own neighborhoods, ten of each of the three groups who are here represented, and try to have an unprepared but frank discussion, it would do more harm than good. . . . My preference is largely for the indirect method, except where under thought-out conditions and with an established technique such people can be gotten together as are here; then the frank discussion is helpful." 

In the section meeting one advantage claimed for seminars restricted to local ministers and religious leaders was that these persons are usually in direct, responsible relationships to community problems. Their discussions, therefore, will have the impetus of practical purpose and, instead of being too academic, may on the contrary be more closely related to opportunities for cooperation between the different churches than discussions with a wider participation. With a similar aim in mind, another speaker suggested that it, might be well to bring leaders of business groups and social organizationswho presumably are predisposed toward practical community cooperationtogether before attempting to reach the masses. (36)

The real problem, as some of these members saw it, was to keep the Seminar a truly educational venture, not an occasion for staging public displays of sentiments. The common experience was that there is popular assent, in the average community, to the more general forms of expressing a desire for mutual understanding; "it is about time the churches got together," is a phrase commonly heard. But the same people who have this attitude, unless they can share in the educational experience of the Seminar, may still be very far from sanctioning any practical steps toward greater mutual cooperation or larger contacts between members of the different faiths. 

There was warning also against using the occasion of community seminars for staging protests against discriminations or persecutions in which local people have no direct responsibility - especially those in foreign countries. 

Such protests not only make for an undesirable complacency but may actually worsen the position of the group with which the meeting desires to express its sympathy. Thus protest meetings held in America against persecution of 

Jews in European countries often have had the effect, when there reported, of strengthening the unfortunate view that "America is dominated by the Jews" and thus undermining all influence that might be exerted from this side on behalf of those who suffer persecution. In the same way, protests against the activities of the Ku Klux Klan in parts of the country where the Klan was not strongly represented, undoubtedly had the effect of strengthening the Klan by providing it with new illustrations for use with unsophisticated audiences of the contention that Catholics and Jews threaten the position of 

Protestantism in America. 

The best way to combat such acute symptoms of prejudice, abroad and at home, it was felt, is to set examples of good inter-group relations in constructive, cooperative relationships. To discourage "Hitlerism," the best way is to work through the recognized political channels for such actions in international relations by our own Government as will remove the fears on which it thrives, and for such actions in domestic matters as will lead toward worlddisarmament and the deflation of militarism. Similarly, the part was discussed which enlightened religious leadership might play in producing popular approval for national policies that would extend helpful American influences in such countries as Russia and Mexico, where religious minorities are suffering persecution, often in an atmosphere of political, international strain. 

5. INSTITUTIONS OF HIGHER LEARNING 

A representative attendance of college people and of other religious leaders made this section meeting an outstanding one as regards the advance of thinking on actual problems encountered in the relations between religious (37)

agencies and institutions of learning in America.

This section was led by 

Dr. Robert L. Kelly, Council of Church Boards of Education. Three topics, more particularly, were covered: the organization of religious teaching in colleges and universities; the content of religious teaching in these institutions; and the attitude of faculty members toward, and as influenced by, religious teaching.

Cooperation with Secular Institutions 

The discussion of the first of these topics was introduced with a case problem from actual experience.

Four years ago the University of Iowa inaugurated a School of Religion with a faculty of four, representing Protestantism, Catholicism, and Judaism.¹ 

Two years ago the Catholics withdrew from the experiment. The causes were set forth by Dr. H. H. Takkenberg, a former instructor, in the Catholic magazine America (for February 28, March 7 and March 14, 1932. This writer claimed the following advantages for the school: 

From the point of view of the University: (1) academic-the popularity. 

of religious books was seen to indicate a new interest in spiritual things; attendance of the courses was seen as broadening the intellectual life of the campus and as giving a greater completeness to academic culture; (2) disciplinary-the University felt in need of strengthened religious loyalties to counteract irreligious trends; (3) political-the establishment of the school was seen as a piece of sound political strategy in the relations between the 

University and the state legislature.

From the Protestant point of view: it was expected that the school would strengthen Protestant influence and attract more students to the ministry and other forms of religious work. 

From the Jewish point of view: the school was seen as an asset because it gave recognition to religion in the curriculum; because it increased respect for Judaism among Jewish students; because it enabled the Jewish instructor to develop a good social and educational program and to strengthen his position as a personal counsellor of practically the entire Jewish student body. 

From the Catholic point of view: (1) the plan was considered favorable in its salutary effect upon all Catholic students; (2) it helped to dissipation of anti-Catholic sentiment; (3) it provided opportunities for spreading truth among non-Catholic students; (4) it was likely to impress favorably the 

University faculty.

The first discouragement was found in the restrictions imposed upon students in electing religious courses; these restrictions caused a relatively small enrollment.

The withdrawal of the Catholics from the arrangement was said to have been motivated as follows: (1) detriment to the attendance of Catholic colleges and universities; (2) inconsistency of Catholic policy which normally is opposed to secular education but in this seemingly tri-faith rapprochement

seems to "whittle down" the Catholic position; (3) the semblance of an assurance given by this arrangement to Catholic parents that the spiritual welfare of their children will be guarded at the State University; (4) discouragement due to the small registration for the Catholic course of instruction. 

A local Catholic chaplain, the Rev. W. T. Shannahan, was reported to have refuted Dr. Takkenberg's criticism as follows: The school has failed to interest large numbers of Catholic students to take courses in doctrine-but not entirely due to restrictions in electing courses. Father Takkenberg does not state fairly the objective of the University or the advantages which Protestants hoped to gain from the enterprise. The University authorities desired academic completeness, help in disciplinary problems, and favorable legislative reaction to requests for appropriations. Yet, there is no inherent selfishness in these objectives. The motives attributed to the Protestants might also be attributed to the other two groups. The possibility that the school might become a support of Protestantism is remote, because, as constituted, it cannot be controlled by any particular group. The objectives of the Catholic promoters listed were not present: no such results as favorable effect upon Catholic students, making of converts, dissipation of anti-Catholic sentiment, and favorable reaction of the University faculty, were expected; and therefore there was no surprise when such results as these did not come to pass. A slight attainment without compromise was hoped for. 

Further questions asked in the section meeting elicited the additional information that with a total enrollment of about eight thousand students in the University, the School of Religion had an enrollment of only about one hundred. All the groups participated in the election of the Board of Trustees. 

There were three Catholic representatives, two Jewish, and one each of the major Protestant denominations. The representative of each group on the faculty of this school was to teach what the group he represented wanted taught. The only concern of the University was that the quality of the teaching should measure up to the University standard. It was not concerned with the subject matter of the courses. The courses were made available to juniors and seniors. The influences of the school began to be felt on the student body and particularly on the faculty. Innuendos against religion in the classroom began to diminish. 1

Withdrawal of Catholics from this school was ordered by the Bishop of Davenport-on the ground, according to a Catholic member of the group, of the small Catholic attendance.

Discussion of this withdrawal of the Catholic group from a common educational enterprise brought out two points: first, the ease with which motives can be misunderstood and misrepresented; second, the fact that there is not in educational matters a single consistent Catholic policy-any more than there is a single, consistent Protestant or Jewish policy. university teacher informed the members: "When we speak of the Catholic Church, too frequently we think of it as 'one,' which acts as a unit. We do not realize that there is a tremendous difference of opinion in the hierarchy of the Church and its constituency. 

There is difference of opinion, for example, whether Catholics should go to Oxford and Cambridge-both non-Catholic universities. In America, some bishops are opposed to Catholic students attending secular universities, and others do not consider this of sufficient importance to object." 

Another member was reminded that in Protestant denominations which maintain colleges of their own a similar difference of opinion often obtains. 

Thus in one state, a Methodist bishop took a stand of opposition toward the state university; in anther, Methodist leaders take attendance of the state university by Methodist students for granted, even though there may be good Methodist colleges.

Again, church officials must consider in their policies the reaction on their own groups.

Thus, the President of a denominational institution was said to be averse to take part in religious exercises at the state university from fear that some members of his Church might consider this an endorsement of secular higher education. 

Another case of Catholic cooperation in the religious training provided at a state university was cited as having been an outstanding success, although there were bitter attacks from Catholic quarters on the ground that the priest involved in this arrangement was drawing Catholic students to the state university. In this case, the participation of the Catholic scholar as a member of the faculty was, of course, endorsed by the Bishop. The opportunity provided for a Catholic of outstanding scholarship thus to extend the influence of the Church was considered by leading Catholics a case of achievement. 

Comparable with the Iowa experience was that of another middlewestern state university where the enrollment of Jewish students seemed too small to justify the continuation of Jewish religious teaching, so that there was pressure to do away with it. However, further efforts which enlisted the full support of the Jewish public greatly increased the enrollment of Jewish students in the second semester. It was felt here that not prejudice but ineffectiveness of the program had been the cause of the lacking enrollment in the earlier part of the experiment.

It was natural that the discussion should proceed from this subject to a consideration of the part played by chaplains in the institutions of higher learning. It was found that in this matter also Catholic opinion is divided between efforts to give Catholic students at secular institutions the best possible aids to a religious life and training and, on the other hand, an attitude of complete non-cooperation. An encyclical of the Pope, it was stated, recognizes the attendance of Catholic students at secular institutions by providing for (40)

their religious needs. The differences in the attitudes of members of the hierarchy, it was thought, were largely due to contrasting conceptions of the functions of the college chaplain "the old notion," as a Catholic member phrased it, "of 'preserving the faith,' and that alone, and the newer point of view that the faith should be developed, that definite instruction should be given, and that knowledge of the faith and respect for it should be fostered." 

Some non-Catholic members were surprised to hear very liberal and reciprocal views expressed on this subject by Catholic members. For example, one 

Catholic institution with two branches, has an almost exclusively Catholic student body in one and a good many non-Catholics in the other; on the faculty of this second establishment there are a good many Jews, and students are organized in Jewish clubs. Of a Catholic university in the middle-west it was reported that from 40 to 45 per cent of the student body are non- Catholic; and the view was advanced by a Catholic member of the faculty that it was not inconceivable that at some future time Protestant and Jewish chaplains might be recognized there.

A change in attitude toward religious teaching, with noticeable results in its enlarged influence on the student body, was reported also from an eastern university with a large enrollment of members of all faiths. Here, in the past, religious instruction had largely been left in the hands of the Christian associations whose efforts produced so little effect as to lead to an inquiry by the university authorities. These substituted for that arrangement the appointment to full faculty positions of scholarly representatives of Protestant denominations, of Catholicsm and of Judaism. Each of the religious groups contributes to the university the equivalent of the salaries paid these teachers; but the academic standards are those set by the trustees of the university. 

Instruction in all three faiths is now recognized part of the university curriculum and, in addition to its direct influence on the students enrolled in the courses, has had a salutary social effect on the student body as a whole. 

What Kind of Religious Training? 

This topic was introduced with a remark to the effect that the positive teaching of irreligion in secular colleges and state universities was one of the causes of widespread crime in America. Others, while not going so far, gave it as their view that a flippant attitude toward religion often is the net result of a college education. A Protestant minister thought this was an aftermath of the recent spurt in the study of natural sciences: "Youth feels today that the only place where truth can be found is in the natural and social sciences." 

Another view was that teachers of religion if they felt outdistanced by the teachers of other subjects in attracting and interesting students might with advantage look into the method of their teaching for at least one of the causes. 

There was some talk about the available literature concerning the three faiths, (41)

and a strong plea was made for the writing of new textbooks. To this a 

Catholic scholar replied that there were already excellent books expounding the faith (which he mentioned); it could hardly be said that here lay the main difficulty. A need was seen, rather, for more opportunities at college. and universities for informal, personal explanation of beliefs and practices that are misunderstood or give rise to controversy. "The Catholic priest who teaches at a secular college," said a member, "is almost always an exceptionally good teacher. Students come to him with distorted ideas which they have received in the classroom or elsewhere. They are often upset by flippant remarks and begin to wonder whether science is going to overthrow religion. It is in laying doubts of that sort and in answering questions of that sort that some of these men make a splendid contribution."

A Jewish religious worker at a middle-western state university spoke of the cooperation of representatives of the different religious groups in an effort to make their teaching conducive to three aims: a better understanding of their own faith on the part of the students, the creation of greater good-will toward those of other faiths, and that of a spiritually sensitive attitude toward the problems of life. Thus a course was given on the historic philosophies of living, using biographical materials rather than abstract doctrines. 

Yet another difficulty was seen in the frequent lack of a clear distinction between religious instruction and opportunities for worship. This came up in criticism of an arrangement at an eastern university under which students have the choice between attendance of chapel and a course in religious instruction as a prerequisite to graduation. In the chapel exercises, prayers are taken from all religions, and universal ethics are taught. The criticism was made that opportunities for worship and classroom courses are entirely different matters and should not be looked upon as equivalent in graduation requirements; a "forced option" was hardly conducive to either study or sincere worship. In answer to this it was stated that both the courses of instruction and the chapel exercises were intended to be informally instructive. In the classroom work, no text is assigned unless a student desires it. His own interests determine the choice of study which is in terms of problems. Similarly, in the chapel exercises the effort is made to deal with the known interests of students in the selection of readings which are given without comment. The genuine concerns of agnostics were not overlooked either; in every case- -both in individual study and in chapel exercises the aim was to help these students come to terms with themselves. This was not, the speaker ventured in reply to criticism, "reducing religion to the lowest common denominator" but a method of introducing religious instruction for all in such a way as to make it vital. (42)

Another Protestant speaker took issue with the view that worship and religious instruction must be distinguished as entirely different in purpose. At a conference of church workers, recently, the subject discussed was worship; and as part of the proceedings a member of each of the three major religious groups, a rabbi, a priest, and a clergyman, were invited to lead in worship. 

The experience was not only spiritually refreshing but highly illuminating: the contents of the Jewish service and the significance of the mass were brought home to Protestant participants as no mere discussion could bring them home. 

The Attitudes of Faculty Members 

Many hard things were said about the relation of faculties generally toward religious instruction in non-sectarian institutions of learning. "One of the greatest contributions religious teaching in the colleges must make,” said one of the members, "is the conditioning of the faculty." Cynical statements about religion in the classroom, it was said, were frequent. "What we need," said the Catholic chaplain at a large eastern university, "is a common facing of the anti-religious forces in the universities. There are so many youthful Ph.D.s who do not know anything except the subject of their theses. They define academic freedom as liberty to criticize traditional beliefs -however arbitrary, superficial, and unscholarly such criticism may be. Any argument tending to defend traditional belief, however objective and scholarly, is regarded as ipso facto obscurantist." "To illustrate the same point," said another member, "there is the saying that the academic mind is always searching for truth but never expects to find it."

One member, from a southern Protestant college, suggested that all would be well if instructors would confine themselves to their own department and not get over into the realm of religion. This suggestion was refuted as leading to intellectual sterility, and as an inadequate notion of religion. For example, a Catholic speaker said that the difficulty lies precisely in the narrowness of intellectual interests on the part of these instructors: "These young instructors search for truth in one direction only. They are not open-minded. They are not interested in other directions, even though the truth might be found in those directions." 

One of the rabbis present reminded the meeting that this form of intolerance, like any other, was precisely what the present Seminar and the movement of which it was a part had set out to overcome. Here also, another speaker commented, the need was for the development of appropriate methods. 

He gave examples to illustrate the appalling ignorance of the Bible among teachers of English literature; the obvious need was, he contended, for a wider range of information among these faculty members. Next he pointed to the frequent inferiority complex of religious teachers (which by inference (43)

was blamed on the fact that in the past they have often not been the intellectual equals of those trained in other disciplines). And lastly, he suggested that other faculty members would gain greater respect for the courses in religion if these stood more "on their own feet."

And finally, it was suggested that more important than all else is the religious attitude of the college instructor as shown in his own way of life. In illustration of this statement, somewhat at variance with the insistence of previous speakers on the importance of other factors, he mentioned several graduates who were quizzed as to what they had gotten out of their university work. The consensus of opinion among them was that the greatest contribution was not concrete information but the effect on them of "Old Jones" -the personality of the teacher. 

6. THEOLOGICAL SEMINARIES 

The section meeting charged to consider programs for students and faculties of theological seminaries provided in its own procedure (whether by intent or by accident the present reporter does not know) an example of a form of discussion which has been found particularly helpful elsewhere for the illumination of controversial subjects. Only members of Protestant organizations were in the room when the proceedings started, and the discussion of faculty-and student-relations with members of other religious groups were correspondingly frank and unreserved. Subsequently, a Catholic priest and a rabbi entered the meeting and answered difficult questions of doctrine and practice, relevant to the topic, that had arisen in the minds of the members. 

Dr. William Adams Brown, of Union Theological Seminary, presided.

Representatives of different theological schools were invited by the chairman to inform the group of ways in which their institution endeavored to contribute to better mutual understanding between members of different religious groups. The first speaker drew attention to the fact that many of the problems that had been presented at previous sessions of the conference hardly existed for the theological seminaries. For example: "Our students are required to visit all churches and synagogues in preparation for class discussion and credit for a degree. Such visitations are largely for the purpose of instruction. But, as a matter of fact, most of the students inform themselves of the differences between the churches and faiths in other ways also. Our Seminar in church history brings us down to modern religious thought. Our course in systematic theology includes Catholic and 

Jewish theology. "We have Catholic and Jewish students in our seminary. In my first class in New Testament I had fifteen candidates for the rabbinate. I told them I was going to present Jesus from the Christian point of view because I could not be fair to myself or to them on any other basis. They stayed in (44)

this course for two years and at the end asked that their own seminary introduce study of the New Testament. Some of them also attended at our institution a course in Old Testament, given by a Christian." 

A representative of a southern Negro college which has a school of religion reported that the only inter-group relations of the students were those of a monthly student forum, made up of white and colored students, rotating between the institutions of learning in the locality and including Jews but not, to his knowledge, Catholics. This forum is not confined to theological students.

From New York was reported the existence of a Fellowship group of students of Union Theological Seminary and the Jewish Theological Seminary, meeting bi-weekly throughout the college year. A Union student testified to the value of these meetings, at which papers are read, followed by discussion, for mutual understanding, deepening of religious concern, and spiritual growth on the part of all participants. In addition, inter-confessional relations are studied at Union Theological Seminary in a course primarily on race relations. 

Another large eastern seminary has special courses in inter-denominational and inter-creedal cooperation as well as in race relations. Here a need is felt for more direct cooperation with Catholics. 

One of the largest Protestant seminaries in the middle west exchanges professors with the Hebrew Union College in Cincinnati, and enrolls Jewish and Catholic students in its summer school. 

In at least two middle western universities, with a predominantly Protestant student body, Old Testament is taught by a Jewish scholar. 

Two speakers from southern communities reported frequent opportunities for their student and faculty members to confer with Jews but practically no relations with Catholics.

At this point a more general discussion developed around the difficulties. experienced by Protestant schools of religion to get into direct educational contacts with Catholic institutions or even with individual Catholic scholars. 

One member stated emphatically that, from the point of view of attitude adjustment, such personal contact for theological students was essential, that reading books was not enough. But unfortunately even Catholic leaders with a reputation for liberalism in social matters often expressed views on theological matters that seemed to make fellowship impossible, except in social service. The chairman pointed out that the absolutist position in theology is not limited to Catholics and quoted a Lutheran leader as saying, only a week ago, "We can have no fellowship with any one with whom we disagree on any point of doctrine." He gave it as his experience that relativism in theory does not exclude absolutism in practice. The need was for a technique that would make possible a discussion of common concerns, with the result of a (45)

gradually enlarging fellowship. The expression of differences was not to be shunned as necessarily harmful to fellowship; it all depended on the spirit in which it was done. "In my course in systematic theology I did not, at first, come nearer than two hundred years to the present time. Then I began to bring the course down to date and to include in it what Catholics believe, what Jews believe, and what Protestants believe today-irrespective of whether I have any sympathy with these beliefs or not. This has helped many men to find Catholics and Jews their 'neighbors.' 999 At this point, a representative Catholic and a Jewish rabbi entered to take part in the discussion. In reply to questions, the former gave a brief summary of the theological basis for the inability of priests to take part in common worship with non-Catholics or to admit them, in matters of religion, to a status of equality. For the same reasons, it would not be possible for Catholic institutions to enter into arrangements for the exchange of professors or for Catholic teachers to take part in theological discussions with non-Catholics. 

On the other hand, there was nothing to prevent a Catholic from accepting invitations to explain the Catholic doctrine. While this was becoming an increasing practice, there was nevertheless an understandable disinclination to permit immature students to enter into discussions with other groups where doctrinal questions were likely to come up. 

Catholic seminaries, he explained, like Protestant and Jewish ones, were giving their students a wide range of information about other faiths and showing them how these differed from their own. 

After other questions had been answered that are not relevant to the subject here under review, a Rabbi gave a brief summary of the existing differences between Orthodox, Conservative, and Reformed Jews in their attitudes toward non-Jewish religions. Like different Protestant denominations, they interpret in a variety of ways the mission of Judaism in the world; but they have in common not only their fundamental Jewish beliefs but also the congregational form of organization, with national bodies functioning only in an advisory capacity, each with its conference of rabbis, but all joining in a common Synagogue Council of America (including also lay members) for the discussion of matters of common interest. This body can take action only with unanimous approval.

Naturally, like the denominational Protestant bodies, each of the three Jewish groups, he explained, has it own attitude toward cooperation with members of other faiths. One Jewish seminary has an exchange professorship with a large Protestant school of religion; another has at times invited 

Christian ministers to deliver a baccalaureate sermon. There was a common striving in all three groups to understand other points of view. 

Jews generally, he stated, look upon problems of religious liberty and 1 (46)

tolerance in America as essentially a problem for Protestants. Jewish organizations do not proselytize, and they respect other points of view. They feel the need for cooperation with others and, indeed, look upon efforts toward peace, good-will, and accommodation as a religious imperative. 

In reply to a question, the Jewish representative explained why Jews do not seek converts. They look upon moral conduct as essential to salvation, not faith in specific doctrines. They discourage marriage of their members with those of other faiths and discourage those who would adopt Judaism by dwelling upon the great responsibilities of Jews, upon the discipline of Judaism, and the difficulties which Jews must meet through discriminations against them. If someone still wants to come, he is admitted. 

Yet he did not think that Jews were theologically inhospitable, as one questioner had intimated: "On most of our synagogues there is written: 'My house is a house of prayer for all peoples.' There is not one service in any 

Jewish synagogue to which everyone is not welcome."

The meeting came to one agreement: that there is a distinction between proselytism and conversion; and that the main effort of the religious teacher must be "to turn people toward God, irrespective of faith." It was said that it was on this basis that many graduates from the seminaries go into the mission field today. 

7. SPECIFIC TASKS FOR THE YOUNGER CLERGY, WITH SPECIAL Reference TO PREACHING AND ITS INTER-GROUP EFFECTS 

Separate section meetings had been planned, one on the Specific Tasks of 

Younger Clergy, the other on Preaching and its Inter-Group Effects. Whether the "younger clergy" saw in preaching the main problems of harmonious group relations, or whether those interested in the effects of preaching on these relations were anxious to know of the attitudes of the younger clergy toward this matter may remain open to question. At any rate, the two groups merged and held two well-attended and exceedingly vivacious meetings. Here doctrinal differences moved into the foreground of discussion, and the general thesis that ran throughout the Seminar, "friendship and understanding without oneness of religious belief or practise," underwent a searching examination, in both its theoretical and its practical aspects. 

A Catholic spokesman, Father Peter V. Masterson, who opened the discussion, at once plunged into the dangers and pitfalls of any effort to bring members of the different faiths together unless it were based from the outset on a clear recognition of their irreducible differences. In seeming contrast with the general assumptions of a seminar that approaches social prejudice from the standpoint of religion, he declared emphatically that doctrinal differences had very little to do with it. What does connect such prejudice with the (47)

existence of differences in belief is lack of contact between the groups, and mutual ignorance of what Churches and Synagogue stand for. He did not think there was much direct attack in sermons upon the teachings of the other faiths represented in this Conference, except on a basis of misunderstanding. Hence the remedies were to be sought primarily in better methods of informing each group concerning the others. To this end he recommended. instructive articles in the religious press of each group and a charitable assumption of the sincerity of the teaching of others when references to it in the pulpit are unavoidable. In addition, both objects, friendship and understanding, should increasingly be sought in carefully selected projects of practical cooperation in the pursuit of social objectives. 

A Protestant clergyman (Rev. Mark Depp), who followed, advocated a more direct and concerted attack by ministers of religion upon prejudice. 

The aim of that attack was primarily to dissipate mutual fear; and that fear was rooted in ignorance. "We are down on what we are not up on." He took a somewhat different view from that of the previous speaker on the part which sermons might play in the dissipation of that ignorance. Without in any way denying or understating differences in belief, it was possible and desirable, he held, to stress the few major notes in religion that all believing Jews and Christians have in common: the fact and intrinsic character of God, the reality of the spiritual universe. It was well to point out that these major notes transcend the existing differences. Even in the public schools it was possible to provide a character education which, though sectarian, took cognisance of the essential, commonly held beliefs. In their own churches, members of the different faiths should stress the common needs rather than common elements in their creeds. It was from that point of view that various churches in Washington had formed the habit of calling in ministers of other faiths to help the members visualize what is "on the other side of the mountain." With the same aim, visits to the services of other groups had been encouraged and arranged. Moreover, there were great public issues on which members of the three groups could come together, not as representatives of a common religious faith but in order that each might make its own characteristic contribution toward the solution of common problems. 

Rabbi Barnett Brickner, who spoke next, emphasized the need for common action in the face of a world-crisis. "The function of religion," he said, "is to change society." At all times it had been its function to challenge the great social sins; but the Churches were usually too slow-so that a revolutionary movement against anti-social forces, after it has ousted the "Bourbons," proceeds to oust the Church. There was criticism today of the alignment between religion and capitalism, and of its endorsement of war. A 

Church which explained the present world-crisis as an act of God would not survive. With reference to the specific object of the present Seminar, he (48)

thought that a preaching of good-will was not enough—it was merely the "kindergarten stage" of the needed process. It was the function of religious bodies to organize for a common program of social righteousness, in concrete terms of economic security, a just share for labor in the product of industry, the abolition of war. But paper pronouncements of a general nature were not enough: the Churches should organize on behalf of a constructive and specific social program.

As a challenge, the rabbi's statement found no clear-cut opposition in the discussion that followed. The only difference which developed was one of method. The Catholic Church, it was stated, is militant and as such aims definitely at the reconstruction of society; but although it initiates and takes part in corporate action toward that end, it stresses more the education of its members for personal action. Also it was found that there were different degrees of the directness with which members, both as individuals and as spokesmen for the practises of their respective Churches, would take part in political action on behalf of social change. Some felt that the Churches had not gone far enough in their obvious task of educating, especially, their younger members for forceful good citizenship, and that it was not wise to have controversial discussions drive people out of the Church. Others felt that in a courageous facing of controversial issues lay the only hope for a real influence of the Churches on the moral and social questions of the day: "We must learn to be poor as institutions, even to lose half the membership, if that is the price of spiritual leadership." To this the rejoinder was that it is often difficult for a minister to determine the right and wrong in a complicated political or industrial situation; there are relatively few cases in which the moral issue is absolutely clear. "The function of religious teaching is to denounce injustice, not to promote a concrete social program," was another objection voiced.

Nevertheless, further discussion disclosed, all three faiths have been forced by the turn of events into the pronouncement of fairly definite programs of social action; and the question was to what extent the opportunities of pulpit and class-room should be used to promote such programs. 

The relevancy of this discussion to the subject of the Conference was seen in the relative preponderance in preaching of themes that lend themselves to a strengthening of the oneness with those of other faiths in the pursuit of noble ideals and the combat of anti-social tendencies. Even in purely theological sermons, awareness to the by-product in social attitudes of any teaching would make the preacher careful to avoid a phraseology that might produce prejudice or hinder the spread of fellowship. Likewise, without actually preaching on economic or political subjects, it is possible for the preacher to sensitize his congregation to their moral implications. (49)

Attention was directed to the fact that false attitudes may inadvertently be implanted by well-meaning efforts to combat prejudice. "Kindliness" too often becomes associated with a sense of superiority and condescension. "Tolerance" itself may be interpreted as a mere toleration of something one does not approve of, instead of being part of an attitude of open-minded curiosity about others and of a desire to find in their beliefs and their practises something akin to one's own ideals, however different these may be in important respects.

There was general agreement with an eloquent plea, at the conclusion of the session, that the exponents of each of the three faiths represented, and especially the younger clergy, should endeavor increasingly to apply the eternal verities, as they saw them, to the concrete problems of social relations in the world today. There is no conflict, it was found, between those who approach this task by dealing in a large measure directly with outstanding evils to which the methods of an inspired social statesmanship must be applied, and those who hold that the function of the pulpit is, in the main, to reform society through reforming the individual.

In the second session of the section they endeavored to translate this agreement more specifically into programs and methods of combating intolerance and prejudice. For the most part, this survey went over ground previously covered. But a few issues particularly relevant to the topic assigned to the section may here be mentioned. Should Catholic priests and Jewish rabbis be invited to join Ministerial Unions? The discussion revealed that such unions are not uniform either in their membership or in their programs. In some cases they are frankly Protestant, white associations, of like-minded men for specific purposes, not shared-or not suspected to be shared-by others. In some cases, Catholic priests are members, with the consent of their 

Bishops; in others such consent could not be expected because of the predominantly aggressive evangelical attitude of the organization. There was need, it was felt, for further study of the functions of such unions-particularly in relation to their potential opportunities of bringing together for a friendly interchange of ideas the ministers of different faiths. In the section meeting on Theological Seminaries the point was stressed that the inclusion of Jews and Catholics in predominantly Protestant ministerial unions should be preceded by long personal acquaintanceship. 

The interchange of ministers and joint services were again mentioned as methods possible only in some communities, and even there with considerable limitations of making church people acquainted with the beliefs and practises of other religious groups. Again Catholics had to explain that it is impossible for them to participate in joint services. Again, definite examples were given to illustrate essential differences in methods and occasions of participation. (50)

More particularly was a distinction made between jointly organized services, on the one hand, and hospitality to members of other faiths, on the other. 

The latter is very general but is also subject to certain limitations which seemed to represent demands of good taste and feeling rather than rigorous requirements as regards doctrine or ritual. 

More emphatic assent was given to the suggestion that the ministers of all religions acquaint themselves more fully with the literature of other Churches. Three distinct purposes were associated with such reading: first, and foremost, a correction of possible misunderstanding; second, a correction of stereotyped thinking about other faiths through coming in contact with a greater variety of expositions of it and of its applications to moral and social problems; third, the growth of an appreciative attitude that helps to overcome social distance.

In both sessions of the section it was recognized that, as regards the matters under discussion, diversity of belief, of temperament, and of objectives as between different branches of the same Church is quite as pronounced as it is between members of different faiths. It was more easy for liberals in the various Christian Churches and for Reform rabbis to come together on programs of social action than for orthodox or conservative and liberal branches in any one Church.

Although a number of the younger clergy of all three groups took part in the discussion, a particular cleavage of opinion on lines of age and generation was not discernable. It seemed as though there had, perhaps, been wider reading and a more comprehensive social training in the seminaries and colleges in recent years than were available to those trained some decades ago; but there was no indication of different attitudes toward the problems in hand. There was general agreement with the remark of the last speaker that the attack on prejudice involves less an application of new information than a change of will. 

8. JOURNALISM AND ITS EFFECT ON GROUP ATTITUDES 

Dr. John Van Schaick, Jr., editor of The Christian Leader, presided. 

The Rev. Robert A. Ashworth, editor of The Baptist, commenced by demanding that the religious press of America acquire a consistent and intelligent philosophy on the subject of religious intolerance. "That means that we must determine to understand those with whom we differ. We dislike those whom we do not know. "Toleration is not enough. It is an odious word that carries with it always a certain assumption of superiority. We must not stop short of respect for unlikeness. That you respect another man's conscience and convictions does not mean that you are stopped from sharing with him your own (51)

conceptions of truth, or whatever you have found to be of value in your own experience."

Dr. Ashworth stated that the religious press was in a particularly favorable position to interpret the incidents of the day in a spirit of respect for those of other opinions, without presenting any less clearly the attitude toward them of the particular Church or denomination. He then made a suggestion, later to be taken up by others, that this task of the religious press might be greatly aided by a press service which would supply material of an interracial and inter-religious character. An effort of his own paper to secure an interchange of information with the organs of other religious bodies, including Jewish and Catholic ones, had remained partially unsuccessful. To report some of the most notable things that other religious bodies are doing is one way, he thought, in which the editor of a religious paper can best advance understanding.

Another step which he favored was greater cooperation between religious organs. Not long ago, twenty-seven Protestant journals had presented simultaneously a joint declaration of what was involved in the Macintosh case. 

He felt sure that there were occasions on which Jewish, Roman Catholic, and Protestant papers could thus join forces on some issue of common significance. 

Already, on a recent occasion, the Social Service Commission of the Federal Council of Churches, the Social Justice Commission of the Central Conference of American Rabbis, and the Department of Social Action of the National Catholic Welfare Council had united in a powerful and illuminating statement on unemployment. The extension of that type of action to the organs of these Churches would definitely make for an increase of understanding.

Byron Price, Washington chief of staff of the Associated Press, explained some of the difficulties which the newspapers and the press agencies have in adequately reporting religious news and thus making their contribution to better understanding. He prefaced his practical remarks on that topic with a historical retrospect showing the close association of Press and Church in some of their problems: "Religious liberty and freedom of the press were cradled side by side in that momentous first article of the American Bill of Rights. Through the years they have endured in common the troubled lot of free institutions. 

It would be interesting to conjecture whether either of them could have survived without the other. "I suppose, the fathers were prodded by a certain expediency when they wrote religious liberty into the Constitution. A great variety of groups had taken definite root in the Colonies. The urge for unity through equality was the compelling desire of the age; and it was natural that formula should be devised recognizing all sects and denominations in equal measure. (52)

"No such impetus of necessity lay behind the provision for a free press. 

The souls of those who wrote that provision must have felt a great prompting of idealism, tinctured with more than ordinary courage. Censorship and suppression had been the rule rather than the exception. The first printing press to be set up on the American continent, operated by the first president of Harvard College, was confiscated on religious grounds, its proprietor having had the temerity to question the doctrine of infant baptism. The first newspaper lasted but a single day." 

As the "personal mouth-pieces of zealous thinkers," the journals in those early days soon became agencies of disunion rather than of national solidarity. 

Their influence did not fall on the side of better understanding between racial and religious groups. "It is inevitable that where complete freedom is permitted a certain section of the press-as is its right-will devote itself to the promotion of group hatred."

Michael Williams, editor of The Commonweal, gave a less optimistic view of the part played by the press in the progress of inter-religious understanding in America. That cause, he held, is "retarded by the eagerness of the secular press to emphasize the exceptional and the sensational rather than the normal and the commendable. The battles of bigotry make better news than the treaties of peace."

The most hopeful factor in the situation he saw in the great newsgathering and distributing agencies which "have of late largely increased the amount of space and bettered the manner in which they handle religious news and views." Unfortunately, there was still-quite inevitably-a tendency, in the reporting of sermons, to pay most attention to the sensational, or at least un-orthodox and original, pulpit utterances. The only way open for the orthodox bodies to redress the balance seemed to be to buy advertising space; but whether this would be a wise step, he was in doubt. 

The religious press was handicapped by another circumstance from functioning in this movement, and that was the natural tendency of each religious organization to emphasize the views and the special interests of its own group to the neglect of the others.

He agreed with Dr. Ashworth that there might be greater cooperation between these papers, perhaps stimulated by periodical meetings of their representatives. After all, their common cause was far more important than the differences that separate them.

The last of the formal addresses at this session was that of Alfred M. 

Cohen, president of B'nai B'rith, and editor of the B'nai B'rith Magazine. 

He drew attention to the enormous influence of the daily press on public (53)

opinion. In comparison with other media of understanding between different groups in American life, the newspaper stands out as a controlling factor. Its power is the greater since it is not sought but is incidental to its functioning. 

The question of paramount importance for better inter-religious understanding is, then, how is the news written? It is not the business of the newspaper, in its news columns, to discriminate between good and evil; yet some papers seem to think it more important to record the latter than to chronicle virtue. 

Injustice to racial and religious groups arises from this tendency when group membership is reported: "Shall the evil-doer be dealt with as an individual or according to the group to which he is supposed, correctly or incorrectly, to be attached? Shall a ne'er-do-well, charged with the commission of a crime, be catalogued in the newspaper as a Jew merely because his name happens to be the same as mine? 

Shall a usurer be called Shylock-which for most people is a synonym for Jew? . . . What has race to do with the matter, any more than other extraneous circumstances?"

From the experience of the Anti-defamation Department of B'nai B'rith, the speaker testified to the disposition of the daily press to be fair, and to make prompt amends when a case reflecting unjustly on Jews is brought to its attention. But he thought that there might more often be references to group membership when laudable acts are reported. Particularly, with the object of the present Seminar in view, he would like to see more frequent mention in the press of friendly services rendered by one religious group to another. "One such is more powerful than many speeches. The average man is moved more by deeds of good-will than by words." 

The religious press also, though it was addressing itself to much smaller numbers, could do more, he thought, to serve as a mission of good-will to those special groups which it reaches: "Let the religious papers speak militantly for their own faiths while calling for a friendly attitude toward their neighbors' faiths, protesting against outcroppings of prejudice and hitting hard at organizations that menace good-will.' 

The discussion which followed the addresses here briefly summarized endeavored to secure further agreement on some of the concrete proposals made for enlarging the use of the press in the inter-religious good-will movement. 

Thus it was pointed out that a mere bringing together of religious editors was not enough, as meetings of ministers of the different faiths had not in themselves proved sufficient to break up prejudice. Success in such matters depends on the methods of procedure. Two extremes had to be avoided in such gatherings: picking out differences as a basis for disputation, and a false "Pollyanna" sentiment. To make such occasions productive of continuing (54)

cooperation, concrete problems requiring mutual explanation should be discussed, with an emphasis on a common objective. 

The opinion was expressed that many editors still consider it part of their job to extol their faith by depreciating other creeds. There were references to particularly obnoxious papers that profit largely from reviling others. But the statements sometimes made about Jewish and Catholic public men in other papers, usually considered quite respectable, had been equally atrocious. One paper, with a circulation of a million and a half, had declared not so long ago that Catholic citizens are traitors to their country. To counteract the cultivation of prejudice in a large proportion of the population was a task permitting of no mild measures and not to be achieved in a single generation. 

He did not believe that many of the methods advocated at this Seminar would prove effective: For a rabbi to preach Christ as a great Jewish prophet, for example, did not lessen the difference between those who believe in the divinity of Christ and those who do not. It was the wrong line of approach. Progress must be sought in truly educational methods of dealing with prejudice, of producing mutual respect in spite of difference. 

So far as the religious press is concerned, the most immediate need seemed to be for interchanges of bulletins of information by the different groups. (55)

CHAPTER FOUR METHODS OF IMPROVING GROUP RELATIONS 

With some training and a rigorous chairmanship it might be possible, presumably, to get a group of interested persons to discuss a subject of common interest to them in an orderly fashion: first agreeing on definitions, then noting the relevant facts, proceeding hence to their analysis, to the study of relevant outside information, and so, finally, to proposals for the solution of the problems thus distilled and illumined. But in reality no conference ever acts in this way; and in the present case, proposals for action were made, and methods in use here and there were described, from the very first session on throughout the seminar. While many of these proposals and accounts have unavoidably had to be reported in earlier sections of the present report, in conjunction with the considerations from which they sprang, many others have been held back in order to permit, in this final chapter, a summary presentation of the many and varied constructive ideas that came out of the Seminar as possible ingredients for a program rich in possibilities of success. 

We are not here concerned primarily, of course, with suggestions thrown up, unaimed at any particular persons or organizations, for activities that might help if someone, somewhere, could be persuaded to take them in hand. Nor are we concerned primarily with suggestions made for the developing program of the National Conference of Jews and Christians itself. The purpose of the Seminar, in discussing concrete proposals for action was, rather to assist all those who desire to take part in this national movement for religious tolerance and mutual understanding. It was soon evident that in that task it is necessary to choose methods appropriate for the particular instrumentalities and organizations through which Jews, Catholics, and Protestants respectively are accustomed to act and can function best. 

There was much talk about the direct method and the indirect method, and members took position on this distinction as the paramount issue in methodology. But those who advocated the one or the other method were well aware that there can be no generalization as to its absolute superiority. 

The direct method, as definied by Dr. Kilpatrick, at the second U-table meeting, "would seek out the differences, and the causes of the differences, between Jews, Catholics, and Protestants, and try to deal with them as such directly"; the indirect method he understood to mean "that they would engage in some common task and for the time being, if possible, lose consciousness of their differences in its pursuit." There was, of course, no question as to the wide and varied uses of indirect methods to bring people together so that, in common interests, they might lose their consciousness of difference. But that does not mean that this approach is always necessary or the most effective; (56)

and furthermore it only indicates the general direction of a type of effort to the working out of which many people have given thought. Thus, a wellknown educator said: "The indirect method is one that can be of very wide use. 

A great many people can engage in cooperation over objective tasks, and the resources of the various religions may be brought out in that cooperation. But I feel, also, that smaller groups and those that have engaged in this cooperative work before, so that they have got to know each other, might well go on to the direct approach. Without that direct approach, I believe, many values and understandings can never be brought out."

A Catholic layman thought that separately and jointly the three groups should carry on studies of prejudice so as to be able to find practical remedies. 

But he did not think that such studies should be diffused very widely as a method of self-education: "The dragging of many of these things into public discussion sometimes embarrasses the very object that we have in view.' 

Or, as another Catholic speaker said: 

"If we go back to the people among whom we are living and tell them all we have heard here, all the ugly things-things which we want to hear to get a full grasp of the problem-we shall put ideas into people's minds that were not there before; and many people will say, 'I never knew there was so much prejudice.' "I think that our effort among the people to whom we are leaders should be to fill their minds with positive ideas. We should tell them of things they can do that offset prejudice, not using that word perhaps, but interesting them in those things—such matters as, say, disarmament and the social and economic problems-upon which we all can meet on a common ground." 

The direct method, a Jewish speaker suggested, nevertheless might very usefully be employed in educational work, provided the prejudices discussed were not those of others but of their own group. The leaders could thus put their own people on guard against those attitudes within themselves—a task which he considered essential if the more indirect methods were to succeed. 

Other members illustrated ways in which this might be done (some of which have already been referred to), particularly stressing the association of such self-examination by members of religious classes with the study of historical events and of current social thought. 

There was agreement that somehow, somewhere, reliable data must be collected concerning the sort of symptoms of community friction that have here been presented in the first chapter, so that they might be dealt with more effectively by the organizations concerned; and furthermore, that there was need for an objective examination of what each group teaches about the other groups, and what each group contributes toward strained or friendly relations with other groups. But some members thought that this was not a task for (57)

trained research workers only; rather, that such collection of data and their careful analysis was a highly educative experience in which many interested people should be permitted and asked to share. A suggestion was considered that in most communities there might be a small group of religious leaders, at least, who might come together for this task, applying the frankest discussion to local causes of discord; but others held that experience so far did not encourage the hope that even such small groups would often "get anywhere." 

The objection was made, also, that some of the religious bodies most concerned would not wish to take part in such intimate and searching discussions. 

Varieties of Inter-Religious Contact 

Cooperation in common causes, then, was agreed by most of the members who expressed an opinion on the subject as the more general area of activity within which the desired educational influence would have to be worked out. 

Some seemed to assume that better mutual understanding would be an inevitable by-product of joint effort; others were eager to learn whether special techniques could not be devised through which such contacts might be made to contribute a maximum of such education. Thus one speaker, a 

Protestant, referred to the large amount of cooperative work already done by the three groups, which, he seemed to think, had not automatically brought with it a full complement of improved mutual attitudes. A Jewish layman also saw possible flaws in the indirect method unless it were carefully worked out: when Jews become better known and are accepted as participants in common tasks, it does not follow that prejudice against their group is lessened -they may merely be regarded as exceptional Jews. When a man says, "some of my best friends are Jews," and seems to take a pride in this show of broadmindedness, there is reason to suspect that he is not entirely free from anti-Jewish prejudice.

This suggested a further scrutiny of personal contact as the seemingly most approved ingredient in the indirect method. Did it really work out as seemed to be generally assumed? The testimony, on the whole, was in the affirmative. For example: "I remember a rather small community where it was difficult to get enough people interested in lectures, in concerts, and the like; a rabbi and his congregation were the main single reliance in that community for these things, and the rest of us who were interested in them came to know these people by working with them along these lines. This is many years ago, but 

I have a warm spot in my heart right now for that rabbi and those people. 

And that has made me feel I know Jews when people talk about them-I know there are Jews and there are Jews, just as there are Christians and Christians." 

A rabbi stated: "I try to think of prejudice in terms of social distance, either between (58)

individuals or between groups. One of the most fruitful ways of reducing the friction and the antagonism and the ignorance that have been spoken about would seem to me to lie in the direction of shortening that social distance, of bringing people together, and of getting them to appreciate each other's point of view and cultural values. We can shorten that social distance by education or by such gatherings as we now have, or through good-will meetings within churches and synagogues; and in that way we are doing a very definite piece of work toward that understanding in which lies the whole basis of this good-will movement."

A number of speakers referred specifically to ways of lessening the distance between religious groups as applied to students. The relative merits of various methods were mentioned: inter-fraternity conference; the introduction of speakers or students from other groups in discussion meetings on the campus; the taking of students to churches and synagogues-often a first introduction to the religious observances of other groups. Such methods had been found practicable also with other groups of young people. "Reconciliation trips," first organized some years ago in New York to increase inter-racial contacts, had developed in many places to foster also contacts between religious groups. In a few cases there had resulted more frequent interchanges of visits between members of different churches; but often such visitation is yet, it was reported, a somewhat one-sided affair. In other cases, mutual visitation occurs spontaneously, as young people accompany each other to their respective churches; and the function of the parent or teacher is only a negative oneto abstain from interference.

Nevertheless, it was recognized that, unless carefully directed by persons with educational experience, such direct contacts with the religious observances of others among the young might contribute nothing to a real understanding and besides produce suspicion that proselytism was being applied. 

So also the introduction of speakers from outside to present the beliefs and cultural valuations of another religious group lends itself to various possible methods. There is the straight lecture and the symposium, the forum with opportunities for debate, the introduction of the outside speaker after a discussion has taken place, to answer questions. Such individual appearances of competent speakers were found valuable especially in two types of situation: where there has been a complete lack of contact, so that the other group is disliked and feared; and where questions have arisen in class-room or forum discussions that make the members eager to have a point at issue cleared up. 

As regards the former, a rabbi testified that, as an invited speaker in a Protestant religious class, he had "watched the children's expressions change from fear to curiosity, and from curiosity to friendliness." Another member of the same faith spoke of the value of frankness when questions are sincerely addressed to such a speaker, even when they bear on matters that are sacred (59)

to him. It was precisely on such difficult questions of belief that honest, uncompromising expressions by qualified spokesmen were most conducive to understanding and, therewith, to better feeling. 

The surrounding circumstances were thought to be important. The minister of one faith cannot permit himself to be exposed to a situation in which he might appear to take part in an act of worship in the religious edifice and with the ritual of another faith. A Catholic leader of nation-wide reputation for a generous devotion of his time and energy to inter-religious cooperative efforts spoke with regret of specific misunderstandings that had occasionally arisen from his inability to keep engagements due to such lack of consideration for the setting of the occasion. To schedule an address on a non-secretarian subject was not enough to make such attendance possible. 

To increase the educational effectiveness of such appearances of speakers before lay groups of another faith, it was suggested that it would be valuable to have the ministers of the three groups meet more frequently to answer each other's questions-in this way preparing themselves for a correct anticipation of what such audiences would be likely to have on their minds in regard to the socially distant group and its religious beliefs. 

There was an almost unanimous disapproval of the staging of joint religious services between members of the three faiths, on the ground that they obscure and do not illumine genuine differences. Yet, it was thought by some that an occasion of secular as well as religious significance, such as Thanksgiving Day or a patriotic holiday, might well be used to signify in some such formal way the unity of aspiration and devotion to an ideal. 

Occasions were cited on which one religious congregation had shown its appreciation of a citizen of another faith by organizing a farewell or memorial service in his honor to which members of his congregation were invited. "There come times in history," said a Protestant member, "when men are really in doubt as to whether there are any resources in the world that are religious and spiritual in a true sense; they come to that feeling in the face of some great common danger, when the whole world around them seems full of unfaith. They feel the necessity, regardless of their particular denominational allegiances, to come together and to affirm in the presence of this danger their common belief in the existence of God, and their common access to spiritual resources.'

He gave as an example a religious service held in the house of Mahatma Gandhi, at a time of great difficulty due to national disunion; here Hindus, Mohammedans, Jews, and Christians, after the reading by members of each group of sections from their scriptures, affirmed their common dependence upon a reservoir of spiritual resource, and thus strengthened their comradeship in devotion to their country's liberation from dominance and dissension. "If that is true in particular historical circumstances then it must be true that in ordinary times if we had the clarity of vision and realization of the (60)

intensity of the evil forces that work in our lives we could come together at least three or four times a year for the common assertion of the existence of God."

Others thought that common worship even on special occasions was of dubious value. As one Catholic priest phrased it: "It is really an apparent surrender of our agreement to hold to our own faith. It is a purely superficial, a purely social thing. . ., and I think it very undesirable. "Looked at from the Catholic point of view, we would not be anxious to have others come into our churches where we believe in the real Presence of Our Lord and the Blessed Sacrament. We feel that nobody else could get that point of view. Our Catholic people who come into our churches have an instinctive reverence, bend their knee in genuflection, adore, believe in the real Presence. If you looked upon it as some kind of social affair-into which [attendance of others] resolves itself-we should feel this a little disrespectful. "Of course, if I went into a Protestant church or a Jewish synagogue, as I have often done, I should not feel that they demanded of me the same reverence that I demand of them, because they do not tell me that they believe they have there the real Presence. "I think that our churches ought to be sacred. They are the expression of the essential features of our faith. If they are not that, they are simply meeting places, or social halls, or prayer houses." 

Further discussion showed that there is a clear distinction between common worship and joint services, on the one hand, and the attendance at church and synagogue services of individual other-believers, on the other. In answer to a question, several Catholic priests declared that they have no objection to the presence in their churches of an occasional outsider who may enter quietly and feel himself uplifted, though he did not share to the full in the religious experience of the congregation. Protestant and Jewish spokesmen expressed a full appreciation of that stand.

A further distinction was drawn between common services and a sense of oneness that might be achieved by simultaneous but separate worship in the various churches, or simultaneous prayer by believers in different faiths at times of national anxiety.

The question of common worship naturally led to a consideration, also, of forms of prayer on occasions when members of the three groups come together for secular purposes. On at least one recent occasion, it was reported, a Protestant minister had refused to attend a peace meeting because he could get no assurance in advance that the Invocation would be in Christian terms. 

More often, offense is taken by Jews because prayers in public do end in the name of Jesus. But significantly it was also stated that in slightly different circumstances the opposite practice may offend sensitive Jews: most Jews do not desire, it was stated, to have ministers depart from their accepted form of (61)

utterance because there are Jews in the room if the meeting is called under Christian auspices. It was thus seen again that different modes of procedure are appropriate to different occasions. A Jew who enters a meeting in a 

Christian institution will expect the proceedings to be conducted in keeping with the Christian tradition and dislike to have them modified out of a mistaken respect for his feelings. On the other hand, where a meeting is held jointly by members of different faiths or where a meeting on behalf of some secular object is opened with prayer, the minister is in the position of expressing a common feeling and a common thought, and therefore should endeavor to clothe his expression in terms that have the same meaning and the same connotations for all.

Civic Cooperation 

There remains, then, to be considered for its inherent educational values that area of contacts between members of religious groups to which the generic term of "civic cooperation" was applied in many of the speeches made. 

Is such cooperation advisable in all circumstances? Are there different degrees to which its different forms contribute toward the end here sought? "Catholics will not cooperate," was a remark made frankly on several occasions. Underlying causes when understood, uncover some condition that makes it impossible for Catholics to take part in some proposed dition that makes it impossible for Catholics to take part in some proposed effort or at least memory of embarrassment on previous occasions and fear of possible consequences. The guardian of a minority faith has to look further ahead than the immediate occasion and shape his conduct in accordance with larger policies. But in one respect, it seemed to be felt by non-Catholics, might there be a better coordination of Catholic and non-Catholic purposes: in refusing to take part in a proposed common enterprise, regrettable misunderstanding could often be avoided by frank explanations. Naturally, it seems easier to avoid controversies by polite evasion; where no entire understanding can be anticipated, a mere refusal unaccompanied by explanation is almost inevitable. But the hope was expressed that the relations between ministers of the different faiths might soon be placed upon such a basis of recognized mutual good-will that it would be possible on all such occasions of difficulty to secure a real understanding.

In some cases, the difficulty has been overcome by insuring simultaneous though separate action on the part of the different Churches on a matter of common concern. In others, after mutual explanations, a program has been reshaped to make cooperation possible. Again, it has been arranged at times to secure participation of Catholic laymen as individuals where that of the Church as such might have given rise to difficulties or possible misinterpretations. And this, of course, is equally true of other religious organizations (62)

whose members frequently can feel that they act in the spirit of their Church without actually being delegated by it to represent it in an official capacity. 

The social programs of the three faiths, it was found, are similar but not identical. Over a large area of civic and social reform, Jews, Catholics, and 

Protestants have common objectives, as evidenced by a comparison of the pronouncements of the three inclusive program-making bodies. Over another area, while sharing the larger objective, the three faiths are not agreed on methods. Over a third area, their programs differ or conflict. Any attempt to accelerate or intensify cooperation at the neglect of vital differences in policy would damage mutual good-will, it was found by experience. Such a case presented itself in the stand taken in Massachusetts ten years ago over the Federal Child Labor Bill, with the result of increased bitterness. Such a case presents itself almost every time that some measure affecting family life is in contemplation; the difficulty here, it was explained is not, as some 

Protestants believe, that the Catholic hierarchy is fettered by the dictates of a distant potentate, but that such measures commonly contain provisions at variance with essential dictates of the Catholic code of morals. On proposals for easing divorce, for example, the Catholic Church is taking a definite stand from which it cannot depart.

In all these matters, controversy over principles does not make for better understanding, since anyone who wishes to take the trouble can easily ascertain the grounds for Catholic opposition. Only a doctrinaire theorist without practical statesmanship would declare that inability to agree on such issues as these closes the door to all inter-faith cooperation on social reforms. As the 

Seminar proceeded, it became clear that the area of social need permitting of joint action is vastly larger than that in which it is impractical. There has, in fact, been an ever increasing extent of cooperation in recent years—both between the national leaders of the three confessions and in individual communities. Social justice, disarmament, regard for a rising standard of life, combat of demoralizing influences in the recreations of the people, a purified home life, all these and other great causes have brought Jews and Christians of every degree of orthodoxy and liberalism together in ever growing numbers, and in ever more intensive forms of collaboration. Members of each of the three groups, in turn, have taken the initiative and assumed the leadership in such movements. "In charitable projects," said a Jewish speaker, "representatives of all faiths can engage together; it affords a common ground; it has extended itself so that today it covers a much larger domain than ever before. The more of these enterprises are engaged in by people of different religious faiths, the better they will understand each other. We are only at the beginning." 

As one practical method of further enlarging lay participation in such common endeavors, it was proposed that commissions on social justice, com- (63)

posed of members of all three groups, might be set up in different communities. 

One such effort, in Saint Louis, was favorably reported upon. But especially in the field of charity, it was felt, was there as yet a largely unexplored opportunity for effective cooperation. Here also, misunderstanding has often stood in the way. Catholics, Jews and Protestants do not see alike, for example, what constitutes the sort of social effort that should be recognized in community fund-raising campaigns. Often agencies with a predominant aim of religious education have been included in community chests, with the result that local Catholic leaders did not feel able to take part in the enterprise. In such cases as these it was, of course, a matter of choice for the social leaders of the community to decide whether they would rather include in their operations the Christian young peoples' associations, for example, or the Catholic charities, a choice which, without bitterness on either side, if fully explained, might well fall upon the exclusion of the latter. 

Unfortunately, limits of time did not permit the Seminar further to examine the different kinds of social effort in order to learn in which of them cooperation is most advanced or most easy to introduce, or which of them have the largest educational carry-over into popular attitudes of mutual good-will. 

But the editor may, perhaps, be permitted at this point to testify from personal experience to the large amount of mutual understanding and fellowship that has quite unconsciously developed from a common concern of Protestant, Catholic, and Jewish, as well as secular agencies in the immigrant and the foreign-born. Here, in simple works of helpfulness to individuals and families as well as in mutual counsel on legislative programs, members of the three groups have found common objects that have brought them very close together. 

Other Forms of Cooperation 

Mutual aid in emergencies was singled out as another method of practical cooperation that has shown excellent results in better feeling. Examples have become frequent in which a congregation offers shelter to one of another faith in case of fire, of flood, or of rebuilding. 

Another form of emergency is that of defamation-a situation in which members of the faith attacked usually are not in the best position to answer slander and defend their good name. It was strongly felt that good-will in such cases demands that the victim-whether an individual or an institutionbe defended by members of another faith. Anti-Catholic statements over the radio, anti-Jewish libels in the press, or a caricaturing of the Protestant clergy on the state usually can be more effectively protested by persons and organs of another religious group. Several members thought that the time was ripe for a more systematized mutual cooperation in this matter. Not only is a direct reply to misrepresentation often ineffective, but a group frequently called upon to answer calumny is apt to develop an unfortunate hyper-sensi- (64)

tiveness and defense reaction which would disappear if at all times it could rely upon prompt and effective support from sources not directly implicated. 

As part of a cooperative policy mention was also made of a type of effort that does not lend itself to systematization but is at times exceedingly productive of good feeling, namely the practice of religious organizations, and of individuals closely associated with a denomination, in matters not connected with their faith, deliberately to go outside the circle of their own set for the purchase of goods and services. Thus it was reported of one Catholic hospital board that, as a matter of policy, it employed a non-Catholic architect. 

Jewish and Protestant social agencies are know, similarly, to make it a regular practice to appoint members of other faiths on their secretarial staffs. Members of other faiths are called upon to serve as trustees on the boards of foundations established by men closely associated with their own Church. The director of one of the largest Jewish foundations is a Protestant, and the head of at least one large Christian foundation is a Jew. 

In summary, it was seen that cooperation with those of other faiths covers a wide choice of actual and potential activities. These range from the engineering of contacts that have better mutual understanding for their primary aim and, with a view to it, make use of joint activities, to projects in which such understanding and the creation of good-will are more or less incidental by-products. It was seen that cooperation, though it offers many avenues toward this desired end, is subject to many limitations. Yet, as an instrument of good-will, it lends itself to a thoughtful application of techniques by which its educative character may be heightened. Social statesmanship is required, according to common consent, to avoid pitfalls and to insure that the happy mood of fellowship established among those brought together in joint labors on behalf of a common cause shall be a means to enrich the relations between the groups more generally. A result in changed attitudes is not produced automatically from such efforts, though its inception may be spontaneous; once given life, such attitudes must be cultivated if they are to be permanent and to affect ever widening circles. Hence the importance of the next inclusive group of methods-those directly educational. 

How Mutual Understanding May Be Taught 

There cannot, of course, be a clear, theoretical distinction between direct and indirect methods of education. For example, few subjects in our public schools are taught for their own sake alone; always the eye of the educator is on the by-products in attitudes and nascent interests, on the development of helpful emotional habits and intellectual processes. In the same way, those unfortunate by-products in credulity, prejudice, intolerance, bigotry, which, as we have seen (p. 31 et seq.), often arise from faulty or deliberately "conditioning" teaching also offer educational tasks where the need for their (65)

correction or for immunization against them is recognized. "Leave the young alone," several speakers said in effect, "and there will be no need for any special education to overcome prejudice." But that was at once seen as an over-simplification of the problem. State and society, and not least the Churches themselves, desire to influence children in given directions, insist that they must with all the arts at their disposal indoctrinate the young so that the ideals sacred to the "we" group shall be firmly implanted in the next generation. The question, then, is, how can the conflict between different doctrines to which the child is exposed-from the side of Church and State, for example-be lessened or at least modified so as not to produce a divided personality; and how can the teaching of any doctrine, whether it have rivals or not, be freed from ill effects on those attitudes that make for openmindedness and mental flexibility.

Tolerance as an object of education, so far as this Seminar was concerned, was seen to be wholly insufficient; at best it was regarded as too negative, too passive a state of mind.

Although there was no attempt at clear classification of objectives, in different round tables and general sessions, first one then another was stressed as particularly deserving attention. There were, first of all, those, who believed that a thorough grounding of the individual in the tenets of his own inherited faith was necessary to make him immune against the temptations of personal and group selfishness that are at the bottom of so much malicious exploitation of inter-religious antagonisms. The "growth of a real, militant irreligion" was seen as a cause of that selfishness. There was testimony (though not conclusive) to the effect that "there is more prejudice from people without any religion than from people of the Catholic, the Protestant, or the Mohammedan faiths. I have observed this not only so far as it affects Jews but also so far as it affects others. I saw in Turkey that the most horrible persecutions of Armenians did not come from really devout Mohammedans but from political leaders who had no religion. You will find that often the prejudice against Catholics, and in other countries probably against Protestants, comes from irreligious and antireligious leaders to a much greater extent than it comes from religious leaders." "Talking about religion is not necessarily religion. If we haven't got convictions, it is a slur upon our mentality, upon our intelligence, perhaps upon our morality. The more we stick to our own faith, the more loyal we are to it, the more free shall we be to respect the opinions and the faiths and beliefs of others."

The speaker, a Catholic, and a Protestant who followed him, both drew attention to the difficulty of teaching comparative religion to adolescents without weakening that loyalty which was felt to be so essential. But it was felt that the teachers of each faith, in their approach to immature minds, in any case, would not wish to stress those doctrines that give rise to difficulty (66)

but would endeavor to create attitudes of reverence, of sensitiveness to the rights of others, and of appreciation for spiritual values. "After all, what we are after in dealing with the adolescent boy and girl is not to get over an exact and impregnable group of ideas but to form the backbone of habit on the basis of the best possible interpretation of the condition peculiar to the people themselves. Once having set those habits going, we can open their minds to the history of religion and to that wide area of similar ideas which lies around and which, when introduced too soon, might break down the very inner cohesion which is necessary to sustain the group habits which we would like them to have."

In the section meeting on Public Schools, a description was given of an experiment in certain Philadelphia high schools, which endeavor to utilize the assembly period for dramatic presentations of the contributions made to American life by different culture groups. Changes in the group attitudes of pupils produced by the collaboration of classes in almost all subjects toward these presentations have been measured by means of attitude tests and found to be considerable. Visual education was stressed as an effective supplementation of more purely intellectual processes; and there were scattered references to the effects of moving pictures, radio, and miscellaneous reading on social attitudes. Perhaps, the only agreement here to be registered was that such education must be concrete and related to actualities. 

The questions of methodology in the education of children and young people were seen to be closely connected with questions of adult education; and the suggestions made in this chapter link up with those previously reported on various ways in which Americans generally might be taught more widely and more intensively to recognize prejudice in themselves and to immunize themselves against it.

The present chapter may be concluded, then, with the statement that seemingly irreconcilable differences of opinion on the best ways of overcoming religious intolerance yielded when the task was considered as, in the main, an educational one, demanding all the experience and thought which the various groups could make available to each other. While at many points a need was seen for further study, even research requiring scientific objectivity and precision, there was found to be already in existence a large fund of knowledge that could more intensively be applied to the common problem. 

Indeed, a Protestant member pointed out that, with the pessimism that seemed to prevail in some quarters about the progress toward mutual understanding in America, one of the most important jobs for the National Conference of Jews and Christians might be considered to be a wider spread of knowledge concerning the large number and great variety of cooperative efforts between the three groups already under way. "The more we call attention," he said, "to what is being done that this is not just a theory but happening, both between national organizations and between local groups-the more progress we shall make in actual cooperation and also in getting rid of prejudice." (67)

CHAPTER FIVE THE TASK AHEAD 

The final meeting of the Seminar was devoted not only to a summary of the previous proceedings but also to a formulation, on that basis, of the task that lies ahead. Roger W. Straus, one of the three co-Chairmen of the 

National Conference, who presided over this meeting, said: "This Seminar has emphasized that the basis of any effort to bring about mutual understanding is that we frankly recognize that each group does and should develop the ideals and the culture best fitted to itself, and that, having done so, it can join with other groups to bring about a progressively better country with a truly great civilization. "This is the foundation on which the National Conference of Jews and Christians was erected. We felt that, in accordance with one of the fundamental principles of this country, from its very inception as a nation, each of us has the right to worship as he may see fit. Many of us have gone a step further; namely, we believe that not only is this the individual's right, but that the encouragement of varieties of cultural groups like the Catholic, the Protestant, and the Jewish, is to the best interest of the nation. "About twenty-five or thirty years ago, Israel Zangwill gave to this country a poetic ideal in the phrase "the Melting Pot." But this phrase, as it became interpreted in the minds of many, brought about a false idea: many felt the "melting pot" meant that every group should, under the fire of American life, be liquified and then cast into a single type. This is a misconception. Allow me to use an illustration from my own business. It is a process known as smelting and refining. We take various lead ores and minerals from all parts of the earth, smelt them in a furnace, and refine them in a pot, and then cast bars of pig lead—each one identically the same as the other. Before getting this exact sameness, this uniformity, we must remove all the gold and silver, the precious metals. In the same way, if we were to try to mold every one in this country to the same pattern we should have to remove the precious elements of individual initiative and individual idealism. "This misconception of the melting-pot idea is the basis of such narrow movements—so opposite in their expressed purposes but so alike in methodas Ku Klux Klanism and Communism. The finer ideal for the advancement of humanity is to permit liberty to the individual and to his group, and at the same time to do two things: first, to promote cooperative community enterprises, and second, to educate for mutual understanding. . . . "The experience of the last few days gives good evidence that we are doing a worthwhile job in this direction even if it also shows that it is at best slow, up-hill work. . . ."

Miss Rhoda E. McCulloch, editor of the Women's Press (national organ of the Y.W.C.A.), who had been asked to interpret the discussions of the Seminar in their bearing upon the task ahead, distinguished three phases (68)

in the movement: "First, timidly talking talking together, fear of each other blotting out frankness; "Second, discussion of irritating discriminations, misunderstandings, and prejudices, not always leading to clear agreements as to ways of eliminating them; "Third, agreement on common objectives, a working program toward those objectives."

She thought that probably every community that tackled the problem afresh would have to pass through some such sequence of stages. And perhaps each individual conference or seminar would have to display, in varying ratios, these three degrees of progression. But it was possible to definie more clearly in advance the objective of any given conference. Such a definition of objectives would make possible a program of more intensive preparation for discussion leaders. And within the larger purpose it was possible to select for any given occasion a more circumscribed topic, selected on the basis, either of the common experiences of all three groups, or of the findings of previous conferences.

But a seminar or a conference is only part of a process that requires both preparation and a technique for carrying on from it: "We need to make a cumulative history of these seminars, showing their progress from a gingerly contact with surface problems to a more radical approach to the real issues. This would make participants more historyconscious, would make it more possible for one seminar to proceed from the point arrived at in other seminars, and would furnish a kind of handbook of method for the patterning of community seminars. "Some seminars ought to concentrate on the educational methods to be employed in carrying on from gatherings of leaders to work among the groups themselves."

As part of the larger educational program, she asked for more publicity- "fence-corner talk"-on the extent of cooperative work already under way on the part of these three faiths: "There is educative value in spreading the idea that it is natural and possible for the three faiths to work together. Many who now take it for granted that we must live in separate compartments would be more convinced by the phrase, 'it is being done; it is good form' than by pages of dialectic." 

She drew attention to a discussion, in the opening session, of the question whether considerations of racial discrimination were included in the scope of the Seminar. This was an illustration, she held, of the fact that the question of prejudice is "a dark field on which educationalists, psychologists, and religionists journey in blind fashion without clear appreciation of the real issues of the conflict. (69)

"With increasing evidences of the unreality of religion in the lives of the people, we may, in our attempt to eradicate the injustices which arise from prejudice, have to pull down religious bigotry as a target and erect those more real forms of competition for survival which explain discriminations. "If we are to campaign against injustice and blighting discrimination in our relationships as citizens of this nation we shall get needed dynamic and moral power only as we attack the real points of friction. . . . 

"Note that so many of the discriminations, both racial and religious, which have received comment here date ultimately from a compulsion to protect a group of 'customers' or clients or ultimate consumers. This is the argument used, for instance, by those who set up barriers of religious affiliation to tenancy in apartment houses. It is the argument of European hotel keepers who are beginning to draw the color line because of American clients.” 

Thus, the need is for activities that would change the attitudes, not only of leaders, but also of the rank and file of members of these three groups, "so that little by little the 'consumer' will begin to register his own changed attitude to these discriminations made in his name. "Such a campaign of education in churches and synagogues would go far to bring the issues out of hiding and display their real nature. We might discover the points at which discrimination or hostility is purely economic rivalry or the discomfort which comes from living in close relations with those whose habits and practises are dissimilar from our own. "Then our education could be construed in terms of those realities as we found them. Now we are shooting a spattering broadside at something which we call vaguely 'discrimination on religious grounds'." 

Not only the objective but also the instruments of campaigning, she urged, require a careful process of selection. For statements, resolutions, announcements of intent, there must be substituted an "engineering approach" to the problems of division between these three groups. "If the basic elements of prejudice are (a) fear, (b) ignorance, (c) generalizations which include whole groups without distinctions, can the professions of religion here represented consistently tolerate 'prejudice'? If not, how is it to be fought? What is the line of march for any group? Could there be united thought and action on it? Could there be a united program of education, displaying the roots of 'prejudice' and utilizing all our hard-won scientific findings in regard to it? Would not such a united program bring new life and integrity of conviction to each group so participating, giving reality to our several faiths, rather than diluting them? Would it not create, as a by-product, possibilities of working toward common social objectives? Would not this be a master contribution to the building of a united nation? "In the last analysis, the crippling discriminations about which we have been conferring are ominously significant, not only because they keep from fullness of life those who are entitled to justice and fair opportunity, but because they are the sign and symbol of our fundamental disunity as a nation.' 

Discussion had revealed, she continued, that there was no magic in the 39 (70)

reading of the Scriptures. Words did not convert. The Seminar had contributed, therefore, to a re-formation of an old problem: it was not simply equality of opportunity in a teaching approach to children in our schools, but "the more difficult road of trueing up our own lives as individuals and as members of social groups to the professions of our religious faiths." Hence, there was need also for "a warmly emotional exposure of the position of our religious beliefs and the objectives in personal life and social relationships to which they compel us: "Our national soul is lean and starving because there are too few occasions on which, in groups of varying faiths, we show each other this more glowing face of our religious persuasion. A conference of leaders of these three groups on their historic aspirations might be more productive than a discussion of their differences."

Only few of the many proposals made in the course of the Conference, said Miss McCulloch, had illustrated solutions that could be said to be typically and fundamentally religious. Ought not, for real effectiveness, mutual understanding be a by-product of united zeal in tackling common tasks?

The elimination of misunderstandings, without some envisagement of further end objectives, in the assumption that knowledge is power, is nothing more than a generation of power unmindful of the end to which that power shall be directed. Misunderstandings are social evils primarily because they preclude a unity in objective. "It will be said that it would be difficult for these three groups to agree on common social objectives; but that difficulty is no greater here than for any group of citizens. It is the essential stuff of the difficulties of citizenship." 

It was futile, she felt, to speak of national planning, now so much in the air, if not even those who, because of their religious faith, might be expected to unite on projects of social welfare, could find common programs and if citizens could not first, in local communities, experience the satisfaction of joint effort. "A new kind of diversity seems to be in process of becoming. We are passing through the stage in which the highest expression of oneness between differing religious groups is tolerance or even amity. We may be building toward a day in which diversity of religious faith will be prized as the strongest substance for the kind and degree of unified action and lifting power which this nation needs if it is to play a strategic rôle in the world community." 

Professor John A. Lapp, head of the Department of Social Science of 

Marquette University, a former president of the National Conference of

Social Work, and a former director of the Social Action Department of the National Catholic Welfare Council, congratulated the Seminar on not having dissipated its attention but having endeavored to concentrate on religious (71)

prejudice. It was a gain to have found agreement in the distinction of prejudice from personal preferences, likes and dislikes. "The most distinctive contribution of this Conference, as I saw it, was the clear recognition that no one was expected to give up any portion of his religion in order that he might be decent toward his fellow citizens; that there was no proposal here for bringing people together on any one set of doctrines; that there was no proposal for any kind of unity of religions; and condemnation of the idea that we should reduce religions to the lowest common denominator that all people believe. . . . This Conference accepts the idea that religious groups can make their greatest contribution by preserving their integrity, by preserving their faith, and by giving the truths of their faith to our common civilization."

As another noteworthy element in the discussions, he singled out their awareness to the part played by history in the development of both prejudice and religious liberty. "I truly believe that a thorough study of true history is the best antidote for prejudices. I am as certain of one thing as I can be: that prejudices have been perpetuated in the past by innumerable statements in histories in elementary and high schools distorting the facts about religions and about races. 

Most of us will not be any too proud about some of the history of the past. 

But if we are studying it with candor we shall certainly not put all of the blame for the past evils on any one group."

Among the many motives for prejudice that had been displayed in the discussions, there was one which to him seemed to include most of the others: the motive of profit-making from the promotion of prejudice. "Time and again, tracing the reason back to the cause, we find that this lies in the motive of some individual seeking to advance himself by means of playing upon these prejudices which would remain latent and would soon die out if they were not retold and impressed upon each succeeding generation. .. Many politicians have used religious prejudices as their political stock in trade, have used those prejudices knowingly for their personal profit and advancement. I am certain that if people generally understood this fact, if people generally recognized the extent to which money-making is a motive for promoting prejudice, their sense of fairness would very soon destroy this as an effective means."

That the Seminar had met was in itself an important contribution, he thought. It was representative of all parts of the country and of influential groups. The message which the members took back with them would be delivered, and re-delivered, to thousands and hundreds of thousands of people. "This is really the great task. There are millions who are not reached in any way by any of the evidences of good-will that have been so prevalent here throughout this week. In the far-distant regions of the country, little of it has penetrated to the rank and file. And it is there that prejudices remain most stubbornly." (72)

He therefore visualized the present Seminar as but a beginning of a great movement which, in the next quarter of a century, might go far to eliminate "these plagues that have been so serious in our lives." But to secure such educational spread, it was necessary to agree on objectives which, when passed on, will be understood and further promoted, objectives to which the spirit of the people will respond. And such an objective he saw in the central theme of the Seminar, in the desire for mutual understanding. 

A passion for understanding and for social justice was evident not only in the content but also in the delivery of the next address, by Rabbi Morris Lazaron, of the Baltimore Hebrew Congregation. What had impressed him, as the conference proceeded, was that its divisions were not only vertical- Catholics, Protestants, and Jews—but also horizontal; that there was apparently a conservative or orthodox and a liberal opinion in each of these three groups, running parallel on many questions. There was no need, then, to reiterate too much the axiom that in this common effort none of the groups relinquishes anything precious to it. But they had gathered there as believers, and they worked through institutions. It was in regard to these, he thought, and not in regard to their respective faiths, that all might cultivate the virtue of humility. There had been, indeed, a remarkable readiness of spokesmen for these different groups to acknowledge past guilt-guilt for injustices that lie back, in some cases, hundreds of years. "But this is the question: are we prepared today to say, mea culpa?.

What is our group doing today by way of repression, by way of prejudice, by way of breaking hearts? The high resolve must be to wipe from our own hearts and lives the things that divide; and we who, in a sense, would be priests ministering to a world, must first cleanse our own sanctuaries.' 

Like Miss McCulloch, he felt that there was need for limiting the discussion of abstractions, and for remembering that the evils faced by the Seminar had social manifestations that must be grappled with intelligently and scientifically. Such treatment precluded, to his mind, too comprehensive a program. Effort must be applied where it will count for most. "Let us take, for example, one problem mentioned often, the problem of the education of our children in the schools. We cannot begin to deal with the schools because of the thousands of teachers in this country who are not trained if we are not prepared to put into their hands a course of lessons on human relations. The task is rather to effect a liaison with the normal schools and with the educational departments in the universities. This is a problem of sufficient importance seriously to challenge our study."

He further advocated the multiplication of standing conferences of Jews and Christians in the various communities, affiliated with the national body, to consider definite local problems of human relations.

The concluding address was delivered by the Hon. Newton D. Baker, of 

Cleveland, one of the co-Chairmen of the National Conference. "What this conference is attacking," he said, "is not faith but prejudice.

Those who have had any anxiety as to whether or not the bonds of faith are loosening would, I feel sure, be very much cheered if they had been here throughout these meetings and heard the sturdiness of the reassertion of the faith of those who spoke of the faith of their fathers and according to their own traditions. What we have been interested in is not what any man believes but what other people think about him and do to him for believing what he does believe. So we have thought to develop techniques by which we might approach the problem of prejudice and leave more fixed than ever the particular form of faith. "We learned how many of the colonial populations at the outset were essentially uniform and homogeneous religious groups who had emigrated to this country and broken their way through the virgin forests for the purpose of escaping the tyranny then exercised in the land from which they came over minority opinion, and how, having this as the primary object-or at least a principal object-of their coming, they dreaded the conflicts that might grow up among themselves by the introduction of a minority into their majority, and so how the early tendency of the colonies was to establish a particular form of faith. "The progress from that, as the Union was established, was most accelerated by the philosophy of Thomas Jefferson, by the Religious Liberty Act of Virginia. It was certainly one of the cardinal principles of George Washington. 

And I know no more striking evidence of the prescience and patriotism of George Washington than the little collection of letters, now printed in pamphlet form,¹ which he wrote to the various religious denominations in the United States upon his first accession to the Presidency in response to letters from them congratulating him upon that event. To each one he wrote sympathy with their point of view, even when he did not share the point of view, assurance of freedom, assurance of religious liberty, and, more than that, a kind of tolerance which did not depend upon condescension from one man to another. "This gave us a great landmark or measure of the distance which we have come.

"It is, perhaps, because of these memories that minorities among us are especially sensitive-though this is countered by the fact that, by multiplying our population and by multiplying the number of divions among us on religious questions, we now have many minorities of opinion. "I was interested to hear a gentleman from the floor ¹ refer to a situation in a foreign country. Obviously, this is not an American question. And yet, I feel sure that all of us, whether we represent minority or majority opinion, are fearful of every exhibition of that sort which may make an entering wedge for dissension that may ultimately spring up among us. And indeed, there have been times, quite recently, even in our own country, when, if there were not legal discriminations and restrictions, there were great bodies of strong opinion, strong prejudice, which were perverting the pure current of our political thought. "The difficulties in dealing with problems of this kind are numerous. I suppose everyone in the United States would agree that the moral education of children is as important as their intellectual education. Whether or not that moral education should be given in the school and concurrently with the mental education is a problem full of thorny difficulties. How it can be done by a teacher or a group of teachers with adequate comprehension and sympathy for points of view of children of different religious faiths is, perhaps, an almost insoluble question. "And yet, this Conference has discussed with gentleness, with firmness, with deep conviction on the part of many from opposite points of view, the paramount importance of having religious and moral education as a part of the fundamental education of the youth of this land. This body comes to no conclusions, passes no resolutions. But the fact that this problem has been approached in an earnest way, that we have faced its difficulties frankly, has been, I think, helpful in the making of sound public opinion on the subject. "But of course, the consequences of prejudice are the things that have interested us most. The use of prejudice by others for propaganda has been referred to. Other causes than the prejudice itself are in the back of men's minds when they appeal to an uninformed prejudice on our part, in order to herd us into this, that, or the other economic, social, or educational camp. And it is from that point of view, looking at the matter in its largest aspect, that our chief interest lies. "For, the fact is that in these modern times the problem of government in a democracy becomes more and more difficult. It is easy, where the government of a state is in one man's head and within one man's will, to change the policy of the state as soon as the conviction arises that a change is necessary. 

But in a democracy like ours, every step forward in progress means the reeducation of a substantial majority of one hundred and twenty million people.

issues which are confronting us for decision grow increasingly severe in their importance. The problem of reconciling the economic conflicts of the world -the world is becoming more and more congested in its population-the contest for access to the raw materials of the world in order that the manufactures and commerce of the particular countries may be served, are occasions of international strife and disagreement. "With the problem of preserving the peace of the world-the world having now to look back only a few short years to the greatest war that has ever taken place in the history of man, and more heavily armed even after the disillusionment of that tragic experience than it ever has been in history before, so that the national budgets in every country, including our own, are burdened with obligations and expenditures by way of preparation for future wars and payment for past ones, so that the whole economy of every nation is shaken and shattered by war-with that problem, the world realizes deeply the necessity of machinery and agencies which will preserve international peace. "Problems such as this press on us from every side. And it is to be remembered that our share in their solution, here in the United States, cannot be determined for us by a single head or a small group of heads. Out of one hundred and twenty million people, sixty or seventy million must agree on America's answer to problems of that kind if the answer is to have any more stability than the passing expression of a temporary and transient emotion. "So, as the world grows closer together, as its questions become more perilous in their consequences, as their determination becomes more intricate because of the number of factors involved, we face a situation in which, so far as we are concerned, continuous education is the only answer that affords even a prospect of safety. "Now, education consists of two things: first, the diffusion of accurate knowledge, and, second, the removal of prejudice. The American Association for Adult Education and other agencies are busy on the affirmative side; they are seeking to plant in the minds of the men of our generation the flowers and fruits of scientific inquiry and rigid, honest thinking. And this National 

Conference of Jews and Christians, it seems to me, is aiming to do an equally important work-to remove from the garden of human thought the weeds which choke the fruits and flowers. ... "This Conference has met in a spirit of amity and good-will. Its individual members and its groups have bravely reaffirmed the faith of their fathers. They have acquired a respectful sympathy for opinions which they do not share. They have realized the extent to which prejudice poisons the fountain of upright and effective action on the part of citizens. They have started as it seems to me, effectively-a method of approach to problems ages old, to the removal of obstacles which have descended to us in one form or another from the fathers. And it may well be that, working in conjunction with other forces, this effort will, after a while, make us a nation of educated men . . . in the sense of a capacity to hold judgment in suspense until we have weighed all the facts, and know."
