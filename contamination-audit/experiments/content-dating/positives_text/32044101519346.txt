FOREWORD 

For three years the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce has been conducting a program of studies looking toward the development of better methods of determining the costs of distributing commodities in this country. In particular, an attempt is being made to study the costs of handling individual commodities and performing individual services. It is believed that if practical methods can be developed for the determination of such facts, it may open the way for distinct improvement in the efficiency of our present marketing methods. A series of studies have been made dealing with wholesale and retail distribution of grocery products and with a few other commodities. 

At the request and with the cooperation of the Wholesale Dry Goods Institute, a study of one representative wholesale dry goods establishment in the Middle West was undertaken. The present bulletin gives the results of that study and forms one of a series of publications dealing with the costs of distribution. It should be remembered that to a large extent the bureau is pioneering in this field and that much still remains to be done before the methods are perfected. This report is of special interest because of the detailed study of sales territories. Perhaps its chief utility lies in its demonstration of how similar studies can be profitably made. 

The present study was undertaken under the direction of J. W. 

Millard, formerly of the domestic commerce division of this bureau. 

It was completed and the report prepared by Wroe Alderson and 

Nelson A. Miller, also of that division.

PROBLEMS OF WHOLESALE DRY GOODS DISTRIBUTION 

By Wroe Alderson and Nelson A.

Miller, Merchandising Research Division 

RESULTS OF THE STUDY 

In the fall of 1928 the Department of Commerce undertook an intensive study of a dry goods business in Kansas City, Mo. 

The study was carried out with the cooperation of the Wholesale Dry Goods Institute. The house surveyed has an annual sales volume of nearly $5,000,000, and the range of commodities and the extent of territory covered is such as to make the business of the concern representative of all the major problems of the trade. The following conclusions are presented as a result of the study, first as to facts disclosed and secondly as to suggested changes in policy. 

SIGNIFICANT FACTS 

1. This house is really an association of specialized wholesaling and manufacturing departments, with each manager being largely responsible for selling and buying policies within his own department. 

2. The business of the house is carried into some remote sections by one group of commodities and extended in the opposite direction by an entirely different group. 

3. The counties which provide well-rounded sales for all departments are concentrated close to the headquarters city. 

4. Correlation of sales with market statistics indicates that the house secures a relatively uniform proportion of all available business in counties near home and that in a few distant counties it secures more than an average share, but in most a very small share. 

5. A very serious small-order problem exists, partly as a result of back orders, but also because of differences in the quality of customers. 

6. Sales expenses, in so far as they can be distributed by States, definitely reflect the influence of competition and distance from headquarters. 

7. The high ratio of sales expenses along the borders of the territory is partly due to the practice of maintaining regular salesmen representing all departments, whereas business is obtained for only a few departments in those sections. 

8. Credit experience grows progressively worse the farther the account is from the headquarters city. 1

9. Study of purchase sources shows strong concentration for the greater part of purchases in most departments, but a surprising number of total sources in each case, most of which supply an insignificant part of total purchases. 

10. Study of sales and turnover in the various classes of merchandise in the hosiery department indicates that averages determined and developed for the department are entirely inadequate as guides to proper buying and merchandising.

SUGGESTED REVISIONS OF POLICY 

1. The internal statistics collected by the company should be extended to cover an adequate system of stock control, number of sales, value of sales, and average inventory requirements by classes of merchandise. 

2. Stronger coordination of sales effort is advised. The general salesman should represent the house and its policies, rather than represent a group of departments. He should also be given standardized policies to work from in handling adjustments, sales promotion, and the selection of new customers. 

3. Too much territory is being covered. It seems practically certain that a greater profit could be made within a restricted territory such as would be indicated if only those counties were served which buy from a majority of the departments in the business. 

4. The few good customers outside this area should be handled by some special procedure other than sending general salesmen through all this thin borderland. Customers within the profit radius should be chosen on the basis of strict standards of customer value. 

5. Current sales and inventory data for the control of purchases is especially desirable in those departments suffering rapid style obsolescence. For several years increase in sales in a typical department of this sort has been offset by a decline in turnover. 

POLICIES OF THE SEVERAL DEPARTMENTS 

It was originally planned to apply the technique that has been developed in the study of wholesale houses in other fields, in order to establish costs for handling individual commodities and for servicing individual customers, to this wholesale dry goods establishment. It soon became apparent that the complete result sought could not be obtained on the basis of existing records. However, a great mass of work was carried through, resulting in a sales analysis by merchandise departments and divisions of the sales territory. 

Numerous special studies were also undertaken, based either on the books of the company or on direct observation of the operations of the business.

This report will need to be considered as a preliminary survey which will indicate the ground that must be covered for successful cost analysis in wholesale dry goods houses, rather than itself constituting such an analysis. It is believed, however, that in tracing the growth of this institution and describing its operations as they are carried on to-day, the influence of every important trend in the dry goods field may be observed.

Buying and all warehousing operations are strictly departmentalized in this house, as is selling, to some extent. The merchandise departments used will differ somewhat from those found in any other dry goods house, but taken together, include everything ordinarily found in this field. These departments are:

Since the period of the survey, three departments devoted to women's wear have been set up, and these are conducted under a slightly different policy from the other lines enumerated. Women's ready-to-wear coats and dresses are handled by a subsidiary incorporated as a garment company but conducted under the same roof. 

Several years ago women's garments were discontinued from their regular wholesale line, it being found unprofitable to sell these articles through the salesman traveling a territory. The garment company makes sales only to persons who visit the house and personally select the merchandise they want; mail and telephone solicitation is also used to facilitate movement of new style items with a "follow up" on a card system every 30 days by phone. It is thus possible to give a style service not feasible under the former policy. 

A second ready-to-wear department handles house dresses and bungalow aprons. These garments, together with overalls, are manufactured by the company itself in the same building that houses the wholesale departments. 

There is a small department for lingerie, corsets, and other accessories. This line is not featured by special salesmen but appears to be conducted largely for house sales, as in the case of the garment company.

There have been successive consolidations in the various piecegoods departments caused by the rapidly diminishing demand for materials for home manufacture of clothing. The woolen dressgoods department has been suffering a rapid decline in volume, which is nearly equaled by declines in the silk and velvet department.

Each of these departments is conducted by a manager who has had long experience in his particular merchandising field. This man's principal work is as buyer, his duties in this direction usually requiring visits to the large eastern markets several times each year. 

In each case, however, he has important selling functions in addition. He has complete charge of the house sales made in his line and also partial supervision over the efforts of the traveling sales- This is particularly true in the several departments where the business has been expanding and specialty salesmen are being used to push the specific line. Instances are the departments handling work clothing, shirts and pajamas, curtains and draperies, notions, hosiery, and underwear. men.

The result of this form of departmentalization has been to make each manager something of an independent agent within his own. domain. Buying and selling policies, as a consequence, largely reflect the personality and experience of the individual manager and, of course, militate against the maintenance of a strict and uniform policy for the house as a whole. The manager of the work-clothing department, for example, happens to believe in pushing a single staple item and supporting it with an advertising campaign and specialty salesmen throughout the territory. This policy has affected particularly the overalls manufactured by the company, which are merchandised under a trade-mark that has become well known in the territory.

The hosiery man is a conservative merchandiser whose constant care seems to be to serve his demand without getting caught with an excess of some faddish or extreme item. The special problem of his department is the rapid color obsolescence in hosiery and the great difficulty of disposing of a color once the vogue for it has passed. He

A somewhat different policy is pursued by the manager of the curtains and draperies department. Instead of following cautiously the change of fashions, this man purchases ahead of the shift. purchases in such quantities as he believes he will be able to merchandise during a single season. Having made a successful venture in a pattern or style for a season, he does not attempt to ride the item until it has fully spent its drawing power, but closes it all out ruthlessly at the end of the season so that he may be able to start with a fresh stock at the first of the new season. 

His policy differs from some of the other department managers in another important particular. He buys quality fabric, while some of the other managers are trying to hit the middle or lower price ranges. The natural result is that curtains and draperies are being sold to a large list of customers who do not take the other lines in the house at all. This situation is being handled largely by the employment of specialty salesmen for draperies. 

Each department manager has a turnover requirement imposed by the president of the company, which requirement he must try to a tain. Each man has his own special problems in meeting this requirement. In hosiery, for instance, the turnover may be greatly reduced by the carry-over of items which developed slow sale during the season and which the department head prefers to carry into another season in an effort to come out even, rather than take his losses and maintain turnover.

In some fields, such as piece goods, the results obtained by the wholesale house are influenced by peculiar conditions in the production field. In any one year the producers may present the peculiar spectacle of idle plants and plants unable to fulfill their orders, side by side. This comes about through unexpected popularity of some patterns or fabrics and public indifference to other patterns. Where a pattern has become the vogue, the mill which owns it has to work night and day in its effort to fill orders. As a consequence, it is necessary for the manager in these departments to estima e his full season's requirement at the beginning of the season and get in his order ahead of the anticipated rush, if possible. With this necessity

for putting in large orders well in advance, it is very frequently difficult for the piece-goods managers to meet the turnover requirement imposed upon them.

Diverging from all the other departments with regard to sales policy is a low-end and miscellaneous department. This department possibly finds its reason for existence in the desire of the house to offer sales leaders to its customers from time to time. The manager of this department is empowered to buy whatever he finds that seems attractive in price. The result of this practice has been to make the department virtually a separate wholesale operation. This manager frankly states that it is his central aim to make money by speculative purchases of items that are attractively priced. 

The manager of the notions department has, to a degree, the most difficult merchandising problem presented by any department. The items in his stock are all of relatively small value, so that a comparatively large amount of routine work is required to clear $100 worth of merchandise through his department. At the same time, he is forced to carry literally hundreds of items to each one appearing in the inventory of some of the other departments. Many of these items yield a very small volume of sales and would undoubtedly appear to be unprofitable if considered item by item. A large proportion of the whole business of the department is secured on a few outstanding staples. The single item of bias tape, for example, accounted for 20 per cent of sales in the notion department. The notion department manager is confronted with the additional problem of carrying a list of sundries which the merchants buy for their own use. This list includes such items as ledgers, bookkeeping supplies, and stationery. The manager states, however, that a relatively small proportion of his inventory is in these items and that they undoubtedly create sufficient good will for the house to justify carrying them.

EFFECTS OF CHANGING MARKET CONDITIONS 

The great problem common to every department is the rapid rate at which hundreds of items lose their salability. Another large concern has estimated that 20 per cent of all items carried become obsolete before they can be disposed of. Although the house under discussion does not have complete statistics on the subject, the president is sure that his own rate of obsolescence runs higher than 

10 per cent.

There are a number of factors which bring about the cessation of sales with regard to one item after another. A very important factor has been changed buying habits and new conditions of competition. With the growth of department stores, the city business of the wholesale dry goods merchant has tended to be restricted to the neighborhood dry goods and notion stores. As a result, an increasing proportion of his business has been with his rural and small-town dealers.¹ Within the last decade the latter class of business has been cut into rapidly by the advance of paved roads and the automobile. Other factors which have brought great changes are the varied occupational make-up of the population, the creation of new fabrics, and a change of popular demand with regard to fabrics used. The growth of employment in the oil fields and mechanical trades has brought on a quickening in the demand for khaki pants and similar types of work clothing. The demand for overalls, a garment more popular with the farmer, has not increased in the same proportion. 

The much-discussed decline in the use of cotton clothing and its replacing by silk and rayon is noted in this territory as elsewhere over the country.

This problem of commodity demand is complicated for this house by the broad extent of the territory covered, including all varieties of market conditions. There are, for example, some territories in which a steady demand is still found for cotton and wool stockings and full-length underwear for women. This condition exists side by side with a growing trend, even in most of the rural sections, toward silk stockings and the newer forms of lingerie for women. 

There has been a great decline in the consumption of piece goods. for making up garments at home. This has, however, resulted in a better garment trade for wholesalers. With the growth in favor of more expensive garments and the increasing rapidity of style change, especially in the item of color, there has been a tendency for everyone, from consumer to manufacturer, to deal more directly with the large style centers. How adaptation to style factors enables the wholesaler to bring back volume lost on piece goods by substituting ready-to-wear clothing has been explained earlier. The result has been to place the garment trade in the hands of the large department stores and specialty garment shops, which frequently handle some nationally advertised brand of garments and deal directly with the manufacturer rather than with the wholesaler. 

Confronted by this constant change in inventory, it would appear that an efficient and inexpensive system of inventory control would be a very vital and necessary requirement. This is recognized by many leaders in the industry, but so far they have been appalled, not without reason, by the magnitude of the task. Several factors combine to make a satisfactory system of control very difficult to attain for this type of house. There is, for instance, the wide range in the value and the character of the commodities handled. system of control which would be quite justified when applied to fur coats would be prohibitive in expense if attempted for thimbles, salt shakers, or many of the other small items in the notion department. There is also the difficulty of establishing standard sales units for some types of merchandise. In the piece-goods department the most convenient unit would be the bolt of cloth, were it not that no two bolts can be found which are of the same length. Starting out with bolts of varying lengths, the wash-goods department offered one-half bolt service, while silks were offered in even smaller quantities. A difficult inventory control problem is presented in that the inventory at any one time consists of whole bolts of various lengths, half bolts, and silk remnants. 

There seems no disposition to attempt any standardization in this

matter at the present time, partly, perhaps, because of the diminishing importance of piece-goods sales in the total business of the dry goods establishment.

A still more serious barrier is the lack of any standard terminology or system of classification for either colors, styles, or qualities. 

While merchandise departments are fairly logical combinations of merchandise items, they are not completely so. For instance, the underwear man may be asked to handle ties because he has had some experience as a buyer in that field. A wide variety of merchandise classifications are dumped into notions or taken out of this field according to whether or not another manager claims them for his own. department. As a result, the merchandise departments of wholesale houses, although valuable for administration, mean nothing whatever for comparative statistics from one house to another. 

SUGGESTED CLASSIFICATION OF DRY GOODS 

The president of the house under survey has undertaken a commendable piece of work in connection with the present study. He has prepared a code list of every subdepartment and item handled in each department. This merchandise classification is presented in the appendix of this publication for those who may wish to consult it in preparing classifications of their own. 

No doubt there will be prepared in time a standard classification for the use of merchants everywhere. When this is done several important requirements must be borne in mind. First, the department and subdepartments must be of uniform content from one house to another. Second, departments and subdepartments must be numerous enough to permit separation of all types of goods which vary decidedly in use or material. If this is done it will be possible to make proper groupings of merchandise classifications under a single manager to meet the administrative needs of the smaller houses and, at the same time, keep the classifications separate for statistical purposes. Third, items under each classification should be so coded as to give a ready record of their period of salability. This can be done merely by giving a code number to each manufacturer serving a department and to take the code number of the item from the manufacturer's number, plus the date when it was first taken on by the house. By this system it would be apparent, at any time when an item was under discussion, just how long the house had been handling it. It would also permit a periodical summary of the rate of obsolescence on items secured from various manufacturers. 

EXTENT AND CHARACTER OF TERRITORY COVERED 

The territory covered by the wholesale house under survey includes all of the United States west of the Mississippi, excepting 

North Dakota, Montana, Oregon, Washington, California, and

Arizona. A great part of this area, however, is what might be called "fossil" territory. It is probably covered now merely because it began to be covered at an earlier day and under radically different conditions. Something over 75 per cent of the total sales volume is now obtained from the three States of Missouri, Kansas, and

Oklahoma. Salesmen are still sent into 15 States, although in some there are only three or four cities which provide any sales. The character of the central portion of this territory is indicated by the accompanying maps (figs. 2 and 3) on population distribution, though some distribution is attempted in most of the States west of the Mississippi.

The trade territory proper may be considered to lie in about a dozen States and within a fairly compact area made up of portions of these States. The principal States or parts of States which enter into this concentrated area with some population factors affecting the character of demand are as follows:

Western Missouri.-Average in population density; number of men and women about equal; and more than average number of cities.

Kansas (except far western end).-The heavily populated portion of the State, with normal sex ratio and nearly all the cities. 

Oklahoma.-Entire State, except southern edge and southeastern corner. Varied as to population; denser on average than either 

Kansas or Missouri; mixed as to sex ratio; eastern half has numerous cities, western half very few. 

Texas (the Panhandle).-Thinly populated; males strongly predominating; few cities. 

New Mexico (eastern third).-Thinly populated; slight male predominance; only two cities over 5,000. 

Colorado.-Scattered over entire State. Population thin, except in half dozen counties. These counties mark the location of the cities in the State, most of which lie in a line extending north and south from Fort Collins to Trinidad. Heavy male predominance throughout most of the State.

Arkansas and Louisiana.-Average population density; normal sex ratio; fair number of cities.

It is likely that the house, at the time that it first spread into the vast territory over which its operations are extended, was engaged more strictly in dry goods wholesaling than it is to-day. At the present time the house is actually a loose coalition between a local wholesale house covering three States only and a group of specialized brokers who have carved out for themselves somewhat definitely constituted territories from the area west of the Mississippi. 

The drapery department, for instance, finds the heavier part of its sales throughout the northern half of this territory, and the work-clothes department throughout the southern half. 

Goods originating in the headquarters city, such as the overalls which the company manufactures, can be sold throughout the territory at a price advantage over the same type of merchandise shipped in from the East. Manufacture has recently been extended to other lines of work clothing and women's house dresses. Further diversification of this local production is prevented by both the style and labor factors.

The minimum stock requirement must be fixed high enough by the department managers to insure a supply of any demand item until a new shipment would have time to arrive. This added operating expense is spared the houses situated farther east and nearer the producing plants. This advantage, however, is enjoyed by the eastern houses only when they stay within their logical areas of distribution. It does not mean that they could compete favorably with the western jobber in his own territory. 

In addition to the increased investment in inventories on hand, which is brought about by this distance factor, there is a further inventory cost which must be borne by either the manufacturer or the wholesaler. That is the cost of goods in transit which are under the ownership of either the manufacturer or the wholesaler and are incurring interest charges for the account of one party or the other. This is a necessary cost of serving a market far distant from the centers of production and would appear in some form, whatever the channels of distribution.

One important element of expense which is brought about by the distance from producing centers is constituted by items of communication and transportation. Whenever an unexpected demand develops in a particular item, the order to the distant plant must usually be forwarded by telegram and usually requests shipment to be made by express. There are several departments of the business in which express charges make up as large a total as freight charges in the total transportation expense. This time factor in shipment causes the managers of several departments to rely on new mill centers close at hand, rather than on older established ones for their merchandise. This gives rise frequently to nice problems of adjustment between the quality factor and the time factor in selecting a source for a shipment of merchandise. Since one or the other of these factors is likely to be of more importance at a given time, it is obvious that these conditions help to increase the number of manufacturers used as merchandise sources. 

Among other factors that multiply the number of sources used is the bargaining factor. Another, already referred to, is the exclusive ownership in a pattern or fabric which a mill may have where several such exclusive patterns are required to make up a representative line of merchandise, as in the case of draperies, for instance. there is a definite tendency toward an increased number of supply sources.

SALES CONCENTRATION IN THE VARIOUS DEPARTMENTS 

Percentage distributions were made by States for the total business of the dry goods house and also for the 16 principal departments. The percentages of total business for Missouri, Kansas, and 

Oklahoma were 28.5 per cent, 28 per cent, and 18.7 per cent, respectively. These three States, which predominate in practically every department as well as in the total business, thus contributed 75.2 per cent of the sales volume for the two months under survey, leaving a remaining volume of 24.8 per cent for the 19 other States.

These States are, for the most part, west of the Mississippi and south of the Nebraska-South Dakota line. The percentage of total sales in the tri-State combination shows a good measure of concentration when the departments are compared against the total. A line drawn along the southern boundaries of Missouri, Kansas, Colorado, and Utah, divides the principal sales area into a northern and southern portion. A percentage was determined for each department's sales in each territory. Total business in the southern territory averaged 29.9 per cent, while that for the northern territory was 70.1 per cent.

The sales territory was also divided into eastern and western sections by drawing a line along the eastern boundary of Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, and New Mexico. The amount falling west of this line for the grand total was 10.4 per cent, while 89.6 per cent of the business occurred to the east of it.

ANALYSIS OF CUSTOMERS 

The house has experienced a great diversification in type of customers served during the course of its existence and also a great shift in the relative importance of the various types of outlets. At the beginning of the century the bulk of the business was done by general and department stores in small towns. This type of outlet has been of decreasing importance. 

In the more prosperous territory in the two tiers of States immediately west of the Mississippi there has been a tendency for drygoods buying to be concentrated in the larger cities. This trend has cut heavily into the volume formerly secured by the dry-goods merchant in the small town. At the same time the developing department stores have frequently been lost to the wholesaler as customers have perfected more direct channels for securing their merchandise from the producers.

There has been a growth of distinct types of specialty shops. not carrying a full line of dry-goods items. This is true notably in the case of draperies. All through this area there has sprung up a great number of shops handling draperies exclusively and usually engaging in interior decoration, or at least the making-up and installation of draperies. Such shops constitute the more important group of the customers for the drapery department in this concern. The department, therefore, gets its business from customers who do not buy the general line of the house who must be called upon. by a specialty salesman experienced in draperies. 

This same tendency is seen in greater or lesser degree in practically every merchandise department in the business. A wide variety of classes of customers is being served, and some of these types are almost exclusively the customers of only one or two departments of the business. This results in a greater need for specialty salesmen, and there is much duplication of effort since every foot of territory is covered by a general salesman and several specialty men in addition. Many problems of administration arise as, for example, determining just which accounts require specialty men and which could be handled by the general salesmen. It is frequently found that the specialty man spends too much time calling on houses already developed, whose business could be given to the general man. This fact, together with the necessity of paying a commission both to the general man on the territory and the specialty man who makes the sale, is indicative of the many problems arising from the present wide diversity in customer types. 

The same thing may be said with regard to customer sizes. A great many accounts are being carried because it is hoped they may some time yield a profitable volume of business. The president of

the company believes that at the present time he could pick out those customers who will develop into good accounts and those on whom useless sales promotion is being expended.

Many unsatisfactory accounts could be made into profitable ones if the dealer could be persuaded to concentrate his business. This company has found numbers of its smaller dealers who buy from 50 to 75 supply sources when 3 or 4 would amply take care of all their needs. A systematic endeavor to sell these merchants on the advantages of concentrated buying, both to themselves and the wholesaler, might produce results of great benefit. When a firm is buying from too great a number of sources, according to the president of the wholesale company, it can readily be shown that it is placing itself in a precarious credit situation. Furthermore, such a firm greatly increases its own difficulty of controlling inventory and turnover.

COMMODITY SELECTION 

A factor which is not always recognized by a merchant doing business over a wide territory is what may be known as commodity selection. This is the tendency on the part of some customers to buy almost entirely from some departments to the exclusion of the others. 

This characteristic of distribution has shown up in every case where the Department of Commerce has made a study of the territorial distribution of the business of a wholesaler.

It is significant that the customer with limited relations with the wholesaler is usually found on the outer edge of his territory. There are several obvious factors which help to account for this situation. 

One is the difference in shopping requirements for various lines of merchandise. Some items can be ordered in a routine fashion from houses located at a great distance. Other types of merchandise. require personal inspection by the customer, who consequently tends to purchase from houses within a radius of easy access. 

It has not been established that commodity selection is always unfavorable to the merchant on the basis of difference in margin. 

It is true, however, that such unbalanced business is almost inevitably more expensive, because the salesman is actually representing not his office as a whole, but only several of its departments, when he calls on such a customer. The ratio of sales expense to sales, therefore, tends to be greater in the case of the limited sales relation.

In addition to the need for inspecting commodities, there is another important reason for limited relations at the border of a sales territory. This is the competitive advantage of houses which are located closer to the customer. He hears of them oftener and knows more about them because they lie in the same web of business relations as himself.

There is the special case of the dealer located in a territory that is equidistant from several centers of distribution. It would seem reasonable that this man would tend to buy first from one center and then from another, depending upon how closely the competitive territory was covered by each and upon price concessions and other competitive devices resorted to by the various distributors. 

Figure 22 indicates the range of commodity selection by counties. 

This map indicates in the case of each county how many of the 16 departments of the business are finding a market there. It is significant that the counties which present a well-rounded market for all the merchandise the company handles are closely concentrated in the immediate vicinity of the headquarters city. This concentration is much stronger than the parallel concentration of counties showing a high volume of sales. It appears that in this territory customers, both large and small, find Kansas City the logical source of purchases and make a practice of purchasing the full line of commodities handled.

Only six counties to which sales from all departments were made are to be found outside the three States of Missouri, Kansas, and Oklahoma. Forty-six such counties are to be found in these three 

States. The shading off to one-half or less than one-half of the departments and finally to only two or three departments shows a definite relation to distance from the headquarters city. DISTRIBUTION OF COSTS BY SALES TERRITORIES 

Every section of the territory covered by a distributor is likely to contribute, in varying proportions, to the various types of costs incurred in conducting a business. While it may appear on the surface that the customer situated in Utah or Louisiana has little effect on inventory requirements or costs in Kansas City, it is seen, however, from the data presented previously, that each portion of the territory influences the cost of internal operations at the center. 

In Utah, for example, where the salesman is mainly a representative of the wash goods, hosiery, and curtains and draperies departments, it is apparent that the stocks in these lines maintained in the Kansas City office are performing a major service to him, while he might be able to get along quite well in other lines without calling on any such headquarters stocks. Wherever a customer of the hosiery department, for example, is to be found, that customer is responsible for the inventory cost of the hosiery department to the extent that it is maintained for his benefit. Apparently the only measure of this service to him would be the volume of his purchases from the hosiery department. In other directions the responsibility of the individual customer or portion of trade territory for costs is more directly to be ascertained and measured. 

The ratio of selling costs to sales exhibits a direct relation to such competitive factors as the distance from the distributor's headquarters city and the location of leading competitors. It also indicates the importance of the factor of commodity selection in customer cost differentials.

departments, in that part of the territory where customers usually purchase from all departments of the business. The outlying section of the territory where customers usually purchase from only a few of the merchandise departments are not being handled by general salesmen, presumably capable of representing the entire line carried. It may be that some adjustment in the use of regular and specialty men might yield the same volume with less sales expense.

The distance factor is also plainly visible in Figure 24 showing the credit experience of the house. This map was based on the ratio of accounts overdue to total accounts outstanding. It indicates that collections tend to be faster in the States nearest to Kansas City. 

This picture is complicated somewhat by the equally striking trend toward poor credit relations in the southern portion of the area.

RELATION OF SALES TO MARKET POSSIBILITIES 

While this case study of a wholesale house was not primarily a market survey, some consideration must be given to potential markets in the territory surveyed. When maps are displayed, showing that in general sales grow less as the distance from the city of location increases, a question arises as to whether distance is the single determining factor or whether purchasing power is also an important influence.

While no exhaustive study has been undertaken, some conclusions are presented on the basis of data for 30 representative counties scattered throughout the territory. Ratios to sales were computed for each of several important market indicators. While the results from use of so small a sample can hardly be considered final, sereral important tendencies are apparent. 

Of all the indicators, rural population seems to be the most reliable. That is, the relation between sales and rural population. is the most uniform and varies less from one county to another. 

Other market indicators which were studied in relation to sales are total population, auto registrations, farm income, and incometax returns. The ratios for the first three factors were fairly uniform while the ratio between sales and income-tax returns was most irregular. Apparently the rural population includes farm population and town dwellers living in places of less than 2,500. This is borne out by the merchant's own statement concerning the importance of the small-town retailer among his customers. This type of dealer is, no doubt, the principal outlet to both the farm population and the people of the small town. 

For each of the ratios which are designed to show the relation between actual sales and sales potential, it is a remarkable fact that greater uniformity exists in the central part of the territory. 

In the case of rural population, for example, sales by this house in the inner portion of its territory run between 10 and 50 cents per person. Around the edges of the territory, on the other hand, most of the counties fall below 10 cents for each person in the rural population. The clear indication would seem to be that near at home the house gets a portion of the business commensurate with community purchasing power. At a distance purchasing power does not rule. In the instances where sales are beyond what purchasing power would indicate, it is apparent that some special condition must exist as, for example, a county being covered by a salesman who has personal connections there.

It would seem, therefore, that the marginal portions of the trade territory are being covered for the sake of a few outstanding counties where business has possibly arisen in some accidental fashion. A saving in sales effort would be indicated immediately by concentrating all regular salesmen in the central territory and arranging some special method for covering the few worth-while counties at the edge of the territory. 

This showing of greater relation of sales to purchasing power near the center of the territory is directly related to another factor already discussed. This is the tendency toward well-rounded business in the counties near home as against business for only a few departments at a distance. Both factors indicate that the logical territory of distribution is within the central territory near at home and that where good distribution is secured at a distance it is to be considered as a fortunate accident, rather than an indication of sales possibilities.

For example, there is to be observed the usual conflict between the credit and sales departments. Salesmen are continually turning in orders from new customers without the very simple investigation of credit standing which they could easily do on the job. Where the amount of the original order is small, however, and this is frequently the case, such investigation is minimized. It costs several dollars to place a customer in the credit ledger and pay for regular investigations. This is frequently considered an undue expense on a very small order. Later on, when the customer begins to increase the size of his orders, he rapidly builds up a fairly heavy account without any further investigation. Having slipped into the ledger by means of an insignificant first order he is granted credit from then on with further check.

Delivery is one of the operations in which the house has seen several changes of policy. At times all trucking, including hauling incoming merchandise from the station, transfers between warehouses and wholesale salesrooms, and delivery of outgoing merchandise either to the local merchant or to the railway station is handled by an independent agency. This agency is a firm in the commercial trucking business charging the regular rate for its services. At other times it has been considered more economical for the house to handle its own merchandise. 

It is interesting, however, to examine the basis on which charges were made when the trucking was done commercially. The piece or package was the basis of the charge, with a flat rate per package; but a surcharge was added, graduated by weight in the case of all packages over a stated maximum weight. Since the house lately bought its drayage on this basis, it has a definite standard by which it may compute the cost of its present draying operations. 

Buying in this field seems to require permanent representation in New York. One of the members of the firm has his office there and is concerned only with the buying phase of the business. But in addition every department manager makes frequent trips to New York for the purpose of observing the new styles and for placing seasonal orders. The selling agencies are of course more fully concentrated in the New York area than is actual production, so that orders which are to be shipped from a variety of points the length of the eastern seaboard may be placed in New York.

Selling policies and administration do not seem to have been standardized for the business as a whole. It has already been said in the discussion of commodities, each department manager is somewhat of a law unto himself in the field of selling. True, the firm has a sales manager who has supervision of the entire traveling sales force. His work, however, seems to be largely concerned with routings, travel expenses, and adjustments with customers having personal difficulties. The salesman deals directly with the manager of each department in any discussion involving proper methods of handling particular commodities. He is responsible to the department manager for the great number of samples which he uses in the course of the year and which must be sold at reduced rates. In those departments where specialty salesmen are used most strongly, the salesman is practically an employe of the department manager. 

In some instances he is a man who works in the warehouse and as a house salesman for a part of the year and goes on the road at certain seasons to represent the merchandise in his department. In every case the specialty salesman is a man with a background of training in that department whose merchandise he particularly represents. 

The technique of actually filling an order for merchandise is rather intricate. When the order is received, separate shipping tickets are copied from it, each listing the items desired from a single department. This merchandise is assembled by each department and delivered to a shipping and packing room. The merchandise is here assembled by orders on long tables and checked both against the shipping tickets and the original order. Just before packing, a customer's invoice is made up from the actual merchandise on the floor. The billing clerk is a girl with a typewriter on a traveling

carriage, which moves down past the rows of orders, while someone calls off to her the separate items making up each order. There are other houses, however, which have set up a successful arrangement for assembling and billing directly from the original order, thus saving a considerable volume of clerical work. 

The house is remarkable and perhaps outstanding for the orderly and efficient methods of administration. At the beginning of each year standards are set, based on the performance of several years past. These standards do not include merely the usual sales quotas but set up a normal expense ratio for each salesman and each department and set an expected stock turn for each type of goods. These ratios are actually being used in the conduct of the business, and each department manager must make his figures show up properly or give a good explanation for any divergence. 

Gross profit is figured for each item and each order handled. 

A corps of clerks is kept constantly busy making these extensions which become a part of the invoice records of the house. The amount of gross profit which he turns in is used as an element of charging each salesman in addition to the sales volume he secures and the expense ratio which his operations show.

There are five aspects of operating cost in every business when that business is looked at from the standpoint of the commodities which make it up. There is first the expense of maintaining the required inventory, which is to be looked at from the two angles of requiring investment and requiring space. Moving goods through the house has the two phases of physical labor and clerical work. Finally, there are elements of cost which are involved in the contact with customers which are affected by customer characteristics only and do not vary with the nature of the commodity. These five general groupings of processes each account for a part of the total cost operation. Each of these cost groups can be measured approximately by one of the several factors shown in the table, as follows: 

Investment (including inventory insurance and other similar charges), average inventory investment, floor space occupied, weight of merchandise handled, number and volume of sales. 

Space (rent, heat, light, repairs, etc.). 

Labor (physical handling, receiving, packing, shipping).

Office and selling (routine selling, billing, etc.). 

Contact (credit cost, institutional selling, etc.).

Average inventory investment was obtained from the records of the company. The value and number of sales were obtained from the special machine tabulation made for the purposes of the survey. 

Space requirements of the several departments were determined by actual measurements made in the warehouse. Weight is the least satisfactory of the five factors set up. To determine a more satisfactory way of accounting for physical labor on various types of commodities would require a rigorous time study of all handling processes such as did not fall within the scope of this survey. 

The company keeps no record of the weights of merchandise received or shipped, but what is thought to be a workable estimate was drawn from a combination of rates and charges paid for the year 1927. These have been completely tabulated for each depart-

ment and in each case divided by the freight rate typical for that department. An adjustment is necessary between the express and freight charges since the express rate is said to be about twice as great as the freight rate for each type of merchandise. Using this method of estimate, weights were arrived at; on these the percentages in the weight column are based.

SPECIAL ANALYSIS OF HOSIERY DEPARTMENT 

In an earlier section of this report, in which the territorial distribution of sales for several departments was considered, the hosiery department was pointed out as the most typical in its distribution. This department accounts for 9.7 per cent of the total sales volume for the period analyzed; 78.4 per cent of the total sales in this department were obtained in the three States of Missouri, Kansas, and Oklahoma. Other States reporting from 0.2 per cent to 5.4 per cent spread out through the balance of the territory. 

The total number of counties which are canvassed to secure hosiery sales is almost as great as the total number buying from any of the 16 leading departments. There is not the sharp concentration in a group of outstanding counties which may be noted in a number of the other departments. These figures bear out the judgment of the president of the concern who stated at the beginning of the study that the hosiery department was the most representative department in the business. On this account considerable additional work was done on sales for this department during 1927. 

For this special study all items in the hosiery department were grouped into five classes and 24 subclasses. The five classes were men's hosiery, women's hosiery, infants' and children's hosiery, gloves, and sweaters and knit goods. Figure 26 (p. 41) shows the per cent of sales, per cent of average inventory investment, and number of merchandise turns for the hosiery department. These details indicate at once how inadequate is the turnover for the total department as a means of control. Turnover in the hosiery department was 2.52 times for the year. Womens' hosiery, with a turnover of 3.68, and gloves with a turnover of over 3.26, stood above this general turnover figure and the other three classes below it. 

This appears most striking, however, when we look at the turnover of the subclasses making up each larger group. 

In women's hosiery, for instance, the three groups of cotton, wool, and silk and rayon, had turnover of 7.82, 0.94, and 3.62. The merchandise in the wool hosiery section barely failed to turn over one time during the course of the year. This section accounted for less than 1 per cent of sales, although requiring nearly 2.5 per cent of inventory. This situation is accounted for, according to the manager of the hosiery department, by the highly seasonal character of sales of wool hosiery. The winter of 1926-27 was so mild in this territory as to leave the house with a large accumulation of wool hosiery stock in the spring. The unused stock has been carried over since with a marked effect on the turnover which can be secured in this item.

The situation is really more serious in the silk and rayon subclasses, although this group shows a turnover ratio somewhat higher than the average for the whole department. This group accounts for 

36.2 per cent of total hosiery sales and hence heavily influences the turnover rate for the department as a whole. A faster turnover rate might well be expected in a merchandise group where sales are so heavy and the manager consequently has an opportunity to keep the stock moving. All women's hosiery accounted for 43.3 per cent of all hosiery sales, which is followed by sales of men's hosiery of 29.5 per cent of the total. Similar disparities in the sales and turnover of subdepartments are seen in men's hosiery. This class as a whole has a turnover somewhat less than the turnover for the whole department and out of its eight classes only two have a higher turnover than that for hosiery in general. These two groups are bundled cotton hosiery and fancy silk and rayon.

The silk and rayon group is largest in point of sales, accounting for 9.7 per cent of the total for the department. As in the case of women's silk and rayon hose, turnover for this group is somewhat greater than for hosiery in general but is somewhat disappointing in relation to the volume of sales. It is quite possible that obsolescence of pattern and color is again a factor in this case. At least it is found that the highest turnover for men's hosiery, and incidentally for the whole department, is found in bundled cotton hose, just as women's cotton hose made the best showing in the women's hosiery section. The turnover of 10.2 in this subclass was probably due largely to the simple character of the merchandise under this heading. To begin with, the range of color and pattern in all cotton hosiery is very limited. Neither is there the variation in quality and fabric found in silk and rayon hosiery. 

This inference is borne out by the items furnishing the best turnover in some of the other classes. In gloves, for instance, the best single item is canvas gloves, with a turnover of 8.5. This item, unlike those that rank first in turnover in the other classes, stands first in sales as well as in turnover. 

The lowest turnover of all subclasses in any of the five classes was for ladies' sweaters and men's fancy wool hose. Rapid style change seems to be the factor slowing up turnover in ladies' sweaters, while with men's fancy wool hose both the fabric and the variation in style and pattern are unfavorable to turnover. 

The trend for the hosiery department since 1917 is somewhat different from the trend for the whole business. The first time chart showing inventory, sales, and turnover trends for the business as a whole indicates a peak in 1920. Since that time both sales and inventory have gone off slowly. The ratio of inventory to sales, however, has remained about the same during this period. 

In the case of hosiery, however, the depression lasted only for the year 1921, during which time both sales and inventory were low. 

Since that time both have been climbing up gradually. The level of inventory, however, has risen somewhat faster than sales volume, and as a result turnover has declined. In other words, it has been possible to maintain turnover at a steady level in the business as a whole, even during the period of retrenchment, while turnover has gradually fallen off in hosiery although it was one of the few departments enjoying the expansion.

Purchases of the hosiery department were made from 124 vendors. 

Of this number there were 18 from whom the department received $5,000 worth of merchandise each during the year. This group accounted for 74.6 per cent of all purchases. If the total for all sources furnishing more than $2,000 in merchandise annually is con-

sidered, there are 38 firms, or considerably less than one-third of the total, which furnish 91 per cent of all purchases. There is a surprising number of vendors furnishing a small volume of merchandise annually as, for instance, 24 firms from which the hosiery department purchased less than $25 each during the year. This group constituted 19.4 per cent of all vendors and accounted for only .03 per cent of purchases.

This wide diversity of sources is accounted for, in part, by shipments from various factories under a single ownership. To even a greater extent it represents small purchases here and there of fancy items which can not be supplied by the principal vendors. 

A very striking source of loss in the hosiery department is in soiled or out-moded salesmen's samples. The hosiery department at the end of 1927 carried in its inventory over 1,700 pairs of hosiery which had been returned by salesmen and on which a part of the original price had been marked off.

Hosiery in this condition, as also from the discarded colors in the regular stock, are sent to the factory for redyeing, usually in black, and carry this additional item of expense.
