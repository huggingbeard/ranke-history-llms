COMMENTARY Part 1 MEDICAL AID FOR THE AGED INTRODUCTION Two provisions of the Social Security Amendments of 1960 

(Public Law 86-778) were of special interest to the 1961 South Dakota Legislature. One provided federal matching grants to the states for medical services to persons 65 years of age or older receiving Old Age Assistance (OAA Medical Care). The 

Thirty-seventh Legislature activated a program for medical services and administration for this purpose. 

The second provision of the Kerr-Mills Act provided federal funds to the states for medical assistance to needy, aged persons not receiving Old Age Assistance. Action on this program--Medical 

Aid to the Aged (MAA)--was postponed by the 1961 Legislature pending further investigation and study. To determine better what course of action South Dakota should follow, House Concurrent Resolution No. 12 was adopted in the 1961 session, directing the 

State Legislative Research Council to complete a thorough study of the advisability and feasibility of MAA and to report to the 

Thirty-eighth Legislature.

The State Legislative Research Council, during the interim between the 1961 and 1963 sessions, established a Committee on Welfare to undertake the study. Because, as the resolution authorizing the study noted, South Dakota lacked experience in the medical care field, the problems encountered and the solutions discovered by other states were carefully scrutinized. 

It was learned that, as a general rule, MAA programs cost more than was originally anticipated, and, after an MAA program had been implemented, it proved difficult to retrench. For these reasons, the 1961-62 Committee recommended that the entry of South Dakota into this part of the Kerr-Mills program be careful and slow, beginning with the enactment of a limited, controlled pilot plan by the 1963 Legislature. The recommended legislation would be unique among the states, since it called for a plan to empower the South Dakota State Welfare Commission to contract with insurance companies to obtain pre-paid medical and hospital care for the medically indigent aged.

The Thirty-eighth South Dakota Legislature activated the program as recommended, with certain amendments--an experimental and exploratory MAA plan (H.B. 510, 1963: Appendix I) and pro vided an appropriation to implement the enabling legislation on a limited scale (H.B. 622, 1963: Appendix II). The Legislature also followed the recommendation of the 1961-62 Committee by the adoption of a resolution directing the State Legislative Research Council to review, at three month intervals, the operations of the pilot program during the first year of its operation (H.C.R. 

2, 1963: Appendix III).

-6- The purpose of the review called for in the resolution is to help answer for the Legislature these persistent--and heretofor unanswered--questions: How many applicants for MAA are there likely to be? What benefits will be necessary to provide the medical assistance needed? How can the administration of a full 

MAA program best be handled?

GENERAL BACKGROUND Health and Age 

As the standard of living has risen, Americans have become increasingly conscious of health problems, and have come to expect more and better medical services. More abundant medical services of increasing quality have been available; at the same time, these services have become more expensive. The effectiveness and expense have had a very marked effect upon aged persons. 

Medical research has made great strides during the past several decades. These advances in turn have contributed to longer life spans for the population. Progress in preventive and therapeutic medicine has also changed the demand for health service, with emphasis shifting from control and treatment of communicable diseases to prevention and treatment of chronic and degenerative diseases. Emphasis is concentrated now on problems like heart disease and cancer--diseases characteristic of aged persons. 

The portion of our population 65 years or older is steadily increasing, and probably will continue to do so. In South Dakota, this trend is amply demonstrated by the changes from 1950 to

There is also increasing pressure for retirement at age 65--with moves toward even earlier retirement. The longer life span and earlier retirement will inevitably result in a larger group of Americans living on a fixed income. 

Because the need for medical services increases with age, and because of a longer life span, health cost for the aged have become an area of major concern. A person reaching 65 today is faced with the prospect of a longer life span, a rising level of medical costs at the period when they are most needed, and a steady decline in real income due to general price increases.

Cost of Health 

Americans today are spending about $25 billion annually on health and medical care-roughly five per cent of the gross national product. According to "Trends in the Supply and Demand of Medical 

Care, (U.S. Congress, 1959), the average family spends about five per cent of its income on medical care, or a family with a yearly income of $5,000 spends about $250 for health. 

The per centage is not even, however--families with incomes of less than $2,000 devote nearly twelve per cent, and families with incomes of $7,500 or over devote about three per cent to health. 

This substantial outlay for health has resulted in attempts to equalize the burden--particularly for persons on a wage or salary. The result is most apparent in the rapid growth of medical insurance. Today more than seventy per cent of the population is covered by medical insurance, and this insurance pays about one-quarter of Americans' private medical care bills. 

Public spending (including federal, state and local governments) also accounts for about one-quarter of the total amount spent on medical care--a substantial portion of this amount being spent on aged persons.

This is an area of major concern to state legislators.

HISTORY OF MEDICAL CAPE IN SOUTH DAKOTA 

Medical aid to aged persons in South Dakota can be divided into five distinct periods, with a possible sixth period under consideration. The first period prior to 1936 when medical care was the responsibility of familes, municipalities, and counties. The second period occurred during 1936-39, when the 

State assumed responsibility for rather extensive medical payments included in money grants to OAA recipients. The third period (1940-61) saw the State withdraw and substitute only limited medical assistance payments to persons receiving OAA. 

In 1961 this aid was changed in nature and its amount increased, representing a fourth period (OAA Medical Care). The limited program to extend medical aid to needy persons not receiving 

OAA (MAA, effective July 1, 1963) will be a fifth period.

Future expansion of the MAA program to something more than a pilot plan would be a sixth distinct period. care was no concern

Very little need be said of the three periods prior to 1961. 

During the first period (up to 1936) medical care was no of state government. During the second period (1936-39) the state did assume some responsibility, but quickly withdrew to make small payments to public assistant recipients during the third period (1940-61).

During the third period the state did, however, recognize the heavy burden being borne by county governments in this area, and a committee of the State Legislative Research Council made

-8- a comprehensive study of medical care for aged persons during the 1957-59 biennium. Changes in federal statutes in 1956 had made increased federal funds available for public welfare. 

Although the 1957 South Dakota Legislature amended the law to take advantage of such funds, no state money was appropriated, and the ability to accept increased federal grants was thus virtually negated.

The State Legislative Research Council Subcommittee on Medical Care in 1958 recommended to the Legislature: a. That a medical care program for public assistance recipients be adopted, subject to financial and program limitations, to be supported by federal, state and county funds and that state funds for such a program be supplied through special appropriation; b.

C. 

That by support through a minimum state appropriation in addition to county participation and federal grant funds, the state-administered program thus financed relieve and aid the counties in their provision of medical care for the indigent; and 

That county participation in the state-administered program be based on the average number of categorical aid recipients within each county.

These recommendations were not accepted by the 1959 Legislature, and medical care remained chiefly a county function, although limited funds continued to be provided to OAA recipients by the Public Welfare Department. The OAA program prior to 

June 30, 1961 provided glasses and denture allowances to a specified maximum; medicine for persons with diabetes, heart disease, and anemia; occasionally nursing home care; home care if necessary which could include a registered nurse; and miscellaneous services. During 1960 this assistance accounted for about 9.5 per cent of total OAA funds expended. In absolute terms, medical care administered by the State Department of Public Welfare to OAA recipients cost more than $620,000 in 1960--about $171,000 were from state appropriations and the remainder from federal matching funds.

Amendments during 1960 to the Social Security Act (Public 

Law 86-778) modified nearly all sections of the Act. This legislation encouraged expansion of medical care under the OAA program, and in 1961 the South Dakota Legislature appropriated $740,000 for the 1961-63 biennium for medical care to OAA recipients. The state thus passed from the third (1940-61) period of limited implied responsibility, to a fourth period (since August 1, 1961) of explicit responsibility for limited services.

Under the OAA medical care program, services to recipients have been expanded over those extended during 1940-61. In

addition to medical, surgical, and hospital services, the Public Welfare Department provided dental care and eye glasses, eye examination, and orthopedic aid. The Department does not include payments for drugs, however. The 1963 Legislature appropriated a "lump sum" amount for all categorical welfare aid programs, including OAA medical care, for the fiscal biennium ending June 30, 1965, to allow greater flexibility, providing discretionary power to the State Public Welfare Commission to apply funds to the several programs where needed. For this reason it is not possible, as of the date of this report, to predict with any degree of accuracy how much, if any, the OAA medical care program will be expanded--and in what direction--in 1963-65. 

The details concerning the fifth period--the pilot MAA era--of medical care in South Dakota are set out in the following sections.

BRIEF ANALYSIS OF THE SOUTH DAKOTA PILOT MAA PLAN 

The South Dakota Medical Aid to the Aged program was established by House Bill No. 510 (1963). South Dakota's lack of experience with prepaid medical care and hospitalization plans, particularly when the "insured" group is not readily identifiable, is reflected in the initial cautiousness of the pilot plan approach. Because of the basic distaste for "big" state government, the Legislature, through the State Legislative Research Council, considered several proposals for MAA. The proposal ultimately adopted, though limited to a certain extent, is one which is believed to fit the conditions prevalent in South Dakota: to satisfy those opponents of ever-increasing governmental bureaucracy and to provide medical care for the medically indigent aged. The full text of the MAA bill, as adopted, is reproduced as Appendix I of this report. The principal features of H.B. 510 have been subjected to a section by section analysis below. 

Section 1 Section 1 grants to the State Department of Public Welfare additional powers which are enumerated in five subsections. 

Subsection (1) authorizes the Department to qualify for and accept federal matching funds under the Kerr-Mills amendment to Title I of the Federal Social Security Act, which provides for medical assistance for persons over sixty-five years of age, medically indigent and not recipients of OAA. Subsection (2) empowers the Department to establish standards consistent with the goals of the Kerr-Mills amendment and establishes income limits for qualification for the MAA program to those single, aged persons receiving less than $1,500 annually or less than $1,800 annually for an aged, married couple or having a net worth of up to $10,000 at the time of application. 

Public Welfare Commission is permitted to lower, but not increase, the income eligibility requirements. Subsection (3) provides for the acceptance of applications and for Departmental determination of eligibility under rules established by the Commission. The

Subsection (4) grants to the Department authority to certify the medical indigency of qualified applicants for a specific period of eligibility. Subsection (5) permits the Department to appoint county advisory committees, leaving the advisory duties of such committees to be established by the Department. 

Section 2 

Section 2 of the South Dakota Act is out of the realm of the ordinary. It directs the Department to purchase, in behalf of certified medical indigents, medical care and hospitalization insurance contracts from insurance carriers. The responsibility of the contractor is limited to in-patient, $25.00 deductible, thirty-day care for each hospitalization and for out-patient hospital services and care stipulated by contract during the patient's period of eligibility. Only physicians who agree in writing to participate in the pre-payment program are included, and services are limited to twelve office or personal calls, to $10.00 deductible laboratory fees with total costs of no more than $100.00 per fiscal year, and to services provided as part of in-patient hospital care for thirty days after each admission. No profit or loss is allowed to accrue to the contractors, renegotiation occurring immediately to lower service levels if a loss is anticipated, or losses may also be eliminated by the use of the MAA contingency fund. The section also grants immunity from state insurance taxes to the contractor for the premiums paid. 

Section 3 

Section 3 provides for inclusion of certain features in the contract, as follows: simultaneous effectivity throughout the state, inclusion of all qualified residents of the state, prohibition of any payment as a condition for eligibility by the eligible recipient, reserves right of Departmental supervision or cancellation upon thirty days notice without liability after date of cancellation, and safeguards against disclosure of information about recipients except for purposes of contract administration.

Section 4 

This section grants the Public Welfare Commission the authority to make rules and regulations for the program and specifies rules providing for the following: (1) A fair hearing for rejected applicants, (2) Efficient administration, including a merit system, (3) Reporting information to the 

U. S. Department of Health, Education and Welfare, (4) Safe guards on disclosure of information about applicants, (5) The right of all individuals to apply for assistance, along with prompt determination of eligibility, and (6) Reports of the Department necessary for full accountability for public funds.

Section 5 

Section 5 specifies that the Act may not become effective until approved by the Secretary of Health, Education and Welfare, and not prior to July 1, 1963.

Section 6 

Section 6 creates the "Medical Assistance for the Aged Contingency Fund" for the payment of emergency disbursements by the contractor.

Section 7 

This section permits the Department to use money from the contingency fund created in Section 6 to remove a temporary deficit condition, with the approval of the Public Welfare Commission.

Section 8 

Section 8 provides that the terms "medical assistance" and "medical or remedial care" shall be assigned definitions as provided in the 1962 Social Security Amendments. 

Section 9 

Section 9 makes fraud by an applicant or the aiding or abetting of property transfers to defeat the purpose of the Act a misdemeanor, punishable by a $500 fine or three months' imprisonment, or both.

Additional Comment 

Although the original H.B. 510, as introduced, carried an appropriation, the appropriation was subsequently stricken from the bill. A separate bill, House Bill No. 622 (Appendix 

II), was introduced to carry the appropriation both for the purchase of the medical care contracts, and the contingency fund. Both H.B. 510 and H. B. 622 were adopted by the Legislature and were approved by the Governor. 

COST OF THE MAA PROGRAM IN SOUTH DAKOTA

According to final 1960 census figures, South Dakota's population was 680,514, an increase of 4.3 per cent over 1950. 

During this same period the number of aged persons increased from 56,000 to 72,000--an increase of over 29 per cent. to state it another way, in 1950 aged persons represented 8.5 per cent of the population. By 1960 this percentage increased to 10.5 per cent and it is possible that the figure could increase to more than 12 per cent by 1970. This group, steadily growing, will become an increasingly influential political factor in 

South Dakota.

In January, 1963, a total of about 8,000 persons received aid from the State Department of Public Welfare under OAA, representing 11 per cent of the aged population in South Dakota. 

Subtracting these persons from the total aged in South Dakota, indicated about 64,000 possible recipients of MAA. 

The great majority of these would probably be excluded because of adequate means for providing their own medical care, but a broad MAA program could cover at least as many as the present number of OAA recipients. Estimates of costs are virtually impossible, of course, without knowledge of the actual number which might be eligible, and the possible "clients" of an MAA program will remain unknown until the pilot plan is in operation. However, rough estimates suggest that there may be as few as 2,500 and as many as 10,000. If these figures are used as the minimum and maximum, and a $15 monthly cost per person is assumed, the overall cost would therefore probably vary somewhere between $450,000 and $1,800,000 annually ($900,000 $3,600,000 biennially) of which South Dakota's share under the matching provisions would be from about $125,000 to $504,000 annually. 

It was primarily because of this broad range of possible state costs that the 1963 South Dakota Legislature adopted the pilot program, with a limited appropriation. The appropriation, $56,034 each year, will with the federal matching funds, make available a total of about $180,000 annually for MAA program expenditures. It is anticipated that the money thus made available will pre-pay health insurance contracts for a maximum of 1,000 of South Dakota's medically indigent aged.

EARLY STEPS TOWARD ACTIVATION OF THE SOUTH DAKOTA PROGRAM 

Although the unique pilot program--South Dakota was the first state to attempt such a program of medical care with the pre-paid insurance concept--was not to have become effective until July 1, 1963, or later, preliminary steps had been taken toward developing the plan material needed to effect federal participation under H. B. 510. Initial determinations were made by the Public Welfare Commission at its March, 1963, meeting, first, to send letters to the home offices of over 300 companies authorized to do accident and health insurance business in South Dakota, apprising them of the legislation, and asking for proposals, observations and recommendations pertaining to the implementation of the program, and, second, to assign staff for preparation of plan material drafts relating to eligibility requirements and application procedures.

EFFECT OF 1962 SOCIAL SECURITY AMENDMENTS On July 25, 1962, the 1962 Public Welfare Amendments 

(Public Law 87-544) were signed into law. Although much discussion has taken place with regard to changes in rehabilitative services to aid in removing welfare clients from relief rolls, significant changes have also occurred with regard to other categorical aid programs in which there is liklihood of major

decreases in case loads.

Authorization is now available for states to combine their welfare programs in Old Age Assistance, Aid to the Blind, Aid to the Disabled and also OAA Medical Care and Medical Aid to the Aged, hereby making medical care applicable to the blind and disabled. Additional federal funds are available under such a plan for increased federal matching of state funds for these forms of categorical assistance. The different matching formulae used in the past for each program can be, in large measure, eliminated, providing for substantial decreases in staff time simply to establish grants for recipients of the aid programs for the blind, disabled, aged and medically indigent aged. It is possible that much confusion can be obviated and some savings can be achieved, if these South Dakota welfare programs were operated under this authorization.

State budgets for hospitals for the mentally ill and the mentally retarded have continued to rise in recent years. The 

South Dakota institutions for the mentally ill (Yankton State Hospital) and the mentally retarded (Redfield State Hospital and School) are not exceptions to this nationwide trend. 

1959 to 1963. for example, the Legislature's appropriations for the salary line item for Yankton State Hospital has risen by 70 per cent, and for Redfield State Hospital and School it has been increased by 52.3 per cent. 

During the same four year period, however, maximum patient fees, for voluntary patients able to pay, have not been adjusted at all. Nor has there been any increase in the contribution by the county of residence of committed patients without a similar ability.

The contradiction suggested by these budget facts caused the 1963 South Dakota Legislature to be concerned, not so much with the rising costs of patient care, but with the lack of adjustment in patient and county fees. Several approaches to an increase in these fees were introduced in bill form, and one which did increase the rates at Yankton was adopted and signed by the Governor. 

The question of patient and county fees at Redfield was, however, referred directly from the 1963 House of Pepresentatives to the State Legislative Research Council for study, and the Joint Appropriations Committee, which had prepared the bill adopted regarding Yankton, asked that at the same time the Council give further study to the Yankton rates. 

YANKTON STATE HOSPITAL 

History and Supervision Yankton State Hospital pre-dates the state of South Dakota-it was opened in 1878 as the Territorial Insane Asylum. Although it has undergone many changes in its history, its basic function has been unchanged except in the repard that it is no longer a purely custodial institution, but has developed a program for treatment and rehabilitation.

The Hospital is under the jurisdiction of the State Board of Charities and Corrections, a constitutional (Article XIV) 

control board the major function of which is overall management of the state penal and charitable institutions. The Board is comprised of five members appointed by the Governor with the advice and consent of the Senate. The law (SDC 55.17, as amended) prescribed that the members of the Board shall not all belong to the same political party, and that one member shall be a physician and one member a veteran. The Board members serve overlapping terms of six years, and are compensated while in attendance at meetings at the rate of $15 per diem. The chief administrative officer of the Board is an Executive Director, whose appointment, term of office, and salary are at the discretion of the Board.

Yankton State Hospital is under the immediate supervision of a Superintendent, whose appointment, term of office, and salary are also at the discretion of the Board of Charities and Corrections.

10 per cent during the same two year period.

The medical staff consists of 14 physicians, with other professional personnel including 33 registered nurses, 1 to 3 clinical pyschologists, and varying numbers of physical therapists, social service workers, dieticians, and so on. The total number of employees averages about 600, representing a staffto-patient ratio of approximately 1 to 2.5.

REDFIELD STATE HOSPITAL AND SCHOOL 

History and Supervision Redfield State Hospital and School, like Yankton State Hospital, is a constitutional institution. It was opened in 

February, 1902, as a custodial institution for mentally deficient and epileptic patients. This institution, also, retains its basic function with one major change: it has developed in recent years a program for training and rehabilitation--and subsequent release--of many of its patients, although, because of the different nature of its population, to an extent much less than has Yankton. 

The institution is under the jurisdiction of the State Board of Charities and Corrections. It is also closely aligned with the State Commission for the Mentally Retarded, of which the Superintendent of Redfield is the chairman and whose other

members are the State Director of Public Welfare, the State Superintendent of Public Instruction, one attorney, and one physician. The latter two members are appointed by the 

Governor for terms of four years. The Commission has authority in all matters pertaining to the care and welfare of mentally retarded persons not confined at Redfield, and determines the conditions under which commitments to the institution are required.

Redfield State Hospital and School is under the immediate supervision of a Superintendent, whose appointment, term of office, and salary are at the discretion of the State Board of 

Charities and Corrections. Unlike the Superintendent at Yankton, who is a psychiatrist, the Superintendent at Redfield is not medically trained.

BASIC QUESTIONS 

Four basic questions are posed by the background information presented, and each should probably be considered in turn in any study of charges for care and maintenance of patients at 

Redfield State Hospital and School and Yankton State Hospital. 

The questions are:

Should the charges at either or both institutions be increased? 

If so, to what level? 

By whom should the fees be paid? 

To whom should the receipts go?

Each of these questions is discussed briefly in the sections below.

Desirability of Rate Increases 

The Senile Survey Committee of the State Legislative Research Council, in 1954, submitted its recommendation to the 1955 Legislature that "the charges for care and maintenance at Sanator and the Yankton and Redfield State Hospitals be increased to correspond with changes in maintenance costs." In 1954, the rates were $35 at Redfield ($10 per month higher than at present) and $35 at Yankton (the present-day rate). However, in 1954, the average daily costs per patient at the two institutions were only $1.85 at Yankton and $1.72 at Redfield.

State-share costs, in other words, were only about $.60 per day-in contrast to about $1.20 today at Redfield and about $3.90 today at Yankton.

In explanation of its recommendation, the 1954 committee cited a recommendation from The Mental Health Programs of the Forty-Eight States (a Council of State Governments study), as 

Follows: 

In most of the states, the amount of reimbursement required from patients or their legally responsible relatives is much less than the cost of care and treatment. In only a few states is the amount set at "actual cost" of maintenance and medical care. Most of the stigma surrounding hospitalization of the mentally ill might be averted if patients could feel that they were making a contribution toward the cost of their hospitalization, as in a general hospital for physical illness. Patients or their legally responsible relatives should contribute to the cost of hospital care and treatment in accordance with financial ability, but such ability should not imply preference in hospital admission or treatment.

Since that time, the trend appears to be that more and more states have adjusted maximum charges upward, more nearly to coincide with the actual cost of care and maintenance. Appendix 

V lists the approach to charges in several other states. 

In January, 1960, the Mental Health Committee of the South

Dakota Medical Association completed a draft of a proposed revision of the South Dakota laws regarding the mentally ill and mentally retarded, which recommended that the charges be based on the full costs of care and maintenance (see Appendix VI). In 1963, the 

South Dakota Association for Retarded Children urged the Legislature to increase the rates at Redfield to $35, as an interim measure until a better plan--for which the Association's suggestions are shown under "Liability for Payments," below--could be accomplished. 

On the other hand, the argument might be raised that, with few exceptions (such as the habitual alcoholic, perhaps) the cost of providing these services is properly one of the state, and that the increased cost of care should not necessitate an increase in charges to the patients or the counties.

Level of Charges 

If the ultimate recommendation of the Committee should be that the rates at either or both institutions should be raised, the increase logically should be predicated on the cost of care and maintenance. Perhaps charges for full costs, or at percentage (60 per cent, for example) of the actual average per patient expenditures at the respective institution might be considered.

On the other hand, at least two special circumstances should be called to the attention of the Committee. First, the "average" approach would not represent a "benefits-received" answer in most cases; the levels of care at Yankton, for example, vary from simple custody, which is very cheap, to intensive treatment, which is extremely expensive. Second, many commitments to 

Redfield are virtually for life, because the patient is beyond training and rehabilitation, while the patient stay at Yankton tends, at least for recent admissions, to be comparatively short-term.

Liability for Pauments 

Within the existing framework, there appear to be four possible sources for payments for care and maintenance of institutionalized mentally ill and retarded persons. They are the patient (or his estate or other sources or responsible relatives), the county, the state, and the federal government. 

The patient may pay under existing South Dakota law, but only if he desires to do so, or, at Yankton, if he is not indigent and his commitment is voluntary. A few insurance companies write health insurance contracts which cover mental illness, but most policies specified exclude payments to state institutions. If the patient does not pay, the county must, up to the limits set by South Dakota law, although the county is given the right to attempt to recover payments made in behalf of a patient, through a lien on his property. State law, however, absolves the parents from legal responsibility for support when a child is committed to Redfield (SDC 1960 Supp. 

30.0605-1, 30.0605-3, 1959-60 AGR 349). Each county is required annually to levy a tax sufficient for the charges incurred for care of the mentally ill and a separate levy for care of the mentally retarded.

The state, of course, pays the greatest proportion of the costs of care, through the appropriations for operations and physical plant at the two institutions, and the state also absorbs the charges which would otherwise be paid by the counties when the committed patient's legal residence cannot be established within any county.

The federal government pays charges on behalf of certain Indian patients at Yankton State Hospital. In addition, since 

March, 1962, the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare has

permitted states to claim federal participation in assistance payments to an indigent person who is conditionally released from a mental institution (and who is in his own or a relative's home, a boarding home, or a nursing home not specifically established for mental patients). This new federal approach could be quite meaningful especially regarding the large senile population at Yankton, since it would allow the state to avail itself of federal matching for these non-psychotic patients. 

To date, however, South Dakota has not participated in the new federal program.

The trend in other states appears to be in the direction of imposing the cost directly on the patient, his estate, or his responsible relatives, where the ability to pay exists. During 

1961 sessions, for example, New York adjusted its charges to patients able to pay to $10 per day for the first 150 days, or longer if intensive treatment is extended, and $5.50 per day for continued treatment; Texas set the charge at $300 per month for the first month and $117 per month thereafter, with geriatric cases charged $105 per month; and Ohio charges patients on the basis of average costs of care and maintenance, about $5.00 per day. The Joint Appropriations Committee of the 1963 South 

Dakota Legislature introduced a bill (S.B. 275, Appendix VII) 

patterned somewhat after that of Ohio, but it died in Committee. 

Particular problem areas in the matter of liability for payment appear to be three. The first of these is the geriatric case, of which Yankton has a great many, who is not mentally ill, but merely senile or ill with the diseases of old age, and whole children have used commitment to Yankton as an alternative to home care (often impossible) or nursing home care (usually very expensive). Many of these senile cases involve no indigency, and the ability to pay the actual cost of care frequently exists among the responsible relatives'. The second problem area is the alcoholic case, who voluntary commits himself to Yankton to "dry out," and is frequently released and readmitted. The third problem area is that of the parents of mentally retarded children at Redfield who have moved from the state after the child is committed, leaving the costs to the citizens of South Dakota. In many cases, there appears to be a certain amount of deliberateness involved when some parents of mentally deficient children have moved into South Dakota, had the children sent to Redfield, and then moved back into their home state--which is frequently one of those states which do charge parents for care. 

There are 109 patients at Redfield whose parents are not residents of South Dakota; 36 of these patients are under the age of 21. 

The South Dakota Association for Retarded Children recommended to the 1963 Legislature the following principles, limitations, and procedures regarding patient fees at Redfield State Hospital and School:

1. The maximum responsibility of parents for the cost of care of the retarded in Redfield State Hospital and School shall be limited to the costs of rearing a normal child at home. 2. 3. 4. 5. 6.

Department of Labor Statistics. 

For families with incomes about the minimum level, criteria and procedures for determining charges should be developed, based primarily upon net taxable income.

Responsibility of parents for charges shall be reduced or cease if the period of institutional care is very prolonged or when the child reaches age 21.

No charges or debt shall accrue for other than the charges set by the procedures outlined above. 

There shall be a statute of limitations to provide that no charges can be recovered which are past due for more than a reasonable, determined period of time. 

Assets or entitlements of patients may be applied toward reimbursement to the extent of average per capita cost, with full consideration of his needs upon rehabilitation and release.

A policy statement issued by the New Jersey State Commission on Mental Health suggests some broad principles to be considered in any proposed action on liability for payment. An excerpt is reproduced as Appendix VIII. 

Recipient of Fees 

Under present law, all fees paid by the counties for care and maintenance at both Redfield and Yankton are paid directly into the State General Fund; neither institution receives these fees for operations (except indirectly, through the appropriations process). Voluntary patients' fees, on the other hand, are local receipts at Yankton, but fees paid on behalf of patients at 

Redfield also go into the State General Fund. 11 The philosophy behind the present system is undoubtedly the commendable one of maintaining legislative control, which is frequently lost when receipts are "earmarked. However, the 1963 

Legislature did adopt a bill (S.B. 308) which requires all penal and charitable institutions to include anticipated local fund receipts in their submitted budgets, and provides for legislative

appropriation of these funds. With the passage of this bill, the primary argument against the direct flow of patient fees from at least the patients, their estates, and responsible relatives--if not also from the counties--to the institutions seems to have been obviated to a degree. 

The principal argument in favor of payments directly to the institutions is that advanced by the South Dakota Association for Retarded Children: such channeling will improve parent and guardian relationships with the institutions (and, secondarily, it would probably result in a greater percentage of voluntary remissions of fees).

Part III ADMINISTRATION OF THE SURPLUS COMMODITY PROGRAM INTRODUCTION 

The fertileness of the soil and the ingenuity of the men who work the soil have not been unmixed blessings to the United States. The two factors have been a blessing, quite naturally, in that the farms of the nation have no trouble producing more than the food necessary to feed the population of the country, but the disposition of the surplus is a burden. The government has undertaken programs to see to it that the surplus of food is not wasted but is put to good use, and in a manner beneficial to the sector of the population unable to secure food at the marketplace. 

While for years, for instance, the federal government has sent surplus foodstuffs overseas under the authority of Public Law 480, pre-dating this venture was the start of the local surplus commodity program. The first federal aid to the states in the form of surplus food for the needy started in the depression years of the 1930's. The program expanded steadily between 1936 and 1941, but came to a halt during World War II as the surplus disappeared and wartime rationing was instituted. 

Following the war, the Department of Agriculture once more undertook programs of donating surplus foodstuffs for state distribution to needy persons and institutions who qualified for the free government food. Substantive action was taken by Congress in the Agricultural Act of 1949 which authorized the Commodity Credit Corporation 

...to donate such [surplus] commodities to the Bureau of Indian Affairs and to such State, Federal or private agencies as may be designated by the proper State or 

Federal authority and approved by the Secretary [of Agriculture], for use in the United States in nonprofit school-lunch programs, in nonprofit summer camps for children, in the assistance of needy persons, and in charitable institutions, including hospitals, to the extent that needy persons are served... 

Legislation since 1949 has placed the administration of the federal program in the Agricultural Marketing Service of the U. S. Department of Agriculture, and congressional action in 1958 was intended to strengthen the provisions under which surplus commodities are distributed. At that time guidelines were laid down by which the recipient agencies were to administer the program at the state level and in turn the state agencies concerned were to establish an effective system of distribution to the decentralized units which are to administer the program at the local level.

ADMINISTRATION OF THE SURPLUS COMMODITY PROGRAM IN SOUTH DAKOTA Historical Development 

Prior to World War II, at which time the surplus food programs, for the most part, came to a temporary halt, the distribution of surplus commodities in South Dakota was handled by the Department of Public Welfare. But, at the first meeting of the South Dakota 

Legislature following the war (the Thirtieth Legislature in 1947) consideration was given to S.B. 115, which would have provided for the establishment, operation and maintenance of a nonprofit school lunch program, with a $10,000 appropriation for administration. 

Authorization was to be given to the State Superintendent of Public Instruction to administer the school lunch program with the appropriated funds. Consideration of the 1947 school lunch bill pre-dated action by the U. S. Congress, and although an amendment was added to the effect that no state funds were to be spent prior to the completion of congressional action on a federal school lunch program, S.B. 115 was killed on the floor of the House of Representatives. 

Two years later, following congressional passage of a school lunch bill, the Legislature adopted Chapter 300, Session Laws of 1949, an appropriation measure which allowed the Superintendent of Public Instruction $5,000 annually to administer the school lunch effort in the state. There was no provision in the measure calling for the Superintendent to administer any program involving commodities from the Department of Agriculture except those specifically earmarked for the school lunch program. 

In 1951, however, the Legislature passed Chapter 358, Session Laws, "An Act to Appropriate Funds for the Operation of a School Lunch Program and the Direct Distribution of Surplus 

Commodities in South Dakota out of Funds Provided by the Federal Government and Placing the Administration of the Program Under the Superintendent of Public Instruction. The reasoning behind placing the administration of all surplus commodity programs 11

under the Department of Public Instruction, as implied in the title of the 1951 Act, seems to have been that, as the Department was already engaged in administering the school lunch program--so closely related to the distribution of other surplus commodities, that Department was the most logical agency in which to place this function. 

It goes without saying that the future size and scope of the welfare programs which were placed under the Department of Public Instruction in 1951 were not foreseen. NATIONAL GUIDELINES FOR COMMODITY DISTRIBUTION 

The program of surplus commodities is unique in that it involves, with virtually no strings attached, a gift of millions of dollars worth of food annually from the federal government for state dispensation. There are no matching funds called for; the only financial obligation on the part of the agencies distributing the government food is that they bear the costs of transportation of the food within the state from centrally located government warehouses. 

The distributed product is not money, yet to those who need it, it has the value of money. Hence the donor, the federal government, has quite rightly set down certain guidelines for the state agencies concerned with distributing surplus commodities to follow in an attempt to ensure that the purposes for which the law was enacted will be met. (23 F.R. 7982; Part 503,

Sect. 1 - 12) The federal law is clear--there is no equivocation on major points. Under the operating agreement between the 

U. S. Department of Agriculture and the South Dakota Department of Public Instruction, there is the statement that either agency may terminate the agreement on thirty days notice. It is also provided that ...upon receipt of evidence that the terms and conditions of the Regulations (governing the contract) have not been fully complied with by the Distributing Agency, USDA may terminate this Agreement immediately by notice in writing to Distributing Agency...

As a minimum, according to the Agricultural Marketing Service, each state distributing agency, in order to qualify for participation in the program, must:

(1) (2) (3) (4) (5) estimate the number of needy persons who will qualify for commodity gifts send to the Department of Agriculture the name of the agency or the agencies which will be responsible for certification of and distribution of the surplus commodities send to the Department of Agriculture a plan of the manner in which commodities will be distributed, including, but not limited to, storage and distribution facilities to be used and the method of financing define the criteria to be used in determining the qualifications of recipients submit provisions for reviewing the certification of recipients to determine any change in economic status which would affect continued eligibility (6) provide for identifying each person certified (7) (8) (9) (10) assure that welfare grants or similar aid shall not be reduced because the recipient is receiving surplus commodities as well assure that the distribution of commodities shall not be used as a means for furthering the political interest of any individual or party, and that there shall be no discrimination because of race, color or creed assure that recipients will not be required to make payments of any sort in connection with the receipt of commodities, and that they shall not be solicited in connection with the receipt of commodities for voluntary cash contributions for any purpose send to the Department of Agriculture a plan for the manner in which the distributing agency plans to supervise the program. 

In connection with the last requirement, the federal law declares that the state agency must submit its plans for supervising its program with regard to quantities requested, distribution and distribution charges, use of funds accruing in operation of the program, processing and labeling of commodities, personnel used in administering the program, facilities used in the program, the maintenance of records which are subject to federal audit at the discretion of the U. S. Department of Agriculture and the filing of monthly reports with the area office of the 

Agricultural Marketing Service.

The federal statute indicates that state agencies who administer the surplus commodity programs are to set further standards than those required by the federal government to assure that local distributing agencies will perform their tasks efficiently and equitably. Recommended especially were even more detailed outlines to be used in deciding the eligibility of the applicant for the surplus food. 

It is clear that the federal government has taken action in an attempt to ensure that the distribution of surplus commodities will take place in a fair and judicious manner; governing rules have been set down, but the enforcement of administrative rules does not always follow as a matter of course. 

STATE GUIDELINES FOR COMMODITY DISTRIBUTION 

In response to the federal requirement that each participating state agency submit a plan of operation prior to receiving surplus commodities, the School Lunch Division of the Department of Public Instruction compiled a state plan of operation. 

There was created within the School Lunch Division the position of Supervisor of Commodity Distribution, to work under the State Director of School Lunches, for the dispensing of surplus commodities. Agreements have been established with the Boards of County Commissioners in each county which chooses to enter the welfare program; the county commissioners provide the funds for the administration of the program in their jurisdictions--the costs of storage, transportation and salaries for the personnel necessary to run the program in the county. 

Other features of the state plan of operation with regard to the dispensing of commodities to welfare cases include statements, in much the same language as the federal requirements, requiring appropriate identification procedures (however, a system of authorized proxies who may pick up the recipient's food if there is a signed authorization has been set up), certifying that welfare grants will not be diminished because the recipient received commodity aid as well, assuring that no recipient will be solicited for any voluntary cash contributions for any purpose in connection with the receipt of commodities, ensuring that there will not be discrimination in donated commodities because of race, color or creed, and prohibiting the use of commodities for furthering the political cause of any person or group.

There are a number of regulations with regard to the handling and distribution of the commodities that enter the state. On a bi-monthly basis, offers are mailed from the 

School Lunch Division to the recipient agencies, informing the local agencies of the produce available. In return the local distributor sends in his request--based upon the total number of welfare cases under his jurisdiction.

Insofar as it is possible to meet all requests, this is done; the recipient agencies make arrangements for the delivery of their commodities, and distribution, and the costs of transportation are paid by the recipient agencies. Records of all commodities handled, complete with receipts signed by the eligible recipients of the surplus food, are to be maintained by the distributor. Implicit in the state plan of operation is the provision that the School Lunch Division may at any time cancel the delivery of surplus foods to any distributing agency if such a step is deemed necessary to prevent an inequitable distribution of foodstuffs. However, the School 

Lunch Division has never ruled that a distributing agency may no longer receive surplus commodities due to faulty distribution practices.

The current state plan of operation under which guidelines to be followed in commodity distribution have been set down by the School Lunch Division, took effect May 1, 1963. Prior to the issuance of commodity quotas for the month of August, 1963, the Director of the School Lunch Division re-emphasized the urgent need for the local distributing agencies to adhere to the state plan of operation by sending to the chairman of each county board which supervises the distribution of commodities to welfare recipients a statement to be signed and returned to the School Lunch Division. The signed statement was to certify that the county commissioners have read and understood the new state plan of operation the commissioners have taken new applications of need for all eligible recipients since April 15, 1963 the commissioners have certified no one to receive surplus food who has not submitted documentary evidence substantiating the truth of statements in their application of need the commissioners assume liability for the payment of commodities issued to recipients who are not eligible to receive food the commissioners understand that violation of any of the terms of the agreement and failure to comply with the rules and regulations could result, not only in the payment for food distributed to ineligible persons, but may also result in the end of the county's participation in the program. 

PROBLEMS OF COMMODITY DISTRIBUTION 

General

In spite of the fact that there are clearly written provisions by both federal and state authorities which are intended to ensure a judicious distribution of commodities,

it has been evident that irregularities have occured in the administration of the program. The Director of the School 

Lunch Division has been quoted as stating that "quite a scandal" would result from the making public of the Division's records of administration of the program at the local level. 

Each year federal authorities enter the state to audit books and observe on a first hand basis the distribution of surplus commodities. In the past U. S. Department of Agriculture auditors have not encountered substantial irregularities in commodity distribution to schools or state institutions, but they have cast an appreciable amount of doubt on the distribution and operational practices of the welfare programs, most especially those at the county level. 

There appear to be two general aspects to the problems involved in the operation of the surplus commodity program in South Dakota--the determination of eligibility and, in a broad sense, the actual distribution of the foodstuffs. 

These topics will be treated in separate subheadings. 

Determination of Eligibility 

Clearly, aside from consideration of the methods used in handing out surplus commodities, the essential point is the determination of eligibility--who is eligible to receive government surpluses? The state plan of operation is quite explicit in this regard. auto- Eligibility is determined in one of two manners: matically eligible for receiving surplus commodities are households in which all or some members are receiving benefits under programs of old-age assistance, aid to dependent children, aid to the blind and aid to the permanently and totally disabled provided for in the Social Security Act or under local welfare programs of "poor relief". Eligibility of persons in these so-called public assistance households is automatic, and there is seldom question as to the validity of an eligible on this list. In the past, however, there has been some confusion in that some have misinterpreted the qualifications of eligibility to read that all persons receiving Social Security benefits of any sort may apply for surplus commodities. 

On the other hand, if persons of a household are not receiving public assistance of one form or another, the determination of eligibility is made by the total monthly income of all the members of the household and the liquid assets claimed by the members of the household. The eligibility of non-public assistance household to receive surplus food is determined by the county officials holding jurisdiction over the applicants and, although there are explicit limits to the amount of income and liquid assets that are allowable, it is this section of the program that has come in for a great deal of criticism.

The state plan of operation places the following limitations on the amount of total (combined) monthly income that a non-public assistance household can earn after deductions and remain eligible for the receipt of surplus commodities: 1 person 2 persons $ 75 

6 persons $ 275 125 305 11 175 11 340 11 210 11 370 ?? 240 10 11 400 

Over 10 an additional $30 per person

Further, allowable liquid assets are $500 for an individual, $1000 for a family of two or three, and an added $100 for each additional member of a family over three in number. 

The state plan of operation directs county officials to demand verification of applicants claims by documentary evidence such as payroll slips, income tax withholding statements, receipts for extra expenses which would allow a recipient to earn a greater income than the stated limitation, and cancelled checks. Also, county officials are directed to make inspections of the living conditions of applicants and to make collateral contacts with banks, employers, neighbors and creditors. Claims of welfare recipients are to be checked out with the proper authorities.

The state plan of operation permits county certifying officials to waive the total income or liquid assets limitation in cases involving "extreme hardship"--where medical expenses, rent or other legal obligations made it impossible for a household to maintain a reasonable standard of living within the limitations imposed regarding total income. 

The county officials, thus, are given rather wide leeway in determining who shall and who shall not be eligible to receive surplus commodities. In many counties, a certification of eligibility must be signed by only one county commissioner. 

The Director of the School Lunch Division has recommended that all certification in the future should be done at a meeting of all the county commissioners at which each applicant is to appear in person.

It is explicit that the burden of proof of need is on the applicant, and that the method by which information which forms the basis for certification of eligibility has been verified must be made a matter of record. All pertinent information is to be open to state or federal authorities; in this area also the federal auditors have been less than pleased with the operation of the program in South Dakota.

A look at the federal audits of individual county programs in the state in the last two years reveals the following: 

in some samplings, more than five per cent of the people receiving commodities were ineligible due to excessive incomes and/or liquid assets in some cases, application forms had not been completely filled out (one form arrived minus the name of the applicant); the master record of eligibles to be maintained by county officials were incomplete in some vital respects there was inadequate reviewing of eligibility qualifications with regard to income in some cases; some cases had not been reviewed prior to the receipt of commodities by the applicant some recipients received more than the maximum amount of foodstuffs per person set by law; in other cases the recipients of surplus commodities were clearly ineligible to receive the government food--in one county the county officials took it upon themselves to distribute surplus commodities to summer camps and to the Boy Scouts.

The determination of eligibility, in conclusion, has raised many serious questions regarding the administration of the program in South Dakota; although there are clear-cut lines as to who does and who does not qualify for eligibility certification, there is also ample reason to believe that determinations of eligibility are not in all cases based entirely on need.

Operational Practices in Commodity Distribution 

While determination of eligibility plays a vital part in the operation of the surplus commodity program, no less significant are the procedures used at the county level to assure that the foodstuffs are received by the rightful recipients. Again, the state plan of operation--in line with federal requirements--does not equivocate. 

Adequate warehousing, providing both freezer and cooling storage and dry storage is to be provided by the local agencies; the cost of storing, handling and transporting all commodities distributed through a particular jurisdiction is borne by the recipient agency. Storage and handling charges are governed by the prevailing warehouse and transportation rates established by the South Dakota Public Utilities Commission. 

In addition, the recipient agencies are to maintain complete records of the kinds and quantities of each commodity received each month, and distributed each month, a record of eligible recipients and a distribution record showing the kinds and quantities of commodities donated to eligible recipients each month.

Full accountability for the dispensing of commodities is to be maintained; to accomplish this, signed receipts for the transfer of all foodstuffs from the warehouses are to be recorded. Identification cards for all eligible recipients are to be issued; they are to be presented at the warehouse as a pre-requisite for receiving commodities. The head of the household receiving surplus commodities, however, may designate a proxy to pick up his commodities, but such authorization must be dated in writing and must be retained by the distributing agency and kept on file. 

Federal auditors, after reviewing the operations in

South Dakota's county distributing centers, cited the following as being among the most prevalent abuses in the administration of the program: 

incomplete records are kept in a great many instances; there is not kept an adequate accounting of the total number of certified eligibles, of commodity inventories on hand, or of the dispersal of the government food from the warehouses large quantities of food have not been accounted for as there is no evidence of receipt by an eligible person in some cases there was no written agreement between the county distributing agency and the commercial firms who were charged with receiving, storing and distributing food some storage areas were inadequate and did not meet the minimum standards necessary for the preservation of the commodities in some cases, authorization for someone other than the intended recipient to pick up the food for the recipient was not required irregularities often occured in the distribution of food; in some cases because of inconvenient distribution quarters recipients would sign for their commodities and have them delivered at their home later--in other cases food was delivered to the recipients residence but he was not required to sign for it.

Irregularities in the storage and distribution practices of the county agencies have focused much attention on this aspect of the South Dakota commodity program, and the dissatisfaction of the federal officials with this program at the county level has been expressed.

SUMMARY 

There are two overriding reasons for the concern that has been aroused over the administration of the surplus commodity program. Primarily, there is a moral issue involved which reaches at the very heart of the program. The surplus food undertaking has resulted from the farmers' ability to over produce and the federal government's attempt to dispose of surpluses in a manner both humanitarian and at the same time practical. The food is intended for state tax-supported institutions and, in a more personalized sense, those people who, for any number of reasons, are most in need of it. 

The program thus stands in the best tradition of American self-help, but the high ideals which served as the impetus for the undertaking of such a program can be scuttled if administration at the local level to the people who need the food is less than totally judicious and equitable. 

Almost two and a half million dollars worth of food was distributed in South Dakota in fiscal 1963, and serious doubt has been raised over the eligibility of some of the recipients and the practices used by the local agencies to distribute the commodities. Insofar as serious doubt exists about certain aspects of the administration of this program, this topic is of prime concern to the South Dakota Legislature.

Less aesthetic than the moral issue involved is the very real fact that the federal government possesses the grounds to end this state's participation in the surplus commodity program at any time. Federal audits of the South Dakota program abound with criticism of many current practices and, as mentioned earlier, the U. S. Department of Agriculture may terminate its working agreement with the School Lunch Division " ...upon receipt of evidence that the terms and conditions of the regulations (governing the contract) have not been fully complied with..." 

While strict accountability for the determinations of eligibility and the commodities which are donated to the distributing agencies to be given to those certified eligibles is provided for in state and federal regulations, there appears to be far from strict accountability in practice in many instances. 

While ending the surplus commodity program in South Dakota would remedy the evils of the present situation, it would probably place an unnecessary burden on the people least able to shoulder it.

2. 

The Committee recommends that the 1964 Legislature provide for the collection and processing of fees due the state for the care and maintenance of patients at the Redfield State Hospital and School, with responsibility imposed on the patient, his parents and guardians, if and to the extent that ability to pay exists. 

Appendix IX to this report contains a draft of a bill which the Committee on Medical Aid recommends be passed by the Legislature in order to provide an equitable method for determining financial obligations for the support and care of patients at Redfield State Hospital. At the present time, the greatest percentage of funds for the operation of the Redfield institution is borne by the state through appropriations for the maintenance of operations and the physical plant. 

A certain amount of money is paid to the institution by the federal government for the care of Indian patients. 

The balance of support is made by the counties, at the rate of $25 per month. No responsibility to pay is imposed on the patient or his responsible relatives, although voluntary payments may be made by the patient and his family to the State General Fund. State law presently specifically absolves the parents of a child committed to Redfield from legal responsibility for his care.

The trend in other states appears to be in the direction of imposing the cost for care at state institutions such as the hospital at Redfield, where the ability to pay exists, on responsible relatives. The Committee on Medical Aid recommends passage of legislation designed to effect a similar provision for payment at Redfield State Hospital. It is recommended that patients--or their families--be charged for care at Redfield if, in the opinion of the Board of Charities and Corrections, ability to pay exists.

The Committee on Medical Aid recommends that insofar as the ability to provide for a patient's care at Yankton exists-as judged by the Board of Charities and Corrections--the financial obligation for the care of residents of the institution should be borne by the patient or the parent, guardian or other person legally liable for the support of the patient. Appendix

It further provides that patients who are financially able to do so--as judged by the Board of Charities and Corrections--shall pay the per diem rate indefinitely.

EXPLANATION OF THE PROPOSED SOUTH DAKOTA MENTAL HEALTH ACT, and PROPOSED SOUTH DAKOTA MENTAL HEALTH ACT; A PRELIMINARY DRAFT 

Submitted by the Mental Health Committee of the

CHAPTER III COSTS SECTION 12. 

A) This section replaces Section 30.0212, 30.0605-2, and that part of Chapter 170, Session Laws of 1959, which deals with the cost of voluntary hospitalization. The committee feels that the voluntary patient who is able to do so should pay the full cost of maintenance and treatment; if a patient is unable to pay the full cost he should pay that portion of the cost which he is able to pay and the county should be responsible for the difference; if the patient is unable to pay any of the cost, the county should be liable. The committee feels no patient should be denied hospitalization on a voluntary basis because he is unable to pay. The trend toward voluntary hospitalization is increasing and should be encouraged. The patient is probably less traumatized by voluntary than by compulsory hospitalization. It is economically advantageous to the county if it can be spared the cost of formal commitment procedures. 

B) a) This replaces in part Sections 30.0212 and 30.0605-2.

The present law limits the amount which may be levied against the county for the care at Yankton State Hospital to $35.00 a month and at Redfield State Hospital and School to $25.00 a month. In both cases this is less than the actual cost of maintenance. Under the present plan, the county pays part of the cost and the state pays part of the cost. The committee finds it difficult to understand why money should be taken out of the taxpayer's pocket both by way of the county treasury and by way of the state treasury for this purpose. In the long run it is more economical and less involved if the entire support comes from one source. If it comes from the county, the county is in much better position than the state to collect from the patient's estate, and this should mean an actual saving in tax dollars.

APPENDIX VI (continued) SECTION 11. Commitment Costs. Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board. The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
