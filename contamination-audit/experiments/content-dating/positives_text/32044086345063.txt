PREFACE 

Some years ago there came into the hands of the author a book entitled: "Nineteen-Two in Vermont" written by a

Mr. Mason A. Green of Rutland. It occurred to the author at the time that inasmuch as the political strife and turmoil of 1906 probably exceeded that of 1902, it ought also to be chronicled in print. 

The political campaigns of 1902 and 1906 are of more than ordinary significance because previous to this the Republican nominee was practically certain to be nominated for governor without having to carry out an extensive campaign. 

The Democratic Party has always gone through the formalities incident to having its nominee placed on the ballot, but without realizing its ambition. However, since the State does not have very many large cities, where the Democrats are strongest; the Republicans draw their votes largely from the smaller communities where the people are engaged for the most part in agrarian pursuits. In 1902, the issue was largely over Local Option; whereas the one of 1906 involved largely personalities, in that two such outstanding individuals were aspiring at the same time for the gubernatorial chair. 

During those hectic days the author viewed the spectacle at a rather close range. Whatever events took place at a distance were ascertained, and the outcome of the same is presented herewith. The book, therefore, is in a sense a sequel to "Nineteen-Two in Vermont."

Proctor, Vt. 

May 1944 

Otto T. Johnson

CHAPTER I. 

The Tempest is Brewing 

The political strife of 1906 for the gubernatorial chair in Vermont was probably equal, if it did not eclipse that of 1902. 

Both Mr. Clement and Mr. Proctor had failed in the realization of their ambition in 1902, and consequently new attempts to secure the nomination for governor were made in 1906. After the political turmoil of 1902, it would be natural to expect that the same rivalry for the position of the chief executive of the State of Vermont would be re-enacted for the year 1904, except for the fact that there was an unwritten law or custom, known as the "mountain rule," which has been in force since the Civil War period, to the effect that the governor shall be chosen alternately from the east and west side of the Green Mountain range which divide the state into nearly equal parts. It happened that in 1904 the governorship belonged to the east side, and since both Mr. Clement and Mr. Proctor resided on the west side, they were not eligible for the position.

In the campaign of 1902, prohibition and local option were the main issues at stake, and these had been settled insofar as the Legislature of the State had subsequently enacted local option into law, and consequently other issues were brought up which will be discussed hereafter. 

In the fall of 1904, Mr. Fletcher D. Proctor was nominated by his townsmen as a candidate to the Legislature at Montpelier from the town of Proctor. Following his election, his name was very favourably mentioned by the press generally for the 'speakership of the House of Representatives, a position which he held in 1902. However, Mr. Proctor authorized the announcement to be made that he was not a candidate for the position, and that he would gladly support Mr. Merrifield, if he would accept a re-election.

YEAH It was said that Fletcher D. Proctor impressed himself on the members of the Legislature as one of the men of real strength in that body. The estimate of him, formed by personal contact and observation, was that of a man of ability, and education in practical affairs, plain, direct common sense, and active personal interest in whatever concerned the state of Vermont and its people. There was a certain sturdy homeliness about him which always appealed to those with whom he came into contact.

Since the contest in 1902, both of the candidates were whetting their edges for the contest which was to ensue in 1906.

The annual banquet of the Vermont Fish and Game 

League in the Fall had become an occasion for a" coming-out party" for the next aspiring governor of the state, when he made the announcement or debut" of his candidacy and some of his policies, or else when the next governor would be groomed for the position by his admiring friends. This banquet had become almost a State institution. 

A few days before the banquet was held on September 

8, 1905, the following public letter was sent out by Mr. P.

W. Clement: 

To the Republican Voters and Citizens of Vermont: 

I have been asked to be a candidate for the nomination for the office of governor at the next republican State convention. The time seems now at hand when it is fair to my friends to state my position. I answer the question: Yes, I am a candidate.

I did not care to enter into a purely personal canvass for the governorship, but if my views on state affairs, which are fairly well known, meet with the approval of the voters, I should consider it an honor to be selected to carry them into effect so far as a Vermont governor can do.

As a result of discussion and investigation we are upon an eve of a complete change in the business methods of our State departments. If this change is properly made retrenchments of State affairs will naturally follow. No private corporation would for a moment think of adopting the Vermont plan of transacting its business. When this change was first suggested it was ridiculed in some quarters but now the question is how best to effect a remedy.

The large amount which would be annually saved to the State when a proper system of purchase, supervision and accounting is inaugurated will be available for internal improvement and I know of none that can

II be made with greater benefit to all classes than a system of permanent roads opening up our mountain regions to the main thoroughfares of travel.

I believe the local option license law, which prohibits, when the people so vote, or brings in a revenue, if the people so elect, should be retained and perfected. 

It is not my ambition or purpose to build up or foster any political machine, but if I am nominated and elected, I shall undertake to give the State a business administration and the taxpayer a square deal. (Signed)

PERCIVAL W. CLEMENT. 

Rutland, September 7, 1905.

The banquet of the Vermont Fish and Game League was held on September 8, 1905, at the Hotel Champlain at Bluff Point, N. Y. Neither one of the gubernatorial aspirants was present. The paramount question, aside from the banquet itself, was the governorship. 

It had been understood for some time that the Hon. 

Percival W. Clement would be a candidate for the governorship, and it had been equally well understood that several republicans of prominence in as many counties were determined to leave no stone unturned to prevent his nomination, owing to the fact that it was felt by many that Mr. Clement had betrayed himself in 1902 by bolting the convention and forming a separate party, with a tendency to weaken the republican party.

For a considerable period these men had been making endeavors to unite on some candidate. An attempt was made to induce Dr. W. Seward Webb to allow the use of his name, but while he did not even care to issue any statement in relation to the matter, he declined to go into the fight for the honor of leading the republicans of Vermont in the coming campaign. There were those present who foresaw the time when Dr. Webb would be a candidate, and it was thought that he would rather wait than to enter a contest for the office at that time.

An attempt had also been made to induce Dr. Webb to support Mr. Clement as a candidate for the purpose of trying

to beat Mr. Proctor; but the attempt was a failure, a fact which, it was claimed, would be likely to be remembered, at the right time.

The name of ex-Governor Carroll S. Page was also canvassed as a possible candidate for the governorship against Mr. Proctor by a few men, but after consultation with regard to the effect which his candidacy for a second term in the governor's chair might have upon his political future, it was thought that he did not deem it advisable to allow the use of his name in that connection. 

At this point one of the men who had been forced by circumstances to take part in the conference openly announced that he would support Mr. Proctor for the governorship.

The opinion was expressed at the Fish and Game League banquet that the publication of Mr. Clement's letter announcing himself as a candidate was timed so that it might make its influence felt at that gathering. The letter did have an influence, but judging from all accounts that effect was entirely different from the one intended. It fell flat so far as influencing support in favor of its writer was concerned, while on the other hand it influenced some who had held it to be early to press the governorship question, to at once take off their hats and coats for Mr. Proctor.

One man in discussing the letter of Mr. Clement said that he was not only asking the republicans to forget what he had attempted to do with the party four years ago; but he was also inviting the people to take up matters which had already been disposed of. He said the republicans had wiped out the direct tax; they had ripped up State institutions where it was needed; they had put in new men and instituted new measures, and the people of the country might as well try to arraign the republicans in the general government for the abuses which President Roosevelt had remedied and made past issues as to answer to Clement's appeal for support against abuses remedied under Governor Bell. 

One republican said: "I had not intended to go to the 

Fish and Game banquet, but when I saw Clement's letter in the morning paper, and realized that an attempt was to be

made to create sentiment in his favor, I determined to drop everything and be there to work against him." 

Another man who had worked for Mr. Clement in the campaign of 1902 said he had enough, recalling the coldblooded manner in which Mr. Clement had treated Judge 

Merrill, one of his most active supporters in Rutland, and other former adherents. 

A former license man said Clement's letter was not unexpected, "But I shall not support him and I think he will be surprised to find how many supporters he has lost."

A supporter of Mr. Proctor was enthusiastic over the showing made at the Fish and Game banquet. He said: "Clement's letter could not have come out at a more favourable time for us. If it had been issued later, we might have wondered in relation to the effect of the announcement of his candidacy, but now we have been in touch with men from all parts of the State, and know that he is a dead one." 

Another man thought that it was "pretty cheeky" for

Mr. Clement to ask for the nomination of the very party he tried to repudiate so recently, and said he should do works mete for repentance before being put at the head of the party. 

There was some talk that a third candidate might be brought into the field, and that Mr. Clement was merely being used to checkmate Mr. Proctor in Rutland County to let another man get the plum, but when it was recalled how Mr. Clement turned on McCullough, after certain men interested in McCullough had started Mr. Clement in order to head off Mr. Proctor in Rutland, the opinion was indulged in that no leader in any county would be likely to care to repeat McCullough's experience by figuring as a dark horse, or a tail to the Clement kite.

A republican expressed his pleasure in the fact that it would be impossible for Mr. Clement to rely upon the democrats to help him to carry the caucuses that year on account of the new primary law, which compelled the democrats to keep out of republican caucuses.

The different sentiments expressed at the banquet, as well as on the trip to and from the banquet, and at the hotel, seemed to be overwhelmingly for Mr. Proctor.

On April 3rd the committee (Vermont Federation of 

Labor) had word from the Clement bureau that Mr. Clement was absent on business, and shortly after it received the following personal letter from the candidate himself, in reply to some pertinent questions submitted to him by the Vermont Federation of Labor.

A similar set of questions was sent to Mr. Fletcher D. 

Proctor, but they were ignored by him. 

P. F. McCarthy, Esq. 

Alexander Ironside, Esq. 

Barre, Vt. 

Rutland, Vt., April 16, 1906. 

Dear Sirs: I am in receipt of your letter of the 30th ultimo, in reply to which has been delayed on account of my absence from home. 

I agree with you that candidates for office should declare themselves upon the important questions of the campaign, and that I have undertaken to do. I have declared myself in favor of a retrenchment in state expenses, for a better administration of state finances to the end that the people may have full value for the money they that spend. 

That, and the building of good roads and such changes in our tax laws as will make the burden of taxation bear more evenly upon the shoulders of all, seems to me to be the most important questions at the present time, because they affect all alike, the laborer, the farmer, the merchant and the manufacturer. The matters which you call to my attention, on the other hand, are intended to benefit one class of society, and it seems to me, are of secondary importance to those questions which are vital to us all. Nevertheless, questions, such as you ask, which call for what is known as class legislation, are important and interesting, and 

I am glad to give you my ideas upon them. 

You ask:

First-If elected as governor of Vermont, will you pledge yourself to use your utmost endeavor to secure the passage of a weekly payment bill along the lines of that presented at the last session of the Vermont Legislature?

I am not inclined to favor laws of this character which undertake to interpose the hands of the government to settle matters of private interest. I regret the tendency of legislation towards paternalism. Let us have general laws, as few as possible, well enforced, applying equally to all, to the end that each individual may enjoy the greatest personal liberty consistent with the rights of others. The question of the time of payment should be settled between the employer and the employee, just as the question of wage is. When the measure was in the legislature at the time, I was a member of the House, I opposed it so far as it related to railroads. The railroads of this state are not the great rich corpora-

tions that some people think they are. The roads are poor and expensive to maintain and operate, and give little or no returns to the stockholders. A weekly payment bill would mean great additional expense which must be met by an increase in railroad tariffs or decrease in railroad service, neither of which would be satisfactory to the public, and further, weekly payment would be a hardship to many railroad employees themselves who live far from the center where payments are made.

Second-Will you take the same action towards the passage of an employers liability act?

We never have had an employers liability act in this state, but have depended upon the common law. I don't know to what extent you propose to go in making employers liable for accidents to employees, but it seems to me that the common law, which makes the employer liable for his own neglect, and which has become the law after ages of thought on this subject has been applied to limitless experiences, is the safest law and the wisest law for all people to adhere to. I can see no good reason for a change. 

Third. Do you favor giving public employees an eight-hour day? 

Why give employees an eight hour day? Neither you nor I are limited by law in our labor to eight hours. The employees of this state are not overburdened in regard to the hours of labor and it would be unwise at this time when we are trying to reduce expenses of state government to needlessly increase them in this way. If elected governor, my influence would be to have the employees of the state give such proper service as the employees of other business do. The state employee should be required to perform his full duty and should not be allowed to consider his job a soft snap. Such service is for the interest of every individual in the state and not in the interest of any one individual parinticular.

Fourth. Do you favor the creation of a Bureau of Statistics and 

Labor giving competent members of organized labor majority representation on such a board?

The State has got more commissions now than we know what to do with. In 1870 all the commissions of this state cost about $4,300. 

In 1904 the expense had gone up to over $90,000. I would try to obtain such statistics as are necessary without the installation of another commission or bureau. 

Fifth.-Will you endeavor to have enacted into law a bill eliminating convict labor in our penal institutions, whenever such labor enters into competition with free labor?

Convicts must be employed. It would be inhuman to confine them in idleness. They must be maintained. I know of no reason why the 

State should not exercise the same care in administering its affairs that a wise business man would do. The labor which our convicts perform must enter into competition with free labor to a certain extent, but it is so small in Vermont that it practically cuts no figure, and unless some other provision could be made which would be equally advantageous to the convicts and to the State, I should not be willing to change.

Sixth.-Will you favor the endorsement of a state law providing for the appointment of practical mechanics to act as inspectors for all plumbing and steam fitting as a safeguard to public health? Rules for the laying of pipe and the making of sewer connections are to be provided for in this bill. 

I entirely agree with you that there should be a law providing for the proper plumbing and steam fitting, and for the laying of pipe and the making of sewer connections, as a safeguard to public health, and there should be appointed competent men who thoroughly understand their business to inspect such work. The expense of that inspection should be borne by the people for whom the work is done. Inspectors of national banks are paid in that way for their service in making examinations.

I am in favor of a law preventing the employment of children in factories at long hours of labor, and I am heartily in favor of the enforcement of that law and all others on the statute books. 

I have always advocated the organization of labor. I think that it is best for the country at large. Time and agitation of these subjects will, I believe, bring the employer and employee closer and closer together, so that eventually they will each be able to look at these questions through the same glasses, while as it seems to me, especial legislation insisted upon by one class for its benefit and objected to by another class, will have a tendency to pull the employer and employee away from each other and erect barriers which will prove unfortunate for both. Believe me, when I tell you that I am not unfriendly to labor.

My whole life has been identified with it and so far as I know, our relations have been mutually satisfactory. 

Yours truly,

PERCIVAL W. CLEMENT. 

The following was the letter written by Percival W.

Clement in which he accepted the invitation to run independently for the governorship. 

Rutland, Vt., May 24th, 1906. 

To Edward J. Hall, George H. Dewey, L. S. Knight, S. B. Hutchings, S. J. 

Beatty, L. H. Lombard, W. H. H. Blow, Frank Wilcox, H. C. Soper, E. B. Walker, C. L. Hathaway, G. T. Shanks, F. B. Maxham, Ŵm. 

S. Eaton and others.

Independent Voters of Vermont.: 

I am in receipt of your request to me to run as an independent candidate for Governor of the State, more extensively signed, you tell me, than any request of a similar character ever before in the State of 

Vermont. 

In the campaign of 1902 the License-Local Option party demanded a caucus law. The legislature which was elected that year failed to pass

one, but the legislature of 1904 enacted a cunningly devised law, which put in operation now for the first time, seems to operate in a way calculated to perpetuate the power of the Republican machine indefinitely. 

I will not at this time discuss the merit of that law or attempt to give the reasons for its failure. The fact that it is distrusted and ignored by a large majority of the voters in all parts of the state, so far as I know, has a majority of the voters registered, and in some towns the proportion of voters declaring their party affiliation is only one-fourth of the total vote. Under these conditions it is apparent that the nominations made through such caucus action will not represent the great majority of the voters of Vermont.

I believe that every man's vote should have its full weight and value in making nominations for office, as it does in electing candidates for office. Yet under present conditions this cannot be, and as I understand it, it is for the purpose of enabling every Vermonter to record his judgment upon the questions which have attracted so much public attention during the past few months that you have asked me to stand as an independent candidate for the office of Governor of this State. 

So understanding the motive which prompts your action, I accept the high commission you tender me, and will give my best effort to accomplish the changes in state government you so earnestly hope for. 

Last fall, when I announced in a public letter my determination to contest the Republican nomination in Republican caucuses, it was not supposed that the caucus law would be repudiated by the people in a way it has been. That fact has completely changed the conditions, while the demand for some action looking towards improvement in state government is greater and more insistent than ever. In view of these facts, I cannot consent to have my name presented to the Republican state convention.

We cannot look to the machine which is now in control for improvement. It offers no change except from bad to worse, which always comes with a maladministration of public affairs. The men who are in control of political power "stand pat" and say to the voter, "What are you going to do about it?" They claim to represent a majority of the Republican party. You and I do not believe it. We know that the Republican party believes in an honest and economical administration of state affairs and that a majority through machine methods, has been denied a fair expression of its wish. We know that in towns oftentimes a 

Republican majority has put Democrats in office when it was understood that such Democrats were better fitted to adminster the affairs of that office than the Republican candidate; and vice versa. Democrats do not hesitate to support those of other political faith, if they believe it for the interests of good government. The best Republican and the best Democrat is he who can and will rise above party when occasion demands it. We believe that this is the occasion which demands patriotism rather than partisanship. 

With you I will join in an effort to turn out the Ring master who uses public office for graft and to perpetuate his own power, and I will join with you to install those who will adminster government for the

benefit of the governed. I will join with you in repealing laws that are obnoxious to the people, and enact such as will in a measure relieve us of our burdens and make those burdens which are necessary bear equally upon the shoulders of all. I will join with you in such legislation as will attract capital to our state and increase the value of all we now have here. I will join in inviting people from abroad to cast their fortunes with us and offering opportunities to our young men so that they will remain here rather than seek fortunes elsewhere. 

The issues of the campaign are all of home importance. The results affect our daily lives and daily efforts. No national questions are involved, but although our cause only affect our own homes, it is nevertheless good, and therein lies the strength of it. We can confidently appeal to all men of all parties and ask them to give our measures thoughtful consideration and support. From this moment let us all move forward to the conflict joined by a common sentiment and a mutual purpose. Let every man of the thousands who have signed this request become an aggressive worker for the general good. With such an army, success is assured from the start. 

Very truly yours,

PERCIVAL W. CLEMENT. 

The determination of P. W. Clement to be a candidate for the governorship on an independent ticket demonstrated conclusively that he had lost whatever hope he had entertained of securing the gubernatorial prize through the Republican party. When the "Bennington idea" was launched at the place which bears its name, after a conference in Rutland in which the petioneer and the petionee were parties, everybody knew that Mr. Clement had given up hope of defeating Fletcher D. Proctor in the state Republican convention and would have to look elsewhere for his support. 

He had gone to the extreme length of supporting in 1904 the republican organization, which he had denounced as the "machine" in 1902, and which he again denounced in a similar manner in 1906. But the republicans were practically solid in their opposition to his candidacy, and so the desperate "Bennington Idea" was incubated, with the fatal effect politically to both the hatcher and the hatchees. 

About the latter part of April a petition was originated in Bennington asking Mr. Clement to run for governor as an independent. Blank petitions were placed in the hands.

of some one friendly to Mr. Clement in many of the towns in the State, and agents were engaged to travel up and down the state to see that proper attention was given to obtaining signatures. 

It appears that the promoters of the scheme spared no exertion in making the petition an altogether spontaneous and overwhelming appeal which could not be ignored. The scheme came to be known as "The Bennington Idea." The object was to place Mr. Clement at the head of an independent movement, which would make him governor, and incidentally "save the state" from republican rule.

The petition, it was conceded by the general public, confirmed the opinion that Mr. Clement had made up to that time but little progress in his candidacy inside the republican party lines. It was the intention of the petition to get Mr. 

Clement to "yield to the demand" and work on independent lines, as being his only hope for ultimate success. 

The petition read as follows: 

PETITION. 

As citizens of the State of Vermont, who love our State, feeling tht its name has been blemished by a shameful mismanagement of state institutions, graft among some of its officers, maladministration and extravagance in its business affairs, due to ring rule throughout; and believing that it is of the utmost importance to the state that its administration should be cleaned, and that good roads, local option and other economic questions demand immediate action; and realizing that no cure can be hoped from existing parties.

We, the undersigned voters of the State of Vermont, hereby ask you that in justice to those who supported you four years ago, and to the people of the State at large, you will run as an independent candidate for governor of the state; organizing state and county tickets, and making a determined effort to end the misrule which has stained the name of our state.

It had been no secret during pre-convention days that the Clement men had been working incessantly for months trying to effect a fusion with the democratic party. They first sought to induce Mayor Burke of Burlington to take the nomination for lieutenant-governor with Mr. Clement at the head of the ticket, and every sort of argument was brought to bear to secure this result. Mayor Burke was shrewd

enough to know that if he became the candidate of his party for governor, it would make Mr. Clement the titular leader of the party, and that in the event of a democratic victory in the presidential election of 1908, he might be in a position to dispense federal patronage in Vermont, and so he persisted in his refusal to fly as a tail to the Clement kite. Later

Clement emissaries turned their attention to Mr. Pape of 

Barre, with the hope that he might be induced to take the second place on the Clement ticket, and that Mr. Burke could be headed off that way, and thus serve to eliminate Mr. 

Burke as a possible factor in the governorship. The persistence of Mr. Burke in standing for the democratic nomination for the governorship, compelled Mr. Clement to enter the field with some uncertainty whether there would be at least three state tickets in the field and with the possibility that the labor men would have another ticket of their own, since they had already repudiated their support of Mr. Clement. 

Moreover, the democrats themselves were sadly divided as to the course that they would pursue, but it was felt that the only possible way to defeat Mr. Proctor was to unite with the independent party and thus obtain a more unified vote than to have it split among three major parties.

CHAPTER II. 

The Republican Convention 

The Republican Convention was held at Montpelier on June 20, 1906. Headed by the Montpelier Military Band, the delegates marched to the armory hall, and promptly at half past ten, the convention was called to order by Thad M. 

Chapman of Middlebury, chairman of the republican State committee. Out of a total of 736 delegates, 717 presented their credentials, leaving only 19 unaccounted for and several of these came later. 

Prayer was offered by the Rev. Dr. H. A. Roberts, pastor of the First Baptist Church at Montpelier. Mr. Chapman announced that the State committee had named W. J. 

Bigelow of Burlington for temporary chairman, A. E. Watson of Hartford for temporary secretary, and E. A. Nutt of Montpelier for assistant secretary. 

On taking the chair Mr. Bigelow said: 

Allow me to thank you most heartily for the honor conferred upon me. I accept with a high degree of pleasure and some sense of confidence because I feel this convention is to be entirely harmonious in its proceedings. The great abundance of good material gives rise to some contests but all the contestants are honorable men determined to be loyal though they cannot all be victorious. The verdict of this convention is already formed. It will be unanimous in its choice of a leader and it is just as strongly determined that the republican candidate shall go before the people this year as the champion of honest, progressive, economical and efficient government.

Vermonters are not merely republicans they are Roosevelt republicans and that means that they are ready to strike at fraud and wrong doing in public and private life wherever it appears. It means the full and faithful performance of duty, no matter what the cost or the sacrifice. It means victory because it is an honest answer to the honest demands of the people.

The record of the republican party in Vermont has been searched in the present campaign as it never has been. But we face that condition in the perfect confidence, because the more it is studied, the more thoroughly will it be understood that the last decade our party has worked for State economy and honesty. In 1898 it was charged that some State officials were too careless in incurring expenses. The republican legislature uncovered the abuse and stopped it. In 1900 it remedied the fee system in the insurance department. In 1902 the republican

legislature began the equalization of taxation by increasing the taxes of corporations and simplified the keeping of accounts of the State. In 

1904 the republican legislature provided for a more thorough accounting in the State monies, the most approved method of securing supplies for State institutions and authorized the most searching investigation into the conduct of our State institutions and State business ever made and the commission appointed to do this work has laid bare the real conditions therein without fear or favor.

We cannot conduct a creditable campaign unless we understand what has already been done, and so I will quote a little about the State expenses. A comparison of the expenses for the fiscal year of 1898-99 with that of 1903-4, the latest for which we have the complete figures, will show that in the whole list of State officers there has been a decrease in expenses. The same is also true of legislative expenses, special appropriations, State printing and in penal institutions. There has been large increases in our educational department work, military department, and board of agriculture in condemning and killing cattle, permanent roads, care of the insane and court expenses. The last item named show the great increase, the figures of the last year being almost $55,000 greater than in the first.

But the republican party of Vermont does not base its claim for recognition on the past alone. It stands ready to enact new legislation whenever the conditions demand it. It has sent its representatives here to-day to instruct the republican legislature and the republican administration of 1906 to continue the work so well begun until our State institutions are purged of evil and made the best to be found in the country, until the voice of calumny shall be silenced by our good works and our beloved State shall be placed in the van of the nation's forces working for a pure ballot, an honorable government and a fearless championship of the rights of good citizenship.

Gentlemen, it is time we begin this work in earnest and I await your pleasure. 

On motion of O. M. Barber of Bennington, the temporary organization was made permanent. 

NOMINATION OF MR. PROCTOR. 

For governor, F. C. Partridge of Proctor nominated. 

Fletcher D. Proctor. Mr. Partridge said in part:

Mr. President and Gentlemen of the Convention: 

We have met under unique circumstances. For some time the consensus of republican sentiment in the State has clearly indicated the results of the nomination which you will make here to-day. The very unanimity of that sentiment has led one who first sought your endorsement to revolt in advance against your action. We are not disturbed

by the peculiar campaign thus threatened for we know that the natural choice of a leader this year by the great body of republicans of the State is based in fact upon the sensible judgment which has always characterized our people.

In this age of progress, Vermont cannot stand still. To a better and greater Vermont we must dedicate our deepest sympathies and our highest efforts. We peculiarly need at this time a wise and sane leader, who will inspire in every department of the public service the application of up-to-date business principles, sound sense and old fashioned honesty. The governor of this State ought to be first of all a true Vermonter, representative of the sterling good sense and sturdy character of her people, in touch with that spirit of progress which characterizes our present age, in sympathy with that conservatism which very properly is natural to our people, and fitted by character and ability to lead, so far as a governor can lead, in every laudable effort along sound lines for the highest development of our State. 

Such is the one in our minds, whose name, because of the, for me, happy circumstance of being his fellow townsman, I am privileged to formally represent. Since we were small boys together, I have been continuously and intimately associated with him. He is more to me then simply a friend and the very strength of my affection for him makes it difficult for me to speak of him as I would and as I ought. 

Before he was 30 years of age he succeeded to the full management and control of a big business. Under his far-seeing plans and progressive management that business has by normal growth more than doubled in size. It is considerably the largest single industrial enterprise in the 

State. It gives employment in the State today of 3,300 men and is carrying one of Vermont's natural products and Vermont's name throughout the world. It has been his great work; it is his great success. Though the relation of employer to large bodies of employees is not an easy one, it speaks for itself of the respect and confidence in which he is held by his immediate neighbors, that of the three times he has been elected by his town to serve in the legislature of 1890, 1900 and 1904, once there was only one vote cast for another candidate and the other two times he was elected unanimously.

He has never permitted the demands of private business to keep him from the full performance of his public duties, however, inconspicuously. For ten years as road commissioner, for twenty years a member of the school board in his community, having charge now of over 500 pupils, he has been schooled, as most of you have been schooled, in the duties and life common to our smaller towns. In them were laid the foundation of much of New England's greatness and in them lies the enduring strength of Vermont today. As secretary of civil and military affairs, thrice a member of the House and once its speaker, and once a member of the Senate, he has had large legislative experience and been in close touch with the interests of the State and her institutions.

His natural talent and business training peculiarly fit him for executive office. Of a progressive nature he is well equipped by breadth

of character and fulness of experience to lead our State along lines of progress and advancement. At the same time such is the soundness of his judgment and his sympathy with the necessarily simple life of a largely rural State that he will not run off after visionary schemes or impractical dreams. In his private business he has demonstrated his great ability, as Speaker of the House of Representatives, his fairness of judgment and uniform tact and courtesy, and in every relation of life his honesty and courage of purpose. Simple and unaffected but always wise and strong, under his leadership and direction the highest interests of the State will be safe-guarded, those things which make for a larger and better Vermont and under all circumstances will be worthily represented. I, therefore, nominate for governor, Fletcher D. Proctor.

MR. PROCTOR'S SPEECH. 

Mr. President and Gentlemen of the Convention: 

I thank you for the honor which you, the chosen representatives of the republicans of Vermont, have this day conferred upon me, the fullest statement of their views as a party must be found in the platform which you will adopt here today, and I doubt not it will be expressive of the good sense and conservative judgment of the best citizenship of our State. Beyond that, however, I wish to avail myself of this opportunity to express my personal views upon some questions of public policy.

Our legislature and judiciary have always been free from the least suspicion of corruption. No State has had a cleaner record. Though charges have recently been made of corruption and extravagance in the administration of our State institutions and the general management of the State's business, a most complete, careful and impartial investigation of them has failed to find the least so-called “graft" or misappropriation of funds, but for the most part disclosed a business like and economical administration. In one institution a great and inexcusable lack of discipline was revealed. Every one directly or indirectly responsible for that should be promptly punished to the full extent of the law. 

I am sure that all true Vermonters are of one accord regarding this. If extravagance or poor management exists anywhere, it should be corrected at once and I believe it is the duty of the governor at all times to assume full authority within the limits of the law for the correction of such abuses in executive and administrative channels. If elected governor, so far as lies within my power the business interests of the State shall be conducted upon up-to-date business principles. Her institutions, for example, should buy their supplies as cheap or cheaper than any individual or corporation, and I should endeavor to see that they did. 

STATE EXPENSES. 

Our expenses to be sure have increased; but that is because the number, extent and scope of the activities of the State for her people have increased. If we expect to hold our own with other States, keep our people at home and draw strangers to our borders, we must keep well abreast of the progress of the age. We cannot mark time, that means falling behind, for surely others are surely advancing. Instead of making less progress we must make more in the future; but let it be done along the line of practical and conservative expansion rather than any radical innovation or parsimonious retrenchment. 

POSITION ON GOOD ROADS. 

Much has been said about the poor conditions of our highways. In some sections many of them are not good and some are very bad. They can and should be better. We are all agreed concerning that. But in our effort to improve them we ought not to be led into higher taxation or a State debt. I am strongly opposed to issuing a State bond for the purpose of building State highways. The condition of our highways at the present time is not bad enough nor the practical advantages to be derived from expensive highways great enough to warrant loading upon the State a debt, nor is it good business to mortgage in advance what someone simply claims can be saved in the administration of our State institutions. Each proposition should stand by itself. We should not deceive ourselves by undertaking any policy with respect to State roads which is not otherwise warranted and which in itself, independent of any question of saving in our State institutions, we should not be willing to undertake.

We are a rural community. Our climate, grades, soil and other natural conditions are such that we have much to contend with, and taxation must not be made excessively burdensome. We have started right and during the first ten years there has been progress in some communities very marked. The present State highway tax by which the large towns in effect contribute some part of their direct highway money to the smaller towns is right in principle, but the method of expending it should and must be decidedly improved. It should continue to be expended by each town, but its expenditure should be under much more complete and rigid supervision and in accordance with a more strictly enforced uniform plan for permanent construction upon roads in the different towns which form parts of selected through highways. State roads should be gradually created out of the State highway money. 

Every dollar of it should be located and expended under State supervision, but it is impossible for one man to properly supervise the expenditure of over one hundred thousand dollars distributed annually in small amounts throughout every town in the State. The State commissioner should give his entire time to outlining the work and to its general supervision, and there should be supervisors under him for each county to designate where the State fund should be expended in each town and closely supervise its expenditure. 

As the method of expending the State highway fund is improved, the State might well add to that fund in so far as that can be prudently done without the creation of a State debt or the levying of a direct State tax. The last two biennial terms, we have been without a direct tax for the support of the State government and we can and should continue this policy. We should so adjust our expenditures and revenues and in every department of the State government practise such economy that this will become a fixed policy of the State. I think some part of the highway fund, and especially if the State contributes to it, should be distributed for permanent work in towns which will pay towards such work a certain percentage of its cost. That system is used in Connecticut and sone other States, and its principle modified to suit our case would tend to increase the construction of permanent roads. 

COURT PROCEEDINGS. 

There should be a thorough investigation of our entire system of court and judicial proceedings with a view of securing, if possible, greater efficiency as well as financial saving. This investigation is especially important in connection with the arrest and trial of persons charged with misdemeanors in the lower courts; and it is my opinion that changes could be made, which would ensure a better and more economical administration of justice in such cases. 

PUBLIC SCHOOLS. 

An important question confronting us is that of the public schools. 

A good public school education is all that the great majority of Vermont parents can give or leave their children. This they can and should give

them, and they will always naturally seek a home where they can best obtain it. I believe in the gradual centralization of rural schools, a higher standard of teachers, a strict requirement of attendance, a rigid enforcement of the child labor law, a gradual introduction in centralized schools of industrial training. I consider, however, the change most fundamental and vital to the welfare of the common schools is skilled supervision. Vermont cannot afford longer to be behind other commonwealths in this respect.

THE LIQUOR LAWS. 

I have always believed in such a law as will to the greatest extent restrict the sale of intoxicating liquor in the State. The most vital thing, however, for the friends of temperance is not so much whether a law by one or another name exists but how saloons-for they are the root of the evil whether existing secretly or publicly-can be most limited and restricted. The present local option law came as a result of a long agitation of the subject. It is not only fair that it should have a further trial; but until then I believe that any agitation for its repeal is not likely to work out any good for the cause of temperance itself, and that those who desire to restrict as far as possible the sale of intoxicating liquor can better work together to accomplish that in their respective communities under the present law. 

A GREATER VERMONT. 

It is a greater and not a new Vermont for which we should strive. 

There is much in Vermont's past; much in Vermont today, that we can ill afford to lose. We have been conservative; situated as we are a conservative but steady progress is the wisest course and in this end will surely bring the best and most lasting results. We should not for one moment defend that which is wrong in principle or in practice in our State government if it exists; nor should we for the sake of an issue, political or otherwise, broadly and generally condemn the past record of our State, thus doing the State great injustice and necessarily bringing her into disrepute from which she must surely suffer. 

Vermont has much to be thankful for and there is every reason why we should take courage and boldly reach out for better and greater things in the future. With material advantage and resources, with reasonable proximity to markets, with improved and improving means of transportation and travel, there is no reason why we should not steadily and surely move forward in every line of progress that tends to make better, more useful and contented people. Let us stop unjust and unnecessary scolding about the past; cease apologizing for our present conditions, and instead cheerfully and proudly recognize and take advantage of these resources and opportunities and unitedly strive for and achieve those things that make Vermont better for Vermonters. 

Our thought and effort should primarily be directed to the accomplishment of such things as will to the greatest degree improve the conditions

of the men and women and children, who are today summering and wintering in Vermont. If we first make Vermont the best possible place to live in our people will quickly recognize this fact and stay at home and strangers will learn of it and come to us. 

We should so shape our counsels here today that we may maintain the honorable reputation of the Republican Party in the State and deserve the fealty and support of all its members. Let us approach the coming campaign determined not only to uphold but to advance the good name of Vermont and the highest welfare of her people. If elected in September such experience, ability and strength as I have shall be devoted to this end."

THE PLATFORM. 

PRAISE FOR PRESIDENT ROOSEVELT. 

First: That we heartily and cordially endorse and commend the splendid administration of President Theodore Roosevelt, and we commend the President as a fearless champion of the welfare of the people. 

He has without fear or favor, uncovered and punished those guilty of malfeasance in public service; he has zealously assailed the violators of the federal statutes; he has promoted friendly relations with other nations of the earth and gained for us a front rank in the world's diplomacy; he has encouraged wise and helpful legislation and has shown his determination to continue to promote protection of home industries, commercial expansion and other national policies that have helped the American people to enjoy prosperity and progress without a parallel in the annals of national development. 

GOVERNOR AND CONGRESSMAN ENDORSsed. 

Second: We commend the administration of Governor Charles J. 

Bell, and renew our expression of hearty appreciation of the highly creditable services in Congress of United States Senators Redfield 

Proctor and William P. Dillingham and Congressmen Kittredge Haskings and David J. Foster.

CIVIC PROGRESS MADE. 

Third: We note with satisfaction the political, financial, and civic progress made, since the adoption of our last platform, by the legislature of 1904. It provided for a thorough and non-partisan investigation of all our public institutions and adopted efficient and economical methods of administration of the State affairs. It provided that accounts for public service should be itemized; authorized the State auditor to prevent unreasonable disbursements; restricted State institutions to the use of one twelfth of their appropriations monthly; it provided penalties for the acceptance of a commission on any purchase of supplies; it required quarterly financial statements by the officers of our public institutions; it required county clerks to make statements of all fees received; it regulated child labor; it limited municipal indebtedness, and otherwise legislated wisely and well. We pledge ourselves to continue our efforts to safe guard the expenditure of the State funds in every way that it can be done consistently with the liberal care for the weak, the depressed, the unfortunate. A careful protection is our first duty. 

HIGHER EDUCATIONAL STANDARDS NEEDED. 

Fourth: We congratulate the State that the last session of the legislature took a forward step in behalf of the education of our children by laying the foundation for a permanent common school fund, by setting apart $240,000 which is now invested, the income thereof dedicated to the use of the common schools. We recommend still further advancement in the education of our children and especially urge the coming legislature to use its best efforts to adopt some means whereby the percentage of illiteracy shall be materially reduced. We recommend that the teachers of the more rural portions of the State, at least, be better qualified and better paid. Let there be a more rigid enforcement of existing laws and a general raising of the standard of education. We advise that consideration be given to the expediency of establishing a system of manual training schools.

CARE OF THE WARDS OF THE STATE. 

Fifth: We endorse the action of that legislature in doubling the appropriation for the deaf, blind and feeble minded. We congratulate the investigating committee for having made its first consideration the moral and physical welfare of the inmates of our State institutions and we urge that the legislature in dealing with these institutions make that consideration paramount. While we urge economy in expenditure be second only to the good of the wards of the State, we do not concur in the political propaganda which makes dollars and cents its sole cry, and has, so far, neither offered nor suggested anything for the welfare of those who by the laws of the State and of humanity are objects of our special care.

Sixth: The searching investigation to which our State institutions have been subjected shows efficient administration of our State affairs.

Whatever there was of weakness and error in the management of those institutions have been laid open to the public and remedied; and we know that at the present time there is no hospital for the care of all classes of insane where the inmates are better cared for than at Waterbury; that in no State prison is the discipline better than at Windsor; and that in no secondary penal institution do better conditions prevail than at Rutland; while the returns received by the State for its convict labor is not equalled, class for class in any other State. 

EVILS OF THE FEE SYSTEM. 

Seventh: We recognize the evil in the fee system of paying public officials particularly in the administration of the penal laws in the minor courts. We believe it is not prudent to entirely abolish the system, for the reason that, to meet the requirements of all towns and cities in the State, it would necessitate the establishment of a large number of new courts with salaried officers. We urge the attention of the legislature to the details of these matters, that remedy of the evil and improvement in procedure may be brought about. We recommend that the law relating to misdemeanors be so changed that the term of imprisonment for misdemeanors shall not be affected by the distance of the committing court from the House of Correction. 

Eighth: We believe that the purity of the caucus should be fully protected by simple legislative measures, and we recommend such endorsements to the present law as will modify the manner in which the voter may announce his political preference.

THE DOUBLE TAXATION PROBLEM. 

Ninth: We deplore both unequal and double taxation as being unjust and in violation of the spirit of the constitutional requirements that each person and corporation shall contribute his proportion toward the expense of government. We recommend that such measures be adopted by the next legislature as will relieve us of these evils. 

A FAIR TRIAL OF THE LOCAL OPTION LAW. 

Tenth: We rejoice that under the local option law the great majority of the people of Vermont have continued to demonstrate their enduring devotion to the sound principles of temperance and sobriety. This law should not be repealed but should be given a further trial. 

THE NEED OF BETTER ROADS. 

Eleventh: We recognize that our highways are primarily for the conduct of the business affairs of the people of the State, that we pledge our adherence to progress in their improvement, in their method of construction and in the continuance of State appropriation therefor. 

We recognize that under the present law some of the funds of the State are wasted in ill directed temporary affairs and upon portions of high-

ways that are not thoroughfares; we, therefore, recommend that the legislature provide some means for the better supervision of the expenditure of this fund and the establishment of lines of highways upon which the same is to be expended, that thereby there may be an increase in the yearly aggregate of permanently improved roads. 

REFORM IN THE JUDICIAL SYSTEM. 

Twelfth: We recommend that in the next legislature careful attention be given to the advisability of reorganizing our judiciary system. 

UNIFORM MUNICIPAL ACCOUNTING. 

Thirteenth: We recommend a uniform system of municipal accounting.

PENAL INSTITUTIONS. 

Fourteenth: That our penal institutions may be made more reformatory we demand that suitable facilities be afforded for the education of the inmates and every opportunity given to become and remain better men and women.

DEVELOPMENT OF VERMONT'S RESOURCES. 

Fifteenth: We are in hearty sympathy with the great battle being fought by the republican party in behalf of the people against the evils of rebates, favoritism and discrimination in interstate commerce. We believe in such State legislation as may be necessary to safeguard our people against similar events in commerce within the State. 

Sixteenth: We rejoice that steps have already been taken by our 

State in the important question of forest preservation. We believe the future of the commonwealth depends in no small way upon the replanting of our waste lands with trees. We believe the State should recognize this fact and that further provision should be made to induce private capital to take up this great work. We request our Senators and Representatives in Congress to urge the passage of the White Mountain forest reservation bill and further to seek national aid toward reforesting the water sheds in our own State which benefit the streams of other commonwealths.

Seventeenth: We believe that the time has come when our people should adopt methodical measures to draw attention to the attractions of our State as a resort, and we recommend legislative action to this end. 

STATE TICKET Commended. 

Eighteenth: The republicans of Vermont have this day nominated for the highest office within the gift of the people a man of marked executive ability and progressiveness in public service, as well as in private enterprise, a believer in the application of thorough business principles, and methods to the affairs of our commonwealth, and a

champion of a greater Vermont. Our entire ticket represents the aggressive younger republicanism of our State, every candidate standing for clean, energetic and efficient public service. With such a leader and such a State ticket, standing for the broadest and most progressive policies, we confidently appeal to the voters of Vermont.

PREAMBLE. 

We, the independent voters of the State of Vermont, assembled today in open convention, hereby affirm and declare: 

That we base our declaration of party principles entirely on State issues, without regard to national platforms or party creeds. 

That we assert our firm belief in a State government "of the people, by the people and for the people," and condemn the practice of party leaders in Vermont who have prostituted the machinery of government to the ends of private gain. 

That we accuse the republican machine of this State in whose hands the responsibility of government has rested for twenty-five years, of

gross mismanagement, and extravagance in the conduct of our public affairs, of a criminal neglect in the control of our State's prison and House of Correction, and permitting the employment of prison labor, the buying and selling of goods and the transaction of a large business in the State's behalf, absolutely without proper oversight and audit, of a failure to enact wise and helpful laws or to enforce those already in existence and of utter unwillingness to undertake progressive legislation of any kind except under the lash of public opinion. 

That we condemn the system of perverse concealment which compelled a private citizen of the State to invoke the costly aid of the courts before being permitted to examine public documents and records; and we submit that the various investigations set on foot by that act have fully demonstrated the need of a rigid and systematic examination of our State institutions and finances by a competent, inprejudiced tribunal, whose transactions shall be fully published and whose recommendations shall be promptly carried out. 

That we deplore the needless multiplication of State boards and commissions and summons the creators thereof to show cause why the public moneys should be withdrawn from the treasury, save and except for the direct and permanent benefit of the public at large. 

That we recite with honorable indignation the increase in State expenses from $262,866 in 1881 to $703,325.18 in 1902 and demand immediate retrenchment, especially in the department of justice, which has increased from $138,952.23 in 1882-4 to $393,251.87 in 1902-4. 

That we denounce the servile and temporizing policy of the republican machine and express our utter disbelief in its promises of amendment as set forth in its recent platform and we point to its inglorious record of inaction and failure for the past twenty-five years as a just criterion of its future. 

That we believe the time has come to rebuke in no uncertain terms the system that allows these conditions to exist, and call upon the free and independent voters of every party to join with us in a united effort to defeat the machine and the leaders which it placed in nomination at the Republican State Convention of 1906.

We favor the use of the labor of the State convicts to produce such articles as are necessary for their own consumption, and for such supplies as the State needs, and, as far as possible, such articles as will not enter into competition with free labor; and we favor the labeling of all goods made in the penal institutions of the State as made in such institutions.

We favor the enforcement of the present laws governing the employment of child labor, with such amendments as may be found necessary to protect to the fullest degree our growing children and oblige all parents and guardians to procure for them the inestimable advantages of our public schools, thus reserving the tender years of childhood for instruction and recreation and laying the foundation for such physical and mental vigor as shall best fit them for the duties of business, home and State. 

15. We express our firm conviction in the personal worth and public integrity of the candidate who has today been nominated by the independent voters of the State of Vermont for the office of governor and we pledge with him our earnest support in the wide measures of reform for which he stands and submit to the voters of our beloved State this our party platform, with confidence in its honesty, pride in its progressiveness and a serene resolution to keep and perform its every pledge and work unitedly for the triumphant election of the leader whom we have today placed in nomination. 

The following resolution was introduced and unanimously carried.

Whereas democratic and independent parties are agreed upon the main issues of the present campaign, we hereby resolve to unite our platforms and candidates. And we, the independent party of Vermont take occasion to recognize the services of the democratic party in the direction of progressive legislation and reform.

Mr. Clement, meanwhile, had entered the hall amidst tumultuous cheering but it was decided that his speech should be delivered in "The Strong" theatre before the joint assemblages. The convention then adjourned, Mr. Clement being escorted to the theatre by Mayor James E. Burke.

CHAPTER IV. 

The Democratic Convention 

The Democratic convention was held on June 28, 1906 at the Armory at Burlington, it having previously been arranged so that the time would coincide with that of the Independent Party, in order that its ulterior motive would be accomplished. The indications on the previous evening were that there would be a fusion and the situation had not changed during the previous day. When the delegates to the democratic convention assembled, the general impression was that fusion of the two parties would prevail and the first test of strength showed such a majority for fusion that there was no further effort on the part of the straight democrats to combat it. 

The convention was called to order at 11:15 by Emory C. 

Harris of Bennington, chairman of the State Democratic committee. He said that while there was some disagreement, as there should be among good democrats, all should work together for the good of the party, and all should go out as a unit and stand by the will of the convention. He announced that the temporary organization would consist of V. A. 

Bullard of Burlington, chairman, H. C. Shurtleff of Montpelier, secretary, Ō. C. Sawyer of Sharon and Edward Mc- 

Guire of West Rutland, assistant secretaries. 

Mr. Bullard made a brief speech of acceptance, thanking the convention for the honor conferred upon him. He said that a few days ago there was a gathering at Montpelier, a most remarkable gathering. It was called a republican convention. They were not there to represent a constituency. 

They did not even represent themselves. They were there at the bidding of a great political machine. Harmony prevailed; but it was not the kind of harmony democrats like. 

It was the kind that obeyed the bidding of the boss. We are here to decide the course we will pursue. We will talk it over and decide to do what is best for the democratic party.

After Mr. Bullard had closed, J. J. Myers of Underhill moved that the temporary organization be made permanent and the motion was carried. 

The seating of the delegates from the town of Rockingham was next brought before the convention. Two delegates were elected from that town, one from the fusion party, and the other from a straight democratic ticket. After a protracted hearing Wednesday night the state committee seated the fusion delegation. A minority report was presented to the convention and E. F. Carmody of Rutland moved that each delegation be seated, and that each delegation be entitled to half a vote. This became the subject for some discussion and the point was raised that either one delegation or the other should be seated as it was a clean proposition that two delegations from the same town had no right in the convention. The action of the state committee was upheld.

A communication from the Independent State convention was received at this point. It was as follows:

In view of the fact that the Independent and Democratic conventions seem agreed on the main State issues of this campaign, we invite a friendly conference between committees from the conventions with a view of uniting our platforms and candidates so that the Independent party and the Democratic party may each have a separate place on the official ballot under their official headings. But the ticket shall be in every other respect the same. Our committee for this purpose awaits your pleasure.

As soon as this communication had been read C. W. 

Melcher of Barre moved that a committee of five be appointed by the chair to confer with the committee from the independent convention upon the question of fusion between the democratic and independent republican parties, and that if in the judgment of the committee fusion is feasible and advisable to report to the convention nominees for office on a fusion State ticket. 

No sooner had this motion been made than Eli H. Porter of Wilmington moved that the resolutions be amended by adding that the committee consider no name as the head of the ticket other than that of a straight democrat.

He thought that if the democrats and independents got together, then a governor could be elected. He believed that the election would be thrown into the Legislature if two tickets were in the field against the republican ticket.

J. J. Monahan of Underhill came out strong for fusion and in closing said that he hoped that the Porter amendment would be defeated.

J. R. Kelly of Burlington moved that all speeches be limited to five minutes and the motion was carried.

He mentioned the fact that Mr. Clement was a republican four years ago, and that two years after, he was defeated by that party, but he was out on the stump again lending his wealth and his voice to the cause of that party. At the end of five minutes Mayor Burke had not concluded and asked for further time. A motion was made to allow him to speak for five minutes more and after two votes, it was carried and he continued.

B. E. Bullard of Hardwick spoke in favor of fusion and after his remarks the vote on the Porter amendment was called for. The vote was by towns, the chairman of each delegation announcing the vote of their respective delegations.

While this committee was out Elisha May, chairman of the committee on resolutions reported. The report of the committee was adopted as the platform of the convention as follows: 

We charge the republican machine of Vermont with continuing the rotten borough system to maintain supremacy. No change can be made until an amendment to the Vermont constitution is enacted, and this cannot be done until 1910 and each ten years thereafter. 

The democrats of Vermont pledge their best effort to secure the submission of an amendment and to change the present unfair and unrepublican method of representation by towns instead of by population.

Only two or three states of the Union, all republican strongholds refuse the right to fair representation. Burlington, by all just rights, is entitled to a delegation of fifteen representatives, while Glastenbury,

St. George, Baltimore, Somerset, Brunswick, Searsbourgh, Brookline,

East Haven, Athens, Granby and Lemington by population are entitled to a single representative. Burlington has over 20,000 inhabitants and a grand list of $13,877,268; the eleven small towns have a population of about 1,450 and a grand list of $896,018.

We invite in the coming election the aid of all Vermont voters who believe as we do, that offices should not be held by families, and that ability should be considered in making nominations for office. 

We recommend the nominees of the convention to all the voters as able, conservative, and worthy of your support. 

Up to a certain period, municipal judges were elected by the people; then the law gave the appointment to the governor. 

In the matter of taxation and the expenditures of taxes, the State is now depriving the towns and cities of a sacred light. Large sums of money are now raised by the State taxation, and expended by officers over whose election neither the State nor the municipalities which raised taxes have any control.

The State levies upon all towns and cities a State highway tax and school tax. This money is paid into the State treasury, and is there divided among the towns, etc., according to the statute then in force. 

The members of the rural districts each year make a mighty effort to increase these taxes.

The following results may be found in the treasurer's report for 1904 and show why the rural districts favor the present plan. Burlington that year paid in the State treasury in school taxes $10,870.40. She was paid back merely $3,502.69. She paid on highway taxes $6,754.63 and drew out $482.47. In other words, Burlington was taxed for schools and highways $17,563.03 and drew back $3,985.16. In other words $13,576.87 of her money was not expended by her municipal officers.

Underhill in the same county paid for schools $418.11 and for highways $261.32 a total of $679.43. She drew back $808.31 and $596.51, a total of $1,404.82; Underhill was bettered $725.39.

As a matter of fact there is no reason why the large towns and cities should be bled this way, for they owe large sums, while most of the smaller towns are free of debt; and the larger towns and cities are in an aggregate otherwise taxed more heavily then are the smaller ones. 

We urge that all moneys raised for the State highways be expended by a competent and trained officer, upon the principal highways of the State, in such a manner as to make all such work permanent. 

THE LOCAL OPTION LAW HAS WON THE RESPECT. 

This has won the respect of its enemies. It is in fact an excellent educator. Its enforcement has been much easier than was the antiquated prohibitory law. But we deplore the refusal of the republican party to allow each town and city to elect their license commissioners. 

We recommend the repeal of the act of 1904, and the enactment of another law giving to towns the choice of license commissioners. 

We commend the prosecuting officers for their activity in the execution of the laws.

JUDICIARY. 

We advocate a trial and law court; if a change is made in the coming legislature, we trust that in the election of judges the minority will not be forgotten.

We also advocate the passage of a statute declaring that no member of the legislature shall be eligible to any office, executive or judicial, during the term to which he is elected. 

NO MONEY UNLESS EXPRESSLY APPROPRIATED. 

For years the officers of this State have been expending money without legal right. Section 17, chapter 2 of the Vermont constitution reads: "No money shall be drawn out of the treasury unless first appropriated by act of the legislature." 

We contend that the coming legislature ought to fix the sum to bẹ given each institution, commission, or executive body. We commend the work done by the auditor of accounts in the direction suggested. 

We call the attention of all voters to this provision of the Vermont constitution, chapter 2, section 26; Whenever an office through increase of fees or otherwise, becomes so profitable as to occasion many to apply for it, the profit ought to be lessened by the legislature. 

John A. Page, when treasurer of Vermont in 1861 to 1865 had $500 a year. It is now $2,700 and clerk hire. The secretary of state in olden times was paid $500 salary. It is now $2,700. The salaries of most of the officers have been greatly increased. 

These salaries are higher in Vermont in proportion than they are elsewhere, and the coming legislature should examine this matter carefully and revise the salary list.

Every legislature regularly increases the number of public offices and secures workers in nearly every town in the State. From only fourteen such public offices in 1870, there are now nearly 200 of them. 

LABOR LAWS. 

We urge upon the coming legislature to repeal section 5041 and 5042 as they punish severely workmen for conspiracy, but do not punish employer. If the statute is to remain, we urge that it be amended as to apply to employers also. We urge the passage of suitable legislation to protect the labor of children and women limiting age and hours of labor. 

FEE SYSTEM. 

We recommend the abolishment of fees in all public offices and the payment of à salary in lieu thereof.

SALARIES AND INCREASED EXPENSES. 

If the management of the State government continues under the few men who absolutely control public offices and appointments, we cannot look for any lessening of public expenses. They are in proportion to our population and wealth, very heavy and are constantly increasing.

They will increase until some strong business man takes the governorship. 

Public appointments are also increasing.

SCHOOLS. 

The public schools must be kept to the highest standard, and compulsory attendance must be obtained. 

CAUCUS LAW. 

The present law is unwise, if not unconstitutional. It was framed in a partisan spirit to obtain partisan end. We recommend its prompt repeal.

CAPITAL PUNISHMENT. 

The last two persons hung in Vermont were women. Man after man who has been duly convicted of murder, received a commutation of sentence. We say the law if it is to be enforced should be equally enforced on both men and women. Mary Rogers did not die in vain, if her death upon the gallows results in the repeal of this law of the dark ages. If the law is suitable then let all executions be public. 

We urge the repeal of the present law of capital punishment. We recommend that the power of pardon be vested in the supreme court. 

Soon after the resolution had been adopted, Mr. Clement made his appearance. He was greeted with cheers. In introducing him Mr. Bullard said the delegates were seeing something happen that had never before occurred in Vermont. 

He said one of the largest republican conventions ever held in Vermont had deliberately come over and sat down with the democrats.

PERCIVAL W. CLEMENT'S ADDRESS. 

Mr. Chairman and Gentlemen: 

The people have had little or nothing to do with nominations for office in the past, or legislation. The party leaders told us sometimes, four or six years ahead of time, whom we must vote for, whom we are going to have in our state offices. Under the crack of the party whip in the hands of the ringmaster, the people of this state have voted like a herd, with the same results that always comes under such conditions,a miserable administration of state affairs, growing steadily worse. 

State expenses doubled, and yet our state institutions run in such a way as to bring a blush of shame to the cheek of every honest man.

You and I believe that the time has come when we must gather together, those who favor honest government-and correct the abuses which have crept into our state system. We stand ready to place country above party. And if in the contest which ensues, we find our hand turned against the republican_ring, it is not our fault. The ring seeks only to perpetuate its power. In its platform it apologizes for the

past, in a feeble, half-hearted way, and makes some vague promises for the future. While the candidate in his speech of acceptance, undertakes to justify the past and promises expansion for the future-whatever that may mean.

We seek a better system. We seek to overthrow the whole system under which these abuses have grown up, and inaugurate in its place a government which will give a dollar of value to the people for every dollar spent. We would not deprive the people of the good things which they now possess, but we would take the money which is now wasted and turn it into channels where we may all benefit from its expenditure. 

We do not claim that in the expenditure of three-quarters of a million a year, Vermont does not get some benefit, but we all know that there is a tremendous amount wasted. It is that waste that we are looking for. Let us take that and apply it where it will do us all good, rather than benefit a little nest of petty politicians.

If in the contest, we look across the party lines and see men of other political faith ready to take hold with us; so much the better. It is by such joint action that wrongs in government are righted. 

I have read with some care the platform which was finally adopted by the Republican party at Montpelier on June 20th. You know that the committee on resolutions drafted a document which condemned the administration of the penal institutions of the state, but afterwards that action was thought unwise, and a change was made so that the plank as it appears in the platform reads as follows:

The searching investigation to which our state institutions have been subjected shows efficient administration of our state affairs. Whatever there was of weakness and error in the management of those institutions have been laid open to the public and remedied. 

The first sentence says that the searching investigation shows efficient management, and the second sentence says that the weaknesses and error have been remedied. Which are you going to believe? Let us see for a minute.

Did you ever read the testimony as it appeared in the newspapers in regard to the conditions in the state prison at Windsor? Did that testimony tend to show efficient management? As Vermonters, were you proud to know what was going on in that institution?

If not, the statement, which admits weakness and error must be correct.

In the next plank, we read: We recognize the evils of the fee system and we recommend that the next legislature give careful attention to the advisability of reorganizing our judiciary system. 

Now that is encouraging. Because the Republican party of this 

State, through its board of managers, has had absolute control of the fee system under which we have lived for fifty years, and for twentyfive years of that time there has been a steady increase in court expenses owing to the evils referred to. 

In 1882-84, for those two years-according to the biennial report, county courts in this state cost $89,418.88 and the administration of justice for the same period cost $138,952.23.

In 1902-04, the same courts in the same counties cost $283,070.27 an increase of $194,000, while the administration of justice for that period cost $393,251.87 an increase of $255,000 in twenty years.

These figures are compiled from the records of the state auditor, by Mr. Pratt, the expert accountant whom the investigating committee have employed. Mr. Pratt is a capable accountant, and I believe his work is right, but the auditors of Vermont in the past have adopted many different systems-many curious systems-of accounting, and I do not vouch for the correctness of their figures nor undertake to justify them. I give them to you for what they are worth.

Now, that increase in court expenses was coming along year by year and every two years the legislature in session, and every two years a whole gang of Republican officials who were in control of affairs were up there in Montpelier, noting or supposed to be noting this increase and paying no attention to it whatever. 

Not until 1902 was there any impression made upon them. In the campaign of 1902 the License-Local Option Party, which was at that time trying to change the law of the state in regard to the sale of intoxicating liquors from prohibition to license-local option, had charged the republican machine with maladministration of state affairs and charged that the expenses of the state had increased without reason and without bringing any adequate return to the people. The Republi can machine at that time was considerable alarmed, and put Martin of Brattleboro on the stump, amd he declared that the state was well run and that there was no expense which was not justified and that he could account for all the increase except $59. He never did it, but just said he could. He claimed that I didn't know what I was talking about. 

That was the attitude of the republican party four years ago. They have now for the first time admitted increase. They admit that there has been error and weakness in the management of the state institutions. 

They admit that the fee system is evil, and they recommend that the next legislature give careful attention to the advisability of reorganizing our judiciary system. Now, that is a good deal gained. Remember that only four years ago they denied that there was the least trouble and now they admit that there is trouble and advise that the legislature give careful attention to it.

If it took them twenty-five years to find out that there was any trouble when trouble existed every minute, how long do you think it will take the legislature to remedy it if it in the control of the republican machine as it has been in the past?

The platform advises the legislature to look into the judiciary system, with a view of reducing expenses, but the candidate in his speech says, in regard to expenses: "If we expect to hold our own with other states, keep our people at home, and draw strangers to our borders, we must keep well abreast of the progress of the age. We cannot mark time; that means falling behind, for others are surely advancing. Instead of making less progress we should make more in the future; but let it be along the line of a practical and conservative expansion."

The platform therefore advises that the legislature investigate expenses with an idea of reducing them, and the candidate says that if we are to make progress, it must be along the line of a practical and conservative expansion.

What do you think a conservative expansion in the expense of the courts in the state would mean? We have gone in twenty years, from $138,000 to $393,000 for the biennial term. Where, under the heavens are we going to land under this conservative expansion? 

I will tell you where the trouble is. After Mr. Proctor wrote his speech they changed the platform and he didn't have time to change his speech.

There is another matter that I want to call attention to. The 

Republican platform says that the administration of the institutions of the state is efficient. Do you know that you have been in business for sixteen years in Rutland, every one of you? You have been carrying on a marble business, under the name of the Rutland Marble Manufacturing Company. The Rutland Marble Manufacturing Company is nothing more or less than the state of Vermont. 

During the lasst sixteen years it has done a business of more than $400,000-$432,000, I think it is. That is all your business, but you never knew of it. The business is that of buying and selling marble, and polishing it and cutting it by our state convicts. Now, that is a large business, carried on by the State of Vermont through all this term of years. We have had four auditors, during that time. In the report of the state prison directors, a statement appears, headed, “Manufacturing Department", but I have yet to find anyone who knew that the business of the Rutland Marble Manufacturing Company is the business of the state of Vermont. No auditor had ever examined one single voucher or bill connected with that business and approved it until after I had made my application to the auditor, Mr. Graham, to examine the vouchers of state institutions. The final examination was made about a year ago, July 1, 1905. Now, is that an efficient administration of state affairs? Understand, this business had been running fifteen years and amounted to more than $400,000 and never examined by a state auditor. In fact, the auditors of the past didn't know there was any necessity for such examination. They didn't know that the state was engaged in such a business. The state has been kept absolutely in the dark in regard to the details of that business, except for the meager statement in the state prison reports which I have referred to. 

Why this concealment?

Well there is one thing about it stranger yet. More than 50 per cent of this marble which has been bought down there has been bought of the Vermont Marble Company, and several thousand dollars worth of that marble has been cut into proper shapes and polished, and resold to the Vermont Marble Company. We are informed by the gentleman who presented Mr. Proctor's name at the republican convention that he is a good business man. Therefore, I assume that Mr. Proctor has known all about this transaction and the fact that the business was being done by the state; because surely selling that amount of marble

he would examine the credit of his customer. So he knew all about it. 

He has been, according to the statement of the gentleman who presented his name in Montpelier at the Republican convention, he has been three or four times in the Legislature, as representative, as senator, as speaker of the house, and yet has he ever told us that the state of Vermont was running a marble business down there that the auditor was not examining, and that he was selling the marble to it? Why has he kept the fact concealed? Can you imagine any reason? I cannot see into his mind, and therefore I am not going to assign any motive. I will leave that for you to do, as you choose. But I say that such a condition of things is unparalleled in any state of this Union. 

I don't know and no one can ever know whether that business has been honestly conducted or not. An examination of the material which has been used there and has passed out into the world and been distributed would be necessary to discover that fact; but I assume that it has been, and if it has been, it is due solely to the honesty of the men who have had it in charge. 

What I claim is this, that for fifteen years the state of Vermont has been carrying on a business which its auditors haven't known anything about or examined or been asked to examine, that more than fifty per cent of that business has been transacted with the Vermont Marble Company, and that Fletcher D. Proctor, by virtue of his position, must have known all about it all the time and has carefully concealed that knowledge. The Republican machine platform calls that efficient management. I won't characterize that transaction. I will let you do that. 

You will find adjectives enough between now and the time you get home so that you will be pretty well prepared to explain it and characterize it to your neighbors. 

Just a word about the insane asylum at Waterbury. Some of you may remember that two years ago I had occasion to say something about the management of the Waterbury institution, and I charged that the expense of maintenance at that institution was more than at similar institutions in other states. I sent an accountant to Montpelier to make an examination of the vouchers and the accounts of that institution, with the idea of finding out the reason for that expense, the reason why it cost more each year to keep a man in this state than it does in other states. You may remember that I was denied the right to look at our vouchers up there in Montpelier, I say our vouchers because they are ours, yours and mine; they belong to the public. They are just as much public documents as any other documents are, and they have always been considered so until the Republican machine found out that I wanted to look at them. When some Democratic gentleman in this state charged Governor Bell with billing the state more times than there were days in the year when he was cattle commissioner, I noticed that vouchers were public and open to their inspection.

But when I wanted to look at them I couldn't get near enough to the state auditor to put my hand on him. The Republican machine

didn't want that I should look at them and so I was fired out of the auditor's office and it has taken two years of time and a mandate of the supreme court of this state to halter-break that auditor so it is safe for an ordinary everyday Vermonter to put his head inside the door of the auditor's office in Montpelier and ask to see one of his own vouchers. 

I have had a man working at these papers ever since I have had that mandate. He tells me that the papers of the office are in a deplorable condition. He says he has opened some files which are supposed to contain public vouchers and not found a single voucher in them which belonged there; every one gone. And it is impossible to make a correct statement of the business of this state for the last twenty-five years. I understand that most of the vouchers which show the detail of the business for the past ten years are in the auditor's office, and that those which accrued prior to that time are supposed to be dumped down on the floor of the dome in the Montpelier capitol. That shows as well as anything what the system has been, or rather the lack of system. 

It is too close to election to make a careful examination of these accounts as I had hoped to do, but I have found out something about the institution in Waterbury. The excess of cost there appears to be almost entirely on account of the help. It is excess in salaries and excess in number of employees. And that excess of help makes the expense of maintenance of the Waterbury institution $62,089.86 for the biennial term, more than it should be, because you and I know that similar institutions in other states-Massachusetts for instance, or New York, or Connecticut, are well and properly run. And therefore I say there is no reason why it should cost us more to maintain our insane than it costs them.

And yet the Republican platform congratulates the people of this state on the efficiency of the administration of our state institutions. 

The inconsistency of the Republican platform is its fatal weakness. 

There seems to have been an effort made by petty officials, grand jurors, etc., to line their pockets at the expense of the state by all sorts of petty prosecutions. For instance, if a man is charged for intoxication, he will be charged with the breach of the peace, and maybe resisting an officer, and when he is brought to trial, he will be tried on the simple misdemeanor of intoxication and the other charges nolle prossed. Nevertheless the officers and the courts collect from the state fees for all these charges of crime which he has nolle prossed. 

I said that many of these charges are nolle prossed. That is true, but it isn't always done that way. There was a man down in Bennington, not long ago, arrested for intoxication and brech of the peace, he was tried first for intoxication and then for breach of peace, and the court declared him guilty of both charges. A mittimus to commit him to the house of correction was given to two officers and the two officers brought him to Rutland, took him over the river, and committed him to the house of correction, each turning in his mittimus and getting a receipt for the prisoner, each of them collecting for that service fifteen cents a mile for travel and various other fees which attend such cases. The bus-

iness of sending men to the house of correction is profitable to some people. 

There was a thrifty deputy sheriff in one county of this state who had a good natured prisoner, -I think it was along in the fall, when the house of correction probably seemed to the prisoner a comfortable place to spend the winter, —anyhow he was an accommodating prisoner, because this officer put him aboard the train fifty miles from Rutland, in charge of the baggage master, telephoned to a deputy sheriff in Rutland, who met the train, took the prisoner to the house of correction, made out his mittimus, got his receipt for the prisoner, and the deputy sheriff whose duty it was to take him there, but who has all this time remained at home, charged the state of Vermont fifteen cents per mile for taking the prisoner to the house of correction. 

I have heard of another well authenticated case where a prisoner took his own mittimus, went to the house of correction, and delivered himself over and the deputy sheriff got fifteen cents a mile for that. 

I want to say just a word about local option. The Republican platform says that the local option law ought to be tried further and the republican candidate agrees to that proposition. He has changed his mind about prohibition, because only four years ago, when he was running as a Republican prohibition candidate for nomination for governor, the only thing he said or wrote in that whole campaign about the affairs of the state, was in a letter to an inquiring friend in which he said that he thought prohibition better for the state of Vermont than any scheme of license. He has kept perfectly silent on that subject and on every other subject of state interest until the other day in Montpelier, and now, apparently because he thinks it isn't safe to attack the license law, he says that he thinks it ought to be tried further.

Inasmuch as the voters of this state have passed directly upon that question, we are glad to know that the Republican machine does not intend to repeal the law at the present time. Alll that they propose to do is to fix it in some way so they can use it to perpetuate their power. 

That's what they are doing today. As a general thing the side judges, as they are called, belong to the orgianization known as the Republican machine. They appoint the license commissioners in the different towns in the state, at the request of the Republican machine managers, and down in my home town the Republican candidate for governor is rounding up the liquor dealers and the whole liquor interest to his support. 

When we agitated the adoption of the license-local option law, four years ago, we made the local option feature of it strong, so far as that particular feature was concerned with the selection of license commissioners. When the law went to the people for their approval, it was provided that the commissioners should be selected by the towns, each town to select its own commissioners. But the republican machine saw a chance to secure the influence of the liquor dealers and at the very first opportunity changed that provision so that the party leaders and managers could control the liquor vote by holding up as a reward to the liquor dealers a license to sell, and as a punishment the denial of a license.

It is a curious situation, when you think of it. Prohibitionist 

Proctor running for governor on a license-local option platform and rounding up, through his heelers, the liquor interest of the State to his support. 

The license-local option law was forced upon the Republican machine by the License-Local-Option Party. It never came up to the best thought of today upon this subject and if we should gain the reins of power, in my opinion, we ought to remedy the defects in our law in accordance with the lest laws the laws of other states which have been found to be best notably that of the state of Massachusetts. We ought to restore to the people the opportunity to select their own license commissioners, and so control the granting of licenses in towns where the vote gives them a right to do it.

The reason why Vermont has not received a greater share of the prosperity of the country is on account of our laws. We have not legislated to invite capital to our state, but we have rather legislated to prevent any man who has a dollar from taking it out of the state. Apparently that is the object of some of our legislation. Of course it has been a failure. One of the laws which I believe has held us back is the law in regard to taxation. The principle that prosperity should be taxed once is absolutely just and right. It is when we endeavor to tax the same property two or three times over that we meet with trouble. Our present law in regard to taxation bears heavily upon the owner of land-upon the farmers and we can and should furnish relief. It is a difficult matter to handle and I shall not take your time here today to discuss it. 

But it can be done and done without loss to the state income, and I promise you that if the Independent Party wins this coming election, as we expect to, that will be one of the first things we undertake. That measure will not save money to the state, but it will relieve those who are today paying more than they ought to and it will shift the burden to those who are not paying as much as they ought. 

In the year 1900 the voters of Rutland County sent me up to Montpelier to represent them in the senate. It happened that my duties required me to make some examination of the auditor's office, and I could see that the business of the office was not properly handled, because the state did not provide the force necessary to examine and audit the state business. The auditor told me that he was obliged to neglect it. He did all he could, but it was beyond the power of any one man to audit the business affairs of the whole state. I introduced a resolution in the senate, asking for a joint committee from the house and senate, to examine the business of the state and to introduce a bill which would remedy the condition of affairs. The bill passed the senate and was killed in the house. The speaker referred to a committee in the house other than the one which had prepared it, and it was smothered in that committee. 

That speaker was Fletcher D. Proctor.

If that bill had passed, you and I would not be here today, and the state of Vermont would not be engaged in the transacting of a business of thousand of dollars each year in the careless and slipshod manner in which it has been conducted. We must have a new system of account-

ing. I might say we must have a system, for up to the present time we have had no system at all. There isn't a successful business man in the city of Burlington today who hasn't got a better plan for looking after, for keeping accounts of his business, than the State of Vermont has. 

That is a very important matter and needs careful attention. It seems to me that it should proceed along the lines which have been found efficient in our great corporations. It will require increased expense in the auditor's office, it will require a much larger staff of men than we now have, but it will be money well invested. It will save the state thousands where it costs hundreds.

In 1902 the License-Local-Option Party demanded a caucus law, and the Republican machine in 1904 gave us one. We are operating under it for the first time this year. The plan of registration is so cunningly devised that I understand more than fifty per cent of the voters of the state aren't registered at all. They don't propose to bind themselves to support the nominees of a party before they know who those nominees are. The declaration which was put before you reads like this: "It is my intention to vote and act with the the coming election!" party at You ought not to be bound to act with any party. We all ought to exercise the fullest freedom in coming to a decision in regard to whom we will vote for. We ought to have the right to change our minds any time during a campaign, even up to the minute when we enter a voting booth and take a pencil to mark our ballots. The plan of the Republican machine is to get votes-not to ask a man to consider what he wants to vote for, what principles of government he desires to have established, -but to get votes, to pledge voters, sometimes years in advance of an election. The question put forward by the Republican machine leaders has been and is today in this campaign 'Will you vote our candidate?'

On the other hand, the Independent Party ask you to carefully consider the propositions which we offer you, address your vote to those propositions. Candidates are of no importance. It's of no importance to you and of less importance to me whether I am your candidate or whether you had decided upon some other man. But the measures which control our government are all important. 

The right of suffrage is the highest right a man possesses. It distinguishes a citizen of this American Republic from the subject of monarchy. But it amounts to nothing unless it is used with thought and with a conscience. The time has come when we should rebuke with no uncertain voice the abuses which have been growing upon us Vermonters for twenty-five years, and we ask the free and independent voters of every party to join us in a united effort to defeat the machine of the Republican Party, which is responsible for the mismanagement, the maladministration of public affairs which we condemn.

PLATFORM. 

The Prohibitionists of Vermont in State convention assembled acknowledging God as the source of all power and authority and that to him all must render a strict account for the exercise of the same, do hereby declare our principles and beliefs. 

First. We heartily endorse the National Prohlbition Party in its constant maintenance that the Prohibition of the liquor traffic is the chief issue before the American people in its financial, social, political and moral relations to our civil and religious institutions. 

Second. In its unswerving stand for the dissolution of the partnership between the government and the liquor traffic whereby for a specified sum of money under the guise of a tax receipt the government licenses the manufacture, sale and transportation of intoxicating liquor as a beverage.

Third. In its reiterated demand for national and State laws prohibiting the manufacture, sale and transportation of intoxicating liquors as a beverage throughout the bounds of the United States and all territory under its authority.

Fourth. In that it has steadily refused, notwithstanding great temptation to seeming present advantage to enter into entangling alliances with other parties not having at heart the chief issues which constitute the reasons for its existence.

Fifth. In that it, as a party, measures up to the most advanced declarations of our highest religious bodies in whose resolutions are set forth the true attitude of the Christian citizens toward the liquor traffic and also toward all political parties not openly hostile to the same. 

We commend without stint the administration of President Roosevelt in so far as disregarding the vicious trammels of his own party he has consulted the patriotic sentiments of the American people of all shades of opinion.

We condemn in an equally emphatic manner the continuance by his administration of the iniquitous license policy inaugurated by the Republican Party in 1862 and extended under his predecessor to our possessions lately acquired from Spain. All this without his protest and with his sanction.

We are in hearty accord with national expansion when natural and not in conflict with the principles set forth in our Declaration of Independence; but, we are opposed to the extension of our sway either by purchase without the consent of the peoples concerned, or by force over peoples to whom we deny the right of citizenship while at the same time refusing to guarantee their ultimate independence.

We view with alarm the disposition of Christian men to still sustain a party that having seen the accomplishment of the issues that called it into existence has so catered to commercial greed and the spoils of office as to jeopardize the ideals bequeathed us by our forefathers, to diminish the sacred regard of our people for the Constitution and Declaration of Independence and to envelop our glorious flag in a cloudy vagueness as to what it stands for, the real meaning of which shall depend on the shifting necessities of the party in power. 

The experience of our State under the prohibitory law demonstrated the need of a party whose chief issue should be not only its maintenance as a statute, but its enforcement as well. And the Republican party in its entire relation to this great evil has proved that its only real issue is that of power and spoil. Its readiness to surrender to the claims of the liquor traffic has given rise to the Prohibition Party and its surrender to corporate greed and unfair distribution of the rewards of toil is giving rise to the Socialist Party. 

The current of western immigration having practically ceased, we realize that the scrutiny of home seekers, laborers, and capital will far more seriously be turned upon our own fair State in search of the opportunities of life. We therefore favor the wise encouragement of communication and the means of transportation, the development of mining and manufacturing with their attendant home markets for agricultural products and also the embodying as far as practicable of the principles of forestry into law.

We favor a broader educational qualification in the case of emigrants seeking naturalization to the end that the requirements in their case may bear a more just relation to the twenty-one years required of our own sons ere they may exercise the right of suffrage. 

We favor the election of United States senators by the direct vote of the people.

The right of suffrage should be based on education rather than on sex.

CHAPTER VI. 

Fusion Campaign 

The fusion campaign was opened by a rally at Bennington on the evening of July 28, 1906. The speakers of the evening were Mr. Clement, Pape and Senter. The Bennington Band rendered several selections before the rally, and a colored quartette made its first appearance and sang several selections during the rally. The speaking by the candidates took place from a platform erected in front of the Court 

House on South Street.

Long before the hour set for the calling of the meeting to order, the streets were filled with people. When the candidates appeared in sight of the crowd at 8 o'clock, a display of fireworks followed by colored fire was seen all along South and North Streets, and in front of the speakers stand. 

The gathering was called to order by Edward J. Hall, chairman of the independent State committee, who presented his fellow townsman, Emory S. Harris. Mr. Harris said he did not seek to make converts to the fusion cause as Bennington was converted already. What they did seek to find out was how to convert the machinists. Referring patriotically to the record of the Green Mountain Boys, he introduced "the flower of Vermont's young democracy, G. 

Herbert Pape of Barre, our next lieutenant-governor." 

Mr. Pape received a hearty and enthusiastic greeting and he opened his speech by saying that he was glad to open the campaign in the house of his friends. Referring to the situation, he said Vermont was in the midst of the warmest political campaign in her history. Two months before election, the Republican State committee had already convened and issued an impassioned appeal to the voters. The reason lay in the fact that the independent and democratic parties were united by a new purpose, a new courage, placing loyalty to principle before party, and that the union had sounded the death knell of machine rule in Vermont.

The interest shown by the machine press in democratic affairs had been remarkable. After advising the democrats not to fuse, they had turned around after the convention in Burlington and said that fusion was just what they wanted. 

If this was so, it is very strange to see the state committee had got such a hustle on. The fact is, the machine press and the machine committee are suffering from the biggest scare in their history. Example: A Proctor newspaper advising its readers to "keep cool and don't bet."

Mr. Pape added that if the election were held to-morrow, the fusion candidate would certainly be elected. The manifesto of the machine committee was composed mostly of personal attacks on Mr. Clement, whose principal crime was that he had expressed his honest convictions contrary to the will of the party. The plight of the machine committee was like that of the little boy with the black eye, who, when the minister offered to pray for him said: "Go home and pray for your own boy; he's got two black eyes. 

The abuse heaped upon Mr. Clement was the same that fell to the lot of Joseph W. Folk of Missouri and other earnest reformers.

The speaker added that the only two planks upon state issues appeared in the republican platform of 1902. The caucus law which they promised never came into the General Assembly until the last day of the session and the local option law was forced on the statute books of the State by Clement men and democrats. As to reform in State expenses, an 

Indiana man said that a party that was responsible for such an increase as Vermont showed in twenty years would be driven from power in his State at the next election. 

Speaking of the working man, Mr. Pape said that the only way to help a man, trying to pay for a home of his own to escape double taxation was to join hands with the fusion party; the only way to secure the return of true local option by making license commissioners appointive by their own communities was by defeating the machine; the only platform that promised any help for the laboring class was the fusion platform. The machine had given labor nothing but

a child labor law in fifty years. How long would it take for labor to secure what they are looking for at the present time? 

Mr. Pape warned his hearers not to be switched from 

State to national issues and said speakers from outside the state would better mind their own business. The paramount issue in the present campaign was really whether our state shall be a government of the boss, by the boss and for the benefit of the boss.

He called for a clean and honorable campaign and said he would rather be defeated than win by political trickery and corruption. He called on every voter to constitute himself a special committee of one and bespoke for the fusion ticket an unprecedented majority in Bennington next September.

After two selections by the colored quartette, which made its first appearance in the campaign, Mr. Hall said that one hundred and twenty-nine years ago their ancestors fought on the battlefield at Bennington and the cause of war at that time was taxation without representation. To-day one of the causes of dissatisfaction was double taxation. He referred to the increases of doing public business, particularly in the courts and said that many were dissatisfied with the administration of our municipal courts. When there is demand for a special remedy, the man of the hour comes forward. Bennington had brought forward the man of the hour in Percival W. Clement, whom he introduced to the audience. 

Mr. Clement said that in embarking on the great undertaking, one has need of courage, but that in the present instance we do not stand so much in need of it, because we feel that we are going to win. If any of that quality were needed, the character of the welcome would furnish it. The campaign had coined a new word and has made the word Bennington mean the same as independence. This was appropriate and right as Bennington had done more than any other town to maintain the integrity of the State when Vermont was an independent republic, owing allegiance to no power on earth.

Continuing, Mr. Clement told how his great grandfather had come to the town of Sherburne in pursuit of in-

dependence and argued that he had a personal right to that quality.

After a brief history of the republican party, Mr. Clement told of its final preponderance in Vermont and the manner in which a few men now control the caucuses and elections. 

There never was a State issue in Vermont until 1902 and the machine, entrenched in power, is responsible for a hodgepodge of ill-considered laws. 

Vermont's political boss is Senator Redfield Proctor.

Every man who wants an office in Vermont has to hit the ties for Proctor. The Rutland Railroad has been compelled to take out its sixty pounds rails and put in eighty pounds, to accommodate the tremendous travel of office seekers to 

Proctor. The senator controls the Vermont delegation in 

Washington and holds federal patronage in his hands; now the son wants to be governor and control State patronage. 

The old senator holds up one end of the yoke, waves and bows and says commanderingly: "Haw, Buck; come under.' 'The horns of the independent party are too big and they won't come. 'No matter what kind of grease you put on my neck, the yoke don't fit me."

The state insane asylum at Waterbury, the speaker said, was one thing that we had to show for our enormous increase in state expenses, but its building was the craziest thing Vermont ever did; the man responsible for it ought to have been its first inmate. There is graft there in the salaries paid and in the amount of help maintained. It costs us $31,000 a year, or $1.16 per patient per week more than it does in Connecticut to run a similar institution. 

Vermont loses money from her antiquated style of doing business. Haying with a scythe and a bull rake is not more out-of-date than trying to handle the public business of Vermont the way we do. And yet, when the speaker was in the Senate in 1900, a bill to reorganize State business methods, which passed the Senate, was defeated by Fletcher D. Proctor when it reached the House. The unaudited business of the State in the house of correction marble mills was explained and Proctor's interest in it shown.

Furthermore Mr. Clement said that he sent an expert accountant to examine state accounts, and it took a year and a half and a mandate of the Supreme Court to give him the right to look at his own books. Up to a week ago last 

Friday, there hadn't been one scratch of a pen made on those books in relation to state institutions for one whole year previous. No one can tell what State expenses have been and the reason those figures are held back is because the cost of doing business has increased to nearly $900,000 and we are face to face with a deficit of $26,000 or more and we must either increase the taxes on corporations or raise a State tax. 

That is why the auditor evades the order of the Supreme Court; that is why we cannot get the figures. 

What will the fusionists do, if elected? The first thing will be to turn every mother's son out of office; then put some legislation on the books that will protect the treasury. 

Concluding Mr. Clement condemned in no uncertain terms the "miserable whelp who sells his vote", and added that, in a certain town where there are four hundred purchasable voters, he would tell those voters what he thought of them. 

I don't want any man who sells his vote for me; take it somewhere else.

He urged the formation of fusion clubs for debating state expenses and said that if all honest voters touch elbow 

September 4th, the Bennington idea will be victorious all over the state.

John H. Senter of Montpelier wasn't on the program of the evening, but Emory Harris introduced him to the audience. He said the democrats told him two years ago that he was old enough to be chloroformed, so he had just come from Montpelier to find out whether he was still alive or not. 

He favored principles; not men; not Proctor nor Clement but Vermont. He supported the fusion ticket because it stood for the State issues in which he believed. Both Mr. 

Proctor and Mr. Clement were millionaires and were out of his class; he started out a poor boy 16 years old and he has had to scratch gravel like the devil ever since.

Condemning commissioners generally, he told of F. T. 

Hutchinson's herd of forty-two cattle, condemned to be

slaughtered at the Swift plant in Burlington. And yet, when the owner went there he saw the carcasses dressed and put in the cooler. He saw three quarters of beef with diseased portions cut out marked to a Burlington butcher. Question- 

Is Vermont paying eighty per cent value of diseased cattle and then permitting the meat to be sold as food?

The State books show $32,000 paid for condemned cattle and $13,000 returned in superphosphates. Isn't that a pretty heavy proportion?

Mr. Senter closed with an appeal for the fusion ticket and explained that every Vermonter but the political boss would benefit from the change.

BRATTLEBORO RALLY. 

The second fusion rally of the democratic campaign was held at Brattleboro on July 30th, the speakers being Mr.

Col. Charles A. Miles called the meeting to order and presented G. Herbert Pape of Barre as the first speaker.

Mr. Pape gave a history of the fusion campaign and he said that the real attitude of the machine republicans was shown by the remark of one of them who said: "Thank God it is not September. Applause followed his reference to W. J. Bryan, a reformer who had been abused as much as Mr. Clement. Speakers of national importance coming into Vermont were on the same principle as a man coming from Barre to interfere with the village affairs of Brattleboro.

He referred to the scurrilous article in the Phoenix and said he should be proud to be elected by the votes of the bakers, printers and hack drivers. He added that labor should not only be interested in its own kind of legislation; but should take an interest in all matters of State importance.

In introducing Mr. Clement, Col. Miles referred to the ante-bellum campaign when the republicans were called mudsills and greasy mechanics and yet they saved the Union. 

Four years ago they called the Clement men rummies, but they have given the State a law incomparatively better than prohibition. He referred to Mr. Clement as the father of the license law and the reform candidate for governor. 

Mr. Clement's speech was delivered ex-tempore and it was different in almost every respect from his opening address. He said that in 1902 when he made his first speech in 

Bethel, he was representing himself; to-day he was representing the independent idea that was growing every minute. 

He referred to the growth of the idea and said that the opposition never said a truer thing than when they accused the independents of being blacksmiths, barbers and farmers.

They haven't a man in the party who knew enough to run a campaign.

We have the people with us and that is good enough. 

This is the first campaign when the republican candidate for governor has had an idea and we welcome the change. 

The spontaneous campaign of Mr. Proctor was spoken of and the machine committee assurance that not a dollar was used to influence the convention called for scorns and laughter. He questioned if there was a town in Vermont that had not had its Proctor heelers.

The present machine candidate for governor was nominated in 1898 to the certain knowledge of the speaker and the withdrawal of Dr. Webb from the campaign of 1902 was engineered by Mr. Proctor when the doctor was in California. 

A clean and concise statement of an auditor's duties followed and the absurdity and danger of the present methods were pointed out through a communication from F. S. Pratt of Brattleboro, an expert accountant.

The republican machine sees the handwriting on the wall and it is frantically trying to side track the main issue. The 

Proctor yoke doesn't fit any too well and a good many people walk lop-sided after they get through wearing it, even though the grease on their necks may be labeled "district attorney" or "United States marshal."

In conclusion the speaker said he was as much of a republican as ever, but not enough to stand for the infernal methods that prevail in Vermont today.

RALLY AT WHITE RIVER JUNCTION. 

The third fusion rally was held in Gates Opera House at White River Junction on the succeeding day. Many people were present from surrounding towns, coming either by team or local trains.

Eighteen men from the town of Norwich drove seven miles to hear the speeches and one of them said: "I was a delegate to the republican state convention at Montpelier, but I would drive five times that distance to hear Mr. 

Clement speak." I was the only man in my district, comprising three towns, who came to the rally four years ago, but this time I am bringing my neighbors with me.' 

Another Norwich man said that no political campaign in his history had ever aroused the interest that the fusion movement has and that Mr. Clement would double if not treble his vote in Windsor County.

A concert was played in Depot square by the West Lebanon band before the rally and the colored quartette sang several selections during the rally.

Dr. Barrons introduced G. Herbert Pape as a sterling representative of Vermont's growing industrial metropolis. 

Mr. Pape spoke of the interest displayed by the opposition press in fusion affairs, and he recommended the voters to disregard it. "The voice is Jacob's voice, but the hands are the hands of Esau." He deprecated the importance of the speakers who made national issues their themes and quoted Senator Dillingham, who had merely praised the machine ticket, but said nothing whatever about state issues. 

Mr. Clement was introduced in a brief speech by George 

H. Fuller, chairman of the independent town committee. 

Mr. Clement took occasion to dispute the recent figures published by the superintendent of the insane asylum, saying they were false and misleading. He offered to submit the figures he offered as correct to a committee composed of the auditors of the three railroads operating in Vermont, and if

they proved incorrect to admit the fact publicly every time he made a speech during the rest of the campaign. 

This was conditional on the machine orators doing the same thing if their figures proved to be wrong.

He also referred to the circulation of the story by the Proctor republicans that he favored bonding the state for good roads, something he had never advocated nor favored. 

After the rally an informal meeting was held in the hall. 

NORTHFIELD RALLY. 

The speaking took place outdoors from the band stand, the people being seated on the slope of a great amphitheatre. 

The speakers were introduced by Dr. W. B. Mayo of Northfield, fusion candidate for senator from Washington County. 

Seated on the platform were G. B. Evans of Waterbury, Captain Joseph Livingstone of Northfield, C. A. G. Jackson of Montpelier and S. S. Kimball of Northfield. The Northfield band played several selections and the colored quartette also sang several selections. Dr. Mayo introduced Mr. Clement briefly as a man who was not afraid to have opinions, nor afraid to express them.

Mr. Clement said the attempts of the opposition to discredit him with the republicans are the same as those employed in the campaign of 1902. He is not trying to destroy the party, but to reform it. The machine denies its own existence, and he read the names of seven stump speakers for Mr. Proctor, all of whom were in the state or government employ, all creatures of machine politics.

At the request of many fusionists in Washington County, Mr. Clement spoke briefly on double taxation, after which 

Mr. Senter was introduced as the next speaker. 

Mr. Senter, during his speech, said it was high time to drop prejudice and work together for our beloved Vermont. 

He applauded Mr. Clement for his stand on local option. He had told Vermont that prohibition was wrong, and that state institutions were improperly run, and he scared the republican machine into passing a license law and looking into our state institutions.

Republics are ungrateful and voters are forgetful, but was it brains that put Josiah Grout and E. C. Smith in the governor's chair? The present campaign isn't a question of 

Mr. Proctor nor Mr. Clement, but of Vermont. Mr. Senter made a sarcastic reference to the impossibility of getting granite into a government building, and added that the state was suffering from too much Proctor. He said the minor courts kept the small offenders traveling to Rutland so as to keep up the marble business at the house of correction, and he paid his respects to Vermont boards and commissions. 

He called attention to the fact that the tax on corporations really comes out of the people. People like a man like 

Mr. Clement, who would beard the lion in his den, the Douglas in his hall, the Proctor in his marble. The attempts at belittling him were merely signs that they were scared. 

The meeting closed with an informal reception to the fusionists.

ST. ALBANS RALLY. 

The St. Albans rally was held on the evening of August 

Ist in the city hall. Charles D. Watson, chairman of the democratic county committee presided. He referred in a happy manner to the unique features of the occasion, the first in his knowledge where the two parties had come together in such large numbers, united by a common purpose and a mutual interest. He introduced the permanent chairman of the evening, Stephen E. Royce of St. Albans.

Mr. Royce began by paying a tribute to President 

Roosevelt, the man behind the "Square Deal Guns" in Washington, and said that the people everywhere were demanding that the public business should be run on business principles and that business men be honest.

He said reform was coming down the pike on high gear, with the throttle wide open and if we did not get into line, we should be hurt and the people have been poisoned and robbed and goldbricked while rascals were acquiring tainted millions, which they cannot take with them as they would wish to do. People will soon understand that it is impossible to fool the Almighty and that absolute failure faces the man who tries it.

The golden rule, not the golden calf, must be the guide as conditions here in Vermont have not been conducted on business principles and have often not been right. The Fish and Game League doesn't promise any practical relief. The heart of the machine has been exposed and it consists of cold hard marble.

Things are going to be fixed, if not at the coming election, then at some future time. Graft, self-interest, money or deviltry cannot stop it. If the leaders of the movement who speak to you tonight show the right spirit and the right purpose back them up. The Senate at Montpelier this fall will need a strong, able man at its head and there is excellent promise of that in the first speaker of the evening, G. Herbert 

Pape of Barre.

Mr. Pape paid a graceful tribute to Mr. Royce, the chairman, and said that Franklin County had no needs to send out of the county for orators.

As Mr. Pape began to speak on double taxation a man in the audience asked why a farm with a mortgage on it was taxed twice while railroad bonds went free. The speaker answered immediately that railroad bonds are taxable under the law and that the only way to make taxation equitable was to vote the fusion ticket. 

He paid his compliments to the local paper, and he said that he read all the other republican newspapers, also the Burlington Free Press, which he added had no right to the name as it failed to print the news. He paid an eloquent tribute to the worth of the common people, and added that the American home was the greatest power for public and personal good that the country afforded. 

He also referred to the frantic efforts of the republican state committee to get speakers who could answer the claims of the fusionists and aded that their exaggerated fright was the truest criterion of the weakness of their cause. 

In introducing Mr. Clement, Mr. Royce said that his defeat four years ago was the most expensive luxury that a rich man ever had enjoyed. The machine press was denouncing Mr. Clement because he left the party four years ago. A man was justified in leaving the party if the party

was wrong. He introduced the speaker as the pluckiest fighter in the State of Vermont.

As Mr. Clement stepped forward, he was greeted with an ovation by the audience, which he acknowledged with considerable feeling and said it would remain with him so long as memory served him. 

He referred to the buttonholing campaign that has been customary in Vermont, and he added that the only way to get office had been to keep still-almighty still. 

He told of the nomination of Governor Smith in 1898 in the Central Vermont depot, at which time Fletcher D.

Proctor was also nominated. Smith nominated Proctor, and 

Proctor nominated Smith. 

When Governor Smith delivered his farewell address, he had learned what the needs of Vermont were and he recommended a general reorganization and retrenchment in State affairs.

The legislature of that biennial term then increased State expenses $68,000. The next biennial term showed $65,000 increase over that and the term just ended an increase of $433,000. That's where Governor Smith landed trying to help things inside the party. Only one platform in twenty years has recommended a retrenchment. That was in 1890, and that legislature increased the expense of doing business. $107,000. 

Where will we land if they pass any more resolutions?

The fusionists are trying to burst the machine, which is responsible for this increase and they are not going to do it with a pillow or a feather duster. It needs baseball bats, and we've got it. If they don't improve things in fifty years more than the machine has done, it is the people's duty to turn them out.

If it is true, as reported, that there is a purchasable vote in St. Albans, it is a shame to every decent institution, including its splendid newspapers. The way to do away with this sort of thing is to vote against the man who does it, and it will not be long before the man who tries to purchase votes in St. Albans will be sent from whence he came, beaten and rebuked.

A large number of voters remained after the rally and shook hands with the candidate and speakers. 

BRANDON RALLY. 

The fusion campaign in Rutland County was opened on 

August 2nd, with a rally in Brandon. Between 6:00 and 7:30 o'clock, a reception was held on the steps of the Brandon Inn, where a large number greeted Mr. Clement, Mr. Burke and the local leaders of the fusion campaign. It was arranged by 

Mr. E. S. Marsh and Q. S. Backus. The Rutland city band played a number of selections during the reception and also during the rally, interspersed by music from the colored quartette.

Mr. E. S. Marsh was the chairman, and in introducing 

Mr. Burke, he said that all Vermonters had heard of the blacksmith mayor of Burlington, who as a result of the confidence reposed in him by his own fellow citizens, was now serving his fourth term as Mayor of the Queen City, —a tried and true public servant.

James E. Burke in starting, referred to himself as a plain laboring man and that he had the honor to be the mayor of the finest city in the finest State in the finest country in the world.

He referred to the efforts he had made towards reform in Burlington, and said that what he had attempted there on a small scale, the standard bearer of the party, Percival W. 

Clement of Rutland would do for the State. Reform is in the air and he ventured to predict that Vermont will share in the benefits that come from the general revolt that is being made against ring rule and graft.

He said that he testified as an expert witness at the investigation of state institutions by the special committee and readily proved that the insane asylum at Waterbury was paying $4.90 for coal that could be laid alongside a Burlington ditch for $3.60, when the highest coal rate on the Central 

Vermont road was fifty-five cents per toon. 

The whitewash given the affairs of Vermont investigations by the committee was an insult to every one at the state's prison, was unspeakable, and he knew from the testi-

mony of one of the witnesses discredited by Attorney General Fitts, that the conditions were as bad or worse in the house of correction.

Mr. Burke paid tribute to Theodore Roosevelt, and said that he was just that kind of a democrat, who could appreciate the splendid services of the president and he thought the fusion platform ought to have contained an endorsement of his policy. For the same reason that he admired and supported the policies of the president, the speaker was prepared to support Mr. Clement for the governorship of the State. 

One of the hardest things in public life, in which I have had considerable experience was to prevent graft, and he had at that time in his possession a bill to the city of Burlington that showed graft as surely as the Waterbury coal bill. Mr. 

Proctor either knows better when he says there is no graft or corruption in Vermont, or else he must be totally ignorant of the public interests of the state, either of which condition ought to insure his defeat.

The party which is losing its potency and the best men of both parties are beginning to realize that there comes a time when the public needs of a state demands the obliteration of party lines. On this score he made an earnest appeal to all his fellow democrats to vote the fusion ticket. 

In introducing Mr. Clement, Mr. Marsh said that the claim had been made that the fusion candidate for governor had done nothing for the state. He knows of one noteworthy reform for which Mr. Clement is responsible and that is the local option law. The chairman referred interestingly to the satisfactory local working of both phases of the law, and presented the speaker of the evening as the next governor of 

Vermont. 

Mr. Clement paid a cordial tribute to Senator Proctor as a man and private citizen, and distinctly made the point that it was on the score of his public record alone that he desired to comment. He referred to the general breaking away from party lines that always took place in public movements of this character, and cited the city clerk of Rutland, a democrat, appointed year after year by republican city governments.

In his opinion Mr. Clement thought that he would carry the state by a fair majority.

RICHMOND RALLY. 

The succeeding rally of the fusion party was held at Richmond. Mr. A. B. Rowley, who had just returned from a trip in the southern portion of Vermont, presided at the meeting and introduced the speakers. In presenting Mr. Clement, Mr. Rowley said he brought good tidings from the southern part of the state. He said that to the best of his knowledge and belief the fusion ticket would be triumphantly elected. 

He referred to the corrupt campaign of McCullough and the birth of the local option party at that time. In that campaign the central figure then, as well as now, was Percival W. 

Clement, whom he had the honor to introduce.

Mr. Clement referred to a previous address in Richmond in 1902 and said that the seeming defeat of that campaign resulted in the local option law. He paid sarcastic regards to the political trust of Vermont and denied the right of the republican leaders to read him out of the party. 

He told of his attempt and those of Governor Smith to secure reform inside the party and declared that the state would never have had a local option law if we had stayed inside the party.

He told of the singular jumble of the figures put out by the machine speakers. Foster does not agree with Fairchild nor even with himself on different occasions and none of them agree with the expert, F. S. Pratt, who had worked for the investigating committee as well as for the speaker. He said it was apparently the intention of the machine to give out a large and confusing mess of figures right or wrong and trust to the people's inability to find out the right one. 

In regard to other matters, the ring speakers and newspapers were misrepresenting him in order to contradict a misstatement.

A brief review of double taxation and its remedy and an appeal in behalf of the man trying to pay for his home was made, as well as an accusation of the republican party, which

had never proposed anything of value to the ordinary Vermonter. 

When a party is wrong patriotism succeeds regularity, otherwise reform will be impossible.

A feature of the evening was when a man in the audience arose and asked permission to tell the story of the Grout and Redfield Proctor political deals in which Grout found the latter in bed directing his campaign, and elected him, but when he asked for similar aid, he failed to get it. 

In presenting Thomas H. Browne of Rutland, Mr. Rowley paid a tribute to the fusion movement that had given him an opportunity to introduce the next speaker. 

Mr. Browne referred to Mr. Clement's clean cut discussion of the business of the voters present and said that all should remember that they are first of all Vermonters, not republicans nor democrats. The voter who could not go outside of party lines when occasion demands was unworthy of the franchise.

He illustrated the manner in which a party continued for long periods in power became corrupt by reference to Pennsylvania and New York, and told how the independent voter must hold the balance of power. 

He also explained how impossible it was for any tax to come elsewhere except from the people. Farms in Vermont have depreciated in value, but taxes have steadily gone up. 

The farmer was continually getting the short end of the lever and yet he was continually being asked to vote the republican ticket. No farmer in Vermont can afford a state expense three times as large as it was twenty years ago, when the value of property is continually running down. As for example farms once worth $26 an acre now sells for $22. There are farms in 

Franklin County, which if their buildings and fences should burn, could not raise enough money to rebuild them. 

He also said that the machine speakers were misrepresenting the democratic platform for the purpose of getting votes. A reference to good roads and an anecdote of a pig stealer, who was acquitted because the jury had sone of the pork, closed the rally of the evening.

After the meeting an informal reception was held at the residence of Rev. M. O. H. Dorion, rector of All Saints 

Church, where a collation of ice cream and cake was served.

BURLINGTON RALLY. 

The fusion rally at Burlington was held on the evening of 

August 7th at Battery Park. The Sherman Band played several selections before and during the rally. Mr. Burke, the mayor, presided at the rally. He paid his regards to Congressman Foster, who had spoken from the same rostrum a few days before, and challenged him to a joint debate on the issues of the campaign at such time and place as he desired. 

Mr. Burke explained his opposition to Mr. Clement on the morning of the fusion convention and his support of him afterwards by the fact that he was willing to abide by the result of the convention.

Referring to Mr. Proctor, he said that the republican candidate had said that he had no apologies to make for the condition of things in Vermont. If that were the case with the record of the investigating committee staring him in the face, he was unworthy of the suffrage of his fellow voters. 

Talk about Roosevelt republicanism. Roosevelt would not stand for the condition in Vermont a minute. The time has passed for ridiculing the independent movement and the reluctance of the machine politicians to stand for the search light of publicity was the same feeling that made the packers in Chicago shrink from the disclosures there.

He spoke of the coal graft in Waterbury and said there was no question in his mind but that economy would save money enough to build trunk lines all over Vermont. The investigating committee had applied the white wash brush freely, but a clever bookkeeper could readily cover the tracks of the grafters and the books did not show what was actually a transaction between man and man. He said Mr. Clement was in the house of his friends in Burlington in introducing him as the speaker of the evening.

Mr. Clement acknowledged the heartiness of his reception. He attacked the machine and its responsibility for state abuses and declared that if the fusionists were put in power one of the first things they would do would be to rip up the

arrangements of the Rutland Manufacturing Company at the house of correction, and to discontinue the graft that existed at Waterbury asylum. 

Cheers followed his prediction of victory in September and when he paid his regards to the machine and its methods, double tax on the poor man's home, and his challenge to compare figures with the machine also came in for hearty applause.

In presenting Mr. Senter, Mayor Burke made a pleasant reference to the Lincoln republicans as good in their way as the Jefferson democrats, one of which would now address them.

Mr. Senter opened by referring whimsically to the manner in which the voters had petitioned Mr. Foster to speak and then had failed to attend the rally. He said he hoped that democrats would drop the party cry for once and vote for old

Vermont. The machine had taken fusion as a joke, but they would find it a damned rough joke before fall and their threatening burial of Mr. Clement would have to do with the liveliest corpse in history. 

He said the railroad commission was not as good as the curl on the end of a pig's tail, not even ornamental. The engine that they condemned to run front end first into Bennington still backs in and the railroad company told the commission to go to the devil.

In Montpelier, there had been an attempt made to do a little graft in insurance on the state house and the demand for publicity had saved the state over $1,200. This was merely an item, but it showed the way the state expenses had grown.

Mr. Senter thought Vermont should run her business at least as well as the Vermont Marble Company runs theirs. 

After paying his compliments to the Proctors, father and son, he went on to tell how impossible it was to get anything but Proctor marble into Vermont public buildings. As to that corporation tax, the claim that some one else pays it is old republican doctrine served up in a new way. All the voter has to do is to sit up nights and burn the money.

BARRE RALLY. 

From Burlington the fusion speakers moved to Barre where the next rally was held in the city hall. Music was rendered by the Stone Cutters' Band, a body of musicians, who had been recruited from the stone sheds of the town and vicinity. S. D. Allen presided and he introduced Mr. Clement as the first speaker of the evening.

Mr. Clement paid an earnest tribute to the personal worth and public ability of Mr. Pape, and he added that the appreciative applause of the audience showed that they valued their townsman at his true measure as a man and as a candidate.

An utterance that excited some surprise and enthusiasm was in regard to the wholesale attempt of the state committee of the Proctor republicans to buy up all billboards in the state as to shut out the "red pyramid" posters which show the enormous growth of state expenses in twenty years. 

Referring to the attempts of the machine to discredit Mr. Pratt's figures, Mr. Clement said that he would agree to have Mr. Pratt meet any speaker they desired and answer any objection to the figures of the expert. He also repeated his challenge of the correctness of the so-called machine figures to the auditors of the Vermont railroads with a penalty if such were found to be correct. 

Mr. Allen thereupon introduced as the next speaker of the evening Mr. Burke of Burlington.

Mr. Burke told of his fight against graft in Burlington and declared that President Roosevelt was the greatest democrat since Jefferson, and the greatest republican since Lincoln.

In the present campaign he urged the voters to support a man, who offered them a remedy rather than one who declares that none is needed, and takes refuge in a policy of concealment of the conditions as they actually existed. The mayor then referred to his endeavor to represent fairly both republicans and democrats and added that the present crisis called for a man with something better than a reputation for being an exemplary citizen and "A nice man to meet." 

He predicted the election of the fusion ticket in September and stated further that the remedy for an ulcer was not a poultice but the knife.

RANDOLPH RALLY. 

Orange County, the supposedly republican stronghold, was invaded on August 9th with a rally at Randolph. Mr. 

H. C. Colbert presided and he introduced himself as a Jeffersonian candidate of fifty-two years standing and referred humourously to the usual biennial amusement of the democrats, reading the announcement of the usual 30,000 to 35,000 republican majority. A party intrenched for years behind a barbed wire fence always becomes corrupt. It is high time that the people began to vote and think for themselves. He presented Vernon A. Bullard, the fusion candidate for attorney-general.

Mr. Bullard began by saying that this year was the first time that he had ever appeared on the platform with a republican and in explanation he told of the connection in Burlington when the independents marched into the democratic convention hall and sat down with them. 

It was a great day for Vermont and Mr. Clement had not swallowed democracy; neither had democracy swallowed

Mr. Clement. They were rather standing shoulder to shoulder for the defeat of a condition which permits two or three men to control a great political machine that controls the state.

This, next to a standing army, is the greatest misfortune that can come to a state or a nation. 

He also told of the need of going outside of the party to secure reform and he predicted that Mr. Clement would be the next governor.

The republican platform admitted that state expenses were too much, but the orators of the machine have been claiming that we haven't spent too much money. If the 

Waterbury asylum has accomplished its purpose of saving the towns money, why has the tax rate not gone down?

Mr. Bullard pointed out that Orange County under its purely republican administration had steadily lost in population; one thousand acres of land had grown up to waste and the only towns that had gained were those represented by independents or democrats in the last legislature.

Vermont was becoming a mere hatchery for Western States. The speaker vigorously attacked the caucus law and told of his unavailing effort to have it modified. The principal advocates were C. C. Fitts and Fletcher D. Proctor, who wanted to make a man buy a collar himself whenever he pays a dog tax. As a reward for his labors, in the opinion of the speaker, Mr. Fitts has been made the first attorney general of Vermont. 

For a candidate, Mr. Bullard made the argument against the usefulness of the office of attorney-general and he added that the principal thing the present incumbent had done was to mix and furnish a coat of whitewash for the officials involved in the investigation of state institutions. 

Chairman Colbert then introduced Mr. Clement as a good business man, the father of the local-option law, and the man who compelled the auditor of the state to show the books to the people who owned them. 

Mr. Clement devoted a part of his time to answering questions put to him by a man in the audience, as well as a refutation to the false statements of the machine press and speakers. He denied the charge of having a secret coal rate with the Bristol road, and said the daily falsehoods of the machine must arise from a panic.

He also paid his respects to the commission and their absurdities, making satirical references to its uselessness.

Double taxation and good roads were also touched upon during the course of the address.

WINDSOR RALLY. 

The Windsor rally was held in the afternoon of August 

IIth in the town hall, George F. Guernsey presiding. Mr.

Guernsey introduced as the first speaker Percival W. Clement, as the man Vermont needs to do her house cleaning. 

During the course of his address, Mr. Clement referred to James L. Martin, who had undertaken to explain state expenses. The gentleman is not an accountant, he said, but a lawyer, which no doubt gives him a right to speak on any subject, whether he knows anything about it or not.

Screw Speaking of the purchasable vote, he said, it gave him no uneasiness as any fellow who was willing to sell out had received his price before now. References to good roads, taxation and conditions at the house of correction were dwelt upon. 

Mayor Burke arrived about an hour after the rally had begun, owing to a late train, but he was in time for a speech. 

He proposed to call things by their right names without mudslinging no votes in that, he said. He proposed to answer the challenge of any republican press for one specific instance of graft, and then he proceeded to give several. 

The definite instances of graft were related in the files of the daily press, and were brought out in the investigation of the state asylum for the insane at Waterbury. The issue of February 17th, 1906, contained the evidence of Dr. Berry, who was in charge of the patients at Waterbury. He testified that on one occasion he had been given a vacation of three months under pay and had besides brought in and received pay for a special bill of three hundred dollars, contracted during that time. He confessed to having received a gift of thirty-five dollars from a firm who did business with the asylum, and had accepted ten dollars worth of medicine from another firm under the same conditions. This, he added, was certainly graft.

A trustee of the asylum had bought for one dollar a wagonette that cost $125, and would sell at auction for fifty dollars; also a three seated sleigh had been sold for ten dollars.

It cost $150. Numerous instances were on record of goods bought at higher than the market prices and the folly of the coal graft was again emphasized with additional evidence showing that secret rebates and combination among the dealers entered into the high price of coal used at Waterbury. 

A specific rebate was that allowed Elias Lyman, who received from five to fifty-one cents per ton rebate on Alburgh coal. 

Most public officials regarded public office as a goose to be plucked. Perhaps it was not time, but it looked to him as though "there was a little something" in the coal trade. 

Speaking of Mr. Clement, he said that he supported him because he saw a chance through him to give our beloved state a much needed cleansing, and quoted an example the union

of the Jeffersonian democrats and Lincoln republicans under the leadership of Aldrich in Rhode Island, a brother to the noted republican boss. 

A sarcastic reference to the lack of discipline in Windsor prison was made, also the name of Winston Churchill, running for governor of New Hampshire on a reform ticket, was mentioned.

The rally closed with a prediction by the speaker that the fusion ticket would be elected. 

BELLOWS FALLS RALLY. 

From Windsor the party went to Bellows Falls where a rally was held on the same evening. The democratic and independent town committee met at Hotel Windham and marched to the opera house, escorting Messrs. Clement and 

Burke, and then took places on the platform. Dr. J. T. 

Rudder called the meeting to order after a brief concert by the band and he introduced as the presiding officer of the evening J. J. O'Connor.

Mr. O'Connor made a brief speech in which he spoke of the purpose of fusion in eliminating bossism in the state and the fight against machine rule. He was glad to note the interest in the campaign as evidenced by the large audience and congratulated the democrats for their patriotism in placing the good of the state above party. He also paid his respects to the machine press that was pointing out Mr. Clement's followers as mere barbers, plumbers, blacksmiths, etc., and added those were the very people who were going to elect him governor. The governor was thereupon introduced by Mr. O'Connor.

Mr. Clement touched on the utter failure of the machine orators or newspapers to account for the great bulk of the increase in state expenses and added that the ring seemed to think that these figures, like the "red pyramid" were only for politicians. All the common people had to do was to pay the deficit. If publicity for such things was wrong, we might as well make it free-for-all race and let every man help himself from the public treasury.

If the same policy was followed in a man's private affairs there would be only one result-that man would go broke. 

Property values were steadily decreasing and the only way to stop it was to quit wasting money. 

Mayor Burke was introduced next and he spoke along the same lines as at the Windsor rally. 

BETHEL RALLY. 

The next fusion rally was held at Bethel on the night of August 13th. The chairman of the meeting was Dr. L. W. 

Clough, and after two songs by the colored quartette, Dr.

Clough made a few remarks saying that the time had come for the voters to choose their state and national representatives, and added that the same care ought to be exercised in the selection of public servants as selecting those in private employment. The election heretofore had meant that one man would shake the plum tree and the fruit would fall; it now looks as though that man would have to buckle on his creepers and climb if he wanted any plums. Mr. McCarthy of Barre was thereupon introduced to the audience. 

Mr. McCarthy declared himself as a plain working man, who knew more about plugging a hole into a piece of granite than he did about a public address. He challenged anyone to open debate on the platform where everyone in the audience could see them. Continuing, Mr. Halvosa declared that no matter how much money was used on election day, there was not enough in the whole State of Vermont to buy the labor vote. 

He spoke of the impersonal element of the campaign, stating that he was not advocating either Mr. Clement or Mr. 

Proctor, but simply the best interests of the State. 

The speaker dwelt at length on the increase in state expenses, mentioning the $26,000 decrease in value of Vermont farming lands in the last twenty years due to increased tax rates caused by careless management of state affairs. The opponent of the fusion movement, Mr. Halvosa showed that the only corporations whose tax rate had been appreciably raised were the savings banks, which cater to the working classes. many Mr. Halvosa spoke with scathing ridicule of the incompetents who made up the tuberculosis commission. He told of their visit to the granite workers of Barre, a great of whom are affected with this dread disease. A meeting was held for the benefit of the workmen, who attended in the hope of gaining some practical information on fighting the "white plague." Instead of the relief they sought, various commissioners outlined elaborate and expensive cures, which would cost more money than any of their auditors could earn. 

One of the members, a certain Mr. Bullard, told to the disgust

and disappointment of the audience how he had cured himself by taking a Pullman car for Colorado, and there engaging the topmost room in the best hotel to be found, he cured himself by sleeping with his windows open, and eating only the best food and having the most skillful medical attendance. 

This commission, the speaker declared, did not offer one practical suggestion by which the poor man could fight the awful malady. It was akin to the railroad and other useless commissions which cost the state many hundreds of dollars. 

The speaker closed his speech with an appeal to the voters of the state to think carefully before casting their ballots. 

If a man gave the question the amount of thought it deserved, the speaker said there could be little doubt as to the way he would cast his ballot.

The next speaker was Elisha May of St. Johnsbury, who dealt with the various issues of the campaign, explaining the reasons for the fusion of the democratic and independent party for the good of the State. 

At the conclusion of Mr. May's address, there were loud calls for John H. Senter, who was on the speakers platform. 

Mr. Senter replied with a short address in which he thanked them for their testimonial of esteem, but excused himself from a long speech because of a sore throat.

He also dwelt upon the refusal of the auditor to open the books to him and his subsequent resort to the courts. He warned the audience against the use of money in elections and declared that he would rather have the votes of fifty honest men in this state, and no money, than to be elected by a majority of the votes secured by corrupt methods.

Every vote that is bought, he affirmed neutralizes the vote of one honest man, and if this republic is to be main-

tained, the purity of the ballot must be preserved. It was within the power of the honest to make the ballot pure. If when it was known that any man or party were buying votes -and these purchases are always known-the honest voters of every political party, should join together and cast their ballots against the candidate in whose interest such votes were bought, the corruption that existed four years ago would disappear. This year we know for a fact that Proctor money has been planted to buy these votes. 

That the McClellan machine in 1902 bought votes throughout the state in counties where they were purchasable, Addison County being no exception, that the Proctor forces are using similar methods, and that such action cannot be too strongly condemned was the statmeent made by Percival

Mr. Davenport of Brattleboro followed and he spoke on double taxation, the insane pauper law, which has increased the number of persons cared for by the state two and one-half times, and the cattle scandal were discussed. He pointed out that while the population has increased 3½ per cent and the wealth 7½ per cent, the expenses have increased, according to Auditor Graham's own figures 289 per cent. SPRINGFIELD RALLY. 

Mr. Clement and Mr. Childs were driven to Springfield where another rally was held the same evening.

Previous to the rally, the Springfield Brass Band gave a concert at the hotel, followed by another one in front of the opera house, and later the band played a selection inside of the hall. Mr. W. H. H. Putnam acted as chairman of the meeting and introduced the speakers. 

Mr. Clement was introduced first and the principal point which he dwelt upon was that the fusion campaign sprang directly from the people, and was not manufactured. The republican campaign, he said, was supplied and kept going by its own bosses, and the people had nothing to do with it. 

The political machine of the state cannot effect reform within itself. If it were not for the graft, the machine would go to pieces. Reform was bound to come, and it would come from the people.

In 1890, Mr. Clement said, a republican convention met at Montpelier to retrench the burden of expenses of the state. 

What did it amount to, he asked, when the expenses increased $117,000 in the last biennial term.

Mr. Clement told how he started a bill to investigate the auditor's accounts, how it passed the Senate and how it was killed in the House. In 1904 he tried to examine the auditor's books, but was denied the right, Auditor Graham stating that there were things in the books they did not want the people to know. It took Mr. Clement a year and a half and a mandamus from the Supreme Court was necessary before any man could see the books or papers. He declared the books would never again be closed. One great fault with the auditor's office, he said, was the fact that the Proctor people never mention revenues in their speeches. 

Taking up the cattle commission, Mr. Clement stated that Dr. Rich sells cattle to the Swift Beef Company for $7.00 each. They are cut up for meat and resold at $50. He said we have been eating tuberculosis beef for the last two years, and Attorney General Fitts tells the people a lot about what he does not know and after six months will do something about the meat scandal.

Mr. Child was the next speaker, and he said that he had been a hopeless democrat all his life, but that they had a chance to join hands with the independent party, throw aside partisanship and fight for a common cause, for the benefit of

the Vermont government. He told of the stringent law passed in 1902 by the Vermont legislature with regard to the sale of diseased cattle and providing punishment by fine, and asked why Attorney General Fitts devoted his spare time to campaigning. He said it was probably to effect his own political salvation. He declared that the fusion party had discovered enough graft to keep Mr. Fitts busy for a year. 

WOODSTOCK RALLY. 

Woodstock was invaded on the following evening by the fusion party, and a rally was held in the town hall. The republican party ran a special train from Woodstock to accommodate those who wished to attend the republican rally at White River Junction, but, in the words of chairman William S. Eaton, "there seemed to be plenty of people left in Woodstock."

Chairman Eaton in a brief speech introduced Mr. Bullard, who referred to the so-called "renegade democrats and renegade republicans" said to compose the independent party. Mr. Bullard said that he was not an advocate of the election of himself-or of any special candidate, and had never asked a voter of Vermont to support him, but he had simply stood upon certain principles and asked the voters to support them. He referred in sarcastic terms to the machine posters, which had been stuck up in various places about the state and which urged the voters to support a victory for the schools, the posters seeming, he said, to claim for the republican party some special service on behalf of the Vermont schools. The latter subject, he declared, was very dear to him and he had taken the trouble to look up how Vermont stands as compared with other states on the question of education. He found that whereas fifty years ago Vermont stood among the first of all of the states of the Union as regards school attendance and attendance to educational matters, it was today twenty-seventh in this respect. 

Another phase of the machine, "Decency in Politics" was given a share of the speaker's attention. He referred in scathing terms to the corrupt campaign in the past, in which money has been used to corrupt votes, defying the popular

will. He paid tribute to Mr. Clement and his legislation for the benefit of the voters and added that his distinguished services in behalf of local option and his suit against the state auditor, which permitted the Vermont voter to examine his own accounts, were enough in themselves to entitle him to the support of every honest voter in his contest for the governorship.

Mr. Bullard spoke also of the evils of double taxation as he had observed it in connection with his work among the workingmen of Burlington. He advised his hearers to vote for Mr. Clement and Mr. Pape, if they expected relief from the conditions as described. 

Mr. Eaton thereupon introduced Mr. Clement as the next speaker. Mr. Clement referred to the large audiences which have gathered everywhere to hear his arguments in behalf of fusion and the principles that fusion stands for and added that a general interest in political matters now pervaded the state to an extent which must be alarming to the managers of the machine. The latter, he said, had systematically manipulated the caucuses and party primaries in the past for their own selfish interests.

The machine, he declared, cannot create reform, as it needs the oil of graft and patronage to keep the cog wheels moving. Continued applause followed this assertion that the people are coming into their own, and also when he told of the futile attempts of himself and others to effect reform within the party. He said he was getting tired of waiting for reform by the ring politicians, who controlled the republican party. He also spoke of the attempt of the Proctor machine to influence voters by improper methods and said the way to rebuke that sort of thing was to vote against the candidate in whose interests it was made.

LUDLOW RALLY. 

The rally at Ludlow was held in the town hall on September 2nd.

Mr. Percival W. Clement was the first speaker and he spoke of the heavy increase in the state expenses, which, he said, had more than quadrupled in twenty-two years. He said that a portion of this increase was justified by the establishment of the hospital for the insane at Waterbury, the soldiers home at Bennington, and the state industrial school at Vergennes, but said that there still remained nearly half a million dollars unaccounted for. He dissected the management of the state hospital, declared that graft existed to a slight degree in fuel and supplies; but affirmed that the chief graft lay in an excessive number of doctors and nurses at the hospital. He showed that one nurse was employed for each six patients, as against one nurse was employed for each ten patients in Massachusetts and Connecticut. 

He said that some saving had been effected in the biennial period last past, but that it was the wrong kind of saving, as it was made in the food for the patients. He showed that the total cost of the institution per patient was excessive as compared with other states. He criticised severely the state auditor, Horace F. Graham, showing that on or about the end of the fiscal year ending June 30, 1906 not a scratch had been made on the books of the state regarding the expenses of state institutions for that year. 

After exposing the traffic by the state cattle commission in diseased meat, and their subsequent use for food, Mr. 

Clement spoke of Dr. Rich as veterinary of the cattle commission, condemning tubercular cattle for the state and then of the same Dr. Rich as the paid employee of Swift & Company passing the same cattle for use as food. 

The commission, he said, would turn down bids of $13 head for these cattle and then sell the same diseased animals for food at seven dollars per head to a company dealing per

in food products. He treated of double taxation and said that the real estate owner should be allowed to offset the amount of the mortgage on his property against the value, and should be taxed only on the amount he has invested, while the mortgage should be taxed against the holder in the town where it was recorded.

He spoke of the decline of farm values in Vermont in twenty years, amounting to $22,000,000. This decline he ascribed to double taxation, and the vast increase in state expenses. He asked if there was a farm which was worth that it would have brought twenty years ago, and added there was hardly one which would sell for the cost of the buildings and fences on it, leaving nothing for the value of the land. 

In closing Mr. Clement made a plea for the purity of the ballot.

He was followed by Mr. James E. Burke of Burlington who spoke on graft in the state institutions and on clean politics.

WATERBURY RALLY. 

The Waterbury rally of the republican party was held in the Village hall on August 3rd. The meeting was called to order by Mr. C. L. Moody, member of the town republican committee, who introduced the Hon. Fletcher D. Proctor, naming him as the next governor.

Mr. Proctor said that the more interest people take in state politics and state institutions, and the more we know

about them, the better it is for the state. We ought to know more than we do about these things. He showed how the progress of civlization, which is on everywhere, was evidenced in our institutions, in the schools, and the working out of these problems of highways. Improvement has been demanded, and there has been great improvement along all these lines. Because of this progress the expense of the State naturally increased. 

In regard to the charge of graft, or rather stealing, which has been brought against the State government by the opposition party through its literary bureau, Mr. Proctor read an extract from the statements made by Mr. Pratt, an expert who accompanied the State commission in their investigation of our institutions. His report based on painstaking investigation showed that in none of our institutions was any graft or rake-off possible, nor could any evidence be found of any such rake-off or graft in the past. There is no need to conceal the true state of affairs but great harm is being done and the fair name of Vermont is being brought into disrepute by the circulation of unfounded charges for political purposes, something which should be strongly condemned by every true man. 

Mr. Clement in his personal platform is absolutely silent on the subject of schools. Mr. Proctor said that the democratic platform merely states that it is in favor of keeping them at the highest standard. In all the reforms which have been demanded or urged through Mr. Clement or his bureau, there has never been a mention of the public schools. The schools have greatly improved, but there should be more rapid and marked improvement. The State should assume more responsibility. Mr. Proctor said that we should strive for equality both of school taxes and the school privileges. He believed that a better administration of the present law was more needful than new laws on this subject. 

Mr. Proctor in speaking on the rotten borough charges of Mr. Clement pointed out how the present system worked to the advantage of the small towns as well as the large ones. 

This system was adopted by the fathers of the State, and has always worked so well that there has never been the least suspicion of corruption.

It is the duty and right of all, said the speaker, to know about our taxation as it touches all. He reviewed the history of the corporation tax, first imposed twenty years ago, and the collateral inheritance tax, and showed how the revenue derived from these two taxes, amounting now to $713,000 has taken the place completely of the direct tax for carrying on the State government. 

Double taxation has been the subject of many accusations of Mr. Clement's literary bureau. This is an evil, but it is one of which it is a question how it can be relieved and no satisfactory solution has yet been reached. Mr. Clement's platform offers no solution, but it is simply in favor of some law to relieve double taxation. Mr. Clement is no more honest in wishing to alleviate this evil than is the republican party. Mr. Proctor believed the matter should be discussed and he favored the appointment of a committee to investigate carefully the subject with research so complete as to give a satisfactory solution of this question. 

School expenses have increased because we have improved the schools, but in his comparison, Mr. Clement makes no allowance for growth. The real test is whether the people get what they are paying for, said the speaker, and whether they could get it for less. There is room for greater improvement, but no one would begrudge the progress we have made in the treatment of the inmates of our institutions during the past twenty years.

There should be economy and business methods in State management, in particular the system of the courts should be investigated with a view to improve them.

If elected governor, and I expect to be, Mr. Proctor declared, my determination will be to administer the affairs of Vermont according to modern methods and for the best welfare of the State. The nearer Vermont comes to performing her duty according to modern standards of civilization, he said, the better it will be for the people and the better for the fair name of Vermont. Vermont has a higher duty to perform than to live cheaply.

RICHFORD RALLY. 

The Richford rally was held in Ayers Opera House on 

August 9th, the speakers being Fletcher D. Proctor and Attorney General Fitts. Music was furnished by the Richford 

Cornet Band and the Crescent Glee Club of St. Albans. The 

Hon. B. H. Coombs of East Berkshire presided and introduced 

Mr. Proctor, who spoke in part as follows: First he denied that Vermont had been stationary for the past thirty years and added that it was forging ahead and keeping pace with its sister states.

Speaking of the charge by Mr. Clement that the House of Correction had purchased more than fifty per cent of the marble it had used of the Vermont Marble Company, Mr. 

Proctor said that during the past sixteen years the House of Correction had purchased $116,000 worth of marble and of this amount only $40,000 had been purchased of the Vermont Marble Company. If the Vermont Marble Company has marble to sell and will sell it as cheaply as any other company I see no reason why it should not sell marble to the House of Correction.

He brought vividly before the audience the pitiful inconsistencies of the two platforms which Mr. Clement is attempting to straddle by story telling, and then referred to the importance of the public schools, and in closing said that if elected governor, he would try to serve the State in accordance with its best interests.

The audience was given another song by the glee club, following which Mr. Fitts was introduced. He denied the charges of Mr. Clement that state institutions are mismanaged. He told of how he had employed a democrat, Mr. F. 

S. Pratt, an expert accountant, and turned him loose in the state institutions. He read from Mr. Pratt's testimony a report which showed that there was not one bit of evidence of wrong doing or mismanagement. He turned to Clement's charge that the figures at the State asylum are juggled and read from the expert's report that the accounts were properly kept and could not be improved upon. He showed how well the State's prison and House of Correction are managed so that the State receives a much larger sum for its prison labor than any other state. He denied that there is a discrepancy between the auditor's books and those of the State Treasurer, and showed that prior to 1902 certain expenditures were made by law without the order of the auditor, and how the law was changed in the session of 1902. That change in the law has muddled Mr. Clement. 

He went at length into the question of railroad rates and control of them by the State. He told how the investigation at Waterbury had disclosed a secret rate to one company and how the State had suffered from it. He showed how the re-

publican administration of the State had stopped it without the aid or suggestion of Mr. Clement. He showed how Mr. 

Clement had a special rate on coal from Rutland to New Haven for the Bristol Railroad, which Mr. Clement owned paying only twenty cents per ton when all others had to pay $1.25 per ton. He said that the republican party of the State had pledged itself to pass a law putting a penalty on that sort of thing and to give a square deal to everyone. 

Mr. Fitts ridiculed the cry of "smash the machine" and warned those who were growing fat on special privileges that they would have to "smash the machine" or the machine would smash the system that enabled them to grow rich by secret favor and discrimination. He lauded the republican party, its candidate for governor and added that the only thing that could be said against Fletcher D. Proctor was that he was the son of a rich man, and that it bespeaks the high character and strength of the man he has not frittered away the talents which have been given him, but he has made the most of them, and as a result stands today the foremost young man of Vermont and the logical character for the high office of governor. The rally closed with a song by the glee club.

NORTHFIELD RALLY.

Charles A. Plumley, chairman of the meeting introduced the speakers, Mr. Fletcher D. Proctor being the first speaker.

He favored strongly the retention of the State highway and State school tax, which the democratic party wants to abolish and advocated keeping abreast of the times in the care and management of our State institutions. He asked his audience if they begrudged the small amount expended on the soldiers home at Bennington for the care of those now old and feeble, who from, 61 to '65 gave the best of their lives that the nation might live? If we care to drop the present high standard of education and return to the old antiquated system of the district schools of years ago? He urged upon the voters the importance of a strict administration that would give a return of a full dollar's worth of result for each and every dollar expended. He believed that the State had a higher duty to perform than that of trying to see where a dollar could be squeezed, that she should do her duty by her poor unfortunates, the feeble-minded, deaf, dumb and blind, the insane and keep step with her sister states in the onward march to a higher and better civilization. 

Mr. Prouty was then introduced and he spoke for twenty minutes on the so-called machine politics, graft and the important questions at issue. 

Thereupon Hon. James J. Martin of Burlington, United

States District Attorney followed Mr. Prouty, and he took up the charges of the fusion party, and the fallacies and weaknesses of the opposition party. 

BRANDON RALLY. 

To accommodate the audience, a temporary speakers stand had been arranged adjoining the porch of the town hall, from which Mr. Proctor and Mr. Flynn spoke. Before the rally a concert was rendered by the Proctor Cornet Band, which rendered several fine selections in front of the town hall.

Dr. C. W. Peck, who placed Mr. Clement in nomination at the great convention of 1902, presided at the rally, which was opened by the singing of the two campaign songs by A. J. 

Maxham, the veteran singer. Dr. Peck then in a brief speech introduced Mr. Proctor as the next governor of Vermont. 

Mr. Proctor at once entered into the subject of state expenses, the care and maintenance of our institutions, the public schools, and State highway question. Mr. Proctor referred to the cry of graft by Mr. Clement and told of the work of the special commission appointed by the governor to investigate state institutions. He quoted from the testimony of Mr. Pratt, the expert accountant, giving all a clean bill of health. Mr. Proctor told of the invitation by the commission to Mr. Clement to appear before that body and furnish evidence of proof of graft and of Mr. Clement's failure and inability to do so, and of the questions at issue, with which Mr. Clement had repeatedly been interrogated and to which he did not dare to attempt to reply. 

Mr. Proctor said that Mr. Clement's personal convention platform is simply a restatement of his hobby that an economic administration of our state affairs would permit the construction of a complete and permanent system of good roads throughout the State within a few years. It is a thoroughly misleading proposition. No good business enterprise would start out on a large and long extended expenditure on the face of some hoped for saving. Good business absolutely dissociates the two propositions. He said we ought to build good roads and save what we can by economical administration, but the two things have absolutely nothing to do with each other.

Mr. Proctor said that Mr. Clement's platform called for the abolition of the State highway tax which is characterized as an effort to bleed the larger towns. He contended that the 

State highway tax and the State school tax are right in principle and are not unfair towards the larger towns and cities. 

The State has a duty with respect to schools and highways generally and may properly raise money to perform that duty. The highways of the State, for example, are for the common use of the State to a certain extent and it is not right

nor fair that small towns with a large mileage of highways should be compelled to assume the entire burden of through roads.

He said the democratic party platform is declaring contrary to Mr. Clement's whole theory of State aid for highways. Mr. Clement's first political advertisement claimed that highway taxes are an enormous strain upon the smaller towns and that the remedy was State Highways, built and maintained by State aid. If the people should and can undertake the construction of permanent highways out of common State funds, why may it not raise a common State tax for the purpose? It is the same thing in principle whether money is raised by a direct tax or from corporation taxes or saved by economy. It is the State money and belongs accordingly to the grand list, to the people generally of the State. When you take it and put it into highways and Mr. Clement admits that ought to be done, you are doing the very thing which his democratic platform calls "bleeding the larger towns and cities" to relieve the smaller towns.

The speaker said that the whole highway matter resolves itself into this. Mr. Clement stands committed to the abolishment of the State highway tax without a substitution of anything in its place, but simply promises to build a complete system of roads out of economy in State administration. The republican platform proposes to continue the State highway tax to improve the method of spending it by stricter supervision over its location and expenditure. Mr. Proctor added that he believed also that the State should contribute something from its general fund to build permanent highways as the finances of the State would warrant it without the creation of a State debt or the levying of an additional or direct tax.

Mr. Proctor said in all that Mr. Clement and his literary bureau have put forth regarding the increase in our state expenses, we find no acknowledgement of the improvements which have been made in our State institutions, of the new burdens which the State has assumed and of the increased activities for her people which she has undertaken; but the expenses of the present are unblushingly compared with the

expenses of the past, leaving the impression that similar things are being compared when in fact they are not. He referred to the fact that the expenses of the national government are twice as large as they were a few years ago, but that it did not follow that the national government is extravagantly administered. Instead of simply comparing the gross expenses of today with what they were some ten years ago the only fair way is to take those expenses in detail, consider what changes, improvements, or enlargements have been made and whether they have been wisely made. The real question, he said, is whether the people today are getting what they are paying for or whether it could be produced for less.

Mr. Proctor then paid a tribute to the State, and then 

Mr. Maxham sang a couple of more songs.

Dr. Peck introduced next Edward B. Flynn of Spring field, who said that as Mr. Proctor had devoted himself particularly to the men, he wanted to talk to the women. He urged those who had republican husbands to persuade them to attend the republican caucuses and vote the straight republican ticket; if anyone had a democrat for husband pray for him.

He paid his compliments to Mr. Clement, and said that the question of the campaign for the opposition was simply how many voters each could throw for the other to try to get elected. He said that the democrats had many good men to nominate for governor, but passed them all by and placed at the head of the ticket a second-handed republican refrigerator.

Mr. Flynn took up Mr. Clement's charges of graft. He told of Mr. Clement's failure to prove his case and showed up 

Mr. Clement as a grafter in his handling of the coal business at Bristol, when he had shut out all competition by employing a rebate of $1.05 per ton on all coal shipped there paying but twenty cents per ton from Rutland, while all others paid $1.25. He discussed at length the issues of the campaign and emphasized the points made by good stories. Mr. Flynn spoke especially to the young voter on the importance and sacredness of the ballot and strongly urged them to think right and

start right. He closed with a tribute to the grand old party of Lincoln, Collamer, Garfield, McKinley, who had promised and done so much towards making the State what it is today and guaranteed the fulfillment of the present republican platform. 

RANDOLPH RALLY. 

The Randolph rally was held in Dubois and Gay's Hall on the evening of August 15th. The Bethel Band gave a short concert in the square between 7 and 8 o'clock, and then led the big crowd into the hall, where the seats were rapidly filled. 

The Hon. Dave H. Morse was the presiding officer and in a happily worded speech in which he paid high tribute to the party and its candidates introduced Mr. Proctor. 

Mr. Proctor discussed educational matters first and then he called attention to the fact that in the platform of the fusion candidate, the subject of public schools had not been mentioned. He believed that the children of today needed a better and more general education, that the schools can and should be better and that higher efficiency and better results could be obtained particularly in the rural communities by trained and skillful supervision and that without additional cost. He believed that it was the duty of Vermont to keep abreast of the times and educate her children for the upbuilding of the State and the nation. 

He referred to the cry of graft sent abroad by Mr. Clement and his emissaries and deprecated the unfounded charges created for political purposes through which the fair name of the State was being dragged in the mire. Mr. Proctor claimed that such statements were all very wrong, but their repeated reiteration at home and abroad was a great injury to the State and that he did not believe that the people would stand for such a thing.

The speaker gave a detailed account of the purchase of marble by the House of Correction from the Vermont Marble 

Company, and showed that the average purchases by the State for a year amounted to $2,500, and he asked his audience if they thought for a minute he was trying to corrupt the Legislature and manipulate political machines for the profit that was in that amount of business.

He referred to Mr. Clement's participation as chairman of the finance committee of the Senate in 1900 in reporting the bill favoring a direct tax on the people of fifteen cents, which cost the town of Randolph $3,291. The passage of this direct tax and the killing of the bill advocating an increased tax on corporations saved the Rutland Railroad of which 

Mr. Clement was president $28,000.

Mr. Proctor also discussed the subject of highway taxation generally, and then he referred to Mr. Clement's Windsor address in 1904 where the latter said that the State paid in the care of the insane in 1871, $2,000 and in 1901, $158,000 and that if the State had put the promoters of the Waterbury asylum in Brattleboro before they got the bill through apparently the State would be better off by more than $100,000. 

Such a statement, said Mr. Proctor, could not come from a fair-minded man. In 1871, the State only cared for criminal and transient insane, having no settlement in any town in the State. In 1901 it was caring for all the insane poor in the 

State and yet Mr. Clement withheld an explanation of the difference.

As far back as 1844 the appropriation of the State for the care of the insane was only $300. În 1858 a law was passed by which the State undertook to care for the transient insane poor having no legal settlement in any town. In 1878, a law was passed by which the State undertook to care for all insane poor having no legal settlement, whether they were transients or not, and in 1886 the State took the entire burden of the care of the insane of the towns and care for all insane people in the State. He said that the whole idea of taking care of the insane in asylums was a matter of gradual growth. 

That the State got along once without any insane asylum within its borders, but that he did not believe in reverting to the old method nor of putting the expense of the insane back upon the towns.

Mr. Proctor contended that the only cost figures worth considering is whether or not the weekly cost per patient is right and proper and he showed notwithstanding Mr. Clement's statements to the contrary the average weekly cost per capita for the care of the insane for the biennial term ending

June 1903 was only $3.58 per patient, compared with the average cost per patient in 14 similar institutions, eight of them in Massachusetts was $4.04 per week. Considering the difference in cost of supplies, distance from markets and the fact that Waterbury takes care of all classes of insane was a remarkable showing.

He also referred to the fact that Mr. Pratt, who is now 

Mr. Clement's personal accountant after making a thorough investigation for the State's investigating committee, said that the Waterbury asylum accounts, both in system and accuracy, compared favorably with accounts of well managed business enterprises, and that in his opinion no change should be made which would be of any substantial benefit to the 

State. 

Mr. Proctor pointed out that the weak point in the 

State's relation to its insane is not any undue cost for their care but a tendency on the part of some towns to send a class of unfortunate persons to the asylum that could be safely cared for at home. This matter ought to be carefully investigated and by closer watchfulness on behalf of the State in regard to the patients sent there, the number of patients could perhaps be kept down and something be saved in that way. He said that it requires constant vigilance to keep a business enterprise upon an economical basis and such vigilance should be given to the asylum and all of the other state institutions, but that Vermont cannot afford as a matter of common duty and civic responsibility to maintain this or any other of her institutions in any way not fully up to the standard of modern civilization.

BARRE RALLY. 

The Barre rally was held on the evening of August 15th in the city hall. After a short concert by the band in the hall, the gathering was called to order by Mr. R. S. Currier, who introduced Mr. Proctor.

Mr. Proctor spoke for some thirty minutes in which he touched upon the various issues of the campaign and explained to his audience several of the canards that Mr. Clement had thrown out. Mr. Proctor went into the details of the

graft charges, defended the State institutions and made a plea for the upbuilding of the rural schools. He spoke also on taxation and made suggestions for the consideration of a commission to deal with the problem of double taxation. 

The Hon. Horace F. Graham, State Auditor, who was unexpectedly called on owing to the illness of Congressman Foster gave a concise account of the expenses of the State institutions. He compared the expenses of twenty-five years ago with the present, dealing particularly with the courts, and gave the reasons for the increases, most of which had resulted in the saving of thousands of dollars in fees to the State. Mr. Graham said that Mr. Clement had claimed that he was not a good book-keeper, and he did not deny that, but he challenged Mr. Clement to show where one dollar had got by him, where he did not have a lawful warrant for it. 

The band gave a couple of selections when the Hon. 

Charles H. Darling, former assistant secretary of the navy was introduced. Mr. Darling told of Mr. Clement's love for the common people, how he raised the rates on mileage books as soon as he got into power on the Rutland Railroad, how he had reduced the track laborer's wage from $1.25 to $1.10 per day, and how the wages of all the Bennington railroad men had been cut, when that road was absorbed by the 

Rutland railroad. He said that Mr. Clement never had an idea of running for governor until Fletcher D. Proctor's candidacy was announced and Mr. Clement's sole idea was to beat Mr. Proctor.

Judge Darling told of Mr. Clement's advice to the republican State committee to spare no expense in the campaign of 1904, how Mr. Clement had subscribed $100 for the cause, but up-to-date that subscription had not been paid. 

He paid an eloquent testimonial to the greatness and worth of Senator Proctor. He also spoke of the State's responsibility and care of its poor and unfortunate, and went at length into the expenses and management of the state institutions.

To show

HINESBURGH RALLY. 

The Hinesburgh rally was held on the evening of August 18th on the fine and spacious natural amphitheatre extending from the band stand to the buildings on the west side of the square and in either direction it was filled with people. A short distance away from the speaker's stand floated in the breeze a large campaign flag bearing the names of Proctor and Prouty, while a large reflecting light illuminated the immediate vicinity. 

Mr. H. M. Page presided over the rally, at first introduced the well-known campaign singer, A. J. Maxham, who sang two selections.

The first speaker of the evening was Fletcher D. Proctor. 

He spoke of the presence of the large audience gathered as evidence of the widespread interest that is being taken in the present campaign and hailed this interest as a promising manifestation on the part of the people, who came to become more thoroughly informed concerning the affairs of the common weal.

Mr. Proctor then proceeded to speak of his conception of true progress and its significance for the people of Vermont. He then arraigned Mr. Clement for failing to help the committee of investigation of our State institutions, when he was invited to give them the benefit of any graft or wrong doing that he had discovered, and showed that the democratic candidate was now trying to arouse suspicion by senseless and baseless charges after the committee had completed its work. Mr. Proctor read from the report of Mr. Pratt, the very expert accountant whose authority Mr. Clement was trying to use in his manifold assaults on the good name of Vermont, showing that there was no ground for Mr. Clement's ridiculous and groundless charges.

Mr. Proctor demonstrated the pitiable plight to which 

Mr. Clement had been reduced by the contradictory statements of his two platforms and the democratic speakers and writers, and proved that Mr. Clement was insincere in conflicting promises made to the large towns and the small towns. 

In like manner he showed the absurdity of Mr. Clement's scheme of tax relief for the people as compared with what Mr. Clement did for the people when he was in the Legislature, where he was in a position to make both his vote and his voice count in behalf of the rights and interest of the taxpayers.

After two more campaign songs by Mr. Maxham, Congressman Foster was introduced. He showed what hard work Mayor Burke was having in trying to live down his declaration that Mr. Clement was "the arch-grafter of Vermont," and he spoke of the graft which Mr. Clement enjoyed as a result of the secret rate on coal delivered to the Bristol Railroad, which he still owns. Mr. Clement now says that he has no secret rate which is true, for it was changed as soon as it was discovered, but he does not say he never arranged for a rate which gave an advantage of $1.05 per ton as compared with other dealers in other towns.

Congressman Foster spoke with sarcasm of Mr. Clement's financial zeal and how he tricked Dr. Webb into buying the stock of the Rutland Railroad. He showed that the State had not been standing still as claimed by Mr. Clement, and

he pointed out different ways in which the people had progressed during the past quarter of a century, particularly in connection with dairying and manufacturing interests and our granite and other stone industries. Mr. Foster spoke at length of the progress which had been made under our present national policies. 

Turning to Mr. Clement's treatment of United States 

Senator Redfield Proctor, Mr. Foster showed that the democratic candidate must have been inspired by bitterness when he referred to "old Proctor." Mr. Foster discussed a variety of issues and he closed by inviting them to roll up a grand majority for the ticket in September. ALBANY RALLY. 

The rally at Albany was held in the afternoon of August 20th on the village green. Mr. Proctor reached Albany at 

2 o'clock and the Albany Cornet Band saluted him with "Hail to the Chief" as he passed through the cheering crowds to the hotel. Then the band escorted the speakers from the hotel to the speaker's stand, on the green. Mr. F. W. Tenney of Albany presided and introduced Mr. Proctor as the next governor. 

Mr. Proctor expressed his thanks to the audience for the large attendance and warm welcome and congratulated the town in maintaining the fine band it had. He discussed the issues of the campaign in general paying particular attention to the matter of state expenses and the care and maintenance of our institutions, taxation, public schools and highways, and unfair treatment of larger towns.

Mr. Proctor said that Clement's democratic platform charges the republican machine of Vermont with continuing the rotten borough system to maintain supremacy. In the original draft given out to the press, the charge was against the republican party, but in the fusion process at Burlington, Mr. Clement's favorite expression "machine" was substituted. The system referred to is the one which has always prevailed in this state by which each town and city irrespective of its population has one representative in the lower branch of the Legislature.

Mr. Proctor added that our forefathers as a counterbalance devised a legislative body of two houses, one elected upon the basis of population, and one upon geographical basis. 

The Congress of the United States is constituted in the same way, of a House elected upon the basis of population and a Senate of two members from each State irrespective of population. This dual system in the United States Congress is generally accepted. Mr. Proctor said that he had no apology to make for our legislative system. It was devised by our pioneer builders of the State, and it follows the national precedence and it has worked well in practice. 

He was followed by Mr. G. H. Prouty, who dwelt primarily with machine politics and the charge of extravagance in state expenses.

Mr. F. G. Fleetwood followed with a speech in which he referred to the convention in Burlington as a case of Jonah swallowing the whale. He likened Mr. Clement to the colossus of Rhodes standing with one foot on the democratic and the other on the independent platforms, beckoning on the voters to land him in the harbor at Montpelier. 1 The speaker proved that Vermont had not been tied to a post for the past fifty years, and to prove the assertion he called attention to the state's 45 savings banks with deposits of $49,430,000; its 221 creameries; its $63,000,000 in manufacturing establishments; $6,000,000 in lumber; $33,000,000 in agricultural products for 1905, an increase of nearly 48 per cent on the capital invested here in the past five years, and an increase in wages of 33 per cent. He insisted that 

Vermont was not hitched to a post but was progressive. He urged the voters for an increased vote for increased good by the party which accomplished things. 

Auditor Graham followed and he paid his personal compliments to Mr. Clement in no uncertain terms. He said that he was tired of denying the charges that Mr. Clement brought and hereafter he would show him up as he knew him. He exposed Mr. Clement's attitude on the labor question and showed how he had reduced the wages of labor at every opportunity.

Mr. Stanton was the next speaker and he dealt with the general issues of the campaign.

BRADFORD RALLY. 

The management of the Bradford Fair had learned of the presence of Fletcher D. Proctor in that vicinity and invited him to visit the fair. He drove to the grounds and he was escorted to the stage from which he spoke, and while being escorted, the Woodsville band played "Hail to the Chief."

Mr. Conant presented Mr. Proctor to the large audience that had gathered. 

Mr. Proctor said in part: "I thank you for this kind reception. It was very gratifying to meet so large and represent-

ative an audience and not to be compelled to talk about politics, such an affair as this is typical of the very best environment. We have growing towns and small cities but Vermont is essentially a rural state. We are rapidly developing manufacturing and mining industries, but we are primarily a farming community. One of the best instruments for helping the State to progress, and it is a fact some times overlooked, is that the State is really making quite a remarkable progress in many ways. The United States census bureau has recently published some interesting comparative figures as to the industries of Vermont in 1905, compared with what they were in 1900. They show that in these five years the capital invested in manufacturing enterprises in the State has increased $20,000,000 or 47.4 per cent. The value of its manufactured products has increased $11,500,000 or 22.5 per cent, and its total of wages paid in industrial enterprises $3,800,000 or 33 per cent. Although it is generally supposed that growth in this direction is chiefly in the cities in Vermont, the increase has been greater in the rural sections than in the urban districts. "But a more suggestive thing than this increase in our industrial life is the increase in our farm products in the State in 1900 was over $6,400,000 as against $5,500,000 in 1895, an increase of over $900,000 or 16.7 per cent. This is simply the butter and cheese from the factories and it does not include dairy butter and milk. "Notwithstanding the great size of the granite, marble and slate industries of the State, dairy products are today the greatest product of Vermont, and it is for the interest of the State that they always continue so. The best part of the life of the State is built upon the life of its smaller communities and I for one would rather see Vermont remain essentially a great farming rural section with all the blessings and virtues which go with that. It certainly is not easy to wrest a living and much less a competence from off these green hills or even to dig it out of the ground, and yet, in some way here in Vermont our savings bank deposits for these same five years, that is from 1900 to 1905 increased from $38,000,000 to over $49,000,000 or 29 per cent. Such is the spirit of pro-

gress everywhere that if we would hold our own with other states, we must be progressive. We want the best of all things and especially those things which makes life the better worth living.

Two things which are especially important to a rural community are good schools and good highways. About all the average man can give or leave to his children is an education and certainly he should have the opportunity to do that. 

With this end in view, it is the duty of Vermont to provide the best possible public school system.' 

Speaking of automobiles, Mr. Proctor said that of course they had come to stay, but that he thought we might as well consider whether they should not be limited by law to the use of certain of the highways. He suggested that they might well be kept off from some of our narrow hill roads, where the passing of automobiles and teams was impossible with a great danger to the occupants of the teams. He said that in time our horses would without doubt become used to them, and much of the present trouble done away with, but meanwhile he did not think it right that the men, women and children, who spend their summers and winters in Vermont should be debarred from their natural and rightful use of their roads because of the machines. He felt sure that the regular use of our highways by the people of Vermont, both for business. and pleasure was at present greatly interfered with. Whenever he saw an automobile and a horse meet his sympathy always went out to the horse.

BRATTLEBORO RALLY. 

The rally at Brattleboro was held on the historic common, shaded by noble elms. The speaker's stage faced the soldier's monument in front of which a large crowd had assembled. The Hon. J. L. Martin presided and introduced 

Mr. Proctor as the son of his father, Senator Proctor, and as the next governor of Vermont.

Mr. Proctor started by saying that Mr. Clement has been repeatedly urged by the press of the state and republican speakers to give some hint as to the proper remedy for double taxation; he has at last brought forth what his ad vertising bureau terms: Fusion's Definite Remedy." This allows the owner of real estate to offset the amount of his mortgage in the town where it is recorded. This plan seems simple, innocent and harmless on the face of it, but has often been tried in other states, and as often been found wanting. 

It was tried in Missouri and at the next session at the urgent request of the farmer interests, that had asked for its enactment, it was repealed. It was tried in Michigan and was repealed at a special session of the Legislature, called for that purpose. It has been tried in other states and given up as entirely unsatisfactory. In a State like Vermont with a fixed legal rate of interest, it practically prevents the borrowing of money on real estate, except in towns where the rate of taxation is so low that money loaned would bring a good return above the tax imposed. The tax rate in Vermont in 

1904, varied in different towns from 55 cents to $3.00. Under 

Mr. Clement's plan, no one would loan money in a $3.00 rate tax town and very few in a $2.00 tax rate town. The higher the tax rate, the higher would be the rate of interest exacted by the lender. It has been fully and clearly demonstrated that in practice this kind of a tax is eventually paid by the borrower, or otherwise, aside from the increased rate a scarcity of money would surely follow, as money would steadily flow out of the state, where it could obtain a better market. The payment of mortgages would be demanded, foreclosures would follow, and the condition of the borrower would be infinitely worse than under the present condition. 

You can readily see that our savings banks and Trust Companies that are now paying 32 per cent to depositors could not pay in addition to this tax on any mortgages they might hold in a $2.00 tax rate town, especially where they are loaning money on mortgages at 5 per cent as many of them are. 

The problem of double taxation cannot be remedied by any single provision as Mr. Clement suggests, but can only be satisfactorily solved by some readjustment of our whole system of taxation.

Mr. Martin said that there had been a great deal of talk about "the machine" that he had tried to find the machine but could not. He introduced Senator Dillingham as the man who could tell something of what that great machine known in the United States as the Republican Party had done for the world, by striking shackles from millions of slaves, and in developing and upbuilding this great nation. 

Senator Dillingham reviewed the grand onward march of progress and prosperity of the party, from its birth, fifty years ago, to the present. He told about the great prosperity which the State is now enjoying, and of her splendid standing among her sister states. He reminded his audience that during the past four years, Vermont had enjoyed the proud distinction of being one of the six of the forty-five states in the Union that had no direct state tax. He said that the state had been conducted at less expense than any state in the Union that had made progress, and he would have been ashamed of her if she had not made progress. He paid his compliments to Mr. Clement, showing that "the Bennington idea" was born when the people wanted Fletcher D. Proctor for governor. Mr. Clement's glaring record of inconsistencies in his political career was pointed out, and he reminded his audience that in all her history, no charges of a reflecting nature nor derogatory character had been made, until Mr. 

Clement thought that by such things he might squeeze himself into the governor's chair. 

Following Senator Dillingham, Mr. Martin made a brief address and closed by calling for three cheers for Mr. 

Proctor, which were given. The first Regiment band furnished music for the rally, closing with America.

His speech was brief, and he impressed upon his audience the importance of state matters. Mr. Proctor spoke of the charges made by Mr. Clement with reference to the insane asylum. He mentioned the increase in the number of insane patients and he referred to the time when such patients would either be cared for at home or else farmed out. 

He asked if the voters wanted to go back to that system and place the burden of expense in the individual towns. He referred to the expense of caring for the patients per week as $3.522 while the average in other institutions was $4.04. 

He asked if Vermont wished to complain of that showing?

Referring to the charges of graft being made by Mr. 

Clement, the speaker said that there had been a thorough investigation honestly and fairly conducted. He said that the committee in charge of the investigation had invited Mr. 

Clement to appear before it and give any evidence that he might have; but he never showed up. He stated that in all the literature issued from his literary bureau and in all the speeches made by Mr. Clement, not a single instance had been given where a dollar had been improperly diverted. 

He spoke of the unusually clean record of the State saying that no State had a cleaner record and he doubted if a State could be found with one as clean.

Mr. Proctor spoke of the public schools and referred to the lack of reference to them by Mr. Clement and his bureau. 

Mr. Proctor stated that he believed in a more efficient school system. He thought that it was a sacred duty for Vermont to provide for her children the best possible school system. 

Speaking of the tax question, Mr. Proctor referred to 

Mr. Clement's record as senator in the legislature where, through his influence, the bill providing for a corporation tax was killed. By killing the bill the speaker said Mr. 

Clement saved the Rutland Railroad $28,000 and at that time he was president of the road. On the same day the corporation tax bill was killed, Mr. Clement introduced a bill which provided for a direct tax on the people. While the corporation tax bill saved the Rutland road $28,000, the speaker said that the direct tax bill introduced by him cost the people of Burlington $19,000. That direct tax bill was the last direct tax ever voted. The speaker mentioned that the conditions now were such that for the present no direct tax was needed.

Mr. Proctor spoke of Mr. Clement's claim that the State had not progressed. The speaker claimed that under republican control conditions in Vermont had improved. He asked the voters if they wanted to go back to the roads of thirty years ago, if they wanted the old-time schools, if they wanted the old-time methods of caring for the insane. He asked if the voters begrudged the money for the state laboratory of hygiene. He said that that institution had saved the State

money, but, what was more important, it had saved life and suffering. He asked if the voters begrudged the money paid for the care of the feeble-minded children and for the care of the old soldiers. Mr. Proctor said that when the time comes that Vermont begrudged these appropriations, the wheels of progress would be turned back. He thought that instead of going back the State should reach out for better and more lasting results.

The speaker stated that if elected, and he added that he fully expected to be, he could only promise to endeavor to work on business principles and for the highest welfare of all the people. He said in closing that Vermont had a higher duty to perform than to see how cheaply they could care for the unfortunate and how cheaply the State could be operated. 

After this address, Mr. Proctor left the hall and proceeded to the station, where he took a train for Bristol. 

W. J. Van Patten was the next speaker. He stated that he believed in the man who had just left the platform. He thought that Mr. Proctor was one of the greatest men Vermont had produced and that he was the son of a worthy father.

Mr. Van Patten spoke of the charges made by Mayor 

Burke, that he had been fighting graft in Burlington. He refuted such charges and asked the voters if they believed that such men as Dr. Hawley, Robert Roberts and H. S.

Peck had been guilty of graft? He said that the mayor should be rebuked for making such statements as he had been making up and down the State. He closed by urging the voters to vote for the entire republican ticket, not forgetting the county ticket.

Mr. Joseph Auld was the next speaker, and he spoke of the State issues, saying that Mr. Clement had attempted to indict the whole State. He answered the arguments made by Mr. Clement that there had been mismanagement and extravagance in state affairs, and denied that the State had been tied to a post for thirty years. Mr. Auld spoke also of the county ticket and briefly referred to the representative fight.

Congressman Foster was then introduced and he said. that he was before the audience simply because the Attorney- General Fitts had not arrived. He referred to Senator Proctor's splendid career in the Senate, and added that he was one of the three United States Senators who had given Vermont a standing in the country out of proportion to her size. These three being Senator Morrill, Edmunds and Proctor.

The speaker referred to the cry of "too much Proctor." 

He mentioned that a similar cry was raised against Moses and that it has followed down the ages to Lincoln, Grant, McKinley and last of all Roosevelt. He referred also to the allegation made by Mr. Clement that people, if they wanted anything at Washington, had to go to Mr. Proctor. Then he read a list of some of the appointments given to Vermonters by the federal government, mentioning Governor-General 

Ide in the Philippines, Judge Henry C. Bates in the Philippines,

Judge W. P. Stafford in Washington, Interstate Commerce 

Commissioner Prouty and many others. He added that the list could not be excelled by any other state. He asked what fault people had to find with such appointments and queried whether Senator Proctor did not always appoint good men. 

He referred to the fact that one of Mr. Clement's speakers had referred to him as "Old Proctor." The speaker asked how the people of Vermont liked to have the senior United States Senator characterized on the platform as "Old Proctor.' 

Mr. Foster spoke on the campaign issues and he urged the voters to be true to the party, saying that the vote in Vermont was being watched all over the country and that if Mr. Clement should get a big vote or win, it would be heralded from coast to coast as an indication that the people were repudiating President Roosevelt. 

States Attorney Sherman was the next speaker. He spoke briefly on State issues and then touched upon county affairs. He mentioned the charges made by Mr. Clement that condemned cattle had been sold. He said that an investigation had been started and that everyone who had been connected with the affair, and that everyone who had sup-

posed to have any knowledge touching upon the matter had been summoned to appear before the grand jury which is to meet here this week.

Mr. R. E. Brown was the last speaker. He made a strong appeal for votes for the State and County ticket, and he referred also to the issues of the campaign. 

BRISTOL RALLY. 

The closing rally for Mr. Proctor was held at Bristol in 

Halley's Hall. Mr. W. A. Lawrence presided and introduced the speakers.

Mr. Bigelow was the first speaker, speaking for some forty-five minutes, dwelling especially upon the fact that there is no graft in the State, that State institutions are properly conducted and that supplies for State institutions are purchased according to the provisions of the law, at the cheapest figure possible.

Collector Darling was the next speaker and he spoke on the necessity of party organization, and that because a party has been in power is no reason why it should not be continued. 

About 9:20 o'clock, Mr. Proctor arrived, having made the trip from Burlington to New Haven Junction in 35 minutes. A special train brought him from New Haven Junction to Bristol.

He was given an ovation as he entered the hall. Mr. 

Proctor was introduced at once and he emphasized the fact that Mr. Clement's whole campaign had been one of vituperation rather than constructive criticism and quoted figures regarding the insane asylum, showing that Vermont cares for the insane cheaper than any other similar institution outside the state. He made a declaration for good roads, good schools, and the natural wards of the State. He closed by promising that if elected he would strive to do his best in conducting the State's business honestly, economically and according to up-to-date methods.

At the conclusion of Mr. Proctor's address, chairman 

Lawrence announced that the assemblage might come forward and shake hands with the next governor of Vermont.

When Mr. Proctor left, he was taken by special train to New Haven Junction and thence by another special train to Proctor. He was accompanied home by Benjamin Williams.

OTHER RALLIES. 

Besides these rallies, others were held in almost every hamlet, however insignificant, throughout the State, by men from the republican headquarters, who expounded the doctrines of republicanism to the inhabitants, and in addition repudiated the invectives of the Clement forces wherever they went.

The rallies as given heretofore were the ones in which 

Mr. Proctor took the leading part.

AT PROCTOR 

167 

CHAPTER IX. 

At Proctor 

The election returns were received at Proctor with great enthusiasm. As soon as the election had been conceded for 

Mr. Proctor, a bonfire was kindled on the town Commons and the bell of the Union Church proclaimed the glad tidings during the late evening hours. However it was not until the following evening that the real celebration took place. 

The impromptu celebration had its inception at a meeting of young men composed of George Z. Thompson, C. I. 

Hunter, Charles Erlandson and J. E. Euno, who took charge of the matter, and with the aid of other young men of the village, they soon had things moving. A string of electric lights illuminated the road from the old store to the residence of the governor-elect. The public buildings around the station were festooned with colored bunting and a large picture of Mr. Proctor adorned the entrance to the Y. M. C. A. 

Both the Rutland and the Proctor bands were present and played selections during the evening, and a display of fireworks was given in front of Mr. Proctor's residence. Bonfires were kindled on both of the mountains to the east and west of the village. Each sandbucket making its way over an adjoining mountain during the evening held a burning torch, which presented a very beautiful spectacle from afar. 

The members of the Proctor Boat Club had decorated their canoes with Japanese lanterns, which gave the whole scene on the Otter Creek a very beautiful appearance as the crafts slowly glided over the surface of the water. In reality, it seemed that the place about the home of the governor-elect had been transformed into a veritable fairyland for the occasion.

Word that a celebration was to take place quickly spread to the adjoining towns and Rutland. Many came by automobiles and by teams. Rutland sent a special crowded train load and in addition thereto six large touring cars car-

ried the political leaders, who made such a gallant fight for 

Mr. Proctor in Rutland city and county.

It was expected that Mr. Proctor would make an address during the evening, but instead he remained near his home receiving and acknowledging the good wishes of the people who filed by him. He apparently knew them all, calling them by their Christian names, having a pleasant word for the ladies, and a gentle pat for the children. If he missed a face or two, he asked for them. In speaking of this fact one lady remarked: "I believe Mr. Proctor knows every child in this town. If one of them goes away he is sure to notice the fact and inquire about it.' 

Between ten and eleven, especially at the latter hour, things began to quiet down, and the people from Rutland returned by the special train which had brought them to Proctor. The occasion is a memorable one in that it was by far the largest impromptu celebration ever attempted in Proctor and everything passed off smoothly.

CHAPTER X. 

Vicissitudes of the Campaign

During the campaign the two largest newspapers of the State took a leading position, as being the mouth-piece for the two factions. The Burlington Free Press championed the cause for Mr. Proctor; whereas the Rutland Herald was entirely pro-Clement. It was natural, therefore, that each should print such material as was favorable to its own side and derogatory to the other. 

Among the many epithets of the campaign, the following satirical one was printed in the Rutland Herald:

A PROCTOR PINK TEA PARTY. 

A Wayfaring Citizen reports the Events in Detail. 

The author has no apologies to offer and presents his travesty to the reading public with a deep feeling of thankfulness that this is a free country, that does not punish "lese majeste." If such an attack was made upon the "ruling house" of Russia or some other old world country, the author would expect to be secretly poisoned. But if this feeble effort at facetiousness raises the ire of the substantial citizen, who controls the destinies of our city, or causes a smile to rise to the face of the frivolous unregenerate, who vote as they choose and view with amusement the intrigues of our "king makers" the author will not have labored in vain. The actors in this little drama are all well-known members of our community-some a little too well known; they have all eaten of the crumbs that fall from the table of our "ruling house," some have felt its royal displeasure in days past, but being devotedly loyal and not over sensitive, have returned to the fold and now "lick the hand that struck them."

CONCLUSIONS 

173 

CHAPTER XI. 

Conclusions 

We will here summarize the campaign and its results. 

Fletcher D. Proctor had been seen and heard by thousands of people in every county in the State, and while it was not known before that he was an able and estimable man, he grew immeasurably in popular strength as well as in public esteem as a result of his direct contact with the people and his discussions of the issues of the day. Indeed it might be safe to say that most people realized that Fletcher 

D. Proctor would have been governor of Vermont, before, but for the circumstance in which he was placed. 

Percival W. Clement, as a leader of the fusion movement, proved as strong a fighter as in 1902, but he lacked the issues of that year, and was unable to make one that would stand the scrutiny of the thinking voter. This was the principal reason why he so completely failed. Large audiences came to hear Mr. Clement in his campaign hour, but he failed to convince them of the truthfulness of his charges against the republican administration or even favorably impress his personality upon them. In fact he was not a vote getter" and that was particularly emphasized because of the direct opposite qualities of his opponent. Mr. Clement had assumed an aristocratic attitude and tended to hold aloft from the people, as against the democratic demeanor of his opponent. This naturally reacted to the detriment of 

Mr. Clement and his hold upon the esteem and good will of the people. In fact Mr. Proctor was more 'democratic" than his democratic opponent. 

On the other hand, Mr. Proctor, without any pretensions as a finished orator, enthused his hearers, impressed upon them with the soundness of his reasoning, and made new and staunch friends wherever he went and spoke. In fact, during the campaign he seems to have obliterated the last flickering impression that he was simply "the son of his

father." He demonstrated to the people that he stood upon his own merits as a political candidate, and so well did he impress the voters that the cry of "too much Proctor" by the opposition was treated with utter indifference. 

Fletcher D. Proctor's record had been vigilantly and critically searched by his opponents in the campaign, and they failed to discover a single valid reason therein, why he should not be elected governor. The Clement men were forced to go to the ends of the state, and to lift up one stone after another in the political field in the vain hope that they might find some act that would weaken Mr. Proctor with the people. 

Wherever Fletcher D. Proctor came into contact with the people, he increased his hold upon their esteem, their respect and their enthusiasm. The people learned through personal contact with Mr. Proctor, as he went among them, that he was a man of unassuming manner; thoroughly democratic in his bearing and tastes; endowed with natural qualifications for public position; possessed of the courage of his convictions; anxious to promote the welfare of Vermont in every possible way; gifted with practical ideas as to the development of his State and her people. 

The victory for Mr. Proctor is all the more significant when one considers the combination of influences that was arrayed against him. Percival W. Clement, after considerable effort on the part of his lieutenants, ably directed, secured an endorsement of the State democratic convention, and he also caused an independent nomination to be filed for himself, and the other candidates placed on the ticker with him under the head of "fusion" thus providing a trap to catch the unwary, who might suppose they were choosing. between a straight democratic ticket and an independent ticket, when in reality they were voting for the same identical candidate no matter which of the two tickets they might select. 

Mr. Clement not only claimed the support of all democrats under the crack of the party whip, but he also sought the support of every republican, who for some cause, or no cause whatever, had become disgruntled and dissatisfied with

CONCLUSIONS 

175 his party. The labor leaders had also attached themselves to Mr. Clement, although he was not altogether in sympathy with the labor cause. The fact also, that Mr. Clement had been a champion of local option and high license enabled him to appeal to many republicans four years ago, and this one influence tended to draw a considerable portion of Mr.

Clement and his followers shouted "machine" and "ring" and "Boss rule" which are always popular cries with the unthinking, and every political appeal that could possibly be directed to partisan prejudice and class feeling was made with unblushing effrontery by the fusion party. 

A victory for Mr. Proctor under these circumstances was in no small degree a personal triumph, although leading republicans entered zealously into the campaign. Mr. Clement attacked prominent leaders of republicanism left and right to his own detriment.

Mr. Proctor boldly attacked Mr. Clement on his own ground, and every charge brought against the republicans as a party was flung back with a force that was at once disastrous to the person making the charge and convincing to the people. Mr. Proctor indulged in no personalities, but discussed the legitimate issues of the campaign in a frank and fearless manner which convinced thoroughly his audience of his sincerity.

Many things had been said by Mr. Clement in the campaign which ought not to have been said; and doubtless the republican management was not entirely free from criticism.

Why doesn't Proctor speak? Why don't he tell the voters where he stands on the leading issues? Why doesn't he outline his views on the public policy of the State?

speaking. It came on the day when he received the unanimous nomination for the governorship at the hands of his party. In response, he stepped modestly to the platform and in an half an hour's plain, straight forward, honest talk, blazed the way for a longer step in material progress for his native state than had ever before been outlined by a candidate for office. It came as a revelation. Here was a man of clear-cut ideas, plainly and forcibly set forth; a man skilled and schooled in the art of upbuilding public, as well as private enterprises to a plane of eminence, by applying the same principles to the one as to the other; a man versed as few were in the affairs of common, everyday life and conditions that prevail in our state, aware of the limitations that could not safely be traversed, yet anxious to reach those limitations in promoting the state's welfare. And he told his hearers, who sat enrapt, what he favored, what he opposed, what he expected to do, how he expected to do it. When he finished first a hush fell; then applause, long and hearty-and Fletcher D. Proctor had won the hearts and confidence of the delegates as he had already their votes, unanimously. 

Proctor had spoken, His friends rejoiced. His party rallied to the standard. It recognized leadership. It welcomed the fray. In the strenuous time that followed, Proctor spoke again, spoke often and everywhere throughout the state, avoiding no place, slighting no place that he could reach. And everywhere he made votes. His plain direct words, his common sense views on public matters, his honest, straightforward manner, his democratic, altogether unaffected personal bearing, proved irresistible. Despite the most energetic opposition that the Fusion forces could muster; despite active campaigning by able speakers assailing the Republican policy; despite the use of all the enginery of modern political warfare directed against the regular ticket, it won a most notable victory. A majority of 14,171 votes demonstrated the faith of

Vermonters in the old party, under the dispensation of a new leader. 

And admittedly, the most potent single factor in the outcome was the young man bore the standard, who went among the people telling them of the things that were, and the things that were to be. 

The promises made at the convention and during the campaign, unlike some campaign promises, were not made to be forgotten after election. They were made to be redeemed. The governor-elect lost no time in impressing that fact upon all who had the burdens resting upon them. When the day came for the assembling of the legislators, he, figuratively speaking, met them at the depot, and, after the first warm greeting, renewed his urgent personal appeal for assistance and cooperation for the work in hand. The effect was not lost, but was intensified when, in a clear inaugural address, the new governor laid out a definite program for action along many lines, so well considered and presented that the duty of the session revealed itself there and then. But he did not stop here. From that hour to the end of the long session that followed, Governor Proctor's hand was visible in almost every bit of important legislation enacted. It was a smooth touch, but an iron grasp that showed complete mastery. The executive chamber was not a cold storage vault into which the timorous legislator ventured haltingly.

CONCLUSIONS 

177 It was the rendezvous of ever-welcome members, anybody, everybody, at all times, who found in the occupant a man of heart and strength, ready to advise with them, anxious to consult with them, his fund of information always at their command, square in his opinions for or against, and preeminently recognized as a Governor who governed. 

The influence that came from that chamber was always strong, always wholesome, always exerted in a tactful, delicate, considerate way that never once stirred resentment or bore ill fruit. It was an administration that counted. By its very strength, appealing only to their sense of fairness and loyalty, it commended itself to the lawmakers and earned their hearty support. When it is appreciated how easy it is to suggest and how difficult to convince a body of nearly three hundred men that the suggestions deserve their favor; when it is understood how important, even radical, were some of the propositions; and when it is recalled how relatively few of the recommendations of incoming governors in previous years have been endorsed at the hands of the assemblies to which they were submitted, it will be seen how striking was the record of accomplishment, and how fully the Legislature of 1906 adopted and endorsed the policy of the Proctor administration.

Governor Proctor knows Vermont and Vermonters as few do. He possesses unusual tact in handling men, shown in numberless ways. His rare good nature and love of wholesome fun pervade all, sets everyone at ease and reveals the true side of everyone he meets, of course to the advantage of both. There is not the touch of the aristocrat about him, and it would be impossible for him to assume that cold stare of dignity that too often makes public men disagreeable. He is just a plain, sensible, loyal, whole-souled, lovable son of Vermont, splendidly equipped to be at the head of our State government.

ADDENDA Fletcher D. Proctor As Governor 

Subsequent activities as Governor of the State showed that the people of Vermont had not misplaced their vote, as many reforms and new laws, beneficial to the State as a whole, were enacted during his administration, and it may be said to have been one of the most progressive administrations in the history of the State of Vermont. 

Among the many measures advocated by the Governor and subsequently adopted by the State Legislature were reform in the management of state institutions; reorganization of the judicial system; reform in methods of comitments of prisoners; elimination of fee system as far as practicable; expert supervision of public schools; abolition of railroad crossings; amendments to the high license local option laws where they had been found faulty; increase in taxation of railroad corporations; voluntary incorporation. act for villages; the appointment of a commission to study double taxation and to report in 1908; taxation of interestbearing deposits in its national banks; new system of bookkeeping in the auditor's office; the establishment of committees to visit the penal institutions and educational institutions of the state and report on the same, as well as other enactments were among some of the outstanding enactments during his administration. A bill embodying the weekly payments by corporations, although not advocated by the governor, was nevertheless passed and signed by the governor. 

History has rendered her verdict and in speaking of his administration, Mr. Crockett said of him in his History of

Governor Proctor had a definite program and he possessed to a remarkable degree, the ability to secure the co-operation of the House and Senate. Biography

While this is not a biography, nor in any sense intended to be, nevertheless, we will append herewith a short sketch of the life of Governor Fletcher D. Proctor, from the Crockett

History: 

Fletcher D. Proctor was born at Cavendish, November 7, 1860.

He was educated at Middlebury High School and at Amherst College. 

He received the degree of A. B. in the class of 1882. 

While still a student, Mr. Proctor worked during his vacations for the Vermont Marble Company, and he entered its permanent employ as soon as he left college. He became assistant superintendent, then general superintendent, and since 1889 has been president of the company and its active and responsible manager. 

In 1889 the Vermont Marble Company employed some 1,100 or 1,200 men. Today the company employs 3,200 men in its works at 

Proctor, West Rutland, Rutland, Pittsford, Danby, Brandon and

Beldens. Under his management the company has grown rapidly. 

Mr. Proctor is also president of the Proctor Trust Company and of the Clarendon & Pittsford Railroad, which owns and operates a steam railway connecting the different quarries, mills and shops with each other and with the Rutland and D. & H. R. R. Co. roads. 

Mr. Proctor was brought up in the midst of the great business which he now directs, and since he was ten years of age has always resided in the village which is its chief center. He believes that many of the abuses and misunderstandings arising out of the employment of large bodies of men are due to absenteeism, or the living of the employers apart from the employees. The Vermont Marble Company's policy has always been in the direction of encouraging and supporting a liberal policy of public improvement. To the same end Mr. Proctor has devoted his personal energies, actively participating in the local public interests of the community in which he resides. In 1884 and 1885 he was selectman of the old town of Rutland before the incorporation of the town of Proctor, and from 1887 to 1890 he was selectman of the town of Proctor. He has been a member of the school board of the village of Proctor since 1883, in which time the schools have grown from four ungraded schools to a graded system with seventeen teachers and 

550 children.

Mr. Proctor has held the office of road commissioner in his town ten years. Thus it is seen that his interests are centered at home, where he lives his summers and winters.

In 1883 Mr. Proctor enlisted as a private in Rutland Company of the Vermont National Guard and served four years, rising to second lieutenant and then to first lieutenant.

Governor Ormsbee. In 1890 he was a member of the House of Representatives, serving on the committee on ways and means, and as chairman of the general committee. In 1892 he was a member of the Senate, and served on the committee of finance, and as chairman of the general committee. He again represented the town of Proctor in 1900, and was elected speaker of the House of Representatives by the unanimous vote of the Republican members.

In 1902 Mr. Proctor was a candidate in the gubernatorial election, but withdrew from the convention after the third ballot in favor of General McCullough. This action meant the defeat of Hon. Percival 

W. Clement, who was also a candidate, and was responsible for the well remembered bolt of that candidate.

In 1904 Mr. Proctor again represented his town in the Legislature. 

He served as chairman of the committee on ways and means, was a member of the standing committee on rules, and a member of the joint committee on public health. 

Mr. Proctor married Minnie E. Robinson of Westford, May 26, 1886. They have three children: Emily, Mortimer Robinson and Minnie.
