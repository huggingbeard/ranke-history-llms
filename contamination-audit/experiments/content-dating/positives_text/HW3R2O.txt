INTER-FAITH RELATIONS AND HOW TO IMPROVE THEM 

Relations between religious groups in America are far from satisfactory. Suspicion, hatred and discrimination are rife. 

Protestants line up against Catholics; each of these groups turns against Jews. Often the members of one group are antagonistic to members of the same group who differ in belief or practice. As we enter upon the post-war period, we 

Americans can ill afford the tensions and disunity which result. We shall have economic and political adjustments of prime importance to make, and shall have to come to decisions on international questions which may determine the course of world history for generations to come, if not for ever. If we fail to solve the vexing problem of inter-faith relations, we shall not only cause ourselves much distress and pain, but also distract ourselves from sober consideration of great problems.

Inter-faith relations are of serious moment particularly now, because our country is entering upon a period which will undoubtedly be one of painful reorientation. The war has been won, but victory has brought us innumerable problems of an economic and political nature. Whenever economic and political issues are at stake, unscrupulous men resort to any means to gain an advantage, and will not hesitate to exploit real or potential antagonisms between members of differing races and creeds. We have seen how destructive of national unity and progress such dissensions can be, especially when instigated and magnified by those who seek power.

The Inadequacy of Most Good-Will Efforts 

The problem is, of course, not new in the United States. 

At various times in our history, Catholics, Protestants and Jews have come in for their share of persecution. Sporadic efforts, mostly of the hortatory type, have been exerted to 1 G+E

reduce inter-faith friction, but not until the twenties of this century have organized campaigns been set in motion to deal with the question. Today, there are the National Conference of Christians and Jews, the Council Against Intolerance, and numerous other agencies seeking to improve interfaith relations. Yet, after all these years, the problem seems to be as pressing as ever. For a short time after Pearl Harbor, we are told, relations improved considerably; then they deteriorated again. They may even have become worse than before December 7, 1941. Thus national danger, war-time living standards and other economic and political conditions determine the improvement or deterioration of inter-faith relations. Obviously, the question has not been dealt with effectively. 

The basic inadequacy of most organized "good-will" movements has been their failure to study the fundamental causes of inter-religious friction. They have been mainly attempts to bring men and women of differing faiths together, either to enlist their cooperation in some enterprise outside their respective religions, or to achieve better "understanding" by studying one another's faith. The former procedure succeeds in diverting everyone's attention for a while from the problem of mutual relations; the latter confirms each in his own faith, armed, now, with enough knowledge of his neighbor's to know precisely why his neighbor's religion is inferior. 

At no time (or rarely) is the root cause of the trouble, brought to the surface.

The Root Cause of Interfaith Hostility 

That root cause, stated briefly, is the contention of each of the three major religious groups that it is the sole possessor of the truth. Each religion is, by its very nature, exclusive of all the rest. Each group assumes that there can be only one true or legitimate religion-its own. Because of this, each believes the problems of the world will never be solved until peace, justice and love in the world unless they be attained 

Page Four

by means of God's unique revelation. That revelation each claims to have received exclusively. 

The result is that each faith must regard all others as "error," which must sooner or later be eliminated. The elimination of "error" can be achieved through education, propaganda and missionizing. In the past, the ambition to convert the world to a particular faith has led to tragic consequences, for peaceful suasion was not always successful, and other means were resorted to. Political, economic and social sanctions were used; and sometimes the torch had to be applied. 

Although religious groups in contemporary America are no longer inclined to use force, they are nevertheless committed to the fundamental proposition, from which they have not deviated. That proposition is that their respective religions are the only true ones, or, at least, are possessed of such an overwhelming degree of the truth that salvation for mankind will not be achieved until everyone is converted to the true faith. The Christian Church is still committed to the proposition that Christianity must become the religion of all mankind before we can hope to see the world perfected. "The Gospel is the only way of salvation. . Its very nature forbids us to say that it may be the right belief for some, but not for others."* Unless all become Christians and teach the truths of the "life complete and abundant" on the basis of the Christian Gospels, they cannot be said to have qualified for true salvation. 

To be sure, "rays" from the "light" have penetrated even non-Christian religions; but partial faith is not sufficient. "All the good of which men have conceived is fulfilled and secured in Christ"; Christianity is, in the words of one Christian leader, "not a religion, but religion."**

Most Christian denominations, therefore, continue to subsidize missions. The greater proportion of missionary energy is expended on foreign soil; but a good deal is directed toward converting unbelievers who are to be found close at hand. This missionary activity creates friction. Those who are missionized call the missionizers "intolerant," and those who missionize regard as intolerant the people who resist them.

Strangely enough the friction is caused by the non-proselytizing attitude as well as by the proselytizing attitude. The 

Jews in the United States have been criticized as exclusive and particularistic because of their refusal to engage in proselytizing. The Jews theoretically are a missionary people. Traditionally, Judaism looked forward to achieving spiritual hegemony over the whole world. The Messianic era was pictured as one in which everyone would acknowledge the God of Israel. In modern times, the Orthodox movement retained this Messianic hope in the liturgy but permitted its implications to lapse into desuetude. The Reform movement is committed to the ideal of Israel's mission to be realized through the example set by the exemplary lives of the Jews. But, truth to tell, no program has ever been worked out to implement the terms of the mission, mainly because the entire plan is so unrealistic. For practical purposes, Judaism is not a missionary religion.

Nevertheless, Judaism as well as Christianity still clings to the belief in the "chosenness" of its system of salvation, and even when no action is taken to seek converts, the avowed insistence on the possession of unique truth serves to create the climate in which intolerance flourishes. 

The suggestion that contemporary religions abandon this basic doctrine is generally met with serious opposition. No religion, it is claimed, which professes to be a universal one can possibly surrender the hope that it will ultimately become 

Page Six

the religion of mankind. In a democracy, competition between religions is as legitimate as competition in business. 

Let each religion appeal to the masses, presenting its virtues as clearly as possible, and eventually the best religion will drive out the inferior one.

The True Basis of Interfaith Accord 

The truth is, however, that religions are not acquired like radios. People do not change their religions easily. They are born into them, raised in them, and have their being in the social group which shares a common religion. In order to understand the peculiar relations of individuals to their religion, it is necessary to understand more about the function of religion in a civilization. 

Religion is an aspect of a culture or a civilization. It is the self-consciousness of a group expressed in that group's orientation to the cosmos, to society as a whole, to history. The religion of a group is the unique articulation, in terms of the historic experience of the group, of those values which constitute its highest ideals. The values which a group cherishes and the means which it provides for the realization of those values constitute the "salvation" it offers its adherents. The rites, ceremonies, and liturgy which the group develops are the characteristic expressions of those ideals and values. The expressions are characteristic of a group but the ideals and values themselves are universal. 

Now, religious groups are not necessarily different from one another; they may be just other. If, for example, the values cherished by two groups are for the most part the same, the groups cannot be said to differ. Yet the groups are not identical. They are other, as two individuals who may agree are other: each believes in filial love, and each loves another father his own. Two religious groups may believe in freedom; each may derive that belief from a similar view of life and express that belief in the celebration of festivals dis- 

Page Seven

tinctive and unique. But while the first draws its inspiration from one historic experience, and the second derives inspiration from another history, they may see eye to eye with regard to the values accepted. 

The distinctive and unique experiences of a group, symbolized by special events, heroes, sacred places and such, are the sancta of that group, the sacred associations toward which members of that group feel a special kinship, love and reverence.

Since, however, sancta are susceptible of a variety of interpretations, it is possible to find members of the same group sharing the same sancta but differing as to their values. Contemporaneous adherents of the same religion may differ in their ethical valuations, while invoking the same authority. 

The problem of inter-faith relations, therefore, should be approached from an entirely new point of view. We hold that the adoption of new values does not require conversion. 

Since ethical ideals are universal, they can be incorporated into any system and sanctified by the sancta of that system. 

There is no need to give up one's whole tradition, associations, social environment, sancta, if one happens to find in another religion values which are more appealing. By appropriation and assimilation, these values can be made part of one's own civilization and religion. 

The mistake generally made is in thinking that a religion should be "universal" in the sense that it should be accepted_ by all men, that every people on earth should adopt the civilization, traditions, associations, sancta of that religion. 

The truth is that the universalism of a religion should consist in the universal applicability of its values. Missionary activity, therefore, should consist in getting others to accept one's values, not in making proselytes or getting others to adopt one's cultural patterns or ancestors and history. 

The usual objection to this conception of religion and of 

Page Eight

the relation of religions to one another is that no cognizance is taken of the fact that some religions are better than others, not only superior for their own adherents but objectively superior. However, upon reflection it will appear that no religion can be regarded as superior to any other. The mere fact that, at any given time in history, it may possess a superior set of values does not make the religion itself superior, since those same values can be appropriated by other religions and expressed in terms of the sancta of those religions. To say that the sancta of one religion are better than the sancta of another is, however, meaningless. The fact is that the sancta of one religion are invaluable and precious to the members of that religion: and are generally of no value at all to members of another. They may have certain purely esthetic values, but they cannot possibly have the appeal to outsiders which they have to those born and brought up within the group.

On the other hand, religious conversion, which includes the adoption of sancta as well as values, is à natural procedure for those who sincerely wish to throw in their lot completely with the members of another group. If a man who for some reason has been alienated from the sancta of his own group, wishes to marry a member of another religious group, he should become completely assimilated into that group, provided the group acknowledges his right to interpret its sancta in accordance with his own conscience. He may find that the values cherished in the new group are no different from those on which he was brought up; but in the new environment, in order to express a loyalty to those values, he should talk the "language" of his new co-religionists. 

As soon as we recognize, therefore, that different religious groups really represent different culture patterns, we see how futile and wrong it is to attempt to impose them upon those who prefer their own. If there is truth in a particular religion, and that truth is lacking elsewhere, it can be easily adopted and adapted and everyone will benefit.

What the Religions Must Do to Establish Peace Among Them 

This approach demands a complete reorientation to the traditional faiths. Such a reorientation is as revolutionary in character as that required by the nations of the world at the present time in their attitude toward national sovereignty. 

Students of world affairs are coming more and more to believe that wars will never be abolished so long as nations cling to their age-old habit of regarding themselves absolutely independent. Unless they are prepared to recognize a universal law greater than their own, they will continue to prey upon one another. The change is so fundamental that it may require several generations to counteract nationalistic fanaticism.

In religious groups, the analogous process may be as slow. 

For centuries, Christians and Jews have been nourished on the belief that their religions had no choice but to extend throughout the world or perish. From now on, they must adjust themselves to their new conception, that Judaism is best for the Jews, and Christianity is best for the Christians, and Islam best for Moslems.

To achieve this new understanding, it is not enough to do what many well-wishing Christians and Jews are doing today, namely, eliminating from religious text-books all disparaging references to members of other religions. It is not enough to clear the name of the Jew from the "guilt" of the crucifixion, and it is not enough to erase from Jewish texts the few derogatory references to non-Jews. Religious history must be taught in an entirely different manner. The child must be made to understand at an early age that his religion is the result of the sincere efforts of his ancestors to discover the will of God, that members of other civilizations carried on the same search, and that as long as human history goes on, the search will continue; that through it all, various cultures learn from one another, enriching one another, correcting one another. They must realize 

Page Ten

that the desire to make one religion the sole religion is as wicked and as fraught with danger as the attempt on the part of one nation to dominate all the rest and to make over the world in its own image.

We Jews are one of the smallest minorities in the United States, and are in the exposed position of being the only considerable non-Christian group in an otherwise Christian civilization. We have seen that one of the few ideas on which divergent groups can attain unanimity is the hatred of the Jew. 

It therefore behooves us to take the lead in this new religious reformation. It will not be the first time that we have been called upon by the exigencies of our history and destiny to act as pioneers of new and challenging religious doctrines. 

Therefore, we Jews should not hesitate to incorporate the new orientation into our religious life. 

What Jewish Religion Must Do 

This involves the elimination from our prayerbook and our textbooks of the doctrine of the Chosen People. This doctrine has, of course, been sadly misunderstood both by Jews and by non-Jews to mean that the Jewish people is the possessor of special privileges at the hand of God. Certainly, from 

Biblical times to our own, being the Chosen People has meant not privilege but responsibility. "You alone of all the peoples of the earth have I known, therefore I shall punish you for your sins." Furthermore, being a Chosen People has not meant that Israel is a closed and exclusive clan. Anyone willing to accept the beliefs and practices of the Jewish religion has always been accepted within the Jewish fold as a full-fledged Jew. In recent years, the doctrine of the 

Chosen People has been interpreted to mean the obligation of the Jewish people to carry out God's mission of teaching ethical monotheism to the world. And more recently, Israel's claim to be Chosen has been made to refer to the historic fact that the Jews were most influential in bringing the message of justice and peace to the civilized nations. 

Page Eleven

Yet, no matter how liberally the doctrine is taught, one basic attitude remains, and that is that the Jewish people occupies a position of centrality in the destiny of all the world in accordance with God's own purposes. As long as this attitude is held, the implication is that Israel possesses a unique truth not shared by other peoples.

Why is it not sufficient for Jews to be grateful to God for whatever truth has been vouchsafed to them, to seek to cultivate it to the utmost, and to strive to discover in their history and in the history of other nations the further revelation of God to man? Certainly, there is enough in our heritage to make us proud to be what we are without indulging in odious comparisons between the Jewish and other civilizations. 

To be sure, the elimination of the doctrine of the Chosen People will be resented both by traditional-minded Jews and by Christians. Jews will feel that it will deprive them of a psychological staff to lean on, that without it they have no genuine reason for surviving. Non-Jews will object (and have objected) because it challenges their missionizing activities, and removes religion from the arena of competition. 

These objections are to be expected and should not discourage us from our purpose. They are the natural impediments to progress, and have their analogies in every phase of life, where fundamental changes are made. They will gradually diminish as men come to see that the policy of "live and let live" brings peace between religious groups and undiminished satisfaction to those who cling to their own faiths.
