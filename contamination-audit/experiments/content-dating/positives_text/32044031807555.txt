PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

The relationship of Landlord and Tenant is created by an instrument denominated a lease. A lease is essentially a contract, express or implied. The contract aspect of a lease of real estate, however, is superimposed upon basic real property law. Moreover, the influence of many common law principles of real property persist, despite the attempts to codify the law. 

This outline is not a digest of the law of landlord and tenant. A knowledge of the substantive law on the subject is assumed. The approach is rather that of analysing the form, content and legal significance of a lease. 

The basic requirements of leases generally will first be treated, followed by specific comment on problems applicable to leases for the common types of real estate, which require special provisions not germane to other types of real estate.

A. Definition. 

I. INTRODUCTION TO LEASES 

A lease is a conveyance or grant or demise of certain described land or tenement for a prescribed period or at will, but for a less time than the lessor possesses in the premises: Morrisville Shopping Center v. Sun Ray Drug 

Co., 381 Pa. 576, 112 A.2d 183 (1955).

The essential element of a lease is the granting of an "estate" in or the right of possession to real estate for a term in consideration of the payment of rent. 

B. Lease Distinguished From Other Contracts. 

(1) Contracts for board and lodging. Lodger has only a right of personal use as distinguished from possession. Landlord and tenant relationship is not created between lodger or boarder and so-called "landlord". See,

Davis v. Hartel, 56 Pa. Super. 557 (1914). 

(2) Master and Servant. Occupation of real estate incidental to performance of services creates a master and servant relationship rather than a tenancy: Smith v. 

Reimer, 179 Pa. 442, 36 Atl. 313 (1897). (3) License. A license is a purely personal privilege to perform certain acts on or over ground, without creating a right of exclusive possession for a term. McFerrew v. Deardorff, 69 Pa. Super. 154 (1918). (4) Concession. A concession in a department store or merchandise mart, is usually a license and not a lease, because the element of exclusive possession is lacking. (1)

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

(5) Sales. Purchaser of real estate taking possession under an executory agreement is not a tenant. Hence, a lease should be executed to protect vendor in event of buyer's default. See, Campeotto v. Sochanski, 26 Lehigh

(6) Ground Rent. A ground rent has some similarity to a long term lease. However, its legal attributes are essentially different. A ground rent is created by a grant of the entire estate or fee, reserving rent. A ground rent is real estate, whereas a lease is personalty. See, Sachse 

Estate, 26 D.R. 709 (1917). (7) Mortgage. A mortgage is not a lease, since the payments of interest are for use of money and not the real estate. Mortgagee has only an equitable estate. (8) Partnership. Farming on shares and percentage leases possess elements which may be consistent with either a lease or partnership. If there is no participation by the owner of the real estate in losses, the relationship is usually that of landlord and tenant. The agreement should state affirmatively that it is a lease, and not a partnership. 

C. Statutory Requirements. 

Pennsylvania law governs leases of real estate situate in the Commonwealth. The Landlord and Tenant Act of 

1951, 68 P.S. 250 et sequa establishes basic provisions applicable to the relationship of landlord and tenant. (1) Oral leases of real estate for a term of not more than three years are valid: 68 P.S. 250.201. (2) Leases of real estate for a term of more than three years must be in writing. 68 P.S. 250.202. (a) Must be signed by Lessor or may be signed by Lessor's agent if agent's authority is in writing. 

68 P.S. 250.202. (b) Effect of oral lease for more than three years is a tenancy at will, except where tenant continues in possession for more than one year, tenancy becomes a lease from year to year. Ferri v. Liberatoscioli, 338 

Pa. 454, 13 A.2d 45 (1940); 68 P.S. 250.202. (c) Assignments, grant or surrender of lease for more than three years must be in writing signed by person assigning, granting or surrendering lease, unless occurring by operation of law. 68 P.S. 250.203.

D. Validity of Leases. 

Leases, like contracts generally may be invalidated by fraud, mutual mistake, duress or illegality of purpose. 

Where tenant anticipates a particular use which may be unlawful, or for which a license must be obtained, lease must contain an unequivocal escape clause to permit tenant

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

3 to rescind. Wissahickon Realty Corporation v. Boyle, 385 

Pa. 198, 122 A.2d 720 (1956).

E. Form and Contents of Leases. 

(1) No particular form of words is required to create a lease. In fact a lease may be implied from the entry into possession of land with the consent of the landlord and the payment and acceptance of rent. Carr v. H. W. 

Johns-Manville Co., 60 Pa. Super. 500 (1915). (2) An agreement to make a lease, however, is not a lease and does not create the relationship of landlord and tenant. Zook v. Zook, 104 Pa. Super. 374, 157 Atl. 355 (1931). See, Frey v. Nakles, 380 Pa. 616, 112 A.2d 329 (1955). (3) Proposals for a lease which are incomplete do not constitute a lease, since mutuality of agreement is lacking: 

Upsal Street Realty Company v. Rubin, 326 Pa. 327, 192 

Atl. 481 (1937). (4) Certainty of terms must be supplied either from written agreement, oral agreement, or by implication. 

Terms of a landlord's "standard agreement" (Emerman v. Baldwin, 186 Pa. Super. 561, 146 A.2d 440 (1958)), or other writing may be incorporated by reference. The leased property, the rent and the term must be certain, but all of these may be determined and made certain by rules of construction, the acts of the parties, or presumptions of law. An agreement to lease for "a fair rental" was held sufficiently certain, Weaver v. Wood, 9 Pa. 220 (1848). The term may be found to be month to month or year to year by legal presumptions. See, Aaron v. 

Woodcock, 283 Pa. 33, 128 Atl. 665 (1925) for policy favoring a year to year tenancy. (5) Terms supplied by implication of law. Common law supplies many terms in the absence of specific provision to the contrary. For example, a covenant of quiet enjoyment is implied in every lease, as is an implied covenant not to commit waste. Rent is not payable in advance, unless so specified. Gilmore v. Dwyer, 53 D. & C. 428 (Luzerne, 1945). Rent is payable on the premises. unless place of payment is stated in lease. See, Kiefer v. 

Schoffstall, 69 D. & C. 478 (Clinton, 1949). The requirements of notice to terminate leases from month to month or from year to year are supplied by statute. 68 P.S. 250.501.

F. 

Construction of Leases. 

(1) Construction of a written lease is matter of law for court to determine. Diamond v. Drucker, 177 Pa. Super. 

226, 110 A.2d 820 (1955).

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

(2) Where no ambiguity exists, the terms of the lease itself determine the intention of the parties. Bogutz v. 

Margolin, 392 Pa. 151, 139 A.2d 649 (1958). (a) Intent derived from consideration of lease as an entirety, not from a single word, phrase or clause. (b) Written portions of lease prevail over conflicting printed portion. Whitaker Company v. Cohen, 7 D. & C. 2d 105 (Phila., 1955). (3) Negotiations prior to execution of the lease are merged in the written lease. Marmon Philadelphia Company v. Blocksom, 103 Pa. Super. 542, 157 Atl. 510 (1932).

But in some cases prior negotiations have been admitted to arrive at true intent or to resolve an apparent am biguity. In re Reading Iron Works, 150 Pa. 369, 24 Atl. 617 

(1892). (4) If more than one interpretation, the construction most favorable to tenant will be adopted. Bogutz v. Margolin, 392 Pa. 151, 139 A.2d 649 (1958); City of Pittsburgh v. C. Zubik & Sons, Inc., 404 Pa. 219, 171 A.2d 776 (1961). (Rule usually applied where landlord has prepared lease.) (5) Where tenant has prepared lease or modified landlord's lease form, rule favoring lessee will not apply. 

Prudential Insurance Company v. Goodman and Theise, 

Inc., 396 Pa. 366, 152 A.2d 664 (1959). (6) Restrictions on use are strictly construed and will not be extended by construction. See, Dickey v. Phila. 

Minit-Man Corp., 377 Pa. 549, 105 A.2d 580 (1954). (7) Restrictions on assignments and subleases are strictly construed against landlord. Berman's Estate, 58 

D. & C. 678 (Del., 1947). (8) Powers to confess judgment are strictly construed. 

Power must expressly give right to exercise successively, otherwise only one judgment may be entered, and invalid exercise will terminate power. Philadelphia v. Johnson, 208 Pa. 645, 57 Atl. 1114 (1904). For instances of strict construction see Housing Mortgage Company v. Tower De velopment & Investment Corporation, 402 Pa. 388, 167

A.2d 146 (1961), and Park-Main Company v. Fayette Na tional Bank and Trust Company, 397 Pa. 75, 152 A.2d 714 (1959). (9) Covenants will be construed as independent covenants, not mutually dependent, and a condition will not be found except where expressly stated, Ruth v. Golden Slipper Restaurant, 167 Pa. Super. 558, 76 A.2d 498 (1950).

II. DRAFTING LEASES 

The foregoing resume of the elementary principles applicable to leases, indicate the necessity of a written lease

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

5 to protect the respective interests of both the landlord and the tenant. The minimum requirements to create a relationship of landlord and tenant subject both parties to inequities which can be avoided by the simple precaution of a written lease. Even the letting of a private residence, a farm or a small store warrants a definition of the respective rights of both the landlord and the tenant in a written lease.

A. Essential Requirements of Every Written Lease. 

There are seven primary requisites for every written lease. These are the barest of minimum provisions and are given in the usual chronological order that they appear in the simplest form of written lease. (1) Date. Establish the date on which the agreement is executed. If lease is acknowledged, date of execution may not be later than the date of acknowledgement. 

Dating lease on Sunday should be avoided as lease may be invalid if not ratified. Acceptance of possession under lease will validate lease as an executed agreement. 

Cf. Chadwick v. Stokes, 162 F.2d 132 (3rd Cir. 1947).

No prohibition on execution on other legal holidays. (2) Parties. Both landlord and tenant should be correctly named, with designation of status as individuals, partners or corporation. (a) Lessor as Party. (1) Identity of Lessor. Name should conform exactly with lessor's designation in deed (or lease if landlord is himself a tenant). (2) Individuals. Individual lessor's spouse need not join unless there is an option to purchase. (3) Partners. All partners of a partnership should be named "A, B and C, copartners trading as ABC Company". (4) Corporations. Corporations should include state of incorporation. If a foreign corporation, require proof of qualification in Pennsylvania. 

The authority of the corporation to lease should be ascertained by examining charter and verifying that it is active and in good standing. (5) Fiduciaries. Examine will or trust instrument for power to lease. Executors and administrators have power by statute to lease for a term not exceeding one year after death of decedent, unless terminable by personal representative on thirty days' notice. 20 P.S. 320.542. Trustees, unless otherwise provided in the trust instrument, are empowered to lease for a term of not more

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

than five years without court approval. 20 P.S.

320.962, (6) Mortgagee in Possession. A mortgagee in possession may renew an existing lease or make a new lease, but if mortgagor pays off mortgage, lessee's rights may be terminated. Shanefield v. 

Magners, 82 D. & C. 272 (Phila., 1952). (7) Agents. Determine authority of agent. 

Judith v. Dicola, 317 Pa. 353, 176 Atl. 238 (1935).

If lease is for more than three years, authority must be in writing. 68 P.S. 250.202. Suggest written approval of lease by principals. 

(8) Governmental Agencies. Check statute for authority. See Purdon's Index, Leases for specific powers of State and Municipal Subdi visions, Departments and Agencies. 

(9) Title of Lessor. Examine Lessor's title. 

Encumbrances can destroy lease. If lessor is himself a tenant, obtain a copy and examine his lease to determine authority to sublet and duration of term. A title search or title insurance is recommended in any substantial lease. (b) Lessee as Party. Incapacity of minors, incompetents, and fiduciaries can render lease unenforcible. Lease of a home to a minor may be valid as a "necessity". See, Rineer v. Kline, 9 D. & C. 2d 670 (Lancaster, 1956); Trickett, Law of Landlord and

Tenant, Section 17, pages 18-19. Verify legal entity and include a description such as "co-partners" or "a corporation organized and existing under the laws of the State of... 

(3) Statement of Demise. The lease should use the words of grant, such as "let and demise", or "lease", but such words are not essential to its validity. Morrisville 

Shopping Center, Inc. v. Sun Ray Drug Co., 381 Pa. 576, 112 A.2d 183 (1955). 

(4) Description of Premises. 

(a) Premises should be accurately described. In residential properties or apartments the street address and apartment number are customarily used. In other leases a description by metes and bounds, tax map, block numbers, by reference to a deed description, or an actual plot plan is recommended. Where part of a lot or part of a building is leased, a plan is the most practical way to avoid misunderstanding. (b) Comment. A lease of the entire building includes use of the roof. In a multiple occupancy building the top floor tenant does not have use of roof.

Adler v. Sklaroff, 154 Pa. Super. 444, 36 A.2d 331 

(1944). A lease of a part of a building does not include common areas except as specified. Kirchhoff v. 

Murray, 35 Del. Co. 293 (1948). A lease of an entire floor to a single tenant includes use of exterior walls of that floor for signs, etc., unless specifically excepted. 

Yoder v. Cohen, 6 Berks 314 (1914).

Physical examination of premises is recommended before drafting lease. 

(5) Term of Lease. The commencement date of the term, the duration and expiration date including the hour should be stated.

If possession is to be granted prior to the commencement of the term, as where tenant is to make repairs, or install fixtures, specify that the possession held during such interim is as a tenant. 

The term must not exceed the term of the landlord's estate.

If no renewal options are granted, provide that the lease shall terminate without notice on the specified date. (See II, D12, 13 infra, for Renewal Options and Purchase Options.) 

(6) Rental. 

(a) The rental is usually expressed in a fixed dollar sum. The total rental for the entire term should (but need not) be stated, followed by a provision as to the time and amount of each installment. For example, in a one-year lease the rental should be stated— "A rental of Twelve Hundred ($1,200) Dollars payable in monthly installments of One Hundred ($100.00)

Dollars each on the first day of each month in advance, beginning the first day of January, 1963, and monthly thereafter". (b) The provision "in advance" is essential as unless so stated rent is payable at the end of the term. 

Gilmore v. Dwyer, 53 D. & C. 428 (Luzerne, 1945). (c) The place of payment must be stated, as otherwise rent is payable on the leased premises. 

Trickett, Law of Landlord and Tenant, Section 74, page 86. The usual clause is to direct payment to be made at Lessor's office, with his address given, or at such other place as the lessor may from time to time direct. (d) The rental clause should include the words "without demand”, as in the absence of this provision the landlord may not avail himself of all his remedies unless a demand has first been made. Elizabethtown 

Lodge No. 596 v. Ellis, 391 Pa. 19, 137 A.2d 286 (1957).

(e) The requirement of former leases that rent be payable in gold or a particular kind of coin or currency or in an amount of money of the United States measured thereby is unenforceable as against public policy. 31 U.S.C.A. 463. 

(f) The tenant may be required to pay additional rentals such as all taxes and water and sewer rents; increases in taxes on the real estate; fire insurance premiums or increases in fire insurance premiums; or a percentage of the gross receipts from business conducted. These are discussed infra in more detail. If additional rentals are to be paid, the lease should specifically provide that all additional payments are collectible as rent, to insure that all remedies applicable to the collection of rents and priorities in insolvency proceedings are available as to the additional rental. (For acceleration of rent on default, see infra under Remedies.) (g) Where lease provides for renewals, at same rental as in initial term, specifically provide that no concessions of rent in initial term apply to the renewal term. 

(7) Execution of Lease. 

(a) Lessor's Signature. Lessor or agent authorized in writing must sign lease for term of more than three years, or effect of lease will be a lease at will or year to year. 68 P.S. 250.202. It is suggested that names be typewritten under signature to aid in determining identity of person signing.

Spouse of owner need not sign, unless lease contains an option to purchase. Both husband and wife should (but need not) sign where property is jointly owned. O'Malley v. O'Malley, 272 Pa. 528, 533, 116 

Atl. 500 (1922); cf. Schweitzer v. Evans, 360 Pa. 332, 63 A.2d 39 (1949).

One partner may sign for a partnership, if authorized, but good practice requires signature of all partners.

Corporate signature should be in the corporate name by principal officer such as President or Vice 

President attested by Secretary or Assistant Secretary, and corporate seal affixed. However, signature of one officer, if authorized, is sufficient with or without seal. Protection is afforded by obtaining a certified copy of a resolution of the Board of Directors authorizing execution of the lease by one or more designated officers. Informality of execution by a corporation does not invalidate lease. 21 P.S. 116.

All trustees must sign, unless trust instrument authorizes less than all to sign. Ascertain authority of any fiduciary and obtain Orphans' Court approval if in doubt. (See Parties, supra.) 

Attorney in Fact-Signature of attorney in fact should be "A. B. by C., attorney in fact". Reference to the date and book and page number of the letter of attorney is preferable, and should be verified to make certain that power is not limited, terminated or revoked. (b) Lessee's Execution. Absence of tenant's signature will not invalidate a lease for more than three years if tenant has taken possession under lease. 

Carnegie Nat. Gas Co. v. Philadelphia Co., 158 Pa. 317, 27 Atl. 951 (1894). However, tenant's signature will avoid dispute as to existence of lease and its terms and should always be obtained.

The comments as to landlord's manner of execution are generally applicable. One partner or one officer may bind the partnership or corporation, but the signature of all partners of a partnership, and the requirement of production of a certified copy of a resolution authorizing officers to sign are advisable. 

(c) Witnesses and Acknowledgments. Witnesses are not required, but are customary. See, Barnard v. 

Kell, 271 Pa. 80, 113 Atl. 836 (1921).

Acknowledgment by Lessor is required if lease is to be recorded, Act of June 2, 1959, P.L. 454, Section 1, 21 P.S. 404, but absence of an acknowledgment does not otherwise affect the lease. See, Maguire v. Preferred Realty Co., 257 Pa. 48, 101 Atl. 100 (1917).

B. Special Provisions to Be Included in All Forms of Leases. 

The seven basic requirements of a written lease, will effect the relationship of landlord and tenant, insuring the tenant possession for the specified term, and affording the landlord the minimum benefits of the relationship, namely his right to collect rent, and the remedies provided by law for collection of rent or recovery of possession at the end or prior determination of the term. However, every lease should include specific provisions for the following conditions, since both the common law and statutory provisions of landlord and tenant law supply terms which if not modified by the parties are usually contrary to the actual intended agreement of the parties.

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

(1) Use Clause. If no provision as to use of the premises is set forth, the tenant is entitled to use the premises without restriction, except that he may not commit waste. 

Philadelphia v. Merchant & Evans, 296 Pa. 126, 145 Atl. 

706 (1929). The landlord must therefore restrict the tenant's use to the actual intended purpose. In a dwelling or apartment house the tenant should be limited to use as "a private dwelling and no other", or to use as a "single family dwelling, and no other".

A farm property should be let for use as a farm. 

This has been held to require the tenant to farm the land in accordance with good husbandry: Lewis v. Jones, 17 Pa. 

262 (1851).

In commercial properties a restriction limiting the use of the demised premises to a particular business may be necessary to protect other property in the vicinity owned or acquired by the landlord. In Shopping Centers particular care must be given to limit the type of business and to avoid infringement of use clauses in leases of other tenants. (See Shopping Center Leases, III C, infra.)

In percentage leases the use clause is most important, and must be coupled with an affirmative covenant to operate. (See Percentage Leases, III B, infra.)

The use clause may be in the form of a covenant or a condition. If specifically drawn as a condition, breach will permit forfeiture of the lease.

If use is limited to a particular use such as a tavern and no other purpose, and such use becomes unlawful, the lease may terminate under the doctrine of impossible performance. (No Pennsylvania cases directly in point.) If only part of the purpose is unlawful, the lease will continue, for the tenant can use the premises for the other purposes intended. Girard Trust Co. v. Tremblay Co., 291

Pa. 507, 140 Atl. 506 (1928). 

Tenant may require a reciprocal covenant from landlord as to leasing of other property by landlord for a competitive use. (2) Repairs. There is no implied covenant that premises are tenantable or fit for any particular use. Rosser v. Cussani, 97 Pa. Super. 255 (1929); Davis v. Pierce, 52 

Pa. Super. 615, 617 (1913). Rule of caveat emptor applies.

Spaulding v. Justice, 68 Pa. Super. 133 (1917). Tenant must therefore require a specific agreement on the part of landlord to make repairs if this is part of the bargain. 

See, Silverman v. Bullock, 165 Pa. Super. 216, 67 A.2d 586 (1949).

In absence of any provision in the agreement, landlord has no obligation to repair or rebuild. Lopez v.

PENNSYLVANIA LEASES 

11 

Gukenback, 391 Pa. 359, 137 A.2d 771 (1958). Tenant has an implied obligation to make ordinary repairs founded on the implied covenant not to commit waste. Smith v. Chappell, 25 Pa. Super. 81 (1904). This extends only to ordinary repairs, and does not include repairs resulting from acts of third parties or conditions in the nature of "wear and tear". Patton v. U.S., 139 F. Supp. 279, 283 (W.D. 

Pa., 1955).

If building is destroyed by fire or casualty and no express provision in lease, tenant of entire premises remains liable for rent. The Pa. Co. for Insuring Lives v. 

Bodek, 77 Pa. Super. 473 (1921). Leases for only part of building will terminate. Moving Picture Co. v. Scottish 

U. & N. Ins. Co., 244 Pa. 358, 90 Atl. 642 (1914). 

As to third parties landlord is liable to comply with notices of public authorities, and is liable for negligence resulting from failure to repair common areas. Tenant in possession is liable as to third persons for negligence in failing to make repairs as to leased portions. 

Usual lease will require tenant to keep leased premises clean and in good repair and at termination to return premises in same condition reasonable wear and tear and damage by accidental fire excepted.

Specific provisions recommended: 

(a) LEASE FOR ENTIRE BUILDING ALTERNATE PRO- VISIONS. 

(1) Tenant liable for all repairs, interior and exterior, structural or otherwise. (May apply in long term lease.) (2) Tenant liable for interior repairs and sidewalks, driveways. (3) Tenant liable for all repairs except roof, walls, heating and plumbing system. (4) Provide specifically for replacement of glass. Provide for release of lessor from any liability by reason of holding over of existing tenant, or delay in completing new structure, alterations or repairs. (2) Additional Rent. (a) In long term leases, increase in taxes, water and sewer rents may be substantial. Include provi-

In negotiating and drafting the lease, counsel for the landlord should be familiar with the basic law applicable to landlord's liability. B. Percentage Leases. 

The Percentage Lease is widely used for retail stores in prime commercial locations, and in virtually all Shopping Center leases. The advantage to the landlord is to insure a rental commensurate with the economic value of the location. The advantage to the tenant is the possibility of obtaining a longer term lease, and assurance that the rental is fixed with relation to the volume of business done. 

(1) Types of Percentage Leases. 

(a) Percentage of gross business with a fixed minimum. (b) Straight percentage with no minimum. (c) Percentage of gross business with no minimum initially, but with minimum fixed periodically based on gross business of a specified period. (d) Percentage of profits. (e) Fixed amount per sales unit.

C. Shopping Center Leases. 

The development of shopping centers has created a new relationship and special problems between landlord and tenant. A shopping center lease is in fact a tri-party agreement between landlord, tenant and other tenants of the center. (1) Rentals. Percentage rentals are almost universal in shopping center leases. Comments on Percentage Leases are generally applicable.

Tenant will seek covenant restricting leases to other tenants which will compete. 

Provide remedy for enforcing breach of covenant by either landlord or tenant.

Leases for industrial properties include the provisions applicable to commercial leases generally. A special type of lease has been developed that is particularly adapted to single occupancy industrial properties, and in some instances to commercial uses also. This is known as the "net lease". 

(1) Net Leases. 

(a) Economic Factors. Industry has found that capital can best be employed in machinery and inventory, rather than tied up in real estate. A long term lease gives the industrial tenant most of the advantages of ownership without a permanent investment of large amounts of capital. Moreover, tax considerations make leasing attractive to the tenant. The long term net lease is essentially a financing transaction with the tenant's lease a primary source of credit for the capital invested in the real estate, without the tenant investing cash or being directly obligated on a mortgage. In the concept of a "net lease" the tenant bears the expense and risk of an owner of real estate without actual ownership. Use of the real estate for the economic life of the building is obtained through an initial term and renewal options.

A net lease is also used in sale and leaseback transactions for either industrial or commercial real estate. If the tenant has an option to purchase, option price must be substantial or for federal income tax purposes the "lease" may be considered an installment purchase contract and not a "lease". (1) Net rental during initial term sufficient to pay interest and amortization on landlord's investment during initial term, with allowance for income taxes. (2) Substantial reduction in rentals during renewal terms. (3) No abatement of rent in case of fire. 

(d) Construction of Building.
