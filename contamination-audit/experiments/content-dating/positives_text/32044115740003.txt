SUMMARY Purpose of and Need for the Proposed Federal Action. 

The pro- posed federal action is the negotiation of an international regime covering exploration and exploitation of Antarctic mineral resources. 

Conclusion of such a regime would take place through negotiations initiated by the Antarctic Treaty Consultative Parties. The precise form of the regime would be determined during the course of the negotiations. However, it would be designed to be consistent with the 

Antarctic Treaty of 1959 and to further the principles and goals of that treaty.

The primary purpose of the regime would be to establish an agreed system for determining the acceptability of potential mineral resource activities in Antarctica and for governing any such activities that were determined to be acceptable. The system would include mechanisms for assessing the potential environmental consequences of mineral resource activities in Antarctica and evaluating the sufficiency of information for such assessments; for determining whether activities would be acceptable in light of potential environmental impacts; and for governing the ecological, technological, political, legal, and economic aspects of those activities determined to be acceptable. regime would cover all mineral exploration and exploitation activities on the Antarctic continent and in the adjacent offshore areas. 

The need for the proposed action reflects several factors. The first is the growing interest in potential Antarctic mineral resources and the fact that there is no agreed legal basis for the conduct of

The Antarctic Treaty, which was originally signed by 12 countries, including the United States, sets aside Antarctica and the waters south of 60° south latitude for peaceful purposes only. In addition, the treaty provides for freedom of scientific research in Antarctica and cooperation in undertaking research there. It also calls for regular consultative meetings for the purpose of recommending measures in furtherance of its provisions and principles. Among recommendations adopted have been measures aimed at the protection of the Antarctic environment and Antarctic living resources. To date, the Antarctic Treaty and its system of consultative meetings have been unusually successful examples of international cooperation. 

The Antarctic Treaty does not address the issue of possible Antarctic mineral resource activities. At the time the treaty was negotiated, the possibility of mineral resource development seemed remote. In recent years, events in international resource markets and resulting perceptions of resource scarcity have increased interest in the resource potential of Antarctica. No significant mineral reserves have been discovered in Antarctica, but the possibility of such reserves has been inferred from scattered mineral occurrences as well as from what is known of the geological history of the area. Developing any possible mineral deposits would entail overcoming formidable obstacles because of the remoteness of Antarctica and the severity of environmental conditions. Some experts believe exploitation of possible offshore hydrocarbon reservoirs may be feasible within the next few decades, although exploitation of possible onshore minerals is considered more remote. Nevertheless, exploration could occur now and this raises not only environmental issues but issues bearing directly upon the legal and political status of Antarctica. 

Seven of the original 12 signatories to the Antarctic Treaty claim sovereignty over parts of Antarctica; but the remainder of the signatories, including the United States, neither assert nor recognize vi

claims. The Antarctic Treaty does not resolve this issue. However, it establishes a system within which all parties, claimants and nonclaimants, are able to cooperate in the conduct of the specific activities dealt with by the treaty. Possible exploration and exploitation of minerals raises difficult legal and political questions relating to the sovereignty issue. The regime envisaged in the proposed federal action, therefore, is designed to provide a system in which claimants and nonclaimants can work together to achieve its purposes and its effective implementation.

The need for an international regime dealing with possible Antarctic mineral resource activities has been recognized by the 

Antarctic Treaty Consultative Parties. At the most recent meeting of the Consultative Parties, the participants concentrated on the requirements for such a regime and committed themselves to specific discussions on the subject. (See Recommendation XI-1 of the Eleventh 

Antarctic Treaty Consultative Meeting attached at Appendix C.) 

Potential Environmental Impacts. Future exploration and exploitation of mineral resources in Antarctica could result in significant harm to its environment. The proposed federal action is designed to insure that these potential impacts are assessed in future decisions on possible mineral resource activities and that such impacts are minimized with respect to any activities that are permitted. Two major difficulties are inherent in attempting to evaluate the potential nature and severity of environmental impacts associated with Antarctic mineral exploration and exploitation. The first is the lack of information about the possibie location and potential of mineral resources. The second is incomplete knowledge and understanding of 

Antarctic ecosystems and how they could be affected by human activities.

It is possible, however, to identify the basic types of activities that could take place and the primary concerns associated with those activities.

The exploitation of offshore hydrocarbon reservoirs could result in the accidental release of large amounts of `crude oil into the 

Southern Ocean from tanker accidents and well blowouts. Blowouts would be difficult to stop or control in a timely fashion during 12 14. 66 fis... vii

periods of ice coverage--pack ice or sea ice. Oil spills could have severe effects on the nearby marine communities because of the lethal and sublethal effects of oil. Large numbers of penguins could be affected if an oil spill occurred near a rookery. The major direct impact to penguins would be the oiling of feathers. Indirect impacts to penguins and other organisms could occur through consumption of oil contaminated krill. The aggregate impact from many oil spills is unpredictable, but any event threatening the stability of the krill population, or a lowering of the krill population and its replacement by other zooplankton, would have a major adverse impact on the Antarctic food web, which is so dependent on krill. 

Onshore support activities or mining activities would have significant local impacts. The simple terrestrial communities found in 

Antarctica are particularly susceptible to perturbations. These communities tend to be concentrated in the ice-free areas of Antarctica, which represent a small proportion of the area of the continent. 

Antarctic seal and bird communities depend upon ice-free areas. These same areas would likely be the sites of any onshore mineral-related activities. Unregulated development in these areas, therefore, could involve significant environmental damage. 

Antarctica plays a significant role in influencing global climate. Some experts are concerned that a large amount of particulate air pollution from mineral development could alter the reflective properties of the surface (albedo) of Antarctica enough to alter global climate. The chance of this seems remote, but the potentially. dire consequences justify further careful study.

Antarctica holds special aesthetic and scientific value because it is virtually unspoiled. This value could be tarnished or lost by activities related to mineral development. In particular, Antarctica serves as a monitoring site for global pollution. An increase in man's activities in Antarctica could seriously affect its use as a global monitoring site, as pollution from sources in Antarctica increases relative to present background levels. 

Environmental Consequences of the Proposed Federal Action and 

Alternative Actions.

The United States could pursue a variety of viii

actions to deal with possible Antarctic mineral resource activities. 

The alternatives considered in this statement are listed below. They are: • No action;

Negotiation of coordinated national activities to deal with 

Antarctic minerals;

Negotiation of a regime covering exploration activities only; 

Negotiation of a regime with the presumption that activities are permitted;

Negotiation of a regime allowing only activities which would be judged acceptable (the proposed federal action); 

Negotiation of a regime coupled with a temporary moratorium on mineral activities; and

Negotiation of a permanent moratorium on mineral resource activities in the Antarctic.

The above alternatives, with the exception of the "no action" option, could apply to the entire area of the Antarctic Treaty or could be limited to Antarctic offshore areas. 

Selection of the proposed federal action as the preferred alternative is based on the conclusion that it would provide an effective mechanism for developing measures to protect the Antarctic environment in the context of satisfying the full range of United States Antarctic interests.

Under the proposed federal action, proposed activities would have to meet environmental standards before they could be undertaken. 

There would also be standards relating to the sufficiency of information for making necessary decisions, thereby creating incentives for research to fill data gaps. The proposed federal action would permit

site-specific considerations to be evaluated as well as the aggregate impacts of mineral resource activities in Antarctica. As noted, the proposed federal action is designed to protect the full range of United States interests, including nondiscriminatory access for United States nationals and firms to engage in any permitted mineral resource activities. It would offer a stable international basis for necessary economic, as well as environmental, decisions. 

The other alternatives appear less desirable for a variety of reasons. The "no action" alternative could result in the most significant environmental damage. It rests on the assumption that no measures are needed at the present time to deal with possible mineral resource activities. This assumption runs counter to the fact that certain mineral resource activities can take place now. Moreover, delay in addressing the framework within which such activities should be regulated would likely make the development of the framework itself much more difficult.

Coordination of national activities with regard to Antarctic mineral resources would provide insufficient assurance that environmental safeguards would be adopted by each nation involved. This alternative does not create the basis for resolving necessary legal and political issues and, therefore, for establishing adequate mechanisms for taking necessary environmental decisions. Consequently, the environmental impacts of this alternative, should exploration or exploitation activities occur, could be similar to those under the "no action" alternative. 1 A regime dealing with exploration only would have certain advantages because it would concentrate upon those activities which are most likely to become feasible within the immediate future. A gradualist approach could simplify handling of the complex issues inherent in possible Antarctic mineral resource activities. At the same time, however, this approach would make it very difficult to achieve necessary legal and political understandings beyond the exploration phase, if exploration created perceptions of significant resource potential. 

Once these perceptions were generated, it would be very difficult for countries believing that they had a special interest in the areas of perceived resource potential--either through specific activities or Xx

territorial claims--to participate in a viable international regime. 

Resource development, in the absence of a regime, could have serious environmental impacts. 

A regime that presumes mineral-related activities are permitted, unless specifically forbidden, would not create incentives necessary for development of adequate data bases for decisions. Decisions in the absence of such data could result in undesirable and unnecessary environmental impacts, both in terms of site-specific and aggregate impacts. 

A regime equivalent to that described in the proposed federal action, coupled with a temporary moratorium, offers a mechanism for environmental protection quite similar to that reflected in the proposed federal action. The difficulty with this alternative, however, is that a temporary moratorium would apply an artificial framework to decisions regarding Antarctic mineral resources. Too short a moratorium could create pressures for premature decisions about possible mineral resource activities; too long a moratorium could reduce incentives to undertake research necessary to establish an adequate data base for decisions, when decisions were necessary. 

A permanent moratorium offers the possibility of the greatest measure of environmental protection. If effective, presumably, there would be no environmental impact from mineral resource activities in Antarctica. The difficulty is that such a moratorium, even if negotiable, would likely be unstable over time. In the absence of realistic information regarding mineral resource potential, pressures for exploration and possible development could become overwhelming, with a consequent breakdown of such a moratorium. In these circumstances, no structure would exist for mineral resource-related activities. This alternative, in part, rests on the assumption that final decisions on Antarctic mineral resources can or should be made at this time. There is little justification for this assumption and the alternative does not accord with United States interest in possible participation in mineral resource activities, should these activities be determined acceptable.

1. PURPOSE, NEED, AND DESCRIPTION OF PROPOSED ACTION 

1.1 

PROPOSED FEDERAL ACTION 

The proposed federal action is the negotiation of a regime covering possible exploration for and exploitation of mineral resources in Antarctica. The regime would be incorporated into an international agreement which would set forth the objectives of the regime and establish the obligations, standards, and machinery necessary to achieve those objectives. The form of the regime would be determined during the course of the negotiation itself. However, it would be designed to be consistent with the Antarctic Treaty of 1959 and, in fact, would be designed to further the principles and goals of that treaty. 

The basic purpose of the regime would be to establish an agreed system for determining the acceptability of possible mineral resource activities in Antarctica and for governing any such activities that were determined to be acceptable. This regime would include the following elements: 

• Provision that the Antarctic Treaty be maintained in its entirety;

Provision that the parties to the regime be specifically bound by Articles I, IV, and VI of the Antarctic Treaty; 

• Provision that Article IV of the Antarctic Treaty would not be affected, and that the principles embodied in Article IV would AP 19

be safeguarded in application to the area covered by the 

Antarctic Treaty;

Provision for the protection of the Antarctic environment and its dependent ecosystems and, to this end, means for: (i) Assessing the possible impact of mineral resource development activities on the Antarctic environment in order to provide for informed decision-making; (ii) Determining whether mineral resource activities will be acceptable; (iii) Governing the ecological, technological, political, legal, and economic aspects of those activities in cases where they would be determined acceptable, including: (a) Establishing, as an important part of the regime, rules relating to the protection of the Antarctic environment; and (b) Requiring that mineral resource activities undertaken pursuant to the regime be undertaken in compliance with such rules;

Provision that the regime cover commercial exploration (activities relating to minerals involving the retention of proprietary data and/or exploratory drilling) and commercial exploitation, including development, production, un-site processing and storage, and transportation;

Provision that the regime apply to mineral resource activities on the continent and surrounding offshore areas, with an emphasis upon the offshore area as the most likely to be considered first for mineral resource activity (while attention has been given to icebergs as a possible source of fresh 1-2

water, questions dealing with this topic are not considered by the proposed regime but will be dealt with separately if the issue arises);

Provision that activities covered by the regime take place only as authorized in accordance with its provisions; 

Establishment of a decision-making process that would effectively judge the acceptability of such activities in the context of established standards and criteria and that also would determine the sufficiency of information to approve or reject the proposed activities (for further discussion see response number one to the Marine Mammal Commission in Appendix D); 

Establishment of mechanisms for defining and applying the terms and conditions under which any permitted activity would take place; and

Provision that the interests of all mankind in Antarctica not be prejudiced by the regime. 

1.2 BACKGROUND 

Antarctica, from the original voyages of discovery until World War II, was primarily an area for exploratory expeditions and seasonal harvesting of whales and seals. With the acceleration of scientific and technological developments following World War II, scientific activities came to the fore. As a largely untouched continent, Antarctica offered an excellent opportunity for study of global processes. For this reason, cooperative scientific programs in Antarctica were a major element in the International Geophysical Year, which took place in 1957 and 1958. The successful conduct of these scientific programs required informal political and legal understandings among the 12 nations participating in the program. These informal arrangements formed the basis for an initiative taken by the United States to conclude a treaty to provide for lasting international cooperation in scientific research in Antarctica, and to prevent Antarctica from becoming the scene or object of international WITH 163W 147 

DRANY 

L.. 1-3

competition and conflict. The Antarctic Treaty was signed in 1959 and entered into force two years later. 

Of the 12 original signatories, seven--Argentina, Australia, Chile, France, New Zealand, Norway, and the United Kingdom--claim sovereignty over parts of Antarctica. The United States, Belgium, Japan, South Africa, and the Soviet Union neither assert nor recognize such claims. The treaty establishes a basis for international cooperation in Antarctica, largely through provisions under which the parties agree to disagree over the issue of territorial sovereignty. 

The treaty sets aside Antarctica and the waters below 60° south latitude for peaceful purposes only. It prohibits nuclear explosions or the disposal of nuclear waste there. It also prohibits any measures of a military nature such as the establishment of military bases and fortifications, the carrying out of military maneuvers, or the testing of military weapons. Employment of military personnel or equipment is permitted only in support of scientific research throughout Antarctica. In order to promote these objectives and insure the observance of the treaty provisions, each contracting party has the right to designate observers to carry out inspection activities. 

In addition, the treaty provides for regular consultative meetings of representatives of the 12 original signatories and such other parties as demonstrate their interest in Antarctica by the conduct of substantial scientific research there. The meetings are for the purpose of recommending measures in furtherance of the provisions and principles of the treaty. Eleven such meetings have been held since the treaty formally came into effect in June 1961. Poland joined these meetings in 1977 as the thirteenth Consultative Party, and the consultative status of the Federal Republic of Germany was recognized in March 1981. 

The Antarctic Treaty has been an unusual and successful example of international cooperation among states with differing political systems as well as different legal and political views regarding Antarctica itself. However, the treaty, it should be stressed, is a limited-purpose agreement. It defines specific objectives and establishes an imaginative legal and political framework to permit achievement of those objectives. The treaty does not apply to activities in 1-4

Antarctica other than those enumerated in it. Among those activities with which the treaty does not deal are questions of resource development. At the time of the conclusion of the treaty, the prospect of these activities seemed too remote and the issues that they posed too difficult. 

1.3 UNITED STATES OBJECTIVES IN ANTARCTICA 

United States policy towards Antarctica reflects interrelated objectives that have remained consistent over the two decades since the conclusion of the Antarctic Treaty. A number of these objectives are achieved through the provisions of the treaty; others have been pursued through the system of regular consultative meetings established under the treaty.

The objectives of the United States with respect to Antarctica may be summarized as follows:

Prevention of Antarctica's becoming the scene or object of international discord or conflict;

Maintenance of Antarctica as a demilitarized and denuclearized area;

Maintenance of Antarctica as an area of free and cooperative scientific research; 

Maintenance of cooperative relations among all states active in Antarctica;

Preservation of United States access to all areas of Antarctica and surrounding marine areas for peaceful purposes; 

Preservation of any basis for a United States claim of territorial sovereignty in Antarctica that existed prior to the 

Antarctic Treaty; 

Protection of the Antarctic environment;

Conservation of living resources in Antarctica and the Southern Ocean;

Development of a system for possible mineral resource activities in Antarctica; and

Assurance that the United States have non-discriminatory access to any mineral resource activities in Antarctica determined to be acceptable.

As noted, the Antarctic Treaty has been and remains the primary vehicle through which the United States seeks to achieve its interests and objectives in Antarctica. The treaty and its system of consultative meetings has worked very well and has served very well the interests and objectives of the United States. Therefore, insuring the vitality of the treaty and the treaty system, including its capacity to deal with newly emerging resource issues, is in itself a major component of United States Antarctic policy. 

1.4 EMERGENCE OF RESOURCE ISSUES 

During the two decades since the Antarctic Treaty entered into force, important changes have taken place in perceptions regarding the possibilities of resource development in the Antarctic Treaty area. 

These perceptions related both to living resources found in the waters surrounding Antarctica--which are known to exist in large quantities-and to possible mineral resources. Beginning in the mid-1970s, the 

Consultative Parties began to direct their action to these issues. 

Priority was placed upon the subject of Antarctic marine living resources, and in 1977 the Consultative Parties agreed upon the need to develop a regime to provide for the conservation of such living resources. During the next two and a half years, detailed negotiations took place on Antarctic marine living resources, resulting in the successful conclusion of the Convention on the Conservation of 

Antarctic Marine Living Resources at a diplomatic conference held in 

Canberra, Australia, in May 1980. The signatories to that convention are now undertaking the steps, through their domestic processes, to 1-6

seek ratification of the convention. Eight ratifications are neces sary to bring it into force. 

Attention to Antarctic mineral resources developed in parallel to that directed to the living resources of the area. By the mid-1970s, events in international resource markets--particularly with regard to hydrocarbons--and resulting perceptions of resource scarcity, roused interest in the resource potential of new and frontier areas, including Antarctica. As later sections of this EIS will indicate, there is no firm information on the resource potential of Antarctica; however, the possibility of significant mineral deposits, including offshore hydrocarbons, has been inferred from scattered mineral occurrences and from the geological history of the area. 

Mineral resource development in Antarctica, however, would face formidable obstacles. New technologies would be required. The remoteness of Antarctica and the severity of the environmental conditions encountered there would make the costs of development activities extraordinarily high. Given the uncertainties, it is not known whether development of mineral resources in Antarctica will become a practical possibility. Whether development occurs depends largely upon economic factors, particularly the availability of mineral supplies elsewhere.

At the same time, exploration activities designed to locate resource deposits can, in fact, take place with existing technology. 

The conduct of commercial exploration activities--involving the retention of proprietary data--raises legal and political issues bearing directly upon the claims and non-recognitions of claims to territorial sovereignty in Antarctica. States claiming such sovereignty would assert, in the areas over which they claim jurisdiction, the right to authorize and control commercial exploration in the same way that they would assert the right to control any mineral resource development. 

States neither claiming nor recognizing sovereignty (including the United States) would assert the right to authorize and control commercial exploration by their nationals anywhere in Antarctica. Therefore, though it is not known whether development of mineral resource activities will take place in Antarctica, the prospect of commercial exploration has raised the need for decisions regarding mineral 37 RARY

1.5 CONCEPTS UNDERLYING THE PROPOSED FEDERAL ACTION 

The proposed federal action is to initiate negotiation for the establishment of a regime, that is, a system for making decisions about possible activities that might lead to mineral resource exploitation and for governing any such activities that may be permitted. primary justification of the action is that decisions regarding mineral resource activities--commercial exploration activities at least-will be required in the near future and that there exists, at present, no agreed basis for making such decisions. The regime is designed to establish this agreed basis. It should be stressed that negotiation of the regime will not in itself answer the questions of whether mineral resource activities will or should take place in Antarctica. The regime is designed to establish an effective mechanism with the necessary legal obligations; scientific, environmental, and resource management; and necessary decision-making procedures and machinery to answer these questions. 

The international regime would involve a system for making judgments about Antarctic mineral resource activities withcut any presumpticn for or against the activities. It would involve answering threshold questions about the acceptability of possible mineral resource activities in any or all areas of Antarctica. If such thresholds were crossed, the system would impose the specific terms and conditions under which those activities must take place--terms and conditions designed, inter alia, to insure that they did not pose unacceptable environmental risks, and to insure the security of tenure and related resource management requirements to provide for their orderly conduct. 

From the perspective of the United States, negotiation of a regime for Antarctic mineral resources is designed to serve all those objectives that have been identified within the United States' Antarctic policy (Section 1.3 above). These include the United States' interests in the right to engage in any mineral resource activities 1-8

that become practicable and acceptable. Therefore, the development of a regime must enable the United States to take part on a nondiscriminatory basis in any mineral resource activities that might be permitted in Antarctica.

The regime is also designed to serve the United States' environmental interests in Antarctica. From an environmental perspective, therefore, one of the basic purposes of the regime would be the determination of the acceptability of mineral resource activities in Antarctica. The system incorporated in the proposed federal action would involve agreement upon standards guaranteeing that any mineral resource activities would not pose unacceptable risks to the environment. In order to make such decisions, there would have to be sufficient information to judge the possible risks. Therefore, it is envisaged that the regime would also set forth criteria relating to the sufficiency of information in relation to decisions about mineral resource activities.

This regime would be designed to be consistent with the ecosystem approach taken in the Convention on the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources. This ecosystem approach would provide the necessary perspective to maintain the balance and productivity of the 

Antarctic environment. 

An additional concept underlying the proposed federal action is that the system for making decisions about Antarctic mineral resources--the regime--should be international in character. Specifically, it should involve the 14 Antarctic Treaty Consultative Parties and others who, in the future, may be in a position to undertake the activities covered by the regime. 

The need for the regime to be incorporated in an international agreement rests upon several factors. First, among the Antarctic 

Treaty Consultative Parties active in Antarctica, there are differing perceptions regarding the legal and political status of Antarctica; specifically, over the validity of claims to territorial sovereignty there. These legal and political issues must be dealt with in any decisions about the mineral resource activities made in Antarctica. 

Second, since there are a number of countries with specific interest in activities relating to Antarctic mineral resources, collective decisions are required--binding upon all active states--if United +4 MLI CRANT fic... 1-9

States objectives regarding Antarctic mineral resources are to be effectively implemented. Finally, an explicit assumption of the proposed federal action is that an international regime is necessary to preserve the important goals achieved under the Antarctic Treaty, i.e., the reservation of Antarctica for peaceful purposes only, including free and cooperative conduct of scientific research. A corollary assumption is that the Antarctic Treaty and the treaty system should be maintained and strengthened as the framework through which the United States pursues its interests in the Antarctic. 1-10

Negotiation of the proposed regime may be viewed as one point on a continuum of possible approaches to dealing with exploration for and exploitation of mineral resources in Antarctica. Eight approaches have been identified on this continuum and are discussed in this statement.

These approaches include the proposed federal action and alternative actions which range from taking no action to the negotiation of a permanent moratorium on mineral resource activities. These approaches can be described as follows:

Seeking no action to deal with Antarctic mineral resource activities; 

Seeking coordinated national activities to deal with Antarctic minerals;

Negotiation of an international regime that would deal with mineral exploration only;

Negotiation of an international regime that would deal with mineral exploration and exploitation and which would presume that mineral-related activities would be permitted;

2.1 NO ACTION 

This alternative is based on the premise that development of an agreed regime is either unnecessary or undesirable. 

There is a strong possibility that there will never be extensive mineral resource exploitation in the Antarctic. This factor in itself raises the issue of the need to negotiate at least a mineral resource regime at this time. Inevitably, development of a regime that achieves a satisfactory accommodation of the diverse national interests of the Consultative Parties in the Antarctic will require the expenditure of considerable political capital. Also, a question can be raised as to whether the United States should seek to pursue its mineral resource interests through unilateral action rather than through the pursuit of an agreed regime.

On the whole, however, the drawbacks of the no action alternative far outweigh its advantages. In the absence of a regime, there will be no set of broadly effective environmental regulations to govern possible mineral resource activities in Antarctica. Although extensive mineral extraction activities are not expected at least for the rest of this century, certain exploratory activities, such as the 2-2

drilling of exploratory offshore oil wells, are more likely. In the absence of environmental regulation, these activities could easily produce environmental problems far out of proportion to their extent, because of the harsh climatic conditions in the Antarctic and the lack of knowledge of the area's ecology. 

Without an international regime, relatively small-scale exploration activities would also have a great potential for causing political friction and conflicts among those nations most active in the Antarctic. In an age when every country is concerned with obtaining needed petroleum, even relatively insignificant mineral activities would likely create difficult disputes relating to territorial sovereignty in Antarctica, which, in turn, could prevent adequate environmental measures from being developed. 

Furthermore, in the absence of a regime, those who would desire. to engage in exploration and exploitation would face considerable legal and political uncertainty as to their ability to obtain clear rights to whatever minerals they might discover.

COORDINATED NATIONAL ACTIVITIES TO DEAL WITH ANTARCTIC MINERALS 

Under this alternative, each country would formulate its own regulations for Antarctic mineral activities and there would then be coordination among states to attempt to avoid unnecessary conflicts. 

This option would require little action at this time and would presumably be relatively easy to negotiate. It would also leave considerable flexibility for whatever eventual arrangements were to be worked out for mineral activities. 2.2 

A system of voluntary coordination, however, entails a considerable likelihood of friction between the different national regimes. 

It does not create the incentives necessary to resolve the legal and political issues relating to mineral resource activities, and thus to establish the basis for effective environmental regulation. 

Voluntary coordination could leave each country free to interpret its environmental obligations. This approach, therefore, could lead to inconsistent environmental standards and to inconsistent requirements for assessing possible mineral resource activities.

2.A REGIME COVERING EXPLORATION ACTIVITIES ONLY 

A The only mineral resource activities that are reasonably likely to occur in the near future are those related to exploration. regime that dealt only with exploration activities could avoid the disputes that can be expected in seeking to resolve questions relating to possible development, particularly now, when the existence of such commercially exploitable resource deposits is entirely speculative. 

Such a regime could provide adequate environmental protection over the near term.

An exploration-only regime poses difficulties, however, as it would be extremely difficult to negotiate and would tend to be unstable over time.

The difficulty of this type of regime is illustrated by the situation that would arise if a resource discovery were made. In those circumstances, there would be little incentive for those states with territorial claims in the area of the find, or those states that are particularly active and influential in the area where the find is made, to agree to a regime that would diminish their control over the activity. This, in turn, could lead to exactly the type of jurisdictional dispute to be avoided if the Antarctic Treaty framework is to be preserved and if any effective system of environmental protection is to be kept in place in the Antarctic. 

Any regime that seeks to enforce environmental restrictions while allowing for the possibility of eventual commercial activity will have to strike a balance between the essential accommodations that will need to be made between the claimant and non-claimant states and the essential need to assure international viability for whatever system is developed. It would thus seem more sensible to proceed directly to a regime that regulates the full extent of possible mineral activities from the beginning. 

2.4 FULL REGIME WITH PRESUMPTION THAT ACTIVITIES ARE PERMITTED 

In this type of regime, all activities would be permitted unless they were expressly forbidden either by rules set up under the regime or by a specific decision of the regime.

The advantage of this approach is that it would appear to make mineral resource activities more likely if valuable mineral deposits 2-4

were found. It would make it more difficult for a single state or a small group of states to block mineral resource activities for political or other reasons.

A regime with a presumption in favor of mineral resource activities has significant drawbacks, particularly with regard to the question of the acceptability of mineral activities in Antarctica. The present state of knowledge of the Antarctic environment does not allow any confident decision to be made as to the acceptability of mineral activities there, except possibly for preliminary exploratory activities. If the decision is made now to develop a regime biased in favor of exploration and exploitation, there is the danger that the necessary information for rational environmental decisions will never be developed. Governments might then proceed to mineral exploration and exploitation activities without making a thorough environmental analysis, a course of action that probably would have disastrous impacts on the environment. 

2.5 THE PROPOSED FEDERAL ACTION 

The proposed federal action is an effort to meet United States environmental, political, and economic needs for the Antarctic area. 

The proposed federal action calls for a regime that permits only those activities shown to be environmentally acceptable, based on sufficient information relating to the Antarctic environment. Because approval will be possible only if information is available, those who wish to engage in mineral activity will be encouraged to support the development of scientific information on the Antarctic ecosystem. 

The proposed federal action calls for the maintenance of the present Antarctic Treaty. system with its freedom of scientific research and its provisions that are designed to prevent friction among the Treaty Parties over the legal and political status of the Antarctic. One of the primary goals of the regime will be to assure that the discovery of mineral resources in the Antarctic does not lead to political conflict.

2.6 NEGOTIATION OF A REGIME COUPLED WITH A TEMPORARY MORATORIUM ON MINERAL ACTIVITIES 

A regime that includes a moratorium for a fixed term would be similar in some ways to the existing situation wherein there is an agreed policy of voluntary restraint while a mineral regime is being considered.

A moratorium for a fixed time, as part of a regime, would have the advantage of providing more time for the collection of scientific data before any decision on mineral resource activities would be made. 

A moratorium would eliminate the possibility of environmentally harmful activities taking place during the time it is in effect. 

Not enough is known at this time, however, to make it possible to set an appropriate period for a moratorium. There is no rational way to determine the length of the period without clear knowledge of the Antarctic environment; the minerals that might exist there and the environmental impacts of various mineral activities that might take place there; and how long it will take to develop this information. 

If the moratorium is too long it will artificially decrease the interest of governments and others in exploring for minerals and, possibly even more important, in developing the information necessary for making rational decisions on the environmental acceptability of mineral activities. If, on the other hand, the moratorium is toc short, when it expires there will be an inevitable pressure to begin exploration and exploitation right away.

It should be pointed out that a regime as envisioned in the proposed federal action will require that there are no mineral activities until they are shown to be environmentally acceptable. This requirement will have the practical effect of providing that mineral activities should not take place in the absence of data upon which to make intelligent environmental decisions. 2-6 2

2.ESTABLISHMENT OF A PERMANENT CLOSURE OF THE ANTARCTIC TO RESOURCE ACTIVITIES 

The primary advantage of a permanent moratorium on mineral activities in the Antarctic is that, if such a moratorium were to be established and respected, there would be no environmental degradation of the Antarctic environment as a result of mineral activities. 

Unfortunately, such a moratorium would probably be unstable over time.

As mineral resources in the more extensively explored regions become exhausted, there will be a mounting pressure to explore other areas. In addition, the tendency to believe that vast resources exist in inaccessible areas will further fuel interest in Antarctic mineral activities. As time passes, the pressure to break the moratorium will grow and there will be a possibility that it will collapse, leaving no structure to regulate mineral activities and opening the Antarctic to environmental harm.

In addition, the United States does have an interest in providing itself with needed minerals. In an era when people are concerned about shortages of natural resources, it would seem impractical to attempt to set aside an Antarctic preserve outside of the ambit of natural resource activity. The other Antarctic Treaty Parties face similar concerns and it would thus seem unlikely that a permanent moratorium could be negotiated in any case. 

2.8 SUB-OPTION: A REGIME THAT DEALS ONLY WITH OFFSHORE HYDROCARBONS 

The consensus of expert opinion is that the only resource activity that is possible in Antarctica in the next decades is the exploration for and exploitation of offshore petroleum deposits, should they exist. This view raises the possibility of establishing a regime to cover only offshore activities. This approach has the attraction of focusing negotiating attention on those resource activities most likely to take place in the foreseeable future. 

At the same time, it could be argued that it would be easier to settle all mineral issues at one time and have a regime in place for whatever activity might be considered.

3.1 AGREEMENTS PERTAINING TO THE ANTARCTIC REGION 3.1.1 The Antarctic Treaty and Agreed Recommendations 

The Antarctic Treaty provides the overall political framework for activities in the Antarctic. The treaty grew out of the increased interest in scientific research in the Antarctic during the International Geophysical Year (1957-1958) and the need that was recognized at that time to have a firm political foundation for the continuation of scientific cooperation among the nations active in the Antarctic. 

The treaty was negotiated at a conference held in Washington in 1959 and participated in by those states then most actively interested in the Antarctic: Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Chile, France,

Japan, New Zealand, Norway, South Africa, the USSR, the United Kingdom, and the United States. It came into effect, after its ratification by all participants in the conference, on June 23, 1961. 

In addition to the 12 original signatories, the treaty has also been acceded to by Poland, Czechoslovakia, Denmark, the Netherlands, Romania, the German Democratic Republic, the Federal Republic of 

Germany, Brazil, Uruguay, Peru, Italy, and Papua-New Guinea.

The treaty is designed to assure that the Antarctic is used for peaceful purposes only, that it does not become the scene or object of Cumich. 3-1

international discord, and that freedom of scientific research is respected there.

Among the provisions of the treaty are the following: • A prohibition of military activities, including testing of weapons and military maneuvers; A ban on nuclear explosions and the disposal of nuclear waste;

Freedom of scientific investigation in the area, coupled with sharing of the results of such research; • Advance exchange of information on plans for scientific activity and expeditions in Antarctica; A formula relating to territorial sovereignty in Antarctica that protects the position of states claiming such sovereignty and those nations neither asserting nor recognizing such claims, but permitting all parties to cooperate in the conduct of the activities covered by the treaty; and •Rights of on-site inspection to insure observance of treaty obligations.

In addition, and equally important, the treaty provides for regular consultations among representatives of the original signatories as well as representatives of states that later accede during such time as those latter states demonstrate their interest in Antarctica by the conduct of substantial scientific research there. Two additional acceding parties--Poland and the Federal Republic of Germany--have joined with the original 12 in participating in the regular consultative meetings. These meetings, held roughly every two years, involve consideration of matters of common interest pertaining to Antarctica and the treaty, and the development of agreed recommendations to governments.

5. DESCRIPTION OF THE EXISTING ENVIRONMENT* THE CONTINENT 

Antarctica is a geographically isolated land mass approximately centered at the South Pole and surrounded by the Southern Ocean (Figure 5-1). South America, the nearest continent to Antarctica, is 700 kilometers away. The land area of Antarctica covers approximately 

13.5 million square kilometers, slightly larger than the size of the continental United States and Mexico. Approximately 98% of this continent is permanently covered with ice; only sections along the coast, the Transantarctic Mountains and the Ellsworth Mountains have extensive snow- and ice-free areas. 

The continent is divided into three geographic provinces: East 

Antarctica, West Antarctica, and the Antarctic Peninsula. East Ant arctica, the area between approximately 30°W and 165°E (see Figure 5-1), is characterized by a vast dome-shaped sheet of ice. Beneath the ice sheet the bedrock surface is made up of large lowland plains bounded by several large interior and coastal mountain chains. The 

Transantarctic Mountains separate East Antarctica from West Antarctica and range in elevation from 2,000 to 4,530 meters. A late Cenozoic volcanic province exists along the Transantarctic Mountains in the vicinity of the Ross Sea. Evidence of volcanic activity (past and present) is also found along the western border of the Ross Sea, Marie 

Byrd Land (West Antarctica), and the Antarctic Peninsula.

West Antarctica is younger than East Antarctica and its geologic structure is less well known. The enormous ice sheet covering Antarctica averages about 2,000 meters in thickness but can be as thick as 4,500 meters. The Transantarctic Mountains actually divide this polar ice cap into the thicker East Antarctic Ice Sheet and the thinner West Antarctic Ice

The Antarctic Convergence is a zone in which the cold waters of the Antarctic dive underneath the warmer surface waters of the oceans to the north. South of the Convergence Zone, between about 40°S and 

60°S, is the world's largest current which is known as the Antarctic 

Circumpolar Current or West Wind Drift. Strong and steady westerly winds at these latitudes drive this current clockwise around Antarctica at an average speed of half a meter per second. Some of the roughest seas in the world occur in this area. 

Between the Antarctic Circumpolar Current and Antarctica is a current called the East Wind Drift, which is driven by easterly winds counterclockwise around Antarctica. However, the flow of this current is complicated by the irregular coastline of Antarctica. For example, gyres can form in bays such as the Weddell, Ross, and Bellingshausen Seas.

Between the Antarctic Circumpolar Current and the East Wind 

Drift is the Antarctic Divergence, a zone of upwelling which is important to the marine ecology of the Southern Ocean. The upwelling water is rich in nutrients that support the marine food web. 

Sea ice occurs annually in the ocean adjacent to Antarctica. 

Whereas icebergs calve off the leading edge of ice shelves, sea ice or pack ice forms from the freezing of seawater. During the winter, sea ice completely encloses Antarctica and vessels cannot reach the Antarctic coast. The area of the winter sea ice is approximately equal to that of the Antarctic Continent; the sea ice sometimes extends as far north as 55°S. As summer progresses, the sea ice moves northward where most of it melts.

The continental shelves surrounding Antarctica are relatively narrow and deep. They comprise an area of approximately 4 million square kilometers and are found at a depth of 500 to 900 meters at the shelf edge. This is two to four times deeper than the world averags shelf depth and may be due to isostatic subsidence in response to the weight of the ice cap. 5.4 THE CLIMATE 

The Antarctic weather is the world's most adverse. Average conditions are not only harsh but can be extremely variable as well. In coastal areas, mean temperatures range from about 0° C in summer to -30° C in winter. The Antarctic Peninsula and nearby islands have slightly milder winters. Mean temperatures on the continental plateau are much colder, ranging from -40° C in summer to -70° C or lower in winter. The coldest recorded temperature on earth, -88° C, was recorded on the continental plateau. 

The weather in the coastal areas can also be violent. Storms are associated with atmospheric depressions which are frequently generated around Antarctica between 60°S and 70°S. The southern extent of these storms can penetrate coastal areas, causing windy conditions and heavy snowfalls. Between storms, coastal areas often have severe winds that blow from the continental interior. These "katabatic" winds can blow at speeds greater than 70 knots for several hours, creating blizzard conditions as they pick up snow off the ground. 

Global climate is strongly affected by that of the Antarctic because heat flows from the tropics to the poles. Weather is a result of this transfer of heat. The role of Antarctica in influencing global climate is very complex and depends on interactions between the Southern Ocean, the polar ice cap, and the Antarctic atmosphere. The extent to which these interactions are resistant to perturbations is unknown, but changes could have severe consequences. Further cooling of Antarctica could initiate an ice age, whereas a warming might melt the ice cap, causing the sea level to rise more than 50 meters. 

5.5 TERRESTRIAL ECOLOGY 

Antarctic terrestrial ecosystems are simple: they lack diversity because they exist under extreme conditions for life support and 2146 ALI fic... 5-7

because they represent, in many cases, the early stage of colonization following deglaciation. Because energy pathways or food webs are simple, these ecosystems are vulnerable to disruption and are slow in recovering from disturbances.

Antarctica's terrestrial ecosystems comprise primarily nonvascular plants and small invertebrates. Very few vascular plants are present. Ecosystems differ between the Antarctic continent and the more maritime areas on the Antarctic Peninsula and associated islands. 

On the Antarctic continent, certain coastal areas contain the majority of species, while very few species inhabit the inland continent. The vegetation on the continent consists principally of scattered patches of cryptogams (mosses, lichens, algae, and fungi). The invertebrates found are bacteria, protozoa, and certain arthropods, such as springtails and mites.

The maritime areas of Antarctica have higher temperatures, more precipitation, and less snow cover. Thus, they have more developed vegetation, consisting of ferns, fruticose and crustose lichens, mosses, algae, larger fungi, and some flowering plants. Invertebrates and other lower life forms are more abundant. Springtails, mites, small wingless insects, annelids, spiders, bacteria, molds, and algae endure where conditions are most suitable. 

In general, the different types of terrestrial ecosystems appear to have a wide distribution and few species are endemic to Antarctica. 

The Antarctic Treaty has designated Specially Protected Areas as hav ing extraordinary value. 

5.6 MARINE ECOLOGY 

The marine food web of the Southern Ocean consists of large populations of seals, whales, penguins, squid, crustaceans called krill, and other animals. Upwelling at the Antarctic Convergence brings nutrient-rich water to the surface. These nutrients combined with the round-the-clock sunlight during the summer allow extremely high primary productivity from phytoplankton photosynthesis in coastal regions and along the ice edge. Dense swarms of krill--shrimp-like creatures that feed on the phytoplankton and which can grow up to three inches in length--serve as food for seals, penguins, whales, squid, and fish.

The exploitation of on-shore minerals in Antarctica would consist of an extraction phase (i.e., surface mining, underground mining, solution mining, and placer mining) and a processing phase (i.e., beneficiation and refining). Impacts on these soils would be significant and long term because they would take a very long time to recover.

Terrestrial Biology. Land use competition between terrestrial flora and fauna and man due to mining operations, support facilities, scientific establishments, and industrial institutions, would be severe in ice-free areas especially in coastal environments. Existing ice-free areas are the site of the primitive Antarctic soils that support vegetation and are used by seals and penguins as reproduction Those sites with the most potential for development, the coastal areas, contain the majority of the living species, while inland areas are largely uninhabited. Maritime Antarctica, the northern half of the Antarctic Peninsula and associated islands, are examples of relatively productive and biologically important areas. Annual reproductive areas are highly susceptible to human interferences as evidenced by the disruption of the Adelie penguin from 1956 to 1963 in 

Cape Royds, Ross Island.

Traditional methods or techniques for rehabilitating disturbed areas do not apply to Antarctica. At best, reclamation could return the land to its original contour but the living organisms could not be restored and the soils would remain chemically and physically altered over a very long time.

Air emissions such as S02 from power plants, beneficiation, and refining processes could damage terrestrial vegetation such as lichens. The lichens have disappeared completely from the McMurdo Station Area and may be threatened elsewhere. This is likely to be a local effect but may be long-term.

Exploiting offshore hydrocarbon reserves would entail drilling, extraction, storage, and transportation. The possibility of an oil blowout during drilling and extraction represents one of the most serious environmental hazards that could occur as a result of mineral exploitation in the Antarctic. However, the possibility of a blowout from an individual well would be very small. Zumberge (1981) described relevant statistics in his description of the history of oil blowouts. From 1953 to 1972, 20,373 wells were drilled worldwide but only 47 blowouts occurred. Thus, for each well the probability of a blowout was 0.23%. The largest blowout during this period, the Santa Barbara spill in 1969, released 780,000 barrels of oil, while other blowouts resulted in smaller spills. During this same period, over 18,000 wells were drilled in United States waters, but only eleven spills released more than 5,000 barrels of oil. From 1972 to 1976, no major blowout occurred in United States coastal waters. Similarly, no significant oil spill occurred from the 

102 wells drilled on the outer continental shelf off the east coast of Canada during the mid-1970s, and only one serious blowout nas occurred from 800 wells drilled in the North Sea.

These statistics reflect the fact that technology is available to minimize the occurrence of blowouts and halt the flow of oil in such an event. Some blowouts stop naturally because of clogging of the holes from underground debris. However, blowout preventers, relief wells, and other techniques to prevent or stop blowouts are available.

6.3.3 Aggregate Impacts 

The environmental impacts from individual mineral developments were discussed in sections 6.3.1 and 6.3.2. Limiting discussions to single developments, however, is inappropriate. If economics indeed become favorable for mineral exploitation, many developments might ensue; therefore, the environmental consequences of multiple developments must be considered. 

The environmental impacts from the overall development of several oil fields or basins, several onshore mineral deposits, or any combination of resources could be greater than the sum of the impacts from each individual development. For example, any single coastal mineral facility would have a severe impact on the local bird and seal populations and on the local physical features such as soils; however, the overall impact on these living and physical resources could be negligible because of the large size of Antarctica and the widespread distribution of seals, birds, soils, and other resources. 6.4.1 No Action

6.4.2 Coordinated National Activities to Deal with Antarctic Minerals 

This alternative would require each nation interested in Antarctic mineral activities to coordinate in establishing environmental measures and to agree to them, but implementation of these measures would occur through unilateral action. This alternative has the advantage of being relatively easy to negotiate because little initial action is required and eventual arrangements are flexible, allowing each nation the chance to determine its own level of environmental commitment. There are disadvantages to this alternative. 

Environmental concerns vary significantly among nations, and it is impossible to predict the type of environmental controls that would be established. With minimal environmentai controls, the potential environmental impacts from mineral-related activities would be similar 6-32

to those described in Section 6.3. Moreover, this voluntary approach to enforcement of environmental controls would not create strong incentives for nations to accede to controls. There is no assurance that environmental standards would be uniformly enforced by each nation, and this weakness could become particularly evident in the event of substantial mineral resource development. The nations most active in mineral resource development could have the weakest enforcement of environmental controls. Thus, these nations could cause a disproportionate amount of environmental damage, and, in addition, this could create friction with the environmentally concerned countries.

This alternative also fails to adequately provide the necessary overall environmental perspective which deals with aggregate impacts. 

Voluntary coordination among nations to deal with aggregate impacts would be insufficient because the less environmentally concerned nations would have little incentive to stop a planned activity and forego potential economic gains.

Furthermore, this alternative fails to deal with or provide incentives to deal with the political and legal issues of mineral exploration and exploitation. Thus, disputes between nations having territorial claims in Antarctica and those nations that neither assert nor recognize claims would surely occur, particularly if substantial mineral deposits were found. Disputes would interfere with the effectiveness of any environmental protection.

Finally, depending on the various national actions, incentives to undertake research to improve the understanding of the Antarctic environment in order to make better environmental decisions may or may not be created. 

6.4.3 A Regime Covering Exploration Activities Only 

This alternative provides sound environmental management over the short term. The regime would provide an internationally agreed-on mechanism to determine unacceptable exploration activities and to impose environmentai standards on those activities allowed. It would also provide an adequate overall environmental perspective of exploration activities.

6.4.4 Full Regime with the Presumption that Activities are Permitted

This alternative, if successfully negotiated, would mean the establishment of a legal and political basis for mineral resource activities, and would eliminate a prime source of conflict that would be detrimental to any program of environmental management. Also, internationally agreed-on environmental protection would be extended to Antarctica. Under this regime, exploration and exploitation activities would be allowed unless they were expressly forbidden by rules set up under the regime or by a specific decision of the regime. 

Local impacts from those activities that are allowed would occur, but would be mitigated by any environmental or technological standards established by the regime. Activities believed to post large-scale threats could be prohibited. 

However, this regime could produce unwise environmental management because the acceptability of mineral activities would be decided in the absence of a sufficient general understanding of the Antarctic environment, in the absence of site-specific environmental data, and without the benefit of a case-by-case analysis that would permit an evaluation of aggregate impacts.

Currently, the scientific understanding of the Antarctic environment or the effects of anthropogenic disturbances is insufficient to confidently allow or prohibit many types of activities. 6-34

Moreover, no incentives are provided to research these data deficiencies. Improper environmental rules could result from this ignorance. 

Furthermore, the extent of any activity would have important environmental implications. An activity could be acceptable at a low frequency of occurrence, but unacceptable at a higher frequency of occurrence. By not judging proposed activities on a case-by-case basis, the regime would not be able to intelligently account for this in its environmental management. In addition, the regime would not be able to analyze the aggregate impacts of activities to judge whether an unacceptable level of damage had occurred to the Antarctic environment.

This regime also fails to recognize the importance of the location of an activity in determining the significance of its impact. An activity could be relatively innocuous in most areas of Antarctica, but near special areas it could have a significant impact. This can be determined only by a regime that judges activities acceptable on a case-by-case basis and in light of sufficient environmental information. 

6.4.5 The Proposed Federal Action 

Similar to the previous alternative, the successful negotiation of this regime means the establishment of a legal and political basis for mineral resource activities, eliminating a possible source of conflict among nations and promoting the success of the regime. This action also calis for sound environmental management. The proposed regime would analyze each proposed exploration and exploitation activity on a case-by-case basis to insure environmental acceptability. 

Since the regime would be constructed and operated in cooperation among the Consultative Parties, the enforcement of decisions made by the regime can reasonably be assumed. 

Under this regime, activities would be determined acceptable only on the basis of sufficient information. The responsibility of providing the necessary information to adequately make such a decision falls on the potential explorer or exploiter. Consequently, potential developers would have an incentive to undertake research to fill general data deficiencies on the Antarctic environment. They would 1. Ac 6-35

6.4.6 Negotiation of a Regime Coupled with a Temporary Moratorium on Mineral Activities 

The environmental impact of this alternative differs only slightly from those anticipated under the proposed federal action. 

Clearly, no activities will be allowed during the moratorium; consequently, no environmental damage will occur. Once the moratorium ands, a regime equivalent to that described for the proposed federal action will control activities. The environmental impacts from such a regime were discussed in the previous section. 6-36

During the moratorium, particularly if it is long, potential developers may have less incentive or interest in undertaking scientific research to fill data deficiencies. Research could be postponed until the moratorium ends, when the opportunity for mineral exploration or exploitation exists. Scientific research may be considered to have a positive environmental result. 

6.4.7 Establishment of a Permanent Moratorium on Mineral Resource Activities in the Antarctic 

If a permanent moratorium could be maintained, no environmental degradation of the Antarctic environment would occur from mineral exploration and exploitation. A moratorium would also eliminate any scientific research that would have been initiated if the opportunity for mineral development had not been prohibited. 

The uncertainty of this alternative is that the moratorium may eventually be disregarded if pressures to tap potential mineral resources in Antarctica become overwhelming. These pressures may build as resources believed to exist in Antarctica, either through scientific inference or accidental discovery during scientific research, become scarce elsewhere. Since the moratorium is an inflexible measure, there is no means to vent pressure to develop, and a collapse of the moratorium could ensue. 

The Antarctic environment would be unprotected in the event of a collapse, and the impacts from the resultant development could be severe and similar to those described in Section 6.3. 

While there has been some interest in some forms of a moratorium in the past, currently the concept of a permanent moratorium is withcut support among the Consultative Parties.

A.2.1.2 Regional Geology 

Knowledge of the geology of Antarctica has been obtained through reconnaissance surveys and geological investigations of the limited rock outcrops and by a process of extrapolation from known geologic formations, events, and mineral resources encountered in Australia, Africa, and South America, which together with Antarctica were part of the ancient supercontinent "Gondwanaland." Antarctica is divided into three geographical provinces: East and West Antarctica and the Antarctic Peninsula. The main geologic and tectonic divisions are the 

East Antarctic Shield; the Ross orogen (an elongate region which has undergone folding, thrusting, metamorphism, and igneous activity); the 

Borchgrevink orogen; the Ellsworth orogen; the Andean orogen; and the

Cenozoic Volcanic province (Figure A-1). 

East Antarctica. The East Antarctic Shield is the oldest and most stable region of Antarctica and it has been inactive since the Precambrian Age. The basement complex of East Antarctica is composed of high-grade metamorphic and intrusive igneous rocks. Flat-lying sedimentary and volcanic rocks overlie the basement complex. The sedimentary rocks are sand and siltstones and belong to the Beacon Supergroup, while the igneous rocks, usually basalts, belong to the Ferrar Group. These strata are exposed extensively in the Transantarctic Mountains but rarely in other sections of East Antarctica (Craddock 1973).

The Transantarctic Mountains, which separate East and West Antarctica, extend from Oates Coast to Coats Land and are part of the early Paleozoic Ross orogen. Precambrian rocks, met amorphosed and intruded by igneous rock, and lower Paleozoic sedimentary and volcanic rocks were deformed and intruded by large granitic bodies during the 

Ross orogeny.

Volcanic activity occurred during the Jurassic, Tertiary, and Quarternary periods and continues to the present (National Research Council 1970). 

A.2.1.3 Cryosphere

The enormous ice sheet that covers Antarctica accounts for as much as 90% of the world's fresh water. The average thickness of this ice cap is approximately 2,000 meters but attains a thickness of over 4,500 meters. From the high elevations in the interior of the continent, the ice slowly flows outward to the edge of the continent (see Figure A-2). The ice flow varies in direction and speed in response to the topography; for example, the Transantarctic Mountains block the ice flow from East Antarctica. This mountain range actually divides the polar ice caps into the thicker East Antarctic Ice Sheet and the thinner and partly marine-based West Antarctic Ice Sheet. At the edge of the continent the ice sheet is much thinner and is more responsive to subglacial relief. Over approximately one-third of the continental perimeter, the underlying coastal relief is gentle. In these areas the ice flows in a smooth sheet at a speed of approximately ten meters per year directly toward the coast. In other areas the underlying relief is pronounced. Ice flows are constrained by the topography and flow to the coast as confined ice flows, usually at a speed of 100 to 1,000 meters per year. However, the movement of these ice sheets is incompletely understood.

A.2.1.4 

Soils 

The soils of Antarctica developed on glacier-free areas such as bedrock outcrops, talus deposits, moraines, and beach deposits. Variations in the chemical and physical properties of the soils depend on the microclimate, topography and lithology, and the age of the parent material. On the whole, the soils are the product of a cold desert climate. Because extremely low precipitation and temperatures severely limit chemical and biological activity, mechanical weathering is the dominant means by which these soils are formed. 

In general, the soils are relatively coarse textured, and classified as gravelly sands, sands, loamy sands, and occasionally sandy loams. Only in the oldest soils are the horizons somewhat defined. 

Some of the very old soils found in dry valleys and other areas have developed a salt cement horizon just below the sandy textured surface. 

If the cemented layer is broken up, these soils can be rapidly eroded by the wind. A common feature of many of the soils is a thin, oxidized surface horizon above the chemically unaltered parent material.

A.2.1.5 Permafrost 

Knowledge of the permafrost conditions in Antarctica is limited and, like soils, comes from the McMurdo-Dry Valley region of southern Victoria Land. Dry permafrost, a condition in which the soil or rock contains no ice or contains very little ice, prevails over most of the continent; however, ice-rich permafrost has been identified at some locations. When dry permafrost materials thaw, there is little or no reduction in volume and conversely no expansion upon refreezing. It does not involve the problems that are associated with the melting of A-S

ice-rich permafrost, which produces large volumes of water. Spontaneous liquefaction can cause the mass movement of sediment and water even on slight inclines.

The base of the permafrost in the McMurdo-Dry Valley area is irregular, ranging from 440 to 550 meters in depth at McMurdo Station to 970 meters near Lake Vanda. This irregularity is explained by steep geothermal gradients, the high salinity of groundwater, and possible solar heating beneath the lake ice. 

Ice-rich permafrost is more prevalent in the Antarctic Peninsula, especially in depressions and on south-facing slopes. Steep geothermal gradients may preclude the existence of any permafrost in some areas of the peninsular region (Ohio State University 1977). 

A.2.1.6 Water Resources 

The relatively few lakes in Antarctica have relatively few associated streams. They rarely receive sediments, and are rarely thermally stratified in the summer. Most lakes are highly saline and have low species diversity. They may or may not be permanently covered by ice (Parker 1978).

Ice-free oases or "dry valleys," occur in some portions of East Antarctica. They are the result of evaporation and sublimation of water and ice, and the restricted flow of glaciers into the valleys. 

The soils, ponds, or lakes within the valleys are usually saline because there is no external drainage (Ohio State University 1977). 

The best known dry valleys are those of South Victoria Land. 

Water at high pressures occurs under most of the big ice sheets as a result of bottom melting. Flow is in the form of channels or thin sheets, and subglacial lakes can be found. 

At low elevations and latitudes, meltwater from ice and snow and an occasional rainfall may saturate the soil, causing smali streams to develop (Ohio State University 1977).

A.2.2.3 Sea Ice 

Whereas icebergs calve off the front of ice shelves, sea ice or pack ice forms from the freezing of seawater and covers enormous areas around Antarctica, with significant physical and biological implications. The area of the sea ice varies several fold between the summer and the winter. In March, the area of the sea ice is approximately 3 million square kilometers; in August or September, it covers approximately 20 million square kilometers, thus, in effect, doubling the area of Antarctica. In winter, this ice pack completely encloses 

Antarctica and can extend as far north as 55°S (see Figure A-4). 

Individual ice flows extend 10 to 100 meters across and can be 3.0 meters thick, but most of the pack ice is "first year ice," which averages 1.5 meters in thickness. As the summer progresses, the ice pack moves north and melts; thus, open water can be found in coastal areas while a zone of pack ice occurs farther offshore. Sea ice moves in response to the winds and can travel up to 55 kilometers in a day. 

The widely varying areal extent of the pack ice is believed to have an important influence on worldwide climatic conditions, however the relationship is complex and only partially understood. Two factors that play a role are the thermal insulating properties of ice and its high albedo. Sea ice acts as a thermal insulator that restricts the exchange of heat between the atmosphere and the ocean. Because of its high albedo relative to water, it affects the amount of radiant energy that is absorbed regionally. These properties along with the wide seasonal fluctuations in the extent of the sea ice cover will affect the heat transfer in Antarctica and thus the global climate.

A.2.3 Air A.2.3.1 Meteorology 

The meteorological conditions encountered on the Antarctic continent and adjacent seas are the world's most adverse. The frequent passage of intense cyclonic storms offshore (60° to 70°S), strong outflow of relatively cold air from the continent (kat abatic winds), extremely low temperatures, and precipitation in excess of evaporation are the major features that contribute to rigorous climatic conditions. Not only are average conditions harsh, but the weather can be extremely variable. This high degree of variability and the relatively sparse number of weather stations makes meteorological forecasting unreliable.

In coastal areas, mean temperatures range from about 0°C in summer to -30°C in winter. The Antarctic Peninsula, particularly the northwest coast, and the islands of the Scotia Arc have a milder winter, with mean temperatures of approximately -10°C. Mean temperatures on the continental plateau are much colder, ranging from -40°C in summer to -70°C or lower in winter. These extremely low temperatures result from the absence of solar radiation during the winter (polar night) and the reflection of solar radiation off the snow and ice cover during the rest of the year. Thus, relatively little solar radiation is absorbed at Antarctica.

These radiative balance conditions contribute to another major characteristic of the Antarctic meteorology, namely, the existence throughout the year of strong surface-based temperature inversions (air temperature increasing with height). These extremely stable conditions near the surface exist most frequently in the interior of the continent during winter, when they are the norm rather than the exception, and often persist whether the winds are calm or strong. 

Coastal areas can be stormier. Stormy conditions are associated with cyclonic centers or depressions which are frequently generated around Antarctica between 60°S and 70°S. The southern extent of these A-17

: storms can penetrate coastal areas, causing windy conditions and heavy snowfalls.

Katabatic winds or gravity winds occur when cold, dense air flows down the slopes from the high continental interior. These winds can be extremely strong. With the passage of cyclonic storms, conditions are enhanced for katabatic outflow from the continent, particularly on the east coast of the Antarctic Peninsula and along most of the coast of East Antarctica. These katabatic winds often last for the entire period between cyclonic storm passages (which can range from one to several days). They are more frequent in winter and may continuously exhibit wind speeds in excess of 130 km/hr (70 knots) for several hours. During the summer, the frequency and intensity of windstorms (both katabatic and cyclonic) is significantly reduced, but by no means non-existent. A.2.3.2 

Role in Global Climate 

The primary role of the polar regions in relation to global climate is that they provide a major heat sink to counterbalance the heat source of the tropics. Atmospheric motions are initiated and maintained by patterns of heating and cooling. Changes in these thermal patterns, if persistent, can change atmospheric circulation patterns which, in turn, feed back to alter the thermal patterns. 

Thermal patterns are influenced by the composition of the atmosphere and by radiative and reflective properties of the earth's surface. Constituents of the atmosphere, both natural and anthropogenic, which can significantly affect radiative properties of the air and hence the climate, include carbon monoxide, water vapor, ozone, and aerosols, and to a less certain degree, chlorofluoromethane, nitrous oxide, methane, carbon tetrachloride, and others (Fletcher and Kelly 1978). Radiative properties of the earth's surface also have a strong influence on the patterns of heating and cooling and, thereby, atmospheric motions. Seasonal fiuctuations in the extent of pack ice around Antarctica strongly influence the heat exchange between the air and sea surface. This interaction affects air temperature and the degree to which Antarctica acts as a global heat sink. For example, the strength, frequency, and latitudinal position and track of دا 11 A-18

The reflective properties or albedo of the surface of Antarctica strongly influence the heat budget of the polar region and hence the climate. Snow and ice can reflect up to 98% of incoming solar radiation but averages 80%, while moderately rough ocean water will reflect 10% to 15% (Stewart 1978). This characteristic of the Antarctic ice cap provides a powerful positive feedback mechanism, affecting climate.

For example, a decrease in the average Antarctic temperature will lead to an increase in sea ice cover, which, in turn, increases the albedo of the southern pole, thus leading to a further cooling. 

While the importance of Antarctica in influencing the global climate is clear, the interactions and feedback mechanisms of the atmosphere-hydrosphere-cryosphere system are far too complex and polar observations far too meager to provide a complete understanding of its role.

A.2.4 Mineral Resources 

A speculative estimate of resource location, quantity, and quality may be made by extrapolating from known resources of other Gondwanaland continents, and by comparing knowledge of Antarctic mineral occurrences with the structure and petrology of other known mineral-bearing formations. It must be noted, however, that many of the world's ore-rich deposits are the result of Quaternary and Holocene supergene enrichment (the enrichment of ore by descending water). 

Because of the severe glaciation that prevails in Antarctica, it is possible that these deposits have been removed or were never formed. 

All observations of minerals in Antarctica are classified as mineral occurrences; no minerals have proven to be present in concentrations rich enough to be classified as commercially exploitable ore deposits. Coal and water are classified as identified reserves, however, their use has not been proven to be economically feasible, so they are still termed "speculative resources." A-19

Metals, non-metals, fossil fuels, water, and geothermal energy are all potential resources of Antarctica. Their further exploration and exploitation depends on economic and logistic factors. Known mineral occurrences are listed in Table A-1 and identified in Figure A-5.

Manganese nodules and metalliferous muds are two possible mineral resources of the deep ocean. The nodules are usually of interest because of their high content of copper, nickel, and cobalt. (Wright and Williams 1974).

It is not anticipated that these deep ocean resources will be covered by the regime.

Continental Shelf Resources 

Because of the global energy demand and world oil prices, hydrocarbons are of special interest. The continental shelf of Antarctica, like those of South America, Australia, and Africa, may be the location of petroleum and natural gas reserves. Any estimates of the quantities of these reserves available from the Antarctic shelf is speculative since only geophysical surveys have been conducted and gaps still exist in the data base. 

It has been suggested that Antarctica is an unlikely place to find large reserves of oil since those continental masses that once formed southern Gondwanaland do not contain as much oil as those of the Persian Gulf region (Ivanhoe 1980). Oil fields found in the continents neighboring Antarctica are only of moderate size. A.3 BIOLOGICAL ENVIRONMENT A.3.1 Terrestrial Biota 

Antarctic terrestrial ecosystems are simple: they lack diversity because they exist under extreme conditions for life support and because they represent, in many cases, the early stage of colonization following deglaciation. Even so, interrelationships between organisms are poorly understood. Because energy pathways or food webs are simple, these ecosystems are vulnerable to disruption and are slow in recovering from disturbances.

Antarctica's terrestrial ecosystems comprise primarily nonvascular plants and small invertebrates. Very few vascular plants are present. Three main types of ecosystems can be recognized: a truly terrestrial community occurs on rocks and primitive soils; a freshwater community occurs in puddles, ponds, and lakes; and communities are found in some snow-covered areas. Furthermore, these ecosystems differ between the Antarctic continent and the more maritime areas on the Antarctic Peninsula and associated islands. On the Antarctic continent, certain coastal areas contain the majority of species while very few species inhabit the inland continent. The vegetation on the continent consists principally of scattered patches of cryptogams (mosses, lichens, algae, and fungi). Liverworts are extremely rare. 

Invertebrates found are bacteria, protozoa, and certain arthropods such as springtails and mites. Plant communities in most ce-free areas of the continent favor habitats on the leeward side of wind barriers, consistent with the pattern of water supply. The distribution

The maritime areas of Antarctica have higher temperatures, more precipitation, and less snow cover. A.3.2 Marine Biota A.3.2.1 Ecology of the Southern Ocean 

In the Southern Ocean, upwelling at the Antarctic Divergence brings nutrient-rich water into the euphotic zone throughout the year. 

During the austral summer, these nutrient-rich surface waters and the 24 hours of sunshine combine to result in extremely high primary productivity, particularly in the coastal regions and along the ice edge. (Department of State 1978). The yearly primary productivity, averaged over the entire Southern Ocean, was previously thought to be quite high but currently is believed to be similar to that of other oceans (Department of State 1978). However, the concentration of primary productivity in certain areas allows for spectacular secondary productivity of unique occurrence and of interest to man as food. 

The Antarctic food web (Figure A-7) is short, allowing unusually large populations of top carnivores to develop. The key element in the food web is the krill. Krill are the dominant zooplankton and serve as an important link between the primary production and the marine mammals and birds characteristic of the Antarctic.

The dense swarms and relatively large size of krill allow economic harvesting of this resource by man. The importance of krill to the stability of the Antarctic marine ecosystem cannot be overstated. The dependence of so many predators on a single prey is unusual. Changes in krill abundance could drastically alter the populations of its predators; the resiliency of the food web to alterations is unknown. 

In general, krill have a circumpolar distribution because of the strong circumpolar currents. Thus, predators of krill generally have a circumpolar distribution. The primary productivity of the Antarctic coastal waters is comparable with the most productive areas in the ocean. Values of approximately 3.0 grams of carbon per square meter per day or more have been reported (El-Sayed 1978). However, a mean primary productivity for the entire Southern Ocean has been estimated to be 0.134 grams of carbon per square meter per day, which is comparable to the productivity of other oceans (El-Sayed 1978). 

Many factors may influence primary production in the Antarctic region. Temperature, nutrients, light, turbulence, and grazing may be important (El-Sayed 1978). The variation in light between winter and summer is extreme and plays a significant role in determining primary production. Phytoplankton blooms occur during the austral summer, which is characterized by 24 hours of sunlight a day. Furthermore, other factors, in addition to the season, control light penetration in the waters. For example, sea ice appreciably reduces light penetration, which can decrease primary productivity in the underlying water column. Upwelling appears to insure that nutrients are plentiful in the Antarctic waters (El-Sayed 1978). 

Water column stability may influence primary production. The low productivity of subantarctic water and the Antarctic Convergence may be due to the instability of the water column, which carries phytoplankton below the euphotic zone. Blooms of phytoplankton in Antarctic coastal water may occur because pycnoclines produced by melting sea ice impose a stability in the surface layer which keeps phytoplankton in the sunlight (El-Sayed 1978).

Krill occur in dense swarms up to a size of several hundred meters across and up to a density of 33 kilograms per cubic meter.

The spawning season for Euphausia superba occurs during the summer, particularly from January to March. Eggs sink to great depths but the larvae rise to the surface layer. Adults are mainly found in the top 200 meters of the ocean. Euphausia superba may have several breeding stocks or populations because concentrations of this species tend to occur in defined areas (Department of State 1978).

The biomass of the benthic fauna on the continental shelf of Antarctica and nearby islands is approximately 400 to 500 grams per square meter. Below 500 meters, the biomass is approximately 1.0 gram per square meter, a value comparable to that of other deep-sea bottoms.

Cephalopods. Squid are the predominant cephalopod in the Southern Ocean. They live in open waters and may be concentrated near the 

Antarctic Convergence. Squid are a major predator of the krill population, and are prey for fish, penguins, seals, and particularly whales. Squid are a potentially harvestable resource. An indirect estimate of the squid standing stock is 12.4 x 106 tons, and annual production is 19 x 106 tons (Green 1977). 

Fish. Fish stocks are believed to be less abundant and fewer in number of species relative to other oceans. In particular, they do not appear to form dense pelagic shoals which are conducive to commercial fishing, although some species are being harvested. Most species are believed to be residents of Antarctic waters; few species cross the Antarctic Convergence. Fish prey heavily on krill but also feed on other zooplankton, ice invertebrates, and squid. They serve as prey for seals, squid, birds, and whales. Both annual production and the standing crop are estimated to be 14 x 106 tons (Green 1977).

Mammals. Several whales migrate to the Southern Ocean in the austral summer and have a circumpolar distribution during this period. 

Baleen whales feed intensively on krill and other zooplankton during their approximate four-month summer stay and can increase 50% in weight during this time. These whales, which include the fin, blue, sei, humpback, and minke, feed mainly in open water but some species and individuals travel south to the edge of the pack ice. Southern right whales are occasionally found in Antarctic waters, but generally inhabit more temperate waters.

Historically, whales have been overharvested by man. Currently, the fin, blue, humpback, and southern right whales are protected from harvest because their populations were severely depleted. The fin whale has been reduced from approximately 400,000 to 90,000. The blue whale has been reduced from approximately 200,000 to 2,000 within the past two decades. The population of humpback whales has decreased from approximately 100,000 to 3,000 (Zumberge 1981). The sperm and sei whales are still harvested, although their stocks are approximately half the size of their initial populations. Minke whales are harvested but the population has not diminished. 

Six species of seals inhabit the pack ice region around the Antarctic continent. The crabeater, Weddell, leopard, and Ross are true 

Antarctic seals. The elephant and fur seals, known as Southern Ocean seals, suffered severe exploitation in the nineteenth century and now have specially protected status, as does the Ross seal. Brief, life history descriptions follow for each species. 

Crabeater Seal (Lobodon carcinophagus). These are the most abundant Antarctic seals with an estimaced populacion of 30 million, and are found in the largest numbers in small ice flow environments that cover between 30 and 70% of the water surface. They feed exclusively upon krill. They breed when the ice is at its maximum extent and bear their pups on the ice during September and October. Young seals tend to occur near the edge of the continent where the ice is dense, while adults tend to occur along the periphery of the pack ice. Their distribution is circumpolar. Maturity occurs within 3 to 6 years, and they have an average life span of 29 years. They are prey for killer whales and leopard seals.

Leopard Seal (Hydrurga leptonyx). The leopard seal has a popula tion of approximately 500,000, is the largest of the Antarctic seals, and is known for its predatory habits. This seal has a circumpolar distribution and frequently occurs around penguin colonies. While this seal is known for preying on penguins, it will also consume krill, fish, and other seals. Little is known about the life cycle, but pupping and breeding probably occur on the pack ice and the timing may be similar to that of other seals.

Weddell Seal (Leptonychotes weddelli). The Weddell seal has an estimated population of one million and it is the best known Antarctic species because of its proximity to existing land bases. These seals congregate along perennial tidal cracks, where pups are born on the ice from September through early November. Weaning and colony dispersal usually occur after six to seven weeks. They eat fish, crustaceans, arthropods, cephalopods, and euphausiids. The distribution of 

Weddell seals is circumpolar.

Southern Fur Seal (Arctocephalus tropicalis). The southern fur seal is found north and south of the Antarctic Convergence. South of this convergence, fur seals inhabit only islands of the Antarctic Peninsula and islands farther north. While stocks of this seal were severely exploited in the nineteenth century, the current population is protected and estimated to be 200,000 and growing. The life cycle of the fur seal is distinct from that of the true Antarctic seals. 

The fur seal comes ashore in late September to have pups and breed. 

It prefers rocky beaches and covės. After harem breakup in late

Avifauna. There are no truly terrestrial bird species south of 

60° south latitude. All 19 bird species found in this area breed on land, with the exception of the emperor penguin. Sustenance is derived mainly from marine food webs. Since niche utilization is extremely efficient, bird populations exist in high densities on the limited ice- and snow-free areas with minimal inter- and intraspecific competition. Breeding assemblages are exceptionally large and have great scientific and aesthetic value. 

The most abundant species breeding south of 60°, the Adelie penguin, inhabits the Antarctic coast but only colonizes areas where the sea ice opens early in spring and tends to disperse in summer (Table A-5). This species is more physically adapted to the extreme cold than other species present. The chinstrap penguin, whose numbers have been increasing since whaling activities ceased, breeds in a few large colonies on the northern, peninsula. Both species feed on krill. The gentoo penguin breeds in a few localities south of 60°S on the northern peninsula area. It feeds mainly on fish. The emperor penguin breeds on ice shelves or fast sea ice, at approximately 30 sites along the coast. Some colonies may have over 50,000 pairs of birds. They feed mainly on fish and squid. A.4.1 History and Historic Sites 

The concept of a southern continent intrigued even the ancient Greeks, who believed that such a continent was needed to balance the land masses of the Northern Hemisphere. The history of early voyages in search of Antarctica can be traced by the use of Table A-6. There are no indigenous inhabitants.

The 1800s brought many adventurers to the continent. Between the years of 1838 and 1843, three expeditions, led by d'Urville, Wilkes, and Ross, were made to Antarctica in order to find the south magnetic pole. A.4.2 Current Scientific and Logistic Operations 

The principal activities on the continent of Antarctica are scientific research and associated logistic operations. Presently, there are 35 year-round stations on the Antarctic continent, and all are dependent on outside support and supplies [Table A-7 and Figure A-9 

(Antarctica: Stations and Claims)]. The United States runs four of these stations.

McMurdo is the main United States base and also the largest multipurpose research and logistics center in Antarctica. The summer population may reach 600-700 people. The United States bases rely heavily on aircraft rather than on ships. However, when fuel or bulk cargo is transported it is shipped to McMurdo where ice breakers are used to open the channel, allowing cargo ships and tankers to off-load directly. McMurdo has one of the two airfields on the continent that are suitable for wheeled aircraft use. The other is at Marambio, an 

Argentina station.

The second largest station is Molodezhnaya, which is run by the Soviet Union and located on the northeastern coast of East Antarctica. 

The Soviets rely heavily on ships to transport materials to their coastal stations, however, the ships are often blocked from reaching the coast and other means of transportation must be used to shuttle supplies. The Soviet Union has a total of 6 year-round stations. 

Other nations having year-round stations are Argentina (8), United Kingdom (4), Chile (3), Australia (3) Japan (2), and South

A.4.4 Sovereignty 

Seven of the original 12 signatories to the 1959 Antarctic Treaty have claimed sovereignty over parts of Antarctica. These countries. are Argentina, Australia, Chile, France, New Zealand, Norway, and the United Kingdom. Some areas of Antarctica are claimed by more than one of these countries, while other areas remain unclaimed (Figure A-9, Antarctica: Stations and Claims). The balance of the original signatories--Belgium, Japan, South Africa, the Soviet Union, and the United States--have neither made claims nor recognized claims made by other countries. In the Antarctic Treaty, the disagreement over the issue of territorial sovereignty is dealt with in a manner that allows the Treaty Parties to cooperate in the conduct of the specific activities addressed by the treaty. 

A.4.5 Shipping and Tourism 

Shipping traffic is considerable in the Drake Passage by vessels too large to pass through the Panama Canal. Commercial shipping, which includes oil tankers, pass through the area, usually north of the Antarctic Convergence (Department of State 1978). 

Tourism in Antarctica is not extensive, but several cruise ships do visit the Antarctic Peninsula, notably the Palmer Station, during the austral summer. Cruise ships can accommodate approximately 

100 passengers and can make several stops annually. Ports of access are limited, thus tourist activities are concentrated in those areas that frequently have bird rookeries and seal colonies. The communities are sensitive to human disturbance. Contamination and disruption A-56

of fragile soils also occurs (National Science Foundation 1979). Some scientists complain that the tourists interfere with their research. 

A.5 GAPS IN KNOWLEDGE ON THE ANTARCTIC ENVIRONMENT 

Early geologic exploration of Antarctica began in the late nineteenth century with the collection of rock fragments from the sea bottom. Extensive programs were carried out by teams of scientists who accompanied voyages to Antarctica from 1890 to the 1920s. Progress was made in mapping and in understanding Antarctica's geology, glaciology, and geography. After World War I, advances in transportation and communications allowed even greater exploration (Gould 1978). 

The International Geophysical Year (IGY) conducted in 1957-58 was the beginning of Antarctica's role as an international science laboratory. Fifty stations were maintained by 12 countries during this period.

New data was obtained on meteorology, upper atmosphere physics, geomagnetism, seismology, glaciology, and geography (Central 

Intelligence Agency 1978).

Examples of recent large-scale projects are the Dry Valley Drilling Project, which was initiated in 1973 to unravel the tectonics, paleoclimate, and geological history of Antarctica; and the Scotia 

Arc-Antarctica Peninsula Tectonics Program, which has as its goal the understanding of relationships between Antarctica and South America. 

The record of biological investigations begins in the 1830s with observations made by James Eights and the Charles Wilkes expedition. 

Robert Cushman Murphy made important contributions concerning subantarctic birds and terrestrial flora and fauna in the early 1900s. 

Between 1920 and 1955 four expedition teams from the United States developed a sizeable data base on Antarctic terrestrial life, includ ing that of the interior. The Second Byrd Antarctic Expedition 

(1935-37) explored the inland mountains and nunataks and discovered extensive cryptogamic flora. Objectives of the United States Service 

Expedition of 1939-1941 were to investigate and survey the natural resources of Antarctica, including botany, zoology, oceanography, and physiological observations on expeditionary personnel. Biologists on two United States Navy projects, "Operation Highjump," 1946-1947, and "Operation Windmill," 1947-1948, made extensive observations on the Tr A-57

biota of the Antarctic pack ice and obtained information on whales and sea birds. In the late 1950s a permanent biological laboratory was established at McMurdo Station (Llano 1978). 

Currently, work is being done in all aspects of biology with guidance from the Polar Research Board and the Scientific Committee on 

Antarctic Research, specifically, the Working Group on Biology. The main focus of the United States Antarctic Program is to "obtain fundamental knowledge about Antarctic life forms and life systems," and to "increase the understanding of the environmental dynamics of the Antarctic relative to the rest of the world" (Llano 1978). 

Antarctica could be the focus of scientific inquiry for several future generations. Both Elliot (1977) and Zumberge (1979) have described the deficiencies in the existing information, and the data gaps addressed below are taken from these two sources. 

Physical Environment. A small percentage of the exposed rock in 

Antarctica has been mapped in detail and an even smaller percentage has been examined for economically significant minerals. "Thus, the data base for evaluation of mineral resources is meager" (Elliot 1977).

Information on glacial hydrology is sparse. The flow law that describes the movement of the ice is poorly understood. Therefore, the response of the ice sheet to changing conditions, e.g., climate, can be described only in general terms. 

Data on soil types and distributions, soil engineering characteristics, and permafrost conditions are scant, except for a few wellstudied areas. Landform and soil maps are needed. 

Ocean. The physical and chemical characteristics of the Southern 

Ocean are known in general terms, but data for the winter season when the sea ice is forming are almost entirely lacking, and there are few direct and long-term observations of oceanic currents. More quantitative data are needed on current speeds and transport. The physical oceanography of shallow coastal waters is almost totally unknown (Elliot 1977).

A-58

Sea ice dynamics are poorly understood on a seasonal and longer time scale. The response of sea ice to meteorological conditions, the motions of sea ice, and the areas and amount of pressure ridge development on the sea ice need investigation. Tracking sea ice and iceberg movements by satellites or other means should be extended. 

The geology of the continental shelves is poorly understood. 

Geological data is nonexistent for most areas; however, geophysical reconnaissance surveys have been carried out over the Ross Sea shelf. 

Climate. Improved weather forecasting in Antarctica can be achieved only by the placement of additional weather stations on land or additional satellites. Understanding the role the Antarctic has on global climate requires more data on the radiation budget at Antarctica, the heat exchange between the Southern Ocean and the atmosphere, and the heat exchange between the Antarctic region and areas to the north.

Biological Environment. The distribution and qualitative under standing of terrestrial flora and fauna and soils are fairly well documented, although no data exist for some areas. A synthesis of current information into floral and faunal maps is needed. The energetics, interrelationships, and limiting factors for terrestrial ecosystems are poorly understood.

Because of the immense size of the Southern Ocean, baseline data on marine biology is incomplete. Quantitative figures on primary production and on the annual production and biomass of the major species of the marine food web need to be improved. For example, the 

Southern Ocean was previously thought to be very productive, but recent evidence indicates that the primary production of the Southern 

Ocean may be similar to that of other oceans.

Data are needed on the reproductive habits of seals and krill. 

Excluding the Weddell seal, little is known about the pupping and breeding of seals. Because krill are the key component in the Antarctic food web, the biology of krill needs to be researched further. is important to know whether krill have special spawning grounds or whether species of krill have more than one breeding population. HISTORICAL IMPACTS 

To date, the major impacts on the terrestrial environment from the present occupation of Antarctica are associated with support facilities; fuel handling and storage; waste disposal; and construction, operation, and maintenance of stations and field camps. Those environmental features that have been affected include rocks, soils, lakes, ponds, terrestrial biota, and glacial snow and ice. The degree to which they have been affected is, in some cases, unknown but is generally only local in extent. 

The mere presence of humans has introduced into the environment microorganisms, bacteria, viruses, fungi, and seeds, thereby changing the natural populations. The construction and physical presence of structures have caused terrain modification and loss of soil and terrestrial biota. Petroleum and chemical spills have contaminated soils and inland lakes. One example is the Dry Valleys of Victoria Land. 

Soils have been contaminated by calcium chloride during scientific drilling expeditions, and the water from the lakes, which has been used for drilling fluids, has been chemically altered (Zumberge 1981). 

Sanitary and solid wastes have also contaminated ice, snow, and soils. 

Air emissions from vehicular traffic, power generation, and burning of wastes have affected some of the terrestrial flora, notably lichens. Lichens have disappeared in the McMurdo Station area and may be threatened elsewhere.

Current scientific activities and tourism in Antarctica have little impact on the marine environment. Those impacts that have been noted are only local in extent. A small number of birds and seals are lost to scientific collecting, and activities near rookeries, such as helicopter overflights and foot expeditions, may cause minor disturbances. Consequently, some areas of special value that were affected by human activity have been designated Specially Protected Areas. 

Impact on the marine environment has been limited except for harvesting of mammals. The disposal of garbage, sanitary wastes, and A-60

solid wastes in coastal waters degrades water quality on a local scale. Vessel traffic and the handling of petroleum products have resulted in small oil spills or oil discharges which may harm local animal populations (National Science Foundation 1979). Near Palmer 

Station, frequent oil slicks were noticed in Arthur Harbor, and nearby

Adelie penguins were observed on several occasions to emerge from the water with oil on their bodies (National Science Foundation 1979). At least 20 Adelie penguins soiled with heavy petroleum were noticed at Cape Bird in 1979; discharge of oily bilge water at sea may have been the source (Wilson 1980). No large oil spills from tanker accidents. have occurred in the Southern Ocean and no evidence exists that indicates a general contamination of the ocean by petroleum. 

From 1974 to 1978, scientists in the Soviet Polar Experiment Southern Group Program analyzed the petroleum content in seawater samples from 161 points in the Southern Ocean located in the Drake 

Straits, the Scotia Sea, between Africa and Antarctica along 20°E longitude, and between Australia and Antarctica along 132°E longitude.

Most of the seawater samples did not contain petroleum at concentrations greater than 0.03 milligram per liter, the detection limit. 

Some samples did contain petroleum at levels between 0.3 and 0.9 milligram per liter. However, many of these samples were from the frontal zone in the Scotia Sea, an area of high biological activity. 

Thus, the high petroleum values may be due in part to hydrocarbons of biogenic origin; the test did not distinguish between petroleum hydrocarbons and biogenic hydrocarbons. At no time during this study period were oil films or patches observed from one ship. This study concludes that the Southern Ocean is virtually free of petroleum contamination (Department of State 1979). 

Commercial harvesting of whales and seals has had significant impacts in the past. Twentieth century whaling greatly reduced the populations of baleen whales, as previously discussed. The International Whaling Commission now regulates whaling. The fin, blue, humpback, and southern right whales are protected from harvesting, and harvest quotas have been set for other species. The reduction of the number of whales in this century from approximately 975,000 to approximately 338,000 means that less krill are consumed by whales (Zumberge A-61

Whereas baleen whales used to consume an estimated 180 million tons of krill, they now consume an estimated 33 million tons of krill, increasing the availability of krill for other predators (Zumberge 1981). Subsequently, the population of crabeater seals increased, and the populations of other predators may also have increased. Thus whaling may have altered the populations of many species besides the whales themselves.

Fur seals and elephant seals were captured for their fur and blubber, respectively, during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, and the number of fur seals was severely reduced. Both of these seals are now protected by the Convention for Conservation of Antarctic 

Seals (Zumberge 1981). 

A whaling station was established in King Edward Cove, South Georgia, to support the past whaling activities. Activities in this harbor grossly polluted the water with organic effluents and fuel oil, impacting the local marine communities. However, contamination has stopped, and evidence indicates that the benthic fauna has recovered (Platt 1978).

The Antarctic environment has been contaminated with organochlorides as a result of human activities outside of Antarctica. 

Impacts of these contaminants are not known. DDT and other chlorinated hydrocarbons have been found in Antarctic birds. Risebrough and 

Carmignani (1972) summarized various studies on organochlorides found in Antarctic birds. Hoenschelmann (1979) suggests the value of these birds as monitors of global pollution. Donnewald and others (1978) were the first to measure endrin, hexachlorobenzene, and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) in Antarctic fauna. DDT, PCBs, and other organochlorides have been found in krill as well, although at concen-trations that do not pose a human health hazard (Informationen fur die

The Marine Mammal Commission, in consultation with its Committee of Scientific Advisors on Marine Mammals, has reviewed and offers the following comments and suggestions concerning the "Draft Environmental Impact Statement on the Negotiation of an International Regime for Antarctic Mineral Resources' 

P. V: The first sentence in the second paragraph reads: "The primary purpose of the regime would be to establish an agreed system for determining the acceptability of potential mineral activities in Antarctica and for governing any such activities that were determined to be acceptable.' The Commission concurs, in general, with the stated 'purpose but believes that the criteria or guidelines against which determinations of "acceptability" are to be made should be incorporated into the regime and that the DEIS should address these criteria or guidelines so as to allow us to better judge the acceptability of the proposed action. We suggest, therefore, that the FEIS be expanded to include a description and discussion of criteria and guidelines that would be used to judge the acceptability of potential mineral activities in Antarctica. 

1. The comments of the Marine Mammal Commission address the issue of criteria in the regime for determining the acceptability of mineral resource activities. The development of the criteria which will be used to determine the acceptability of mineral resource activities is a process which will have to proceed in parallel with the negotiation of the regime itself. The regime should include standards for determining the environmental acceptability of possible mineral activities and for determining the sufficienc of information upon which to make decisions of acceptability.

NOAA Comments on the Draft Environmental Impact Statement on the Negotiation of an International Regime for Antarctic Mineral Resources July 1981 General Comments: 

NOAA concurs that the State Department proposed action is needed to establish an international regime which will both foster environmental protection and manage potential mineral resource development. This is in response to your request for comments on the draft environmental impact statement (EIS) for the Negotiation of an International Regime for Antarctic 

Mineral Resources. Treasury believes that any regime for 

Antarctic mineral resources should encourage the development of knowledge regarding, and ensure reasonable conditions of investment in, such resources. The option identified by the draft EIS as the proposed federal action approach to protecting the environment raises doubt as to whether these objectives would be achieved. 

There clearly are both environmental interests and energy interests in the Antarctic. Treasury agrees that the consultative parties should establish criteria and guidelines to safeguard the environment when mineral exploration or exploitation activities occur. However, Treasury has not at any time agreed to federal action as interpreted by the EIS. Specifically, the EIS implies the establishment of a decision-making body to approve individual projects, with approval of the environmental aspects being contingent on the explorer or developer securing the data to demonstrate environmental acceptability of the proposed activity. It is also implied that this body would determine the acceptability of activities even in the absence of environmental data although it is not clear what rules it would enforce. It is, therefore, the responsibility of the project party to bear the burden of overcoming data deficiencies and provide the information gained to the decision body. This includes the sharing of information confirming that mineral deposits will yield sufficient benefit to override environmental concerns. 

On these matters, it is Treasury's position that: (1) Safeguarding of the environment should be achieved by agreed-upon criteria established by the consultative parties--not on a case by case review by a board which acts on petitions to undertake exploration, development, or production projects. The term "regime" as used in the EIS suggests a rulemaking body with screening responsibility for projects. 

Treasury believes any "regime" should be limited to a set of rules.

1. The first position expressed by the Department of Treasury relates to rules for the protection of the environment. A basic objective of the proposed regime is to provide necessary protection of the Antarctic environment. As noted in response to other comments (see Marine Mammal Commission response number one), the regime will need to incorporate criteria for making determinations of the acceptability of possible mineral resource activities. The regime, included in the proposed federal action, also would need to incorporate the means by which those criteria are applied to specific proposed activities and the means by which permitted activities are monitored. The nature of the means is a subject for the negotiations. D-28
