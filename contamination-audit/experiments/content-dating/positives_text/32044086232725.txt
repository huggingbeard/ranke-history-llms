ACTIVITIES OF THE HOUSE COMMITTEE ON ARMED SERVICES 

The House Committee on Armed Services had its first meeting in the 83d Congress on January 6, 1953. In view of the fact that President Eisenhower had not yet been inaugurated, the committee was unaware of the attitude of the new administration on legislative proposals. Therefore during the period January 6-January 27 the committee conducted a series of inquiries and briefings on matters within the committee's jurisdiction.

On February 4, 1953, the full committee held its first legislative meeting. From that date through August 20, 1954, there were 97 meetings of the full committee, 146 meetings of subcommittees and 104 meetings of the investigative subcommittee, the Defense Activities Subcommittee, for a total of 347 meetings on the legislative and investigative activities of the committee. More than 140 bills and resolutions were considered during the 83d Congress, resulting in 97 public laws and 6 private laws. 

Some of the major legislative accomplishments of the committee were the amendment and extension of the Doctor Draft Act (Public Law 84); provision for the disposal of the Government-owned synthetic rubber plants (Public Lav 205); the establishment of a survivor benefits plan for retired personnel of the uniformed services (Public 

Law 239); the granting of authority to the President to lend, or otherwise make available, naval vessels to France, Italy, Turkey, and friendly far eastern nations (Public Law 188 and Public Law 214); the granting of authority to the Secretaries of the military departments to acquire or construct industrial-type facilitics and acquire and stockpile machine tools (Public Law 130); the granting of authority to the military departments to proceed with construction items totaling approximately $490 million (Public Law 209); provision for a statutory system of promotion for officers of the Reserve components of the Armed Forces (Public Law 773); the establishment of the Air Force Academy (Public Law 325); a substantial increase in the reenlistment bonus for enlisted personnel of all of the armed services (Public Law 506); a military housing bill to provide $175 million in family dwellings for married service personnel (Public Law 765); an additional authorization to the military departments

PUBLIC LAW 16-S. 1229 

To continue the effectiveness of the Missing Persons Act, as amended and extended, until July 1, 1954

This act continues the effectiveness of the Missing Persons Act, as amended and extended, until July 1, 1954. The statute would otherwise have expired on April 1, 1953. The Missing Persons Act provides that the heads of executive departments may continue to credit the pay accounts, and make, continue, or modify allotments to dependents of service personnel and civilians who are in a missing status. If allotments are not in effect when the serviceman is placed in a missing category, the head of the department concerned is authorized to make allotments to care for dependents. This statute was enacted to continue the Missing Persons Act in effect until July 1, 1954, so that a study could be conducted within the Department of Defense after which permanent legislation could be proposed to the Congress. It is estimated that the cost of the act for all military' services would approximate $20 million annually.

PUBLIC LAW 84-H. R. 4495 

To amend the Universal Military Training and Service Act, as amended, so as to provide for special registration, classification, and induction of certain medical, dental, and allied specialist categories, and for other purposes This law extended until July 1, 1955, the authority to induct physicians and dentists. Perhaps of greater significance was the fact that this law made several changes in the basic Doctors Draft Act and removed the discriminatory features of certain parts of the original law. It credited services performed prior to being accepted for medical training during World War II and required the armed services to release doctors within 90 days who would not have been eligible for compulsory service had this amendment been in effect when the original law was passed. Qualification for being placed in priority 

IV was reduced from 21 to 18 months. And in addition, a sliding. scale basis for compulsory service was placed in the law for those with prior service. There were many other features in the law which extended the Doctors Draft Act, but these are probably the most important.

PUBLIC LAW 205-H. R. 5728 

To authorize the disposal of the Government-owned rubber-producing facilities, and for other purposes

This act, known as the Rubber Producing Facilities Disposal Act, was probably one of the most important legislative items considered by the Armed Services Committee during the 83d Congress. Under this legislation, the machinery was established for the acceptance of bids by the Rubber Producing Facilities Disposal Commission for the eventual sale of the Government-owned rubber producing facilities to private industry. The law is surrounded with safeguards and before any sales become final, a report must be submitted to the Congress and either House may disapprove, individually or collectively, any of the recommended disposals. There are ample provisions in the law dealing with protection against monopoly, safeguards with respect to national security clauses and assurances that operators will be competent to produce an item so necessary for our national security. 

The 84th Congress will take the final action on any sales recommended by the Commission.

PUBLIC LAW 239-H. R. 5304 

To permit members of the uniformed services to elect certain contingency options, and for other purposes

There have been no provisions of law which would permit members of the uniformed services who achieve a retired status to receive a reduced amount of their retired pay during their lifetime in order that they might leave some annuity to their immediate survivors. This law will permit, on a wholly voluntary basis, retired officers and enlisted persons to receive a reduced retirement pay in order that they might leave one-eighth, one-fourth, or not to exceed one-half of their reduced retired pay as an annuity to their surviving widow or children. 

Inasmuch as the plan is to be based on sound actuarial tables and requires no contribution on the part of the Government, it is selfliquidating. The enactment of this legislation into law is the culmination of 7 years effort on this subject by the House Committee on 

Armed Services.

PUBLIC LAW 349-H. R. 7103 

To establish limitations on the numbers of officers who may serve in various commissioned grades in the Army, Navy, Air Force, and Marine Corps, and for other purposes

This extremely important law establishes limitations on the number of officers who may serve in various commissioned grades in the Army, Navy, Air Force, or Marine Corps. The limitations are based on a sliding-scale basis and are imposed upon the grade of major or equivalent, and above. The enactment of this legislation should preclude any further necessity for imposing such limitations in appropriation acts. The law is based on the theory that as the number of officers incrcase, the percentage of senior officers decreases. It permits, however, an orderly flow of promotions. Likewise, the law repealed the statutory prohibition against voluntary retirement and restored to the Secretary of each military department the right to grant or withhold the privilege of retiring upon the completion of 20 or more years of service.

PUBLIC LAW 379-H. R. 6374 

To revise certain laws relating to warrant officers of the Army, Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard, and for other purposes H. R. 6374 was one of the major legislative items of the committee during the 83d Congress. This bill became Public Law 379 and is cited as the "Warrant Officer Act of 1954.” This law revises the laws relating to warrant officers of all the Armed Forces and establishes a

uniform promotion system for Regular warrant officers, a uniform elimination system and a uniform retirement system for warrant officers of the Armed Forces. For the first time all warrant officers will be eligible for consideration for promotion after stated periods of service and if selected will be promoted to the higher warrant grades.

REPORT OF SPECIAL INVESTIGATIONS CONDUCTED FIRST AND SECOND SESSIONS, 83D CONGRESS 

House Resolution 125, passed February 24, 1953, authorized studies and investigations by the Armed Services Committee on all matters within its jurisdiction. House Resolution 156, passed March 5, 1953, appropriated moneys therefor. On March 5, 1953, the following

subcommittee was appointed: Mr. Hess, of Ohio, chairman; Mr. Cunningham, of Iowa; Mr. Bates, of Massachusetts; Mr. Nelson, of 

Maine; Mr. Hébert, of Louisiana; Mr. Fisher, of Texas; and Mr.

Hardy, of Virginia. A staff was assembled in March and the subcommittee held its first hearing March 16, 1953. 

The subcommittee was increased during the second session by the addition of Mr. Gavin of Pennsylvania to the majority side and Mr. 

Rivers of South Carolina to the minority side. 

During the first session, there were 33 days of hearings at which 6,657 folios of testimony were taken. During the second session, there were 40 days of hearings at which 5,042 folios of testimony were taken.

Thus, during the period of slightly over 16 months of the 2 sessions of Congress, the subcommittee was in full session 73 days and took a total of 11,699 folios of testimony, a large part of which has been published.

To this must be added a 31-day inspection trip made by the subcommittee to defense installations in the European countries. Exclusive, therefore, of executive business sessions of which no records were made, the subcommittee was in full session 104 days. The prime targets have been waste in the military establishments, irregularities in contracting, influence peddling, and inefficiency in administration. 

The subcommittee had under consideration and reported on 137 specific complaints made by Members of Congress during the first session and 42 such cases reported in the second session or a total of 179 specific cases. The subject matter of these inquiries was not such as to require printed publication but these investigations were made as a matter of service to Members and were handled directly with the Member with the full understanding of the subcommittee itself. The following are some of the subjects having a public interest upon which the subcommittee either has reported upon or which it will report upon.

I. DEFENSE CATALOGING AND STANDARDIZATION ACT, PUBLIC LAW 436, 82D CONGRESS 

The subcommittee held 4 days of public hearings in March on the operation under the Single Catalog and Standardization Act. The hearings have been published and the subcommittee has filed a report concerning the program and progress made under this law. 

The subcommittee's report was critical of the interdepartmental controversies over the implementation of the law and it expressed its concern with the rate of progress. The subcommittee was alerted to see that the single catalog is developed in accordance with the longtime intent of Congress.

II. RESTRICTIVE SPECIFICATIONS AND BIDDING 

The subcommittee made an investigation of the expenditure of money by the Army Ordnance Corps for tire testing and filed its report May 29, 1953. The report condemned the expenditure of nearly one-half million dollars annually by the Army Ordnance 

Corps for a process that duplicates testing of tires bought from commercial producers. The Defense Appropriation Act forbids the

use of any moneys, by the services, for such expenditures by specific amendment.

III. ARMED SERVICES EXCHANGES 

Following numerous complaints and the sworn charges of the American Retail Federation, a complete review of the operation of the exchanges was made. Present operations were compared with the recommendations of the Philbin committee of 1949 which have been approved by the Armed Services Committee. Some of the criticism was answered. It was the opinion of the subcommittee that there were adequate regulations and laws governing the use and operation of exchanges; that the failures were in enforcement of these regulations. There were other findings and recommendations, but in the main it was found that the exchanges were a necessary morale factor; that the beneficiaries were the sons and daughters of the American taxpayers, at their military posts, who required exchanges to make purchases of their small merchandising requirements.

IV. ARMY ORDNANCE (CHICAGO AND NEW YORK) 

A thorough investigation was made of procurement practices for large-caliber ammunition in the Chicago Ordnance District. Over 

2,000 pages of testimony as well as written staff reports are under advisement. This study has been completed and is awaiting the concurrence of the membership..

The subcommittee held 5 days of hearings in New York and interviewed over 100 witnesses concerning charges made of irregularities in that district. The subcommittee was split into four separate hearing groups. Some "leads" developed in these hearings and are now being further investigated.

V. INFLUENCE PEDDLING AND "LEAKING" OF INFORMATION 

The subcommittee held 9 days of hearings on allegations of attempted influence peddling and "leakage" of information concerning procurement of Government contracts. 

VI. STANDARDIZATION 

The subcommittee held 2 days hearings on proposed standardization, by the Army, of 210 c. f. m. compressors to the exclusion of competition under an exception contained in the Armed Services Procurement Act for technical equipment and also under the emergency provisions of that act.

VII. STANDARDIZATION OF DETECTING DEVICES FOR TESTING OF PACKAGED GOODS 

A vast loss occurs by reason of the deterioration of packed_and stored goods. Seven days hearings (and a staff study) have been completed on waste occurring in storage of material not adequately packaged and inadequately tested; and the whole subject of preservation by packaging of defense materiel.

The subcommittee took 401 folios of testimony in December of 1953 and January 1954 on the subject of packaging. An intensive study

was made by the subcommittee into the waste and losses allegedly resulting from faulty packaging and the means of protecting against the same all in furtherance of section 4 (b) of the Defense Cataloging and Standardization Act, Public Law 436, 82d Congress. That report, dated March 12, 1954, has been published as a part of a series of standardization studies. The findings of the subcommittee have been widely circulated in industry and are in frequent demand as a source of information. The report is a composite of experiences of the various military departments and suppliers. 

VIII. STANDARDIZATION OF PREFABRICATED HOUSES 

The subcommittee held 4 days hearings on the proposal to standardize steel prefabricated buildings. Both industry and the services were heard as well as the Standardization Agency. 

IX. DEFECTIVE MACHINE PARTS-INSPECTION 

The subcommittee has paid close attention to the inspection services of the military. It has pointed up failures in this regard. It intercepted a shipment and caused an inspection to be made on the spot, showing that a tremendous volume of defective material (which would have made the tanks wholly inoperative) had gotten into the supply line through failures of inspection. The subcommittee has a continuing interest in failures of this character. 

X. CLOTHING RACKET 

The subcommittee conducted 2 days hearings on alleged irresponsible bidding in the New York Quartermaster Procurement Distract. This report resulted in the Army being able to cancel out irresponsible bidders. ང་ 

XI. MILITARY ATTACHÉ SYSTEM 

The subcommittee took under consideration the deployment of military personnel in the attaché system. The testimony is of a restricted nature.

New York, and Mr. Kilday, of Texas, was appointed to study and make further investigations on the disappearance and death of Maj.

XIII. EUROPEAN INSPECTION TRIP 

None of the testimony in Europe was recorded, but staff notes were taken. A great many of the subjects discussed were of a highly classified nature. A secret report is, therefore, lodged in the files of the

subcommittee. However, the subcommittee did publish a report on subjects of public interest. The demand for this document has exceeded that of any other document published by the subcommittee. 

It is now in its fourth printing. 

XIV. SUBCOMMITTEE RULES AND PROCEDURE 

The subcommittee, in February, formalized, adopted, and promulgated rules for the conduct of its business. It published compendium of all pertinent information on the organization, sources of the authority of the subcommittee, the pertinent rules of the House and of the Constitution concerning the conduct of its business, together with rules of procedure. This document contains 10 subcommittee rules (which are in addition to the Rules of the House of Representatives and the rules of the Committee on Armed Services). These rules were specifically made as a guide to all persons having business before the subcommittee. They have been favorably commented upon in the public press, in law journals, and other professional publications as being a needed safeguard for the conduct of investigative proceedings of the House, considering the legislative purpose served, and the rights of persons who testify.

XV. CHARGE OF NEGLIGENCE AGAINST THE NAVY DEPARTMENT 

The subcommittee conducted an investigation of an alleged charge of negligence, made by the Federal Maritime Administrator, against the Navy Department. Those conclusions, published with testimony, were promptly circulated for the protection of the good name of all concerned.

XVI. UNFORTUNATE DEATH OF TWO OFFICER CANDIDATES 

The subcommittee conducted an investigation into the deaths of two officer candidates who died while in training at the Marine Corps station at Quantico, Va. The investigation resulted in a change in the training regulations and procedures of the Marine Corps to prevent recurrence of any such unfortunate incident. The inquiry was conducted with the complete cooperation of the Marine Corps and of kin of the deceased. The recommendations and conclusions of the subcommittee have been fully implemented and adopted by the interested service and have furnished a valuable guide for training programs.

XVII. AIRSTRIP PAVING MATERIALS 

In February, the subcommittee conducted a public hearing on the respective merits of asphalt as opposed to concrete, as an airstrip paving material. Industry and Government officials were all invited to appear. Their views have been recorded in a public document, which has been widely circulated. The subcommittee's conclusions have been the subject of much favorable comment in the public press. It is believed that this undertaking was a valuable contribution to an understanding of defense costs for the information of the Congress as well as contractors and builders. The savings resulting from the subcommittee's conclusions, based on the evidence before it, and implemented by departmental directives, will exceed $50 million before the conclusion of the fiscal year.

XIII. BIDDING IRREGULARITIES 

The subcommittee conducted open hearings on irregularities in bidding by the Department of the Army, Corps of Engineers, who were in charge of construction of ordnance facilities at Corozal, C. Z. 

This has resulted in a series of recommendations having to do with revising of bidding provisions with regard to so-called late bids. 

Specific recommendations were made for reforming these regulations where fraud is either suggested or indicated, to conform more nearly to judicial processes and to protect the innocent as well as punish the guilty.

XIX. LETTING OF NAVY SHIPBUILDING CONTRACTS 

The subcommittee conducted an inquiry into the letting of Navy Department contracts, on a negotiated basis, for the construction of three destroyers. The information contained with reference to the status of the shipbuilding industry has been widely circulated and is a valuable source for interested concerns. 

XX. RESTRICTIVE SPECIFICATIONS 

The subcommittee is currently investigating alleged discrimination and restrictive specifications in refrigeration units. This is a study in which the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice has manifested a continuing interest because of possible violation of antitrust laws. The contractor has claimed prejudice because of a loss of the source of components required to complete the contract. Other facets of the inquiry have to do more specifically with restrictive specifications.

XXI. MISUSE OF AIR FORCE AMBULANCE 

The subcommittee has issued a report on the misuse and damage to an Air Force ambulance, which was the personal responsibility of an individual airman. This has resulted in corrective administrative action.

XXII. TRANSPORTATION COSTS 

The subcommittee made an intensive investigation of discrimination, resulting in the disqualification of a bidder for the transporting of office furniture and equipment by military departments. A misinterpretation of law and regulations has been reversed as a result of subcommittee action and the savings to the Government is in many thousands of dollars.

XXIII. DISCRIMINATION IN FAVOR OF PROFESSIONAL ATHLETES 

This was a highly controversial and volatile subject; but an investigation was made and a report published which has found most of the charges to be substantiated and all of the complaints subject to correction by administrative action of the Department of the Army (which was the principal source of the complaints). The Department of the Army instituted a monitoring system which it is believed will result in considerable savings in the future, as a result of the subcommittee's findings.

SUMMARY 

Recapitulating, the subcommittee cites the evaluation of its activities made by a newspaper syndicate from a study of its own. The article is as follows: 

(By Robert Crater) 

WASHINGTON, June 26.-A House subcommittee tracking down waste in military spending says it has saved taxpayers $200 million since March 1953. "We have figures to prove that much,' says the chairman, Congressman 

William E. Hess (Republican, Ohio). “Actually, what we've done will save many more millions." 

The Hess Subcommittee on Defense Activities performs like an ambitious private eye. After Congress votes money for the military, the committee checks up to see how it's being spent. 

For instance The committee convinced the Air Force that asphalt paving was as practical as concrete for some runways. The savings amounted to $50 million. 

Committee investigators delved into the case of a subcontractor who had too many workers building plane fuselages. A few months after the checkup the builder cut his force from 3,900 to 1,900 persons. It was a cost-plus contract and the savings for taxpayers was $5 million. 

Chanute Airbase, Ill., wanted 16 concrete, soundproof testing chambers for jet engines. Elaborate specifications showed each would cost upward $400,000.

The Hess subcommittee argued this was too high and recommended steel chambers at half the price. Alarmed, the concrete people cut their price to match. 

Net savings: $3,200,000. "The Air Force needs 68 more chambers," Hess said. "If the savings is carried out in the building of the remaining units, there will be an additional bonus for taxpayers of about $14 million."

Hearings haven't always been necessary to cut down costs in military spending, Hess points out. "The mere fact that our committee exists helps keep the military on its toes. 

If we didn't do a lick of work we'd save the people millions of dollars.' 

Savings amounting to a “mere" half million have been made by the committee in many instances. These include the use of "pop-up" toasters in kitchens instead of the more costly "rotary" type the military liked.

In another instance, the Hess committee made a manufacturer take back and rework defective tank parts, saving taxpayers $211,000. 

Seven employees, under the direction of a special counsel, conducted the staff work. The total expenditures for reporting, travel, stationery, salaries, telephone, etc., amounted to $91,461.17.

The subcommittee published 26 reports which have been circulated for the public information.
