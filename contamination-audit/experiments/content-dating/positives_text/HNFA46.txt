ITALY 

Italy's post-war record is notable for economic progress, defense buildup, and cooperation in the movement toward European integration. 

Modern military equipment is flowing from Italian factories. The 

Italian armed forces are being steadily strengthened as part of the NATO 

(North Atlantic Treaty Organization) defenses for Western Europe. Italy has played a progressive and cooperative role in the formation of the European Coal and Steel Community (Schuman Plan) and in the attempts to form the European Defense Community. 

A long, narrow peninsula with the shape of a riding boot, Italy stretches deep into the central Mediterranean Sea. Its northern frontiers adjoin France, Switzerland and Austria, while Yugoslavia is to the East. 

About three-fourths the size of California, it has 47 million inhabitants compared to California's 11 million.

Its strategic geographic position has been recognized through the centuries. More recently recognized in post-war Europe is the sturdy contribution Italy can make to the defensive and economic unity of free Europe.

THE AFTERMATH OF WAR 

War's end found Italy's economy disrupted, inflation rampant, and unemployment widespread. Almost 6 million Italians were homeless or living in overcrowded, unsanitary quarters. Sixty percent of the state highways were unusuable, one-fourth of the railway trackage and one-third of the bridges were destroyed, agricultural production was 40 percent below prewar levels, industrial output less than half that of 1938. More than half of the power plants were destroyed or damaged, the merchant marine had dropped to 10 percent of prewar tonnage, and foreign trade had come to a standstill. The average per capita daily diet had dropped to 1,700 calories from a prewar level of 2,723 in 1938.

Material hardships were matched by moral unrest. Defeat coupled with bitter civil strife between the Nazi-dominated regime in the North and the pro-ally government in the South had precipitated a deep moral crisis. An unprecedented crime wave followed in the wake of war. 

Government services were disrupted. Black marketeers were amassing fortunes. 54X12

Economic chaos bred political disorder. Entrenched in key posts of the Government and dominating the powerful Confederation of Labor, the Communists were ruthlessly exploiting the hunger and despair of the people. Chaos was their ally, and their weapons were political strikes, intimidation and violence.

Italy was on the verge of collapse. Yet she did not collapse. The majority of the Italian people set to work with stubborn determination: in a few postwar years, plants were rebuilt, railroads repaired, production restored, inflation checked. These accomplishments of the Italian people and their Government were powerfully supported by American help. 

THE MARSHALL PLAN AND MUTUAL SECURITY PROGRAM 

Since April 1948, when the Marshall Plan began, Italy, including Italian Trieste, has been allotted $1.5 billion worth of economic and defense support assistance from the United States. Only the United 

Kingdom and France have received more. The Italian aid program was tailored to the basic characteristics and needs of the country's economy. 

Italy's population density is about eight times that of the United States. The country's total national income ($17.4 billion in 1953) is less than that of California. Over 40 percent of the civilian labor force is engaged in farming; yet agriculture contributed less than 23 percent of gross national product in 1953. Italy lacks an adequate supply of many important natural resources for modern industry, such as coal, iron and natural fibers. Unemployment and lack of full use of industrial resources are major social-economic problems. 

In this general setting the Marshall Plan went to work. Dollars were provided by the United States to finance Italy's import of essential commodities. These imports were purchased by Italian business with local currency at market prices. The lire paid for the goods are called "counterpart funds," and are used to pay for local costs of labor and materials to carry out reconstruction and rehabilitation and defense projects. 

Because of Italy's natural resources shortage, almost one half of all dollar aid under the Marshall Plan went for the Import of three commodities-petroleum and products, coal and related fuels, and cotton. 

The Marshall Plan and its successor, the Mutual Security program, have supplied dollars to finance the importation of $261 million worth of machinery and equipment, bought from U.S. manufacturers, for the development and expansion of Italian industry.

Italian lire, deposited by the Italian Government to match U. S. dollar aid, financed much of the internal reconstruction of Italy after World War II. Counterpart funds financed $190 million worth of low-cost housing, such as the project pictured above. Lack of housing, in Italy as in other countries, has been a major cause of popular discontent and political unrest,

Of this total, $65 millions were allocated for equipment for 11 thermoelectric power plants which were badly needed to reduce the country's almost complete dependence on hydro-electric power. An additional $1,175,000 in U.S. equipment went to the Larderello plant, a major electric producer using natural volcanic steam to generate power. The devel-

opment of a modern iron and steel industry, sufficiently competitive to enable Italy to participate in the common market of the European Coal and Steel Community, has been materially aided by allocations for a dozen plants totalling $60 million. 

Another $61 million worth of machinery and equipment went to a large number of firms in the mechanical and electro-mechanical industries, Italy's industrial production index, based on a level of 100 for 1938, climbed from 99 in 1948 to 156 in 1953 and real industrial wages had increased 33% above prewar. U.S.-financed imports helped rehabilitate the war-torn industries, mines, public utilities and homes. COUNTERPART FUNDS 

Counterpart funds of Italian currency, matching the ECA, MSA and FOA dollar aid, have been used to promote industrial and agricultural production, to spur the output of metals, to build or modernize transportation, communications and utilities, to construct new housing and to provide loans to commerce and industry. 

Projects of land reclamation, irrigation and more intensive cultivation already have boosted farm production by billions of lire, created many additional jobs in agriculture, and contributed to the increase which has been achieved in the average diet. 

Other counterpart lire have been set aside for special programs of loans to small and medium size industrial and agricultural enterprises. 

Larger loans have increased production of sulphur for explosives and chemical processes. Almost 6 billion lire (equal to $9.6 million) have gone into new hydro-electric plants and transmission lines. Under MSA's support for the exploration and development of strategic materials deposits, counterpart has financed a modern electrolytic plant at Nossa for processing zinc ores with equipment costing almost 12 billion lire ($2.4 million).

The counterpart program has made a particularly significant contribution to improving living standards for the economically depressed groups in the field of low cost housing. Lack of housing, in Italy as in other countries, has been a major cause of popular discontent and political unrest. Of the some 6,000,000 rooms built (or reconstructed) in the post-war period, almost 40 percent were partly or wholly financed by the use of 119 billion lire ($190 million) of counterpart funds. However, there still remains a backlog deficit of some 3 million rooms to overcome before a minimum standard of 1½ persons per room can be reached. The 1951 census revealed over 200,000 families living in caves, cellars, garrets, lofts and stores. In Southern Italy, there are areas which average 2½ to 

3½ persons per room, often in unhealthy and unsafe dwellings. In Italy as a whole, 4:7 percent of the families are living more than four persons to a room.

CURRENT FOA PROGRAMS 

Revitalization of Italy's underdeveloped southern and insular areas has been recognized as one of the important requisites to a sound na-

tional economy. Plagued by inadequate agricultural and industrial production, chronic unemployment and underemployment, the 17 million people in the Italian areas south of Rome, in Sicily and in Sardinia have suffered substantially lower living standards than the other parts of Italy. They comprise more than one third of Italy's total population. 

To try to heal this sore spot in the economy of Italy affecting the security and prosperity of all of Italy and even of Western Europe, the "Cassa per il Mezzogiorno" ("Fund for the South") was established in 1950. A public agency, the Cassa is carrying out a bold 12-year program of direct action in the fields of land reclamation, irrigation and conservation, for which more than half of the total budget was earmarked, and for development of roads, railways, aqueducts and sewers. 

The program entails an annual budget equivalent to $175 million, and a significant part of the funds for financing the Cassa projects comes from counterpart funds generated from past U.S. aid programs. 

In June, 1954, an allotment of $20 million in defense support funds for Italy was made by FOA for a double purpose. With the dollars provided, Italy's industries could buy raw materials in the United States or other dollar areas, while the lira equivalent of these dollars-the counterpart funds paid by the Italian industries for the raw materialsare going into a revolving industrial loan fund for the depressed southern and insular areas.

Complementing loan operations already started under Italian Government sponsorship in addition to the basic Cassa program, the new U.S.generated loan fund will be used to stimulate the lagging industries of Southern Italy, Sicily and Sardinia. The emphasis will be on loans to small and medium sized enterprises, particularly in industrial fields new to these areas, in order to help broaden and diversify the area's industrial base. It is expected that the program will also result in attracting private risk capital from local, northern Italian or foreign sources.

During the past fiscal year, Italy also was allotted $18.5 million worth of surplus U.S. agricultural commodities-$15 million worth of raw cotton, the rest in tallow and tobacco. As required by the mutual security legislation, Italy paid the United States the equivalent of $18.5 million for these commodities in lira currency. These lira funds are being used by the U.S. to purchase military equipment in Italy, except for $300,000 equivalent which is going into technical cooperation projects in Somalia, Italian-administered trust territory in Africa.

Counterpart funds helped rebuild Italy's blasted railways, bridges and roads, public buildings and other public facilities such as this dam, completed in 1952. Its water reservoir provides irrigation for 6,000 acres of land growing forage and vegetables. 

The need to improve the competitive status of Italy's export products and to broaden internal markets as well has been the basis for a productivity and technical exchange program in Italy. Under this program, more

than Italian businessmen, labor leaders, technical experts and workers in industry and agriculture have come to the United States to study methods, techniques and attitudes which have given American free enterprise its pre-eminent position with respect to efficiency and productivity. Several score of U.S. experts have gone to Italy as part of this same program to give technical advice to Italian industries and agriculture. Seminars on productivity and demonstration projects in 

Italian plants have also helped to spread the "gospel" of greater productivity as a means of lowering production costs and thus paving the way to lower prices and better wages, along with greater production. 

Agricultural demonstration projects have helped the effort to grow more food for Italy's increasing population. One effort in this field by experts sponsored by FOA and its predecessor agencies has been the introduction of hybrid corn, which Italian farmers have welcomed because of its high yield.

Real interest in improving productivity has now developed in many sections of Italian management, labor and technical personnel, and the program of technical exchange is being continued by FOA as an important yet relatively inexpensive part of the United States program in Italy. 

Dollar costs of the technical exchange program for Italy in the six years since the beginning of the Marshall Plan have totalled less than $4 million, while Italy has spent at least an equal amount in lira costs. 

ITALY ALIGNS WITH NATO 

When Communist aggression in Korea made it clear that Europe must strengthen its ability to resist similar aggression, Italy aligned herself with the other NATO nations in giving top priority to the military defense buildup. Although unemployment remains a serious problem, the remarkable post-war recovery of the Italian economy enabled Italy to finance a sizable defense program. Italy's industrial production index, based on a prewar index of 100 for 1938, climbed from 99 in 1948 to 156 in 1953. 

Production of electric power and gas by 1953 had shot up 102 percent over 1938, metallurgy 50 percent, building materials 61 percent, and mechanical industries 56 percent. Furthermore, these indices have generally continued their upward trend in the first half of 1954. The per capita food consumption in 1952 had moved up from its postwar 1,700 calories to 2,742 per day, close to the prewar level. Real industrial wages had increased about 33.3 percent in 1953 over prewar.

NATO's defense planners were particularly interested in Italy because of its unused industrial capacity and available labor. This meant that a large-scale defense effort could be made without sacrificing the standard of living, provided the additional domestic and import costs could be met. 

In other countries, armament contracts compete with consumer and investment demand for employed industrial capacity, and precautions have to be taken against consequent reductions of living standards. In Italy, defense contracts can put the idle capacity to work and assist the growth of the economy.

Italy's massive unemployment problem has influenced every phase of the American aid program. Ministry of Labor figures, which place the labor force at 19½ million, indicate that registered unemployment averaged 

2,181,000 in 1953-more than 10 percent.

The population of Italy is increasing by about 320,000 net each year.. 

There is an average excess of births over deaths in the neighborhood of 450,000 and a net emigration of about 130,000. The postwar economic expansion has been sufficient to absorb the average net increase in the work force and to utilize more fully the large segment of the labor force which has been technically employed but not working fulltime. However, the expansion has not been sufficient to reduce the backlog of unemployed.

DEFENSE EMPHASIS 

By 1951, with Marshall Plan help, essential reconstruction had been accomplished, enabling Italy to shoulder a much larger share of the common defense effort than would otherwise have been possible. 

But the Korean war period brought new problems to the Italian economy. The western defense boom brought heavy increases in the price of imported materials, tending to widen Italy's dollar gap-the difference in what Italy sells for dollars and what it must buy with dollars. Also, the expansion of Italy's own defense effort, on top of continued high levels of investment, brought new difficulties for its internal economy. 

In the light of this situation the aid program was shifted to attack more directly these new, added problems. Dollar aid was used to supply materials needed for expanded defense production, as well as to fill the gaps which showed up in normal production pipelines. Counterpart funds were channeled, when possible, into the defense effort; in the fiscal year 

1952, half the lira counterpart released went to defense production.

EX U. S. economic aid in post-war reconstruction enabled Italy to shoulder a much larger share of the common defense effort than would otherwise have been possible. Italy has shown a will to defend herself and fight for her existence as a democratic nation in the alliance of the free world. 

More recently, U. S. offshore procurement (OSP) contracts have resulted in expanded production in a large part of Italian industry, and have increased the production base for essential munitions in Europe. 

Among items now being produced by Italian industries on order from the 

U. S. military forces are aircraft, spare parts for jet fighters, mine sweepers, corvettes, radar, small arms and both large and small caliber ammunition.

SOME REPRESENTATIVE EFFORTS 

Roughly one-seventh of Italy's production of "hard goods" is going into material for its own and other NATO armed forces. The Cornigliano 

Steel Plant near Genoa is swinging into production of a scheduled 700 thousand tons of finished steel annually, which will raise Italy's output to about 4 million metric tons and provide the basic material for its mechanical industries. U. S. aid to the Italian government financed $36 million worth of loans to Cornigliano, of which $27 million were for machinery, equipment and technical services from the U. S. and $9 mil-

lion for lire expenditures in Italy. In its shops Cornigliano is casting the parts for a monster press to be used in the United States in forging aircraft frames and bodies. Such presses are capable of hammering out whole aircraft sections in a single operation. 

The giant Fiat plant at Turin, one of Europe's biggest producing units, has received about $35 million worth of loans from U. S. aid funds to Italy. In addition to cars and trucks, its 75,000 workers were quickly able to take on the manufacture of jet engine parts, prime movers for tank transports, marine engines, 4-wheel-drive military vehicles and other important defense items.

One of the most essential items in MSA's commodity import program, copper, has enabled scores of plants to make important defense contributions. In the last half of 1952 copper imports financed by MSA reached nearly $20 million. Copper goes into shell casings and cartridges, army telephone wire, insulated cables for the NATO forces, and certain indirect defense aids such as Italy's growing power network. 

The BPD Ammunition works, one of Italy's two biggest, has been able to import 1,200 tons of copper and 1,500 tons of cotton linters for explosives production. It is using these raw materials in the production of millions of dollars worth of ammunition for the NATO defense program. 

The Microlambda plant near Naples, installed in bombed-out quarters and equipped with the help of a $272,000 loan from the Italian Government made possible by American aid dollars, now turns out electronic controls, radar sets and micro-wave communication equipment for NATO forces at a rate which has helped to place Italy among Europe's leading electronics producers. Its directors have studied production techniques in the United 

States, and American experts have brought technical assistance to the plant itself.

At Genoa, the Firar Company, whose $277,000 loan included $74,000 for American equipment, also produces radio tubes and radar magnetrons. 

The three Galileo plants, restored with $703,000 in loans, now turn out radio instruments, aerial cameras, binoculars and range finders for the 

Italian forces and for NATO.

The zinc plant at Nossa is repaying its U. S. aid in deliveries of important strategic metals. Zinc output, estimated at 60,000 metric tons this year, will be nearly twice that of 1938. The United States will receive approximately 7,200 long tons of zinc and 150 tons of precious cadmium before the delivery contract is completed, in 1961.

In the three years of the offshore procurement program, Italian industries were awarded contracts totaling $411 million-almost one fifth the total placed in all NATO countries. More than half the dollar contracts in Italy have been for ammunition production, while other U. S. orders were for aircraft, transport vehicles, shipping, electronic equipment, artillery and small arms. Orders went to 30 prime contractors and at least 500 sub-contractors. The contracts provided thousands of new jobs and increased work for those already employed. 

FOREIGN TRADE 

Italy's foreign trade suffered a rather severe setback in 1952 largely because of the world slump in textiles and sterling area and French import restrictions which cut. heavily into Italian exports, especially of agricultural products. The export drop from $1,647 million in 1951 to $1,387 million in 1952, in combination with a rise in imports from $2,168 million to $2,336 million, produced a record-breaking trade deficit of approximately $950 million, including such costs as insurance and freight. The increased imports resulted from higher levels of investment and of defense production, and liberalization of Italy's import policy vis-a-vis European Payments Union countries, which the government is maintaining as a major step toward a sounder, more competitive economy. 

The trade situation improved moderately in 1953, due to an expansion of exports in the latter part of the year. Imports at $2,395 million were up 2.5 percent, exports at $1,488 million were up 7.3 percent, and the trade deficit, at $907 million, was down 4.6 percent. That this was a relative improvement, at best, is shown by deficit figures for previous years, i.e., $520 million in 1951 and $278 million in 1950, the most favorable post-war year.

The shift in imports from the dollar to the EPU Area led to a marked decrease in the trade deficit with the dollar area, with gross dollar reserves increasing by almost one third in the year ended June 30, 1954. 

At the same time, however, an even greater increase in the deficit with the EPU area developed. This has resulted in a marked deterioration in 

Italy's EPU position, from major creditor to major debtor status. 

Trade figures for the first four months of 1954 show a modestly encouraging improvement in exports. The Italian Government has followed a progressively liberal foreign trade policy in the post-war period, completely reversing the pre-war policy of autarchy. Its basic assumption

has been that Italy, a country whose imports consist largely of essentials and whose exports consist largely of non-essentials, would benefit greatly from the establishment of a wide, free trading area. Thus Italy has removed quantitative restrictions on 99.7 percent of its imports from 

Organization for European Economic Cooperation countries (including the non-participating sterling countries); maintained in effect a schedule of provisional tariff rates that are in general about one-third below the general tariff rates decreed (though not applied) in July 1950; further reduced import duties on most items by an additional 10 percent; adhered totally to the OEEC common list of freed items; and almost completely abandoned state-trading. The Italians are now asking that EPU creditor countries remove the remaining barriers to their markets, particularly for agricultural products, and permit competition to determine the course of 

European trade.

Italy depends upon imports for much of its industrial raw materials, and four of these-cotton, wool, coal and petroleum-account for almost half of its total imports. 

One bright spot in Italy's natural resource picture is a postwar development of natural gas from fields in the Po Valley and some initial petroleum discoveries in Sicily. In fact, one of the steam-electric power plants which received U. S.-financed equipment was located near the gas fields so it could burn this newly available fuel. the possibility of additional domestic sources of power for fuel-poor Italy, perhaps reducing her dependence on imports.

Italian belief in the concept of European integration was evidenced by the decision to participate in the European Coal and Steel Community (Schuman Plan) despite the danger that the Italian steel industry, with its high costs, might find itself unable to compete with the industries of the other Community countries in a free market. Italian selling prices for steel have been constantly higher than those prevailing in other Schuman Plan countries because of the high prices the Italian industry must pay for imported raw materials, because of high interest rates in Italy, and because (as a result of the serious unemployment situation) Italian industries, including the steel industry, have been under pressure to keep surplus labor on the payrolls, with consequent low productivity. 

The Italian decision to participate in the Coal and Steel Community was, in part, based on the Italian expectation that the Community would aid in the solution of these problems, thereby facilitating a reduction of

Italian steel prices, to the benefit of the mechanical industry and without serious harm to the steel industry. 

The mechanical industry employs some 625,000 workers as compared with only 83,000 in the steel industry, and it was hoped that any reduction in employment in the steel industry-because less efficient producers could not compete at lower prices-would be more than offset by increased employment resulting from expansion of the mechanical industry. 

Although it is far too soon to reach any definitive conclusions, it would appear that the results to date of Italian participation in the Community have been generally satisfactory, as evidenced by the fact that the Government and the groups in the economy most directly concerned (i.e., steel producers, consumers and labor) continue to support Italian participation. The major opposition comes from the Communists and their Left-Wing Socialist allies (the so-called "Nenni Socialists"). 

COMMUNISM 

Emerging from the war years numerically and financially strong, the Communist Party in Italy has since been the most powerful of those in Western Europe. Despite a falling away of adherents since the postwar period, the Communists and their Left-Wing Socialist allies continue to be supported by roughly one-third of the nation's voting strength. The 

Government has recently announced a program designed to weaken the strength of the Communist apparatus. Contemplated measures in the economic field include placing legal East-West trade under a Government monopoly, to deny an important source of funds to the Communist Party; taking over ex-Fascist properties belonging to the State, most of which are presently occupied by trade unions and other organizations dominated by the Communists; and removal from Government payrolls of full-time union activists, mainly members of the Communist-dominated CGIL union. 

TRADE UNIONS 

The complex trade union situation arises out of the formation in 1944 of a unitary organization in which the Christian Democratic, Socialist and Communist leaderships were all represented, but which the Communists soon dominated. This uneasy alliance, marked by thousands of political strikes and growing misuse of their power by the Communists,

broke up in 1948 and 1949, and was followed by the formation of the two present non-Communist unions, the CISL and UIL. 

The Communist-dominated CGIL (Confederazione Generale del Lavoro) had nearly 6 million members in 1947. Today it has less than 3½ million. 

While still a powerful organization, its ability to call successful political strikes during the past two years has been substantially reduced. 

The stronger of the non-Communist unions, the CISL, has about 1,300,000 members and the UIL has about 250,000. So far, efforts to merge them have been unsuccessful, but the two groups have been cooperating more and more in collective bargaining. 

A neo-fascist oriented union, the CISNAL, has a very small membership, and its influence thus far has been slight. 

THE SIDE ITALY IS ON 

Despite all the problems Italy has had to face since the war, the country has made its way along the bitter, up-hill path of material reconstruction, moral recovery and the restoration of a democratic way of life. It has shown a will to defend itself and fight for its existence as a democratic nation in the alliance of the free world. 

The Italian Government has been devoting increasing attention and funds to all aspects of Italy's defense effort. Re-equipped and reorganized according to modern concepts, the Italian army is today becoming an efficient and well-trained force with a high morale. The 

Italian Navy, though drastically curtailed by war and peace treaty provisions, still plays a role in the global defense of the Mediterranean which may be of great importance. The Air Force is constantly adding to its strength and is in the process of converting to jet equipment. 

The Government is currently spending well over $900 million a yearmore than 20 percent of the cash budget-for defense, compared to roughly $500 million in the 1949-50 fiscal year. Per capita defense expenditures have increased from $11 to about $20 in the same period. 

The need to maintain her economic and political stability imposes limitations on Italy's capabilities to devote a greater share of resources to rearmament. Yet a weak Italy means a weak Mediterranean—a grave potential danger to the whole defensive structure of free Europe. Italy must be ready for defense: U. S. assistance has been playing a vital role in helping it to achieve that readiness.
