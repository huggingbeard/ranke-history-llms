A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE FIRST CHURCH IN PETERBOROUGH (THE CONGREGATIONAL SOCIETY, UNITARIAN) 

1752-1952 The First Church in Peterborough, New Hampshire, has had a continuous existence since 1749, when a permanent settlement was made in this town. Three years later, in 1752, the first meetinghouse was built. The church has been connected with three separate denominations, has possessed three meeting-houses, and has been served by twenty-three settled ministers, of whom five were ordained here. For sixty years until 1819, it was the town or parish church, the parish in those early days being geographical in composition rather than ecclesiastical in allegiance, and from 1760 to 1819, every family in town was supposed to be prompt and regular in attendance and was taxed for the support of the church. 

The Congregational Society is still legally the First Presbyterian Church in Peterborough, the First Congregational Church in Peterborough and the First Unitarian Church in Peterborough, though it abandoned the Presbyterian form of church policy in 1799, and it departed from Calvinism in 1827. These changes, however, came about so gradually that the majority both in church and parish passed almost unconsciously from Presbyterianism into Congregationalism and from Calvinism into Unitarianism, though retaining along with their Unitarian faith the Congregational form of church government.

The first settlers of Peterborough were of Scotch-Irish descent, coming here from Londonderry, New Hampshire, either directly or by way of Lunenburg, Massachusetts. It is generally considered that the first Presbyterian church to be founded in America is the Pocomoke Church at Rehoboth, Maryland, where the Reverend Francis Makemie was settled in 1683. But as early as 1718 there was a short-lived Presbyterian church in Worcester, Massachusetts, whose second minister, the Reverend William Johnston, later came here with the early settlers and supplied the pulpit of this church in Peterborough for about a year. This was probably during 17451746, possibly earlier. Among the earliest comers, Captain Thomas 

Morison and Elder William Smith were among the first permanent settlers of Peterborough, and through all its denominational changes, their descendants have retained membership in the church to the present day. 53*12 1

Peterborough began as a frontier town. This whole region was then a gloomy forest, yet though exposed to Indian attacks during the French and Indian Wars from 1744 to 1748, no massacre occurred here, while many of the other frontier towns suffered greatly. Between 1749 and 1759, the settlement grew and prospered but was much inconvenienced under its proprietary organization as to the management of its finances, roads, bridges and the support of public worship. For this reason, Captain Thomas Morison, Jonathan Morison and Thomas Cunningham, on behalf of the settlers, petitioned the legislature for an Act of Incorporation in 1759, which was granted early in January, 1760, and at the first town meeting a committee was chosen to invite regular ministers to preach during that year. There were then about forty-five or fifty families established here, of which five had been here since 1749.

It is supposed that the First Church in Peterborough was formed about the year 1752, but the early church records were destroyed by the burning of Deacon Robert Morison's home in 1791. 

In this town, as in all New England towns in colonial times, the building of the meeting-houses, the choice of ministers, the salary of the parson, arrangements for his settlement, parsonage and lands, and all other parish affairs were the prerogative of every qualified voter in town meeting. Each person in the township was a parishioner and every male of twenty-one or older was a voter in parish affairs. The parson's salary was levied by a public tax, and the procurement of preaching was left to a special committee, or to the selectmen.

Due to the loss of the early church records, we know the names of only seven ministers who preached here before the end of the eighteenth century. These were the Reverend Messrs. William 

Johnston, John Harvey, Peter Powers, John Morrison, David Annan,

Abram Moore and Zephaniah Swift Moore. 

Before speaking of these ministers in more detail, we should note that the first Presbyterian Church in New Hampshire was founded at Nutfield, now Derry, in 1719. Derry was the first parish of Londonderry and was therefore the seat of the Londonderry Presbytery which had oversight of the church in Peterborough. 

The ministers at Derry during this period were: James McGregore, 1719-1729; Matthew Clark, 1729-1735; Thomas Thompson, 17331738; and William Davidson, 1740-1791. 

The Reverend William Johnston, A.B. (Edinburgh, 1704) had been the minister at Worcester, 1725-1736. Later he was settled 2

at Windham, New Hampshire, 1747-1752, and in that year removed to New York state. He supplied the pulpit at Peterborough for a year, perhaps in 1746.

The second minister of whom we have evidence was the Reverend John Harvey, A.M. (Trinity College, Dublin, Ireland, A.B., 1701, A.M., 1704), born in Ireland, 1680, the son of Robert 

Harvey. He was ordained at Palmer, Massachusetts, 5 June 1734, by the Londonderry Presbytery, and was settled there, 1734-1738. 

He supplied the pulpit at Peterborough sometime between 1747 and 1764, but the exact year or years he preached here are not known; later he removed to Blandford, Massachusetts, where he died.

The third minister to preach here was the Reverend Peter 

Powers, A.M. (A.B., Harvard, 1754, A.M., 1758, A.M., Dartmouth, 1782), born at Hollis, New Hampshire, 29 November 1728 being the first white child born in that town, son of Captain Peter and Anna (Keyes) Powers. He was ordained at Lisbon, Connecticut, 2 December 1756, where he remained until 1764. He preached here in Peterborough probably in 1764, for he was soon installed over the two churches at Haverhill, New Hampshire, and Newbury, Vermont, 27 February 1765, serving these churches until 1782, being the first settled minister in Grafton County. In 1785, he was installed at 

Deer Isle, Maine, where he remained until his death on 13 May 1800. 

The first meeting-house in Peterborough, built in 1752, was a rude log structure. Eleven years later, 1763, the town voted: "to lay a floor and build plank seats (and) glaze windows in meetinghouse," which improvements clearly indicate the rugged quality of its original condition. It was built on Meeting House Hill west of the old cemetery on the Street Road, then the flourishing main thoroughfare of the town.

After numerous attempts had failed to secure a parson, at last a call was given to the Reverend John Morrison (A.B., Edinburgh, 1765), who had arrived in Boston in May, 1765, and he was ordained here on 26 November 1766. It was voted to give him “£ 60 for a settlement, and an annual salary of £ 45 until there should be an hundred families in the town, when it should be increased five pounds." Mr. Morrison was born in Pathfoot, Scotland, 1743. He remained the minister at Peterborough until March, 1772; joined the American army in 1775, but soon went over to the British, and died at Charleston, South Carolina, 10 December 1782, aged 39 years. He was a thoroughly disreputable person, in no way con- 3

nected with the Morisons who were among the first settlers of the town.

In 1774, it was voted: "to build a new meeting-house... where the old one stands at some distance west from said house.” 

This building was raised in 1777, but was not finished until 1784. 

It was 60 by 35 feet, was a simple frame structure, barn-like in appearance. The town records contain a description of the size and shape of the pews, aisles and pulpit. It served until the third meeting-house was built in 1826. 

The next settled minister was the Reverend David Annan, who was educated at Queens College, now known as Rutgers College, in New Jersey, and ordained at Wallkill, New York, in October, 1778, with Peterborough for his destination. He was born at Cupar, Scotland, 4 April 1754, and was a brother of the Reverend Robert Annan of the Federal Street Church in Boston. Like his predecessor, Mr. Morrison, he was also an unfortunate choice, and was dismissed, at his own request, by the Presbytery of Londonderry in 1792, and died in Ireland in 1802. 

Three years after Mr. Annan left, the church called the Reverend Abram Moore, (Dartmouth, 1789), who declined. Shortly afterwards, the Reverend Zephaniah Swift Moore, D.D. (Dartmouth, 1793) was called by the church in 1797, but he also declined. Later 

Dr. Moore was president successively of Williams and Amherst 

Colleges, and died at Amherst in June 1823.

In the meantime, however, the church had definitely aligned itself with the Congregationalist order, and on 23 October 1799, the Reverend Elijah Dunbar was ordained minister of this church. 

On that occasion the Reverend Thomas Thacher of Dedham (Westwood) preached the sermon; the consecrating prayer was given by the Reverend Zabdiel Adams of Lunenburg; the charge was made by the Reverend Stephen Farrar of New Ipswich; and the right hand of fellowship was given by the Reverend Henry Cummings, D.D., of Billerica. All these men were Arminians, as was Mr. Dunbar. Three of their churches became Unitarian, and 

Mr. Thacher and Dr. Cummings became outstanding Unitarian clergymen. These facts are important as showing the trend of the thinking, not only of Mr. Dunbar, but also of the church members at Peterborough. 

It will be remembered that Elder William Brewster of the "Mayflower" was a contemporary in Leyden, Holland, of the celebrated Dutch scholar Arminius. Arminius was a mild Calvinist, as were most of the early church fathers of New England. By the year

1800, practically all of the most distinguished clergymen in Eastern Massachusetts and New England, especially the Harvard graduates, were Arminian in their thinking. At that time they did not deny the trinity, the fall of man, hell-fire, total damnation and the creeds and dogmas of Calvinism, but they preferred not to preach upon these subjects and gave only a half-hearted assent, if any, to them. 

Such a man was Mr. Dunbar, mildly liberal in his theology, and, in general, one who believed in covenants rather than creeds. His ministry covered the first quarter of the nineteenth century when many older ministers refused to be pushed into Unitarianism, but viewed old fashioned Calvinism with ever greater distaste. 

Mr. Dunbar was born at Canton, Massachusetts, 7 July 1773, the son of Deacon Elijah and Sarah (Hunt) Dunbar. He was graduated from Harvard College in 1794, and received the honorary degree of A.M. from Williams College in 1798, where he had been a tutor, 1794-1796. He remained minister of the Peterborough 

Church until 1827, when he removed to Milford, New Hampshire, where he died, 3 September 1850, at the age of 77 years. Many members of his family, however, remained in Peterborough and his descendants are still active in the church. 

During the latter part of his ministry, the members of the Peterborough church were not unaffected by the influences that everywhere were rationalizing religious doctrines and broadening and humanizing religious sympathies. Especially enlightening had been Channing's Baltimore sermon in 1819, for the first time clearly defining the issues between orthodoxy and Unitarianism. The sermon also clarified the religious atmosphere as far as the members of the First Church in Peterborough were concerned, and soon the majority of Mr. Dunbar's congregation had outgrown even the mild preaching of their own minister.

In 1819, also, the New Hampshire legislature passed the Toleration Act, taking away from towns and cities the power to tax citizens in support of religion. With the passage of this Act, the old church was soon no longer the "only" church in town. 

Once a year, during most of Mr. Dunbar's ministry, communion was administered by a Presbyterian clergyman, usually the Reverend William Morrison, D.D., of Londonderry. In 1822, there were 

200 church members in the First Church, including the Presbyterians. By the year 1823, forty-one persons withdrew, some of whom were members of the old church, to form a new Presbyterian church, which in 1856 became the Union Evangelical Church of Peterborough. On 15 December 1822, a Baptist Society was gathered

with ten male and five female members, nearly all of whom had been born elsewhere. The Methodist Church was formally organized on 10 April 1839.

Still four-fifths of the church members and a large majority of the parish remained in the First Church, and on 4 July 1826, the old church was reorganized as "The Congregational Society in Peterborough." The members of this church as we have seen, had come to accept the Unitarian faith, though the transition from Calvinism to Unitarianism had been so gradual that no official action registering the change was ever taken. This was the case of most of the 142 colonial churches in New England which became Unitarian.

The first open action showing that the church had accepted the Unitarian position was its election in September, 1820 of three delegates to attend the ordination of the Reverend Levi Washburn Leonard of Dublin. Mr. Leonard was a Unitarian and the Dublin church was Unitarian in faith. This action on the part of the 

Peterborough Society is good evidence that it was in accord with this advanced liberal thought. Mr. Dunbar, whose ministry had certainly fallen upon troublous times, remained with the Society for less than a year after the reorganization and preached his final sermon, 25 February 1827.

When we remember that during Mr. Dunbar's ministry of twenty-seven years, his church had changed from a tax-supported institution to one dependent upon voluntary contributions, that two or three new churches had been established in the town, and that the majority of his own church members had become Unitarian in belief, we are moved to a sincere and profound admiration of his courage, strength and fidelity under circumstances over which he had no control, which put his Christian character to the utmost test. 

The second meeting-house, built in 1777, like its predecessor, served the whole town, there being only one religious society in Peterborough until 1822. During this period of unrest and change, the people still worshipped in the meeting-house on the hill. This was all very well as long as it remained at the center of population of the town, but as early as 1785 dissatisfaction with its location, and its removal to a more convenient place, were subjects of earnest discussion in town meetings. The center of population gradually shifted to the vicinity of the present (third) meeting-house. For by 1810, a cotton mill was in operation near the juncture of the Contoocook and Nubanusit rivers, and soon other mills were built nearby. This drift of population, combined with the formation of

new churches, made the repair or removal of the old building unsatisfactory and unwise. By the year 1824, it had become unfit for use and it was finally torn down and the lumber sold. But it had served its time and purpose, for it had sheltered the congregations of Mr. Annan and Mr. Dunbar.

88 The capital of this association was to consist of not less than 200 shares at $25. each. In all 257 shares were sold, or a total of $6,425. was raised in this way. Mr. Samuel Smith headed the list with twenty shares. When the meeting-house was completed the pews were sold at public auction. There are no treasurer's accounts of the building of the church spread upon the records, except the total cost of construction, which was $6,140.69. What sort of a meeting-house could be built for that sum today?

We must honor and respect those who planned this meetinghouse and made it a reality. It was far different from the two former buildings which had served for seventy years. The aim of the men who built the first two meeting-houses was practical rather than artistic. The task of earning a living and supporting their families, the need of felling the forests, cultivating the fields and building roads left no time for the expression of any artistic impulse they may have had. Accordingly they built as necessity dictated, and their churches were devoid of ornament and without architectural pretentions. But when this building was planned the people were enjoying a relative degree of prosperity. There was more leisure, more time and money for the expression of beauty in both their homes and their churches. 

In those days, the most distinguished architect in America was Charles Bulfinch of Boston, and the accepted and probably correct tradition is that Bulfinch prepared the plans for our present meetinghouse. One tradition is that the plans were prepared for the church in Lancaster, Massachusetts, by Bulfinch, but when they were completed the people in Lancaster wanted a larger church so they were set aside for those of the present church in Lancaster, and the earlier plans were turned over to Peterborough. There is no evidence to corroborate this last mentioned fact. 

The most direct information we have is given by Judge Jonathan Smith of Clinton, whose father, John Smith, was a member of the committee entrusted with the building of the church. Judge 

Smith has stated that his father, with one other member, visited Bulfinch's files, which were still in Boston, was shown various church plans and selected the one that was used. Bulfinch himself could have had nothing to do with the application of those plans to the Peterborough church, as he left Boston in 1817 to devote his entire time to building the national capitol in Washington until his return in 1830. There is nothing in our records nor in the Bulfinch records that gives any connection between this church and Bulfinch. 

This is not unnatural, however, as it is stated that he never made any charges for church plans. The fact that the church is not listed among Bulfinch designs must make us a little careful about our claim.

Even though no positive evidence is found in our records, the church as it stands today confirms that claim. Besides the State 

House in Boston, various buildings at Harvard, Andover and many other places, he drew the plans for a good many churches, of which this church and that of the First Church of Christ in Lancaster

(1816) are superb examples. In his book on Mr. Bulfinch, the 

Reverend Charles A. Place says the Peterborough church is included "because the structure is interesting, displaying one Bulfinch motive at least, that of the arched recesses in the walls for windows and doors. . . . The church is a fine example of a closing period. 

Built at the very beginning of the so-called classical revival, it remains to testify to that integrity of workmanship which makes such buildings so full of interest and delight.” 

The master builder was a Mr. Kilburn of whom mention is made in a neatly written agreement, now in existence, signed by Samuel Smith, and dated 1 October 1825, in which are specified the details for finishing the meeting-house, which was to be fifty-three feet wide and sixty-seven feet long and to have six tiers of pews. The plan states that “there are to be 86 pews on the floor and 22 in the gallery, and 4 long pews for singers. All the pews to be made. . . with mahogany top-railings." 

From Mr. Dunbar's diary we learn, under date of 22 February 

1826: "Dedication of the new brick Meeting-House by the Great 

Bridge. Rev. Mr. Sullivan of Keene introduced by prayer & select lessons of Scripture. Rev. Mr. Beede of Wilton made the consecrating prayer. Rev. Mr. Walker of Charlestown, Mass. preached. 

(John iv 23). I read a sketch of the Ecclesiastical History of this 

Town. Mr. Kelly of the Methodist connection concluded with prayer. The day boisterous & a continual coughing (from the prevalent influenza) from so numerous assembly rendering almost impossible to hear the performances. The music was skillfully performed.” ...

In 1828, a committee was appointed to secure a bell weighing not less than 1200 pounds. A clock was purchased in 1856 by the ladies of the town, which in turn was replaced by the present clock, installed in the church belfrey in May 1947. The clock belongs to the town and not to the church. 

The exterior of the church is practically the same as it was a century and a quarter ago. There have been changes in the interior, the present organ having been installed in 1866, and it was enlarged and rebuilt in 1933. The lectern and the high pulpit with the sounding board have superceded the old pulpit of mahogany and the plain wall on which were painted the figures of Saint Peter and Saint Paul have given place to the present arrangement. In 1880, a new chapel was dedicated, which has been replaced by the present Morison Memorial Parish House. There are now new cushions in the pews, carpets and electric lights, modern heating facili-

ties, large Sunday School rooms, a modern kitchen and a large dining hall in the basement. 

Thus the old meeting-house retains the beauty, simplicity and dignity of color and line, combined with the advantages of the present, to help keep the faith for future generations with the same integrity and devotion which have characterized the worshippers here in by-gone days.

Mr. Dunbar was followed by the Reverend Abiel Abbot, D.D., who had been ordained as a Congregational minister at Coventry, Connecticut, 28 October 1795, and was settled there until his dismission on 1 September 1812 for Unitarian views. Dr. Abbot, probably the most eminent minister Peterborough has ever had, was born at Wilton, New Hampshire, 14 December 1765, the son of

Major Abiel and Dorcas (Abbot) Abbot. A graduate of Harvard 

College in 1787, he received the honorary degree of Doctor of Divinity in 1838, having been tutor of Greek at Harvard, teacher at 

Phillips Academy, Andover, Massachusetts, and, from 1812 to 1819, principal of Dummer Academy. He was installed at Peterborough as an avowed Unitarian, 27 June 1827, was settled here from 1827 to 1848, and died at Arlington, Massachusetts, 31 December 1859, at the age of 93 years, being the oldest living graduate of Harvard 

College at that time.

The choice of Dr. Abbot was in every way fortunate for the society. A man of scholarly instincts and habits, broad and inclusive in his sympathies, a lover of humanity, an untiring worker for education and everything that made for the betterment of the community, the organizer of the first Sunday School in connection with the church in 1827, the founder of the ministerial library for the ministers of the church in 1835, taking a leading part in the founding of the Peterborough Public Library (which is recognized as the oldest public library in the world) in 1833, he was almost all that a minister should be, and his influence on the town has been both deep and lasting. Dr. Abbot resigned his pastoral relations with the Society, 9 September 1848.

From time immemorial, it has been the custom in New England for the minister to meet the children of his parish once or twice a year, at the village church, to hear them repeat the catechism, which they were expected to commit to memory at home or at school. 

These "catechisings" as they were called, were occasion of much interest to the children, who, on a day appointed, flocked to the church, where, seated in ranks on long benches, they repeated the answers to the questions contained in the manual then in use. The 10

Sunday School took the place of these catechisings. Dr. Abbot organized the first Sunday School in 1827, and it has continued to be one of the most effective instruments of early religious culture in our church. For many years it was held in the church auditorium after the morning service, but now has its own Sunday School rooms. 

The Peterborough Ministerial Library, primarily for the use of the ministers of the First Church, was founded by the sole efforts of Dr. Abbot, who commenced it with a large donation of books from his own library, and labored assiduously in its behalf for more than a decade. An act of incorporation was obtained for it in 1838. In 1876, it numbered about 1500 volumes. There is a trust fund connected with it yielding a small income which the settled minister expends each year for new books to be added to the ministerial library.

A Social and Benevolent Society (now associated with the church Alliances) was organized in 1841. At first it seemed to have included nearly the whole parish, but the sewing and planning of the women of the church predominated. Its stated object was the social and moral benefit of its members, but charitable donations and clothing were also made and given to those in need. On 18 

February 1854, the first tea party and fair was held in the church, since which time fairs have been held with unvarying regularity. 

During the Civil War the society made shirts and stockings for twenty-four volunteers from Peterborough. In 1873, funds were raised for the chapel, while in 1875, a harvest supper was served with great success. Throughout the years this society has been an unfailing strength and support to the church. It early became the 

Branch Alliance and from the latter an Evening Alliance was formed in 1936 to accommodate women who were engaged during the day.

As early as 1875, the Young People's Christian Union was said "to be doing a great deal more for the intellectual advancement of Peterborough than any other organization. The Board of Managers reports that in addition to lectures and discussion they have given concerts and plays." By turns called the Young People's Religious 

Union, the Young People's Guild, for many years at the turn of the century, an active organization with largely attended meetings every Sunday evening, and the American Unitarian Youth, it has continued effectively to the present day. For a number of years in the first quarter of the present century the Mary Morison Club was an active organization for young girls. 11

The beginnings of the Laymen's League go back to March 

1906, or earlier, when a group of men known as the "Citizens Class" was organized in connection with the Sunday School to discuss questions of public interest. The Laymen's League was, as far as the records show, organized in 1924, and is affiliated with the national Unitarian Laymen's League. In Peterborough, however, it is a community enterprise and its membership is open to any man who wishes to attend. The meetings are held on Sunday evenings accompanied by a supper and a speaker on subjects of general interest or importance.

The Pew Owners Congregational Meeting House Association was incorporated 22 December 1827, with the following covenant: "We the owners of the pews and shares in the Congregational Church in Peterborough hereby unite for the purpose of assuming corporate powers under the act of this state passed the 3rd day of July 1827 entitled 'An Act empowering religious associations to assume and exercise corporate powers' and hereby assume the name of The Congregational Church Association in Peterborough." This association built and owned the meeting-house of the Congregational Society (Unitarian) in Peterborough, as we have seen, and continued to maintain a separate organization from that which administered the affairs of the Unitarian Church, while at all times loyally cooperating with the latter. It held the title to the church property and the church land. 

Still another board was known as the "Trustees of the Parsonage." This board was responsible for the title and condition of the parsonage, and kept an independent set of books and accounts. 

Thus the church finances were handled by four independent but mutually cooperating groups: The Standing Committee of the 

Congregational Society (Unitarian); the Trustees of the Church 

Funds; the Parsonage Trustees; and the Pew Owners Association.

Each had its separate province. For example, in 1880, when the church was painted, the stockholders and pew owners association assessed the church members to pay the cost. This was the regular procedure for many years. On the other hand the pew owners considered that maintenance and repairs on the furnaces belonged to the Congregational Society and not to the pew owners association!

In 1882, a committee was appointed to study the improvement of the horse sheds. The original sheds had been built and owned by individual members of the parish. They often failed, however, to take proper care of them, and thus some of the sheds had fallen 12

⚫ into a state of dilapidation. Since the sheds were individually owned but situated on church land, it was difficult for the Pew Owners Congregational Meeting House Association, which owned the land, to make the owners of the sheds maintain them in proper condition. 

Finally the association instructed the Standing Committee of the Church to remove the sheds that were in a dilapidated or dangerous condition. Moreover the association voted to allow only church members to own sheds on church property. This matter of reparation and care of the horse sheds appears repeatedly on the records. 

It is of interest, because today, seventy years later, the care and maintenance of the horse sheds is a problem which still demands attention from year to year. It was finally settled, however, by a vote in 1885, that "all who occupy sheds and do not belong to the Society or who contribute less than five dollars per year to 'preaching,' be charged five dollars per year 'ground rent.'" In May, 1905, it was voted to tear down the sheds and rebuild them, - the Society hereafter to own them and to rent them to its members. This was done and the new sheds were built at a cost of $550.95. 

For greater efficiency of church maintenance, after a separate existence of 95 years, the Pew Owners Congregational Meeting House Association (1827) and the Congregational Society (Unitarian) (1752) were merged in 1922. Mr. William H. Caldwell, the clerk of the Pew Owners Association from 1895 to 1922, labored with the problem of getting the pew owners to surrender their deeds, and he finally was instrumental in getting all deeds turned over to the church. He then entered the last record in the books of the Pew Owners Association on 7 January 1922, as follows: 

"Release slips or old deeds for all pews in the Church, having been turned in, making over ownership of the pews to the Congregational 

Society (Unitarian), the Pew Owners Association ceases to exist and the Society becomes the sole owner of the Church property." 

A new set of by-laws was then adopted whereby the church (The Congregational Society, Unitarian) at last became a single efficient organization.

The old brick parsonage on Concord Street, long known as the "Rev. Abiel Abbot place,” which had been purchased for the church in 1883, was exchanged in November 1921 for the present parsonage on Pine Street. During the Fall of 1952, it was remodelled and greatly improved.

In 1929, the First Church joined with the Evangelical Congregationalists and the Methodists to hold union summer services, which were very successful and have continued to the present day. 13

Since 1933, the Reverend Charles Edwards Park, D.D., minister of the First Church in Boston, has preached once a year in the meeting-house, and for many years Mrs. George A. Morison sang a solo on the day he preached.

The two longest pastorates were those of Elijah Dunbar, 28 years, and Abiel Abbot, 21 years: indeed their combined term of service exceeded that of eleven of the seventeen ministers who succeeded them. Of the other six, Messrs. Walbridge and Branigan each served twelve years, Messrs. Robinson and Ferry, each nine years, and Dr. Jackson and Mr. Day, each eight years. 

Curtis Cutler (Harvard, 1829; Harvard Divinity School, 1832), minister of the First Church in Gardner (then Unitarian), 18331836, served as colleague with Dr. Abbot, 1840-1848. He was a member of the General Court from Lexington, 1855, and died at 

Cambridge, 1874.

Colonel Liberty Billings (Meadville Theological School, 1848), was ordained at Peterborough, 25 August 1848, and remained two years. After pastorates at Ware, Bridgewater (Connecticut) and 

Quincy (Illinois), he served as chaplain of the 4th Regiment, New Hampshire Volunteer Infantry, and as Lieutenant-Colonel of the 

33rd Regiment of U.S. Colored Troops. Later he removed to

Florida, where he was member of the Senate, 1871-1874, president pro. tem., 1872, and died in Florida, 1877.

Charles Robinson (Harvard, 1818, Divinity School, 1822), was President of Washington College, Maryland, 1818-1819. He was ordained at Eastport, Maine, 30 October 1822, later serving at Groton, twelve years, and Medfield, eleven years. He was installed at Peterborough, 4 December 1851, and was settled here until 1860, dying at Groton, Massachusetts, 1862. 

Charles Brace Ferry (Meadville, 1859), was ordained at Peterborough, 13 June 1860, remaining until 1869. He was an able and consecrated minister, later settled at Manchester, New Hampshire, and Northampton, Massachusetts, where he died, 1890. 

Isaac Francis Porter (Colgate, 1865, Meadville, 1868), a Civil

War soldier, was ordained at Princeton, Illinois, December, 1868. 

He was installed at Peterborough, 8 June 1870, remaining only two years. His parishioners took sides for and against him. During his pastorate, the coal burning furnaces were installed in the church, and the kerosene lamps were fitted with new brackets. He also introduced evening services which were given up after he left. 

He then had short pastorates at Chicopee, Bolton, Berlin, Littleton 14

and Shirley, in Massachusetts, and at Presque Isle, Maine, and Bath, New Hampshire, and was finally settled in Sherborn, Massachusetts, for eleven years, where he died in 1923. 

Abraham Willard Jackson, D.D. (Colby, 1869, D.D., 1901,

Harvard Divinity School, 1872), probably the most outstanding minister of this church except Dr. Abbot, was a Captain in Colonel 

Thomas Wentworth Higginson's colored regiment in the Civil War.

He was ordained at Peterborough, 2 January 1873, and remained here until 1881. During his pastorate the chapel was built. The 

Peterborough High School was started while he was here, and he served on the school committee, then frequently a duty of the local clergy. He was later settled at Santa Barbara, California, 18811889, and was Professor of Philosophy at the Meadville Theological School, 1894-1895. His superb life of James Martineau, 1901, is still the best biography of that great English Unitarian apostle, teacher, preacher and theologian. Dr. Jackson died at Melrose, 1911. While Dr. Jackson was settled here, Mr. Eben Wilson Jones began his fifty year service as Clerk of the Church, 1880-1939. 

Mr. Jones, who kept a record of attendance in 1880, reported that "about 150 to 200 people commonly were present on Sunday morning at that time, and some of the professional and leading business men attended church regularly. On frequent special occasions the church was filled to its capacity." This in itself is a splendid testimony to the ministry of Dr. Jackson.

William Henry Walbridge was a soldier of the 16th Massachusetts Battery in the Civil War. He was ordained at Stowe, Vermont, 1874, and was settled there until 1880. He was installed at Peterborough, 3 October 1881, remaining until 1894. The weekly offering of contributions was adopted while he was here and has been continued to the present day. Later he was settled at Rochester, New Hampshire, 1894-1900, and was a representative in the legislature, 1909, 1911, and died at Milford, 1916. As in the case of his predessor Mr. Dunbar, members of the Walbridge family continued to live in Peterborough and their descendants have become active and respected citizens. 

George Elmer Littlefield was ordained at Ayer, Massachusetts, 1891, and was settled at Peterborough, 1894-1897. He was more interested in the social and physical side of the church than in intellectual and spiritual matters. He and his wife did much entertaining, and under his enthusiastic leadership the church was remodelled. The Ladies' Social and Benevolent Society presented to the church portraits of Mr. and Mrs. Littlefield.

Later he was 15

settled at Neponset and Haverhill in Massachusetts, after which he became an organizer of People's Forums, and eventually retired to Westwood. 

Wendell Phillips Elkins (Harvard, 1881, Divinity School, 1894), was ordained at Littleton, New Hampshire, for settlement at Bath, 1894-1898. He was minister at Peterborough from 20 March 1898 to 1 October 1899. He was a profound scholar and one of the most intellectual ministers ever connected with the church. During the short time he was here he attracted a substantial following. He was deeply conscientious and resigned after much intellectual unrest because he was compelled to accept the doctrine of the Apostolic Succession. Soon after leaving Peterborough he took orders in the 

Episcopal Church. He died in 1926.

William Henry Branigan (Harvard Divinity School, 1884), was ordained at East Lexington, 14 October 1885, and served churches in Lexington, Duxbury, Dorchester and Wayland. He was installed at Peterborough, 8 June 1900, and was settled here until 31 October 1912, and died at Wollaston, 1940. Previous to his coming here it was the custom of the congregation, as in so many other New England churches, to turn and face the choir during the singing of hymns. The custom arose two or three centuries ago when few congregations possessed hymn books and one of the deacons was chosen to lead the singing. Mr. Branigan did not like to have his audience rise and turn their backs to him, and one Sunday morning asked the congregation thereafter to face the pulpit. This displeased some of the congregation and one good man, at least, never came to church after that! 

Samuel Louis Elberfeld (Meadville, 1895), was ordained at

Revere, 15 January 1897. He was settled successively at Quincy (Illinois), at Danvers and Hyde Park, Massachusetts, and at 

Charlestown, New Hampshire. He came to Peterborough, January 

1913, and remained until 1 March 1916. While here the church adopted the custom of having a different preacher each Sunday during July and August. After leaving Peterborough, he was settled at New Bedford (North Church), at East Boston for sixteen years, 1923-1939, then at Warwick and Bernardston, and again at East 

Boston, from 1949 until his death in April, 1953. 

George Francis Patterson, D.D., S.T.D. (Lombard; U. of 

Chicago), was ordained at Rochester, Minnesota (Universalist

Church), 1904, and was settled there and at Minneapolis until 1911, when he entered the Unitarian ministry and was settled at Kalamazoo, Michigan, 1911-1917. He was installed at Peterborough, 23 16

February 1917, and remained here until 23 January 1919. While here, he suggested that during the singing of the first hymn, the congregation face the choir, and when singing the last hymn, face the pulpit, and this custom was followed for a dozen years or more. 

However, when the church was redecorated the choir was seated in the front of the auditorium at the right of the pulpit, which put an end to facing the choir loft in the back of the church. Dr. and 

Mrs. Patterson were very popular in Peterborough. He was next settled at Concord, New Hampshire, 1919-1923. He then became 

New England Field Secretary of the American Unitarian Association, 1923-1928, Administrative Vice President, 1928-1937, Director, Department of the Ministry, 1937-1939, and Regional Director, Southwestern Conference, 1939-1944, as well as minister at Tulsa, Oklahoma. In World War II, he was minister at Framingham, 19441946, and then Dean of the Starr King School for the Ministry, Berkeley, California, 1947-1949. Having retired, Dr. and Mrs. 

Patterson live in Berkeley. 

Wilton Edson Cross (Benton School of Law, 1915, Meadville, 1918), was ordained at Syracuse, New York, 17 November 1918, and installed at Peterborough, 22 June 1919, remaining here until 4 September 1921. He was then settled at Franklin, 1921-1932. 

During the last twenty years he has been settled at Taunton, Massachusetts.

Arthur Harmon Winn (Union, 1897, Harvard Divinity School, 

1900), was minister at Somerville, Second Church, 1899-1902, Troy,

New York, 1902-1910, and Providence, Rhode Island (Bell Street Chapel), 1910-1920, before coming to Peterborough, where he was installed, 26 January 1922, and remained until 1927. He was the first minister to occupy the parsonage on Pine Street. During his ministry the celebration of the 100th Anniversary of the dedication of the present meeting-house was observed, and he gave the accurate and invaluable historical address on 15 August 1925, which has been very helpful in the preparation of the present brief history of the church. Mr. Winn was a thoughtful scholar and interesting preacher and was much appreciated during his ministry here. He was later minister at Flint (Michigan), Seattle (Washington), Houston (Texas) and Miami (Florida), and he died at Laconia, New Hampshire, 1949. 

Robert Jules Raible (University of Kentucky, 1921, Harvard 

Divinity School, 1925), was ordained at Cambridge, 15 November 

1925, and was installed at Peterborough, 18 October 1928, remaining here until 1932. Next he was minister at Greenfield, Massachusetts, 17

and was installed at Dallas, Texas, 10 January 1942, where he remains. He was Regional Director of the Southwestern Conference, 1944-1949. 

Richard Allen Day (Meadville, 1927), was ordained at Templeton, Massachusetts, 11 December 1927, and was installed at Peterborough, 25 September 1932, and was minister here for eight years until 1940. He has since been stated supply at Francestown, 19421946, and at Dublin, 1951-1952, and resides at Peterborough. 

Arthur Bryant Whitney (Tufts, 1899, Harvard Divinity School, 1902), was ordained at King's Chapel, Boston, 18 June 1902, and was settled at Passaic and Ridgewood (New Jersey), Saco (Maine), and Plymouth, Leominster and Quincy in Massachusetts. He was installed at Peterborough, 7 September 1941, remaining until 1946, and died at Plymouth, 1950.

Robert Lee Zoerheide (Western State Teachers College of 

Michigan, 1940, Meadville, 1943), was settled as the minister at Peterborough, from 14 October 1946 to 1952, and is now minister at Syracuse, New York. 

Mason Fletcher McGinness (Tufts, 1939), is the present incumbent at Peterborough, having been installed here, 28 September 1952.

Deacons Jonathan Smith, Robert Morison, Nathaniel Holmes, Samuel Miller, William Moore, John Smith and their successors were Unitarians. Earlier deacons were of the old school, Presbyterians before 1799, and Congregationalists from 1799 to 1826. 

Sometime previous to 1834, stoves were installed in the meetinghouse, and in 1853, the roof was slated. In 1864, the Sunday School was first held in the vestry, and in 1870, two furnaces were installed in the basement of the church. The building was first insured in 1872, the amount being $6,000. Construction of the chapel in the rear of the church was started on 27 November 1879 and completed during the following year. The remodelling of the church, including the present chancel, pulpit and sounding board, was completed and dedicated at a service on Friday evening, 3 

April 1896. Later, on Easter Sunday morning, 12 April 1903, the reading desk, with a bronze plate suitably inscribed, and the pulpit 

Bible were presented by Mr. and Mrs. George Samuel Tucker, and the furniture and communion table given by the Ladies' Social and Benevolent Society, were used for the first time, completing the work of re-decoration started seven years before. 

The great floods of 1936 and 1938 did much damage to the lower part of the church building, and the hurricane took its toll of destruction, but repairs were soon made and the building was entirely restored. In 1951 the church was again painted. 

Until 1887, the church's annual budget consisted largely of the parson's salary, which naturally, has increased greatly with the years. 1766: $225; 1778: $325; 1799: $400; 1827: $425; 1887 to 

1916: $1000; 1953: $4000. Since 1887, the budget of the church has continued to increase with the expanding financial and economic structure of the nation, as follows: 1887: $1500; 1902: $1785; 1907: $2100; 1922: $3555; 1940: $4800; 1945: $5290; 1950: $9092. The earliest records concerning church music which have been preserved were votes passed in town meeting, 10 April 1792: "Voted,

That Dr. (Isaac) Watts' version of Psalms be used in the congregation of Peterborough for the future. . . . Voted, To choose a committee to procure seats in the breast and front of the gallery, decent and comfortable to accommodate a sufficient number of singers" to form a satisfactory choir. "Voted, That Jonathan Smith, John Gray, Oliver Felt, and Samuel Smith are to set the tune... » Seventyfive years later the organ, which was installed in 1867, was purchased with funds contributed by members and friends of the church as well as from the receipts from a sociable and reception in December, 1866, and from a concert at the dedication of the organ.

The address at the Centennial of the Town of Peterborough was delivered by the Reverend John Hopkins Morison, D.D., on 24 

October 1839. 

The semi-centennial of the dedication of the meeting-house was celebrated on 22 February 1876, the Reverend Minot Savage, D.D., preaching the sermon, and an historical address, prepared by Dr. 

Albert Smith, was also read.

On Thursday evening, 8 July 1926, the Annual Meeting of the Church was held in the newly built parish house. One hundred and fifty-one persons were seated at the first supper in the beautiful new dining room, and a second table had to be set as one hundred and seventy-five members attended the meeting. After the supper, which was furnished by the Alliance, the people gathered in the parish hall, where Mrs. George A. Morison sang and also played selections on the new grand piano, the gift of Mr. and Mrs. Carl 

F. Kaufmann. The total cost of the parish house was reported as $32,686, the building having been given by the Morison family and the furniture by other members and friends of the church. A play room for the young people, formerly the old church dining room, was given by Mrs. George E. Clement.

From the 15th to the 17th of August, 1926, was celebrated the Centennial Anniversary of the building of the present church as well as the dedication of the Morison Memorial Parish House. Mr. 

Winn, the minister at that time, delivered a very interesting and scholarly address on this occasion, and at the evening service the 

Reverend Samuel Atkins Eliot, D.D., LL.D., then President of the 

20

American Unitarian Association preached the anniversary sermon. 

On the following day several of the former and neighboring ministers spoke briefly and Mrs. Jennie Hadley Field read an historical sketch of the building of the meeting house from the records of the 

Congregational Meeting House Association. On Tuesday, the 

Reverend Louis Craig Cornish, D.D., later President of the American Unitarian Association, preached the dedication sermon, and as part of this service there was a piano solo given by Mrs. Edward 

MacDowell and a vocal solo by Mrs. George A. Morison. 

Church and town celebrated the Bi-Centennial, 21 October 1939, with an historical parade, dancing, pagent, and an address by 

Professor Philip Putnam Chase of Harvard University. Mrs. Edward

MacDowell played a number of her husband's compositions, and an historical sermon was preached in the church on the following 

Sunday morning.

During the summer of 1952, a still more important celebration occurred. This was the Bi-Centennial of the First Church in Peterborough. On Friday evening, July 4th, a dinner followed by dancing and fireworks was given at the Golf Club; on Saturday afternoon a tour of Old Houses was enjoyed, followed by tea in the Parish House of the Church; while on Sunday morning, Dr. Charles 

Edwards Park preached the 200th anniversary sermon, the congregation being dressed in old time costumes. 

The widow of Deacon Samuel Miller gave a silver communion service to the church in 1873. It was inscribed, "Presented to the 

Unitarian Church, Peterboro, N. H., by Mrs. Ruth W. Miller, August, 1873," and consisted of two large tankards, two plates and four goblets, and a christening bowl. These, with the choice pewter communion service dating from the earliest days of the church two large tankards, two large plates, two tall goblets and two small goblets, - are now exhibited in a handsome glass front secretary in the Parish House.

After World War I, a memorial bronze plaque inscribed with the names of the soldiers of the parish, was placed in the vestibule of the church.
