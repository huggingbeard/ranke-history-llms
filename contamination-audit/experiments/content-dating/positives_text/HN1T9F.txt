Nevertheless, Dan halted to listen. The crack of a .45 was no new sound to him-no more new than the crack of a falling tree-and something prescient in his mind told him that this was no result of nature's wildness. About him the storm whipped and whistled; in his saddle he bent over under the force of it, let the wind and snow rip about his upturned collar and play fury with every loose element of his outfit. 

Crack! Again it came! 

He raised his head, eyes straining into the whitefaced madness that seemed to have taken possession of the entire known world. Little could be seenbut that second shot had been a pistol crack. Trees didn't blow down in such quick succession.

Little could be seen. As far as the eye could reach, the blinding whirl of the storm-snow and sleet and wind-encompassed nature. The hills were

a mass of white. Vision was a matter of yards, except where some sudden gust of the wind drove away the stinging particles for a moment and left a broad track across the open. The world was white -a white fury. 

Dan Wheeling shivered. A motionless figure he was, there on the quivering cow pony, bent over, listening the lone living thing, it seemed, in all that wilderness which had been given over to the elements. 

His face was quite invisible.

Such of it as protruded was screwed up under the biting snow particles; ice clung to his straight eyebrows, and the reckless, laughing lips were chapped and wet with cold. The eyes were invisible, too-mere pin points, with eyelashes that were white with frost and ice. 

Then came a third shot. This time there could be no mistake. Dan could even get a vague idea of the direction from which it had come. 

His shoulders straightened. Even under the parka, the lean frame could be seen coming to life. 

Down there, where the white-blanketed slope curved into a whiter nothingness, was the end of his father's range. It was where his own range met that of the old "Iron Man of the Border." Old man Wheeling had won that title in earlier days; and now, grizzled and unbending before the march of years, he held to it grimly. Even his son he had tried to force down before the metal of his powerful will. But Dan Wheeling had a way of laughing.

A nudge of the spurs, and the little cow pony started down the slope. Dan knew the way, knew it year in and year out when it was not covered with

snow. Winter had lasted long this year; it had clung to the Southwest ranges and had then suddenly -like the last fling of a spendthrift-thrown this norther down upon them.

The cow pony struggled through the blinding fury. 

Dan Wheeling's frown of puzzlement could hardly be seen, but it was there.

Shots on this range meant trouble. His father hated him or perhaps it was not hate. The old

Iron Man had no use for him, anyway; that was certain. And if there were trouble at the edge of the two ranges here-well, it meant real trouble. 

Dan knew that he was not popular in Shale City, and that his father was. For years men had waited until Dan should go too far, and war should break out between father and son.

A form loomed out of the white mist created by the flying snow-a lone rider on a cow pony.

The rider was tall and angular, and his collar almost completely covered his face. Such of his features as could be seen were wet and iced. There was a pistol in his hand, but he lowered it at sight of Dan Wheeling. 

The two horsemen came together. "What's up, 'Slim'?" Dan yelled to his foreman.

The words were torn from his lips by the biting wind. "Blazes, I reckon!" the other returned. "Heard the shots ?" "Three of 'em." Dan nodded. "Who- 99 "Strangers. Strangers to this range, and I see 'em ridin' across here an hour ago. 

They asked for

you, Dan, when I questioned 'em. Then I saw 'em headin' across to the 'Old Man's' territory. 'Shorty' 

Grayson'd jest got finished warnin' us that no more strays was comin' across, and that the first of our men to cross the new line was comin' there for trouble."

Dan's frown deepened. The troubled look in his gray eyes grew more intense.

Shorty Grayson was his father's range boss. 

There had never been wire on this range before, but it had been rumored that the Old Man intended erecting some along here. There were many draws in this region and numberless box canyons. Often cattle strayed from one range to the other-an ordinary thing in ordinary circumstances, but not simple. when the men of the two ranges were ready to spring at each other's throats at a word.

So it had come, and the Old Man had given his ultimatum. Dan wondered if the Iron Man really wanted to chase him off the ranges of the Big Sandy. 

War. Yet, who had the strangers been? And those shots! What was their meaning?

Slim Lewis was looking at Dan strangely. Under the brim of the tattered sombrero, Dan met his eyes. "What more do you know, Slim?" he asked softly. 

The foreman of the Lazy W 2 shook his head. "They was down in town, these hombres, Dan," he said reluctantly. "Askin' for you there, too. 

Seems they was known. Bad hombres from down along the border. Somebody mentioned-Guitterez!" "Guitterez!"

Dan stiffened in his saddle. These men were

from Guitterez! His eyes seemed striving to pierce through the whirl of the blizzard-actually they were unseeing. Dan's vision was back over other years and upon other scenes.

Guitterez was an outlaw. Before, in the lawless days for which he had never been forgiven, Dan had known him. He had never been in any of the 

Mexican's crooked adventures, but they had been companions. They had shot together, side by side, against common enemies. They had been bunkmates when Dan had signed up his right arm and his guns for a range war against sheepmen. 

Then, afterward, Guitterez had become powerful below the border, to where he had been forced to flee. Dan had sold him horses-quantities of them, from the Lazy W 2. He had given shelter to one or two of Guitterez's men when Mexico got too hot to hold even them.

That was all. In all his life Dan had not been mixed up in a crooked play. But his reputation had gone ahead of him. His father and the others would never believe. The establishment of a paying ranch and the living of a decent life here in this valleythat all meant nothing. To them all he was a killer and an associate of crooks.

And now, three men had come up here and mentioned Guitterez, and had asked for Dan. And three shots had sounded across the frozen creek which marked the boundary of his land and the edge of his father's range. 

Dan shuddered a little. He knew danger and death, and he knew killing. But the prospects ahead

of him meant worse than that. Something stirred inside him and warned him. There was trouble and death stalking abroad through the wild whirl of the blizzard's tail that day. "You didn't see anything else, Slim?" he asked slowly. "I told Pike to leave that bunch o' steers there in the draw, and go on and take a look. Nobody's anxious to cross the line to-day. Then I come ridin' for you. Knew you'd be around here somewheres." 

Dan nodded. "No use crossing the line. I've got a hunch we're in for enough trouble as it is" He broke off as a single fider emerged out of the kaleidoscope of driving snow. It was Pike, who had been riding herd out here, and whom Slim Lewis had dispatched. The hand drew rein, and his pony's head drooped under the drive of the storm. "See anything, Pike?" "I didn't cross, Slim. They was riders comin' from the direction o' the Old Man's place. They was one hombre face down in the snow-dead or hit bad, I reckon and three ridin' off toward town." 

Dan and Slim swore simultaneously. The worst had happened, and they knew it. That man in the snow-if it were Shorty Grayson or one of Wheeling's hands-it marked the beginning of the fiercest kind of range war. Already the Lazy W 2 was hated by every outfit in the county-chiefly because Dan cared about none of them, got on by himself with his crew, and feared no one at all.. 

But those three men riding off

Dan's voice was heavy, and there was a weariness. in it that was not like him. "Let's trail back, Slim. I reckon I'll try to ride into town to-night-if we don't hear things before then. Keep to our side, Pike; and if anybody rides over, don't stop to argue. Jump back for the house. 

No shooting!" "If they come over, I reckon there'll be so many of 'em that nobody'd stop to argue with 'em, anyhow," Pike replied grimly. "I'll jest try to keep ahead of 'em, Dan." 

He needed no 

Dan nodded, but said nothing. reminder of the gravity of the situation. He knew his father. Dan had only his own men to count on -five of them. They were loyal-had been loyal, with their backs to the wall, from the beginning. 

But the trouble that seemed likely to break out here in a quick blaze of madness fanned by hatred would have the whole range around Shale City against him. 

He and the foreman turned back, bent their heads into the storm, and groped their way across the whitesheeted fields for the ranch house. The roofs of bunk house, mess place and Dan's small but substantial home, bulked small and yet homely out of the blizzard.

Dan led the way into the biggest room, which he had fitted up as a kind of library. A huge fire was burning here. It was all homelike and comfortable, even though Dan lived there alone with only a Chinese cook to serve him. His gray eyes took in the sturdy furnishings.

This he had made home. He had carved it out

in the teeth of disapproval and hatred, and he had asked no favors. Now he knew that the trouble he had feared for so long was coming to a head. 

In the firelight, his face had a lean, harried look. 

Yet there was a dangerous glitter in the narrowed gray eyes, an ominous hardness about the thin, straight mouth. The firelight caught that gray glint when he turned to Slim. "This is home, Slim," he said hoarsely. “And maybe they're goin' to plant me here, but they ain't drivin' me out!" 

Slim threw off his coat. "I reckon we're with yuh, boss-if that means anything. And you know more about these troubles than I do. All I can make out is, they hate you-all of 'em-and the first pretext they're goin' to jump yuh." "That's all there is," Dan returned. "Maybe some of 'em like the way the creek spills across my land, and maybe some like the grazin' on our side of the hills. But mostly, they're like my Old Man-they figure I'm plumb bad, no good; and they've been sayin' it so long that it'd break their hearts to believe anything else. I wonder if I'd better see the 

Old Man."

Slim shook his head. "It'd mean trouble to go there, Dan. Shorty was killed-I'll lay odds on that. 

They'll be plumb riled. And"-Slim hesitated; he obviously found it embarrassing to put his thought into words "anyhow, Dan, jest who were these bad hombres lookin' for you in town?" 

Dan did not answer at once. 

Long legs spread

wide apart, he stood with his back to the foreman, staring into the fire. "I thought you'd ask that, sooner or later," he said, unmoving. "I don't think I know them, personally. But they're from Guitterez, and that's enough. Guitterez and I worked together once, off and on.

He wasn't so bad then. He is now. He's the bird who's behind the raiding along the border, down in the Quicapos country." 

Slim's comment was a startled exclamation. "Him? They's several bad ones down there, Dan. 

What've they got to do with you?" "Guitterez knows me." Dan shrugged. "If there's several in that game, maybe he wants to strengthen his own hand. And I reckon Guitterez knows I can be handy when gun play is called." Dan said the last with reluctance. He had never taken pride in the events that had made him known as one of the most dangerous gunmen of the Southwest, while he was still a boy.

Headstrong and willful, trained to these very qualities by his father, he had wanted simply to go his own way. That was all. Except that sometimes people got into that way. 

Slim's voice broke in again: "Was it Guitterez we shipped all them horses to -the three herds that went across the border?" 

Dan nodded slowly. "They got to him, eventually. 

I knew it at the time, I suppose, and maybe I shouldn't have had any dealings with him. But they were my stock, on sale, and we were paid straight cash. That's as close as I ever was to any dealings

with Guitterez-and at the time, I didn't even know what he was up to down there. I don't know now, exactly. I wouldn't know about the Quicapos business except that the Old Man's biggest ranch is down in that valley, and I hear he's been hit hard. There's an organized crowd doin' things down there." "And they figure maybe you'll want to join the organization." "Maybe. We don't know a thing yet, except that guns are going to start popping across this range plumb pronto." Dan swung about. "I'm going to get a cup of coffee and head down for town." "Alone?" "Exactly. Want some coffee?" Dan's face was more grim than ever, his low, slightly drawling voice more cold. "Better take me, and maybe one o' the boys, Dan.

We're all stickin' with you, you know, and there's sure to be a bad time in Shale City to-night." "There sure is," Dan agreed grimly. "But you're not in it. This is personal. When they come messyou and the boys can ing around this range, then jump in. I count on that. But my personal fights are between me and the crowd. Go loop me a fresh pony, Slim. I just want time to change this shirt; it's wet."

There had been rumors of a gold strike and of a railroad, but neither had materialized. It was just what it had started out to be a cattle center for the ranges of that vicinity. 

But these men were stubborn, and the name, Shale 

City, remained. It was the same stubbornness that 

Dan Wheeling knew so well and bitterly.

Four years he had been back now, and his continued success with the small ranch he had bought only increased the feeling that, "Young Wheeling❜ll never turn out no good. He's plumb too wild and bad!" It had been a gesture of grim defiance when he took the property next to his father's range. 

Brooding, the scowl stamped on his lined and leathered face, the Iron Man of the Border sat back to watch his son. He had disowned him after a scrape in Shale City-a boyhood scrape that had sent Dan off on what turned out to be a notorious career. 

Now, Wheeling said nothing. He neither approved nor condemned. He watched, waiting for the break he knew must come.

There followed, in those years, the usual range troubles which are normally settled between ranchers who know each other. But each of these things only widened the break between Dan Wheeling and the ranchers of that part of the Big Sandy country. 

Lost or stolen cows, mavericks, unmarked ponies, trail rights-Dan had to fight his way grimly step by step, always under suspicion and always condemned in advance.

The name of Guitterez had come to be anathema in the Southwest. When rumors drifted from the 

Quicapos Valley and from below the border, it was recalled that Dan Wheeling had once associated with him. In the midst of this, Dan's father, angry over the constant quarrels between his men and those of the Lazy W 2-boundary disputes and the likebitter with his son, had ordered wire strung along the posts between the two properties-posts which had been unused for years. 

And then came the three men from Guitterez.

The three shots at the boundary, and Shorty face down in the snow.

Dan knew that he was riding to a dangerous errand as he fought through the drifting snow that night. The trail was almost obscured by the drifts.

There were places where it was completely invisible. 

He was the only living thing in all that desolate waste. 

The tailend of the blizzard, at a time when spring should have been in power, was taking every

ounce of its fury. And only a few miles south, where the higher ranges had broken the storm and the sun could be seen, the heat was almost tropical. 

Dan, as he rode, could feel his .45s rubbing against his thighs, under his outer clothing. He had worn two guns to-night; it was long since he had worn both. But they might be useful once again. Men knew well that he knew how to use them—perhaps that fact alone would keep him from the need. He prayed that he would not meet his father in town.

Through the white mist of the snow a few pale lights gleamed. A little later, Dan was in the partial shelter of the wooden shacks along the main street and headed for the best lighted structure of all—a wide, false-fronted building of only one story, labeled the Royal Gorge Saloon. Here was the rendezvous of the ranchers when they came to town-the most popular meeting place. It was here that Guitterez's messengers would have looked for Dan first.

The heavy doors were tightly shut against the cold. 

The windows were incrusted with ice and snow, opaque. The street was deserted, and the wind howled, but the lights gleamed cheerily through the frosted panes and promised warmth and shelter. 

Dan took his mount to the lean-to stable in the rear and left it there, saddled. There were a few other horses stabled similarly. Then he opened the rear door of the place and came into the huge barroom from behind the bar.

He could not be sure of a certain silence which followed his entrance, but he detected it in the at-

mosphere.

The howl of the wind that had come through the momentarily opened door had drowned out sound. Now he walked coolly toward the bar, opening his coat as he strode, while the bartender's eyes followed his progress toward a place. 

A few men were there whom Dan knew. Others were strangers to him; he came into town but seldom. 

But though they were all watching him, with a poorly disguised interest, no one spoke. If his eyes met those of a man he knew, Dan noticed that the other averted his gaze.

He smiled a twisted little smile, and nodded to the barkeeper. "How about it? Can you still talk, Mike?" "When I have to." Mike's lowered eyes swept the room uncertainly.

Dan leaned against the bar, ignoring now the men spaced there at intervals. But in the dirty, cracked mirror across from him he could detect three who were definitely strangers to this country, grouped there.

One was undoubtedly a Mexican, garbed exotically for this setting, dark-skinned and intelligent of eye, wearing an air of rather insolent authority. A second, a bigger man, Dan thought he knew as "Red"

Peters, a notorious gunman with a reputation that was more than bad. The face of the third man was not visible from where Dan stood; but he could see that the three were conferring together, and that the others in the saloon gave them a wide berth for standing room.

Dan attended to his whisky. "What's the news?" he asked idly, across the bar.

Mike gave him a sharp look, but Dan looked quite innocent. Mike looked along the bar. "Plenty. Some hombres lookin' for you-asked for you, anyhow." With little more than a shrug, the bartender indicated the trio who occupied the at the farther end. "And-Shorty Graycorner . son's dead-shot." 

Mike waited. Dan wondered what the man expected of him. He frowned. "Yeah, I knew that," he said softly. "Anybody got any ideas about who did it?" "There's plenty of ideas!" Mike grunted. "What brung you to town, Dan, on a night like this?" "Curiosity. Anybody from the Two Star been in since Shorty died?" "Only 'Baldy,' with the news. He come in for the undertaker. It was too late for doc. But the rest of 'em'll be here, all right. And then-"

Mike broke off and worked over a recalcitrant cork. 

He shook his head. "You ought ter be stickin' to home in this weather, Dan." 

Dan smiled and proceeded to roll a cigarette.

There was a boyish recklessness, a youthful disregard for danger, in that smile, that had a charm in spite of the slight tinge of bitterness about Dan's lips.

His guns were exposed with his coat thrown back, and he turned, with his elbows on the bar, for a survey of the room. 

As before, no one met his gaze. He lighted the

cigarette, and turned at a touch on his arm.

Beside him was the third of the men who had been at the end of the bar; and he saw now a small, slight figure and a dark face, scarred by a red cut across the chin. "You're Dan Wheeling?" said a cautiously low voice.

Dan smiled and waved to the room at large. "Couldn't you tell it, after a look around here?"

The men in the place were watching him now. 

Rumor had done its work. Perhaps these men were as certain as he that the three at the end of the bar were responsible for Grayson's death. Already they knew a great deal, and suspected more. They were waiting. Lowered lids tried to hide the eyes that watched Dan's every gesture.

Dan's man motioned toward the group below, and 

Mike moved away ostentatiously. "That is Devarra. He is the friend of Guitterez and he wants to talk with you." The man spoke with a strange accent, but Dan could not make it out. Perhaps he was another Mexican. 

Anyway, Dan Wheeling had heard of Devarra.

He was well known farther south, a country which was not too familiar to Dan. Devarra had taken a hand at every game in his field-gambler, hired gun fighter, the proprietor of honkatonks. And now he was with Guitterez evidently. A smooth character. Guitterez, whatever he was up to, was enlisting careful material.

Dan roused and glanced around the room. There was defiance in his movement as he nodded and started toward Devarra. Every eye followed him.

27Devarra smiled as he came up a white-toothed smile that lighted his darkly handsome face. There was a chilling quality in his features, though. Dan, who had heard much of him, knew instinctively that he had not heard enough. Devarra was a killera ruthless, laughing snake. "It is a pleasure to see you, Señor Wheeling," he said. "Though I regret that this does not seem to be a place where you are too popular." He waved one of his thin hands in a negligent gesture toward the men in the room.

Dan shrugged. "I never aimed at winnin' any popularity contests, anyway. Besides, I reckon you knew I was unpopular before you saw me to-night." 

Devarra inclined his head. "Si. It is so. Down there we hear most everything. Down there, popularity goes to the man who can use his guns well. 

It is much better. Not so?" "Maybe." Dan stepped back a pace. His voice was so low that the three could just hear it, yet it snapped with a confident menace. "What did you shoot my father's foreman for?" 

Devarra's eyes darkened. For a moment his face was uncannily reminiscent of a snake about to strike.

From the Big Sandy across the line-we will be kings!" 

His eyes gleamed with a fanatical light. Dan wondered if this might not be a kind of madness.

Whatever it was, Guitterez and his crowd had huge plans, and they were choosing their pawns with care. 

Still Dan stood impassive; the game was a little beyond him yet. He knew there was more than what 

Devarra had told him-knew that Guitterez had a good reason for wanting him in. 

Devarra revolted him; there was something slimy about him-something cold and slimy. Peters was a dangerous animal who had to be got out of the way of human beings. Guitterez had badly mis-

But Devarra was wary. He was watching Dan's face. "You come with us?" he demanded. "Then you may learn more. Or stay here. Just so we know that when we are ready, we can count on you." 

That was all he would say; but it told Dan a trifle more. 

Then, without warning, the huge doors of the entrance swung open. In an instant there appeared the apparition of horses in the swirling snow outside, ghostly in the light from within making a yellow path across the street. And between the bar and the horses-men. They stood there for a momentten or more of them, sweeping the barroom with avid, questing eyes. The hands of many contained guns. Dan recognized his father as a quick hush came down over the interior. 

The men from the Two Star had come!

Even the arrogance of Devarra underwent a shock at the grim look of the newcomers. They were like avenging archangels there against that setting. Peters shifted nervously; fear rolled in his eyes. "Steady!" Devarra said to him in a low whisper. "If you want to live, hombres, don't let your nerve break now!"

Dan realized that once more he was justifying the opinion and the hatred of the valley. Shorty's

avengers had come-and Dan was found with the killers!

He waited. There was nothing of apprehension in his manner. He faced these men as he had always faced them-cold and unyielding, grim of jaw and fearless, neither asking nor granting favors. And the riders pushed their way into the room.

Lights were almost all extinguished. Gunfire shattered the half darkness. It was a scene from an inferno. Men reeled and fell, shot or grappled. Too

often it was too close for shooting, and men struggled in ungainly positions on the floor. It was to the death-that battle. 

And outside, Maloon's cohorts were coming up.

They had been kept off the building for a moment. 

All had taken place in a matter of three minutes, though it seemed hours.

Dan hurled a limp body from him. His gun butt crashed down across the bared teeth of a man who had flung himself across the room, at his throat. If there were any cartridges left in his .45 he had forgotten them. 

This was a moment for fangs-for fists and weapons of the hand, for bared teeth and slashing feet.

The door crashed inward again and carried several of the fighting men with it. Where was Maloon?

Outlined against the turgid dark of the street were men. Maloon's men! Gunfire swept the interior.

Again men went down, again the surge of battle recommenced. Friend or foe-they were indistinguishable now. Dan only knew that it was over. 

That it was a matter of seconds. 

Then suddenly a new and wilder yell came and permeated. Men heard it, forgot it-and then it came again. It broke upon the street of Cascade like a fury, and on the heels of it came a score of galloping hoofs. 

The cry of the new riders was echoed by other cries. They were taken up; the shouts went from one man to the next. Out of the dark had come an unknown horde.

Through the shifting gun smoke-across the reeling bodies of men, wounded, alive, or dead-Dan saw his enemy.

Maloon, a single man with him, was making for the rear door!

Dan only knew that the fight was lost. He had lost Hardmuth. The few men from the ranches who had joined him seemed to be down. How many of his Los Padres men remained he could not tell; but he had seen three crumple under the gunfire of Maloon's men.

Maloon still lived, however, and for some reason he was retreating. Dan's instinct told him that the boss of the Quicapos was making his get-away. His stunned brain told him no reason. He only knew that Maloon must not escape that this long fight must not be in vain.

Dan had no idea of the fact that he had been hit. 

A bullet had creased his shoulder, and his shirt was crimsoned. A knife had cut him across the temple, and he had no sensibility of the fact that he was seeing out of only one eye. There was a great gash in his left arm. "Maloon-you swine!" 

At the door, Maloon turned, his eyes hunted. 

With him, the other man whirled, also. Maloon was carrying saddlebags. 

In that same instant, though, he threw himself through the doorway. Dan, through the rifted clouds of smoke, saw the gleam of the other man's teeth,

caught the flash of a revolver. He brought up both his guns and pressed both triggers together. 

Only one of them fired, but it was enough! 

Maloon's bodyguard dropped, crashed against the doorway. His guns fell. Dan sprang forward. 

Those wild cries in the street-as yet he had paid them no heed. He knew where Maloon was going.

The outline of a tethered pony stood before his eyes. 

The dam-and destruction! 

Dan could only reason that Maloon was taking the safest way. Perhaps he was aware that the marshal's office and the railroad had evidence. Perhaps he knew that his reign ended soon, and that now was the time to get away, before Dan Wheeling's wolves of the border reached him. 

These things went through Dan's mind in a flash. 

Yet the facts interested him little. Here was Maloon -that was enough!

He hurdled the body of the man he had dropped, careless of the fact that his guns were empty. 

Maloon had got to the saddle. The mount under him gave a leap and was off.

Dan looked around wildly. His guns were no good. Another horse stood in the alley. He ran for it, threw himself into the saddle from three feet away. Grasping the bridle, he turned the wild mount and swung off after Maloon. 

Behind him, the crash of the battle was ebbing.

He did not know. Maloon's men were scattering. 

Strange riders had come out of the North. Men followed him to the doorway, saw him get away.

Dan awakened slowly. His head was pillowed on a blanket, and the stars shone serenely above him. 

Horses were about, and many men.

The Old Man!
