FOREWORD The collection of Egyptian stamps as a field for philatelic specialization has grown remarkably during the last decade, a fact which may be attributed to three reasons. In the first place, all but a very few items listed in the standard catalogs are common so that the average collector may, within a reasonable period of time, expect to complete his major and many minor varieties. Secondly, Egyptian stamps (especially the early issues and the provisionals of 1922) afford a fertile field for the study of paper, watermarks, perforations and printing faults. Plating, moreover, is also possible in many of the issues. 

Thirdly, the designs of the stamps themselves are interesting and reflect the entire scope of Egypt's history, from the dawn of civilization right down to our own day.

In dealing with the stamps of the 

Twentieth Century one cannot accurately begin with the turn of the century. The stamps in use then are theoretically remnants of an issue introduced in 1888. (The first definite stamp of our study came in 1906).

The work, therefore, will begin with the de la Rue stamps depicting the 

Sphinx and Pyramid, and inscribed, "Milliemes," and continue through the regular emissions, postage dues, officials and air mails. The commemorative issues will be mentioned in chronological order, but not treated in detail as this subject was comprehensively dealt with in a booklet¹ published by the author about a year ago, and which may be referred to by those collectors interested in the subject.

Until 1888 the postage stamps of

Egypt were issued in denominations of paras and piastres, the units of currency popularly employed by lands under the domination of Turkey; but in December, 1887, a decree was posted stating that "from the first of next 

January the present stamps of the five and ten paras will be replaced by new stamps of one and two milliemes. In consequence all stamps of five and ten paras will be replaced by such of the one and two milliemes in all post offices."

The new series, therefore, appeared on New Year's Day of 1888. Like the previous set, the new adhesives were produced at the London plant of 

Thomas de la Rue & Company. All values were surface printed on a fine white wove paper, impregnated with a true watermark consisting of a crescent and star.

The one-millieme stamp of the set depicts a dhow, felucca or gyassa, a sailing vessel that is as characteristic of the Nile as the pyramids are of

Egypt. Most unusual is the sail of this type of boat, thousands of which carry freight from one part of the ancient river to another. It is a large triangular rig so fastened to a short mast that the winds, which blow across the Nile, will propel the ship in any desired direction.

Almost all ordinary freight is dependent upon the felucca for transportation in Egypt, despite the fact that railroads and even airplanes carry a great many Egyptian products to markets more rapidly. They can carry heavy loads of sugar, stone, or cotton, their gunwales almost awash; or two or more of them may be lashed together and piled high with chopped straw like a truncated pyramid. When they are empty they seem to ride over the water rather than through it, their gaily painted painted prows pushing the muddy river aside. In the evening, when the wind dies down, they hug the shores so their sailors can punt with poles or tow them along from the bank.

Though generally referred to as the Colossi of Memnon, the two giant statues located at Thebes and depicted upon the 10-milliemes stamp, are actually representations of Amenhotep III, who as the son of Thutmosis VI, reigned over Lower and Upper Egypt about 1,400 B. C. He was a great builder and was responsible for the erection of numerous monuments and statues, particularly those at Thebes and Luxor. He waged a war against the Ethiopians, and dominated all land between Nepata and the Euphrates 

River in Iraq (then called Mesopotamia).

The Colossi were originally a portion of the entrance to Amenhotep's funery temple, the rest of which has since disappeared. He ruled for thirtysix years and near the end of his reign, Egypt's Syrian province was invaded by the Hittites and the Khabiri. 

According to the Greek version supplied by Homer, Amenhotep is referred to as Memnon, a king of Ethiopia, son of Tithonus and Aurora. He came with 10,000 troops to assist his uncle, Priam, during the Trojan War, where he killed Antilochus, son of Nestor. 

On account of the latter's age, Memnon refused to fight Nestor but was later slain by Achilles. According to historians, the statue of Memnon had the ability to utter strange sounds at sunrise each day. At sunset these sounds became lugubrious. Strabo, the geographer who heard the mys terious notes, confessed that he could not determine whether the music proceeded from the statue itself or from the people who continually surrounded it. 

One theory attributed the sound to the action of the rising sun on the dew in the cracks of the stone. Others

Isaid that it was a trick of the p iests, one of whom hid himself in the statue and struck a metallic sounding stone. 

The chief argument in favor of the latter theory is that such a stone still exists in the lap of the statue, with a a recess cut in the block immediately behind it which is capable of concealing a person from view below. It is further stated that the sound was often repeated when any famous person (like Emperor Hadrian) was there to hear it while on ordinary occasions it occurred but once.

The statue was dismanteled at the order of Cambysis and by an earthquake in 27 B. C. Twenty-Milliemes

In the process of making cliches for reproduction a piece of plate glass is placed between the object to be reproduced and the printing surface to which it is being transferred by photography. The plate glass has a given numbe of fine, transparent lines crossing each other at right angles, to break the light into small dots, which then become visible in the printed reproduction. The number of lines within each square inch varies and depends upon the quality of paper to be used in printing. In making a newspaper reproduction there are only about 60 to 70; for rotogravure pictures, or for postage stamps the number may run up to 150 or even 2001. 

At first the stamps of this issue were made with the screen running vertically and horizontally, but it was found, by experience, that the minute vertical lines on the printing surface caused the wiping blade, which scraped excess ink from the printing surface, to sometimes catch while the revolving printing cylinder rotated. 

This catching often caused the blade to spatter printing ink on the paper

The extra cost of buying stamps in this manner will be only one miliieme per packet, and it should prove a very popular innovation, as it affords special facilities for carrying stamps in the pocket. "Alexandria, December, 1902."

The section devoted to official stamps of Egypt is unusually interesting. Few collectors realize that this country had official stamps before it had regular adhesives for ordinary postage. As early as 1865 Egyptian officials used round adhesives, commonly referred to today as Interpostal

The job of preparing a separate interpostal for every city and town in Egypt became increasingly difficult with the expansion of the country, so in 1892 the following notice was posted in various offices in Egypt: "The public is hereby informed that the special postage stamps, which, from January first, will be used exclusively for official correspondence, will be on sale at the Alexandria and

The stamp is unusual in the fact that it has no face value: it was intended that a single copy would be used to frank any one piece of official correspondencel, regardless of size or weight. Under the circumstances, postmarked pairs, strips and blocks, legitimately used, should not exist.

AIRMAILS 

Although the first airmail adhesive was issued in Egypt on March 10, 1926, scheduled flights carried mail between 

Cairo and many parts of the Near East long before that date.

Airmail service was inaugurated by the Royal Air Force as a complement to their flights between Egypt and 

Iraq. In March, 1921, a conference between the Colonial Secretary of 

State for Great Britain, the Chief of the Air Staff, the local British Army and Air Force Commanders was held, at which it was decided to develop an air route between Cairo and Bagdad.

Captain Leslie S. Potter of the Royal 

Air Force quoted the following reasons for having reached their conclusion: "A. Such an air route would afford stu dents of the R.A.F. an opportunity of doing some practical training flying. "B. This route would link communications chains existing between Europe and India, and possibly, Australia. "C. It would establish a line of rapid communication and troop transport from the extensive bases of 

Egypt to unsettled areas of Palestine, Iraq and Trans-Jordania. "D. It was to establish and develop a regular airmail route to the Near East." 

The first flight commenced in August, 1921, when a bi-weekly schedule was planned. A list of dates on which various first flights from Egypt were established is available. In each instance the rate mentioned is a sur-tax, a charge made in addition to the regular first-class letter rate. This list was published in the "Air Post Journal" for September and October, 1941.

The first planes used to carry mail out of Egypt were single-engined De 

Havilands of the 9-A type, powered with 400 h.p. Liberty motors. Various other types, such as Vimys, Vickers,

Vernon and later, Victorias, were all engaged in this first route.

The mail carried was generally landed at Cairo, though delivery was occasionally made at Alexandria, 131 miles further on. 

The Imperial Airways, Ltd., took over the operation of the Cairo-Bagdad route as soon as the airway was well established and charted. Their first plane over the line flew Dec. 31, 1926. Continual extensions and links between that route and existing, or subsequently developed routes of other lines soon connected the Egyptian airmail service with international lanes encircling the world.

SPECIAL DELIVERY 

Egypt had special delivery service for many years, but because this country's stamps were formerly made by foreign engraving firms the cost of preparing separate, distinctive adhesives for special delivery letters was considered to be unnecessary. Until the first adhesive of this type appeared, ordinary postage stamps were used and only the inscription, "Expres," written across the envelope, or indicated by a printed label, was required to procure special delivery service for the letter so sent.

In 1925, however, when the Survey Department of Egypt was assigned to the task of making its country's stamps, the addition of a special delivery stamp was made. The stamp die was made and plates were photoengraved during 1925 and the early part of 1926, but as printing regular stamps and three commemorative sets is the Arabic numeral, 20, and the 

Arabic inscription, "Millieman." "Royaume d'Egypte" is in the bottom panel. 

The first printing of these stamps was made in green ink and they appear rather hazy and indistinct.
