INTRODUCTION 

This Commentary presents an annual rating of state traffic laws to show how they compare with the rules of the road part of the Uniform Vehicle Code as of January 1, 1979. Prior ratings were based on state traffic laws as they existed in 1968, 1971, 1972, 1973, 1974, 1975, 1976, and 1977.1/ This rating covers 

1978 state traffic laws but does not include changes adopted by state legislatures in 1979. This rating also does not include changes in the Uniform Vehicle Code which were adopted by the 

National Committee on Uniform Traffic Laws and Ordinances when it met in August of 1979.

Traffic Laws Annotated2 shows how state traffic laws compare with provisions in the "Rules of the Road" Chapter of the 

Uniform Vehicle Code³/ in over 150 "Statutory Annotations."

Reading these Annotations would provide a general picture of the traffic laws in any one state. Any law that differs from a section in the Uniform Vehicle Code could also be detected by reading all the Annotations. This Commentary attempts to provide a more precise picture of the status of state traffic laws as of January 1, 1979 that will facilitate rapid identification of particular areas of a state's laws that may not be in substantial conformity with the Code, indicate areas of the statutory law where uniformity may be markedly poor on a nationwide basis, and identify states whose traffic laws may need substantial attention on a priority basis as part of contemporary efforts to improve highway safety. This document, when used with Traffic Laws Annotated, is particularly useful in determining which laws in any one state differ from provisions in the Uniform Vehicle Code (and from laws in other states). 

Uniform rules of the highest quality are important for highway safety. Substantial improvement in such uniformity, and the termination of laws that are archaic or that cannot be reasonably expected to improve the safe and efficient use of our highways, are imperative and should occur as soon as possible.4/

NUMBER OF SUBSTANTIAL DIFFERENCES 

The 13 tables in Appendix B permit rapid identification of areas where state traffic laws differ from a rule in the Uniform Vehicle Code. These differences are shown by using minus numbers. 

One of the reasons why this rating was originally published (despite its many imperfections) was to facilitate the location, consideration of and, hopefully, the elimination of such differences. All one has to do is locate the minus numbers in the 13 tables in Appendix B and consult the Annotation in Traffic Laws Annotated indicated at the top of the column. Reading the 

Annotation in TLA will indicate how the laws with a minus score differ from a provision in the Uniform Vehicle Code. If one follows this procedure for a particular state, it does not take long to determine how the traffic laws in that state differ sub stantially from the Uniform Vehicle Code.

SUMMARY OF 1978 CHANGES IN STATE TRAFFIC LAWS 

Six states (Connecticut, Indiana, Kentucky, Louisiana,

South Carolina and Utah) made significant progress toward uniform traffic laws in 1978. Kentucky eliminated five substantial differences with the Uniform Vehicle Code and Louisiana repealed two. 

Because most of these differences involved rules dealing with right of way and the meaning of traffic-control signals, these changes are very significant. 

But the most significant single improvement in traffic laws in 1979 has to be Connecticut's adoption of the rule requiring drivers to yield to pedestrians in crosswalks. Indiana, Kentucky, and Utah also improved laws specifying rights and duties for pedestrians based on the Uniform Vehicle Code provisions. 

The Uniform Vehicle Code rule limiting the use of alternately flashing red lights on school buses to situations where the bus has stopped still has not gained universal acceptance. While 

Idaho approved this rule in 1978, South Carolina adopted a law allowing the lights to be used when a school bus is stopped or preparing to stop.

As to the few states whose total scores decreased in 1978, the decline is attributable to the repeal of laws requiring all motorcyclists to wear helmets.

Though only 15 states amended their traffic laws in 1978, it was an outstanding year for good, uniform state vehicle codes because of the substantial nature of the improvements. Whoever is responsible for the recent modernization of the rules of the road in Kentucky, South Carolina and Utah deserves an expression of appreciation from all people who drive or walk on our highways. 

OBSERVATIONS 

One of the principal reasons for publication of the previous ratings was to stimulate consideration and elimination of major differences between state laws and the Code. Though many states made significant advances toward uniformity during 1969-1978, 193 substantial differences still remain. Particularly in view of the fact that almost 45% of these differences involve traffic control devices and right of way, this number is too high. All substantial differences should be eliminated on a priority basis by each state as soon as possible unless the difference can be clearly justified as improving safe and efficient movement of traffic. 

10

Another disappointment is the failure of 13 states with total scores of less than 426 to make greater progress toward uniformity.7/ 

Not only are the total scores in these 13 states low, but these states account for a large percentage of all substantial differences. Eight encouraging developments in this regard were significant changes in the laws of Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, Ohio, Oregon, Pennsylvania, South Dakota and Vermont resulting in their moving up from the last 13 states in the rankings during 1969-1978. 

Most states with low scores are located in the eastern half of the 

United States.

Legislators in states with scores of less than 426 should be informed that their laws appear to be nonuniform, obsolete and in need of modernization based upon the most recent edition of the Uniform Vehicle Code. Legislators in these states probably do not know about the condition of their traffic laws and cannot be expected to take action without knowledge of the problem. 1/
