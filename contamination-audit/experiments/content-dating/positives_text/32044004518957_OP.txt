Perhaps the worst cold I ever had was during the summer of 1912. I had spent three weeks at a hotel, where I had eaten wonderfully delicious meals of rich food (three desserts at luncheon and dinner). This cold lasted for four weeks during mid-summer, and finally I had to take treatment from a physician. This warm-weather cold was accompanied by eczema and later rheumatism. It convinced me that my cold was not due to exposure, to cold weather, or germs, but to the food I had eaten or perhaps to its failure to digest and assimilate. 3

It was not, however, until after the World War, in 1919, when I became an instructor at a boys' school with a yearly enrollment of a thousand or more, that I discovered that what caused my colds also caused them in my students. During the next ten years I had the opportunity of trying to keep my students well and free from colds. "I had a cold," was the general excuse that the absentee student gave from October to March every year. Of the young men-and there were hundreds of them, from all types of families and many nationalities-with whom I came in contact almost daily, I have not found more than half a dozen whose cases I could not diagnose and whose colds I could not relieve if they followed my advice. Even those students who had previously had three or four colds during the school year learned to keep well.

While the most fertile field for investigation was among the students of the day classes, who for the most part came directly from high schools, the night classes, made up of young men who were employed in industry, provided additional opportunity for research in order to substantiate my theories.

I do not, however, wish to convey the impression that a controlled diet-of which I will have much to say laterwill, in every instance, prevent a cold. Circumstances and conditions may exist which make other modes of treatment and medical attention desirable.

THE COMMON COLD 

It would be unfair if I did not give credit for much of my early inspiration and medical knowledge to some of the pioneers in the successful treatment and prevention of the socalled common cold. I say "so-called," because the word "cold" is in reality a misnomer. The affection is not primarily due to exposure to cold air, water, snow or ice; wet feet, an uncovered head, or a scantily-clad body. These may indirectly contribute to create an unfavorable thermal reaction, but rarely do they cause a cold. Of course, one should not expose himself long enough to become chilled, or purposely sit in a strong draft for a long time. A slight exposure to cold air has the same tonic effect as a cold bath. Persons who are inured to it can withstand exposure to cold for a long time without harmful consequences.

One of the pioneers in research pertaining to colds is Dr. 

Volney S. Cheney of Chicago, medical director of one of the large packing firms in that city. Some years ago Dr. Cheney reported to the American Public Health Association that after eleven years spent in exhaustive study of colds he is convinced that the common cold is not infectious. The organisms usually regarded as the causative factors in colds are only secondary invaders in the later stages, he declares. The primary cause he ascribed to a condition of mild acidosis brought about by too much protein in the diet, lack of exercise, and infections already present anywhere in the body. Dr. Cheney maintained that colds can be prevented and even cured by keeping an alkaline balance in the body through proper diet and small and carefully regulated doses of sodium bicarbonate or alkaline waters along with small quantities of Calcidin (calcium and iodine). I have used calcium-iodide tablets (1 gr.) with good results, according to the directions on the bottle.

Beauty Depends Upon Health 

There can be no permanent beauty without good health. 

Pure air, water, food; sunshine, exercise, and sleep, are essential to our personal well-being. These six elements, when properly combined and balanced, are all the human machine needs to keep it running. We can live only a minute or two without air; we can not live many days without water; nor many weeks without food. We must use our muscles, and we must have sleep and sunshine in order to keep the human machine running efficiently. Generally the eyes are the best indicator of good health or ill health. The person who has bright, sparkling eyes is usually in good health. Cloudy, watery eyes indicate poor health, and frequently a rheumatic condition, due to an unbalanced diet. A good, clear complexion can be had only when all the organs of the body function properly. Anyone, who has no organic ailment, born from healthy parents, can enjoy good health if he or she is willing to study and understand his or her own human machine and discover its requirements of air, water, food, exercise, sleep, and sunshine.

Perhaps the greatest harm to the human system is done at the breakfast table. Getting a good start in the morning is of the utmost importance if we wish to feel good and keep well-and nobody can do his best work unless he does feel well. For that reason I am printing a list of wholesome breakfast dishes below: 

Cream of Wheat, Shredded Wheat, oatmeal, Puffed Wheat, Puffed Rice, Rice Crispies, Corn Flakes, and other ready-to-eat breakfast foods, with thin cream and a little sugar, make palatable foods for breakfast. (Use only one at each meal, and take other foods to complete the meal.)

A slice or two of whole-wheat, oatmeal, or rye bread, a day old, or toasted crisp, and buttered, gives the teeth and gums chewing exercise and adds more to your meal. The so-called Swedish rye cracker is one of the best breakfast breads. Toasted gently to make it brittle, with butter spread on it, this may provide about one-third of the morning meal.

Those who feel the need of anything additional can eat one or two figs. (The dried-in-the-sun, California mission figs are excellent.) They should be thoroughly masticated. So eaten they are slightly laxative; more so if eaten twenty minutes before breakfast.

A glass of ordinary whole milk, warm in winter and cold in summer, is the best breakfast drink. 

If orange, grapefruit, pineapple, or tomato juice is a part of the breakfast, take only a small glass (whiskey size is plenty large enough) fifteen or twenty minutes before starting the meal. But don't take fruit juice every day, unless you are certain that it can be well assimilated. The original fruit, with its pulp in each case, is better than the commercial fruit juices. 

Importance of Drinking Water 

Every person must drink a sufficient quantity of pure water (or other fluid) every day, and at least half of it should be taken between meals. Working in a warm room, or out of doors in warm weather, will of course necessitate a larger quantity of water than is required under ordinary circumstances in a cool temperature. When the outside temperature is so high that it causes active perspiration, the human body loses not only water but important mineral salts as well. The resulting thirst is best quenched by drinking pure, cool water, but this does not replace the salt. It will require several days of ordinary mixed diet to replace the mineral salts lost during a single excessively hot day. A week of very hot weather will cause a noticeable loss of energy even in persons who are 14

ordinarily well. Losing too much mineral salt through sweating may cause a pain in the stomach.

Food must be thoroughly chewed, and not washed down with water instead. Mix saliva in with the food. John D. 

Rockefeller, now ninety-eight years old, in perfect health, says he chews each mouthful about a hundred times; he even chews liquid food. Water or fluids taken into the stomach should not be too hot. Extremely hot liquids, if continuously used, may injure the tube leading to the stomach. Ice cream and iced foods should be eaten very slowly. 

Bananas, apples, pears, peaches, oranges, or any other kind of fruit, if allowed to remain on the bush or tree until fully ripened, contain relatively little acid, and in such condition cause little or no distress if not eaten to excess. But, unfortunately, it is difficult for city inhabitants to obtain fully ripened fruit. Whenever possible, bananas should be bought "off the bunch" when the skin is a golden yellow with small brown spots. Then the inside is sweet and soft, and easy to digest. 

The old saying, “An apple a day keeps the doctor away; eat another one still and it makes you ill," suggests moderation. It is not, however, true that even one apple a day could be consumed with impunity by everyone. Few persons could eat one apple a day during the year and remain well. 

Bananas are an excellent food-but they should be eaten only when they have reached their maximum degree of ripeness. Then they are practically free from acid. They contain acid when they are under-ripe, and also when they are overripe. Bananas must be kept in a warm place—about 70 degrees 

F. or above. They will decay before they ripen if kept too cold. Any slight bruise to the skin may cause decay during the ripening process.

Preparation of Vegetables 

So far I have said nothing about the preparation of vegetables. The U. S. Bureau of Standards has conducted many investigations and tests to determine the best method of cooking vegetables. If we wish to retain the health- and energygiving mineral salts that raw vegetables contain (calcium, iron, phosphorus), they should not be peeled, but only washed and brushed, stems and imperfections removed, and placed in a covered pan. Cooking should be at temperatures from 

180 to 200 degrees F., below the boiling point, and without addition of water. A low flame must be used.

Nearly all vegetables, including potatoes, carrots, turnips, parsnips, tomatoes; apples, pears, and many other fruits, can be baked or cooked in their natural state. 

Protein foods-meat, eggs, cheese-need a low fire because protein "sets" and stays tender when cooked at low temperature, but gets tough and hard with too much heat. Fats for 16

frying foods should be allowed to become hot enough to cook the foods, but not so hot as to decompose and smoke. Indigestion

Stomach trouble and indigestion are the cause of many colds, but indigestion is not always due to the food that has been eaten; nor to over-eating or under-eating. Food is of course the primary cause; but, since nearly everyone suffers from an occasional attack of indigestion, this need not cause undue alarm. Excitement and extreme haste in eating may cause increased muscular contraction in the intestinal regions, and subsequent digestive disturbances. Irritation, worry, fear, or anger just before or while eating will often cause distress. 

It is unwise to eat when one is very tired or nervous. Eyestrain sometimes causes stomach trouble. Before, during, and after the meal, the mind and disposition of the eater should be calm and tranquil. This will remove one cause of indigestion that is frequently overlooked. 

The Engine of the Body 

(Condensed, in part, from "Fortune")

The human laboratory—where energy is manufacturedmay be thought of as a series of interconnected units. The first in the series is the eating machine, which includes the mouth, esophagus (about one and one-half feet long), and the stomach. Normally, the human engine functions about as follows: Food taken into the mouth is crushed by the teeth and gums and is saturated with saliva, and ptyalin, and the first digestive fluid called enzymes. Ptyalin does not affect proteins or fats, but concentrates its energy mainly on carbohydrates (starches and sugars). In the stomach, the second digestive fluid, pepsin, is present. Pepsin, in the hydrochloric acid secreted from the walls of the stomach, acts upon the food which does not pass rapidly through the stomach. 

From the stomach the thick mixture of food is gradually squeezed out through the neck of the stomach into the small 17

intestine. The small intestine is the main organ of digestion. 

Although about twenty-two feet long, the first ten inches are the most active. Here the food is sprayed with the third digestive substance, called the pancretic juice. This juice contains four enzymes: trypsin, lipase, diastaste, and maltase. Fats are split or dissolved by lipase and the bile from the gall-bladder. 

On the outside of the small intestine are two coats of muscular tissues, one crosswise, the other lengthwise. Every few seconds, these muscles squeeze the food inside, thus causing it to move along in the tube. After three or four hours of churning, most of the nourishment of the food-mass has been absorbed; what little remains passes into the third and last section of the alimentary canal-the large intestine or colon. 

This is only five or six feet long. It takes from twelve to sixteen or more hours for the water, unused juices, to be changed into vapor, urine, and carbohydrate fiber or roughage, and then to travel through the colon. 

If You Do Get a Cold

There are times in the life of almost any person, even though he or she is usually careful in eating and drinking, when the human machine gets out of order, and a cold may be the result. If this is the case, ask yourself the following questions: 

I. Have I eaten any pork products-bacon, ham, lard, pork chops, roast pork, sausages, frankfurts; or other meat fats? 

2. Have I eaten much fruit (raw or cooked); prunes, berries? 

3. Have I partaken more or less liberally of sugar, preserves, jellies, candy, nuts, spices, pepper, salt, mustard, vinegar, pickles, strong cheese? 

4. Have I eaten much rich cake, pies, or pastry? 

5. Have I eaten lobster (broiled, boiled or in salad with dressing), or other crustacean foods? 18

6. Have I drunk much tomato juice, orange juice, lemonade, cider, etc.? 

7. Have I eaten cucumbers, onions, or tomatoes? 

8. Have my bowels not moved freely and with regularity? 

9. Have I suffered at all from indigestion? 

If you can answer "yes" to two or three of the questions, you may be certain that the cause of your cold lies in your own answers. If any of the "suspected" foods have been put into the stomach in quantities too large to be assimilated, they are very likely the cause of your cold. You need only to go back two or three days, so it will not be difficult to ascertain which part of your meal has caused a derangement of your stomach. We must know the cause of our discomfort; the symptoms merely indicate the source. We strive to remove or treat the cause of the disease; not the symptoms. 

Variability of Constitutions 

Constitutions vary greatly. Some persons may eat almost anything with impunity. (Nearly all of us could when we were children). Not all foods affect all persons alike. One person may be able to assimilate fruits quite easily, but not pork products; another may be able to partake of candy and other sweets with comparative impunity, and yet be harmfully affected by bananas and plums. It follows, therefore, that each person must study his own constitution, and ascertain which is the best diet for him or her.

General Treatment for Colds 

See that the bowels move freely at least once a day. (Two or three times a day, after eating, is not too much.)

Drink water freely between meals, or before meals. Begin upon rising in the morning; then an hour or two after eating, and continue drinking water until an hour or two before retiring. Do not put ice water or any cold fluid into your stomach. Six glasses of water a day is not too much. 19

Do not drink coffee or tea if they cause you distress. Nearly everyone is better off without them than with them. (Grain coffees, containing less than three to five per cent. of caffein, are harmless.) 

The less you irritate your stomach the quicker you will recover from colds. 

Take a warm shower or tub bath at night before retiring the first day you notice that you have a cold. Rub your body briskly with a coarse towel. Bathe your feet in hot water every night just before retiring. Take a sponge bath every day. 

See that you get at least eight hours of undisturbed sleep in a well-ventilated room. Use enough covering to keep comfortably worm.

Humidify your sleeping and living rooms by placing wet towels over the steam or hot water radiators if there is no other way to keep the relative humidity high enough. 

Do not have the temperature above 62 degrees F. in your bedroom, and not over 74 degrees F. in your living room. Put on sufficient clothes to keep warm in this temperature. 

Go out into the sun and fresh air during the day. Keep the feet warm.

Moderate exercise is not harmful, if you feel strong enough, but do not become over-tired. Conserve your energy.

Alcoholic Beverages 

Since the repeal of the prohibition laws the drinking of beer, wines, and so-called hard liquors, has increased. Any of these will irritate the stomach when taken to excess.

Beer, ale, porter, stout, and light wines must be kept in a dark, cool place in order to prevent them from spoilingif they are in bottles. The best temperatures are from 40 to 60 degrees F. Beer that has been exposed to strong light and high temperatures will deteriorate.

Excessively sweet or sour wines disturb the digestion. 

It is advisable not to drink liquor while taking an alkaline treatment for colds. 

Specific Treatment for Local Chemical Colds

These colds, due to an irritation in the stomach, may be felt, by close observation, in from two to twelve hours after the irritation begins. The symptoms vary in different individuals. Uusually a slight burning sensation in the stomach is noticeable; there will be a strong desire to drink water; the throat may become dry; a feeling of drowsiness may overtake you a few hours after eating. 

Dissolve ¼ to ½ teaspoonful of bicarbonate of soda (baking soda) in a glass of warm (not hot) water and drink it; repeat within an hour or two. Sodium bicarbonate will create an alkaline reaction in the stomach and neutralize the unabsorbed acid. The soda should be taken when the stomach is nearly empty. 23

Later symptoms in simple head colds are a congestion of the nasal passages, with stuffiness and discharge. If there is more than normal discharge from the nostrils, continue to take the soda in small doses, and partake only of boiled milk and crackers or white-bread toast at the next meal (or several meals). Usually this treatment-when taken early enoughwill prevent a cold from developing. 

Milk of Magnesia may be used the second day instead of soda. As both soda and milk of magnesia are slightly laxative, the bowels will move quite freely after taking several doses. (Directions for taking milk of magnesia are on the bottle. Begin with small doses).

If the bowels move more than three times a day, stop taking the soda and magnesia, and take milk of bismuth. Begin with a teaspoonful, or follow the directions on the bottle. 

Milk of bismuth is sedative, anti-acid, astringent, and protective. It has a healing effect on the stomach and intestines. 

While chemical colds in themselves are not dangerous, if one's temperature rises much above normal (98.6 degrees F.) and continues so, it is advisable to consult a physician. 

Some years ago, a nationally known orthopedic surgeon established a sanitarium in which patients suffering from colds were treated and cured by a diet of milk, bread, crackers, and bicarbonate of soda, etc. The patients recovered after a few days.

Among the generally misunderstood symptoms of colds is sneezing. Many persons sneeze who haven't a trace of a cold.

Some of you older persons may recall the artificial sneezes which our grandfathers and great-grandfathers produced by applying a pinch of snuff (pulverized tobacco) to the nostrils and then inhaling it. This fine powder caused an irritation of the membrane of the nostrils, and so induced a vigorous sneeze. Similarly, particles of dust suspended in the air, and set in motion by a draft, may cause sneezing. It is the dust in the atmosphere, and not the draft, that causes the sneezing. If we sit on the deck of an ocean liner, in a draft, we do not sneeze, because the air on the water is free from dust or other irritating particles. Of course, sneezing may be due to other causes. It often occurs in well-developed colds when the nostrils have been chemically irritated and inflamed, making them very sensitive to atmospheric irritation. 

Those persons who sneeze at night, while in bed, might try this: Strip the slips off your feather pillows; take the pillows outside into the sunshine; beat and shake thoroughly; then go over and over the surface with the nozzle of a suction cleaner; hang them on the line in the sun for a day before 25

placing them back on the bed. All feather pillows have some dust in them. 

Dr. Harry S. Bernton, professor of hygiene at Georgetown University Medical School, in a talk given under the auspices of Science Service, in Washington, said in effect, while describing the paroxysms of sneezing: "When cold air strikes the skin (of an exposed body), there is an immediate loss of heat. The warmer body radiates its heat to the colder environment. One of Nature's inexorable laws is the conservation of body heat. This is effected by the contraction or shutting down of the blood vessels in the skin -a mechanism which is quite analogous to shutting off the heat from a radiator by closing the valve."

A contraction of the blood vessels causes a diminished volume of blood to flow through or to the skin, and a corresponding excess of blood finds its way partly into the mucous membrane of the nose, where are located the so-called turbinate tissues-which expand and contract, and thus act very much like a small sponge. The engorgement of the turbinate tissues causes a congestion which produces the familiar sniffing; and the consequent pressure upon the nerve-endings in the nasal mucous membrane gives rise to sneezing. This kind of sneezing occurs most frequently when the body is partly or entirely exposed to air currents.

Colds that are not checked within a few days after the nose begins to discharge will frequently develop into a cough. If the treatment so far suggested has not been followed, or if it has been unsatisfactory, there are a number of so-called expectorants that can be used: Mentholated syrups, syrup of thyme compounds, calcium iodide tablets.

There are also several antifebrin, antipyretic, and analgesic preparations for sale in drug stores. Among them are asperin, Bromo-quinine, anacin tablets, phenacetin, and caffein compounds.

If a cough should continue for several weeks, and become painful, it is best to consult a physician. 26

Dr. C. Ulysses Moore, in an article published in Hygeia (July, 1932), writing under the caption "When Food is Poison," states: "Many a person is hypersensitive to a certain food; to such an individual that food is a veritable poison. Hay fever, bronchial asthma, colds, eczema, hives, intestinal disturbances and migrane (sick headache) may have their source in some ordinary food, to the protein of which the individual organism reacts unfavorably. In medical parlance this hypersensitiveness is known as allergy."

In children these reactions usually manifest themselves in some form of intestinal disturbance and colds. 

Chemists have developed the so-called protein skin test for determining the sensitiveness of individuals to foods suspected of causing trouble. Any food may be tested by placing a drop of specially prepared food extract on a minute scratch on the skin, or through a hypodermic injection. If a large red spot appears within half an hour, the individual is abnormally allergic to that food. It is unnecessary to say that the food producing the unfavorable reaction should be eliminated from the diet, or at least taken only very sparingly. Persons who cannot discover for themselves which foods are harmful to them should go to their physicians and arrange to have the protein skin test applied. 

Some persons are allergic to wheat, milk, eggs; others are allergic to chocolate (sweet fats), potatoes, beef, oranges, bananas, apples, and strawberries. 

Temperature and Humidity

During cold weather many persons live indoors a greater part of the time than they do during warm weather. In winter our living quarters and our places of employment are artificially heated. Only relatively few factories, offices, and residences have air-conditioning or humidifying appliances. To be healthful and comfortable, the air we breathe and live in must not be too warm, too dry, nor too moist. 27

We measure temperature with a thermometer, and moisture with a hygrometer.

The Un-Common Cold 

Although this booklet treats mainly of the common cold, I should like to include his cousin, the un-common cold, because he also can be very annoying. This is the cold induced by over-indulgence in sugars and other concentrated sweets, like chocolate bonbons, etc. When these carbohydrates and fats form a large part of our diet, a fermentation very often begins in the stomach and spreads downward (and also upward). The stomach and intestines become inflamed; a great deal of intestinal gas or wind is formed; pronounced soreness is felt when the bowels are evacuated; there is frequently some pain in the region of the stomach. In mild cases, the treatment is much the same as for acidosis colds. In severe 28

cases it is advisable to consult a physician. The stomach and intestines must be healed by small doses of sodium bicarbonate, milk of magnesia, and milk of bismuth. An essentially bland and non-irritating diet must be followed for several days. Boiled milk and toast or crackers, and cream of wheat boiled in milk (without sugar) is excellent. An enema consisting of four parts warm water and one part antiseptic solution (alkaline) N.F., once a day after a bowel movement is beneficial. Allow the solution to remain in the lower intestine for a few minutes before evacuating it. The first step is, however, always the omission of sugars and sweets from the diet. 

Dr. William S. Sadler's Opinion 

In April, 1928, in the American Magazine, Dr. William S. 

Sadler, in a long article entitled When You Feel Yourself

Coming Down With a Cold, stated that colds can be classified under six or seven groups: 1. Colds with fever; 2. Colds without fever; 3. Recurrent colds; 4. Deficiency colds; 5. Irritation colds; 6. Hay-fever colds; 7. Combination colds. His article and his classification of colds are interesting and enlightening. 

We will consider here only Sadler's No. 4 cold. This he claims is an "acidosis cold caused by an over-amount of acid in the blood. To counteract it, it is only necessary to quickly restore the alkalinity or alkaline balance of the blood." This is exactly the treatment which I have followed for years and which I am advocating for others. Most colds result from calcium deficiency and a diminished alkali reserve. 

The degree of departure from the normal, balanced condition of the body largely determines the severity of the colds. 

In the ordinary local or chemical cold it will be found that the urine is about 50° to 65° acid. A fresh specimen of normal urine, when tested in the laboratory, will show about 30° of acidity. Everyone should have his urine analyzed from time to time. A thorough physical examination once in two or three years is also an excellent precaution against any approaching disorder. 29

In reporting to the American Academy for the Advancement of Science recently, Prof. William J. Kerr and Dr. John

B. Lagen of the University of California have expressed their doubt of the old theory that common colds are infectious or even of bacterial origin. "Bacteria may be a cause of secondary complications, and filterable viruses may sometimes play a primary role," the medical men admitted, but "environment factors and the general body responses to them must be considered as preparing the soil to a great degree." 

In Dr. Alexis Carrel's notable book, Man, the Unknown, this famous scientist states that the human blood is not exactly what chemists believe it to be. "Blood plasma," he says, "really is the solution of bases, acids, salts, and proteins." 

The alkalinity of the blood is maintained near the neutral point. Normally the blood is neither too acid, nor too alkaline. "Although blood plasma can accept a large quantity of acids without increasing its actual acidity, it must, nevertheless, get rid of them through the kidneys and lungs." Dr. 

Carrel believes that some diets increase the susceptibility to disease.
