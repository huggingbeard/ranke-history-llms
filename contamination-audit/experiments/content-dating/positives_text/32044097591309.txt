CONSTITUTION ARTICLE XI, SECTION 21 

(A.C.A. 23 and S.C.A. 4) 

(1) Uniform Laws Governing Claims Against Public Bodies and Public 

Employees It is of course fundamental that the State and its subdivisions cannot be sued without consent. Moreover, orderly processes, in the case of the 

State, its political subdivisions and public agencies generally, are deemed to require the presentation of various types of claims to the governing body or a designated official as a condition to the allowance and payment of the claim, or in case of dispute, as a condition of suit. Likewise, claim statutes, so-called, in some instances include the presentation of claims to be individual public officer or employee who is alleged to have been at fault, as a condition to suit.

Claims statutes (including therein for this purpose, local requirements imposed by charter or ordinance) are extremely varied, undoubtedly by reason of the fact that they have been developed piecemeal. 

The general public and particularly many who do business with the State, its political subdivisions and other agencies, including municipal corporations and districts, can hardly be expected to be familiar with the varying legal requirements. Persons having claims for injury to person or property or the taking of property frequently are unfamiliar with the necessity for timely presentation of their claims and the precise technical requirements.

Moreover, present claims statutes and charter provisions often give rise to difficulties of interpretation and application. Thus, much of the litigation which has reached the highest court of this State in the last decade has produced a difference of judicial opinion.

The purpose of the proposed constitutional amendment (A. C. A. 23 and S. C. A. 4) is to empower the Legislature to enact uniform laws for various types of claims which will supersede charter and other local provisions as well as the present divergent statutes. The Legislature would have the power to prescribe one procedure for claims based upon contract; another procedure for claims for negligence or other tort causing injury to person or property, to cite examples of possible classifications. 

Moreover, provisions could appropriately be made governing the matter of presentation of claims against individuals who may be claimed to be individually liable. Similarly, appropriate uniform provisions, if desired, could be adopted (among other matters) for (1) effect of disability or minority; (2) effect of technical defects in the form or manner of filing the claims; and (3) time when period commences to run in various situations, for example where the alleged wrong is continuous or it arises out of public improvements. 

A constitutional amendment is advocated primarily because doubts may otherwise exist under the "home rule" provisions of the Constitu tion (Article XI, Sections 6, 7, 8) as to the legal effectiveness of statutes on this subject, particularly where the procedure prescribed relates to claims not founded upon tortious acts or omissions. 2-L-4462 (5)

PROBATE CODE 360, 361 AND 362 (Bill No. S.B. 388) (2) Admission of Foreign Will to Probate Where Not Admitted to Probate Else where in the Conventional Sense

In some jurisdictions wills are not admitted to probate in the conventional sense of the provisions of the California Probate Code. This is generally true of areas where the civil law prevails or has had substantial influence. Yet the broad purpose of Probate Code Sections 360, 361 and 

362, relating to ancillary probate, clearly is to give effect to a foreign will which is being or has been acted upon as a will under the applicable procedures of the jurisdiction of the testator's domicile apart from the form of those procedures.

The purpose of Senate Bill 388, amending Probate Code 360, 361 and 362, therefore, is to provide for those situations which are embraced within this purpose but are not covered by the express terms of the ap- &plicable sections. The bill thus provides for ancillary probate where a will is "established or proved in accordance with the law" of the domicile although not "admitted to probate" in the usual sense. The present sections remain unchanged in respect to wills "admitted to probate" and the present protections of the sections are retained. It would be 

2 required that evidence of the establishment of the will elsewhere, in accord with the laws of the other jurisdiction, accompany the applieation for letters testamentary or of administration. It must also be shown that the will is valid according to the place of testator's domicile at the time of his death or according to the law of this State. These provisions, it is believed, will enable the probate court to act, as the facts warrant, when a will not admitted to probate in the conventional sense is offered for ancillary probate.

It may be noted that present wording in Section 361 of the Probate Code, referring to an "order or decree admitting the same (will) to probate," reflects a simplification in ancillary probate procedure adopted by a 1919 amendment to former Section 323 of the Code of Civil Procedure. Formerly it had been held that where the domiciliary jurisdiction had a probate procedure similar to our own, the certified copies of the petition and all proceedings leading up to admission of the will to probate had to be included. Wickersham v. Johnston, 104 Cal. 407, 414 (1894). It is now required only that a certified copy of the order and of the will be filed (following the change in wording made by 1919 Stats. p. 165 mentioned above). Estate of Sankey, 199 Cal. 391, 398 (1926); 

Estate of Mahony, 85 C. A. 489 (1927).

The appellate courts of this State do not appear to have passed upon the applicable procedure where a situation such as is covered by Senate 

Bill 388 is presented.

Collaterally, it may be noted that it now appears settled that the will of a resident of this State which has been admitted to probate elsewhere may be admitted to probate in this State under ancillary proceedings. 

However, the finding of the court of original probate as to the place of the decedent's domicile is not binding upon the courts of this State. 

Estate of Reynolds, 217 Cal. 557 (1933). Conversely, the will of a nonresident may also be admitted to original probate in this state. Estate of Estrem, 16 C. 2d 563 (1940).

PROBATE CODE 553.3 AND 1483.1 (Bill No. A.B. 1220) (3) Procedure Upon Petition for Reduction of Bond of Personal Representative or Guardian

Probate Code Section 553.3, addeď in 1939, provides a means whereby an executor or administrator may obtain a reduction in his bond on a proper showing and subject to the requirements of Section 541 as to amount of bond. As part of the application for reduction of amount, the applicant must now file an accounting, as required by Section 921.

The purpose of the amendment of Section 553.3 proposed by Assembly 

Bill 1220 is to substitute a showing as to the condition of the estate for the mandatory requirement that a formal accounting be filed. 

Similarly, as to guardianships, by Probate Code Section 1483.1, added in 1941, a guardian may now apply for a reduction in his bond, subject to the limitations of Section 1480 which prescribes the minimum amount of the guardian's bond; his application must be accompanied by an accounting as prescribed by Section 1480. 

Again, Assembly Bill 1220, by amendment of Section 1483.1 would substitute a showing as to the condition of the estate for the mandatory requirement that a formal accounting be filed. 

In the case of the personal representative and the guardian there are many instances where a proper showing can be made without the necessity of a formal accounting. In such circumstances the present requirement imposes unnecessary work, expense, and delay. 

Each section (553.3 and 1483.1) would require notice to be given in the manner required by Section 1200 of the Probate Code. 

NOTE: As introduced, Assembly Bill 1220 also gave the probate court authority to prescribe a different mode of notice or that notice be dispensed with. Such provisions were, however, subsequently deleted. 

PROBATE CODE 630, 630.5, 640, 642, 645, 646 

(Bill No. A.B. 1363) (4) Summary Probate and Payment Upon Affidavit 

It will be recalled that the Probate Code provides a procedure whereby, upon affidavit and without probate proceedings, the surviving spouse and certain designated relatives of the decedent may collect money due the decedent and receive other designated assets. (Probate Code, Section 630). In general, this procedure may be availed of where the decedent leaves no real property nor interest therein in this State, and the total value of his property in this State does not exceed $1,000. 

It is proposed by Assembly Bill 1363 to increase this amount to $2,000. 

This increase is advocated on the ground that the larger amount is more in keeping with present monetary values. The amount of $1,000 set out in Section 630 appears to date back to 1915 (see former C. C. P. 

1454; 1915 Stats. p. 532), although the procedure has been enlarged in other respects.

A further amendment to Section 630 is proposed, by referring to the persons who may take advantage of the section, in the plural as well as the singular. It is now required that the heir (of the class described) have a right to succeed to the property of the decedent, in case of intestacy or that he or she be the sole beneficiary of the will, in case of testacy. 

No reason appears why, if all interested parties join, the procedure should not be available. The amendments do not expressly cover the situation which arises when less than all heirs or will beneficiaries execute affidavits. This intermediate situation, it is believed, is often worked out in practical operation. In such a situation it would seem there is no mandatory duty on the obligor to make the payment. (See Quigley v. Nash, 1 C. 2d 502 (1934); Bruce v. Churchman, 55 C. A. (2d) 850 (1942).)

A related section (Probate Code 630.5) authorizes payment of a sum not exceeding $500 to the surviving spouse, from money of the decedent

on deposit with a bank. This section is applicable regardless of the character of the estate left by the decedent but is limited to cases where the value thereof does not exceed $5,000. 

Assembly Bill 1363 proposes increases in maximum amount as follows: 

Amount subject to withdrawal, $500 to $1,000; value of estate, $5,000 to $10,000.

Finally, Assembly Bill 1363 will increase amounts presently specified in the procedure for setting aside estates to surviving spouses and minor children upon so-called summary administration (Prob. C. 640, 646) by increasing the net value of the estate from $2,500 to $5,000; and by increasing the amount of "other estate" of the surviving spouse or minor child from $5,000 to $10,000. 

For purpose of clarification the words "subject to administration in this State" would be added in determining the estate to be set aside; and the words "wherever situated" would be added in determining the value of "other estate" mentioned above.

PROBATE CODE 740 (Bill No. A.B. 821) Of (5) Estate of Deceased Spouse-Claims for Funeral Expenses and Expenses of Last Illness 

At common law it is clear that the husband's duty of support includes the obligation to pay burial expenses and expenses of the wife's last illness. See In re Weringer's Estate, 100 Cal. 346 (1893).

It has, however, been held in certain decisions in this State that where the wife's estate is ample and the financial ability of the husband is limited the probate court in its discretion may make an allowance to the T husband for funeral expenses, even though they were his primary obligation. Estate of Mathewson, 181 Cal. 452 (1919); see In re Weringer's

Estate, supra; Brezzo v. Brangero, 51 C. A. 79 (1921). It has been indicated, though by way of dictum, that a similar discretionary power to make an allowance to the husband exists with reference to expenses of last illness. Medros v. Kohn, 68 C. A. 367, 370 (1924).

A recent decision of the District Court of Appeal has held that by reason of the wording of Sections 7700-1 of the Health and Safety Code 

I and Sections 950-1 of the Probate Code, the common law rule has been i abrogated in this State as to funeral expenses. The code sections mentioned are considered to indicate a legislative intent to shift the primary 1 liability for payment of such expenses to the wife's estate (also see 

Estate of Kemmerrer, 114 C. A. 2d 810 (1952), for general language).

It also seems that if the wife's estate consists of community property the common law rule would not apply, at least to the full extent, by reason of the provisions of Probate Code Section 202 providing that community property passing from the control of the husband by virtue of testamentary disposition by the wife is subject to his debts. See Odone v. Marzocchi, 34 Cal. 2d 431 (1949), involving a transfer inter vivos rather than a testamentary disposition by the wife. 

Proposed Section 740 is intended to incorporate the principle that the estate of a deceased spouse has primary liability for payment of funeral expenses and expenses of the last illness, so that it will no longer be neces-

sary to determine whether or not in case of the death of the wife the probate court in its discretion should make an allowance to the husband for these expenses where the financial ability of the husband is limited. 

The amended section provides: "Expenses of last illness, and reasonable funeral expenses proportionate to the value of the estate and in keeping with the standard of living of decedent during his or her lifetime, may be allowed as claims against the estate, regardless of the financial ability of the surviving spouse to pay such expenses. 

The section is stated in terms of general principles and does not provide specifically for various situations that may arise. It is to be noted that the word "may" is used. Language with reference to the amount allowable as funeral expenses, i.e., reasonable * * expenses proportionate to the value of the estate. etc., is based upon language found in Section 7101 of the Health and 

Safety Code. 

Collaterally, Revenue and Taxation Code Section 13986 provides for the deduction of funeral expenses and expenses of last illness in the wife's estate, in determination of inheritance tax, "whether or not the decedent is survived by a husband financially able to pay the expenses. 

PROBATE CODE 754.5 (Bill No. S.B. 389) (6) Sales of Property as a Unit 

"; 

Section 754.5 of the Probate Code (added in 1943 and amended in 

1945) now authorizes the executor or administrator of an estate, in his discretion, to sell certain personal property with real property as a unit and under one bid. For example, equipment in a store or furnishings in an apartment house can be sold with the real estate itself. The personal property must be property that "has been used" upon the real property.

The purpose of Senate Bill 389 is to broaden the scope of this procedure. It would be available whenever the executor or administrator determines in his discretion that by use or relationship, any assets of the estate, whether real or personal, constitute a unit for purposes of sale and the probate court finds the sale of said assets as a unit for the best interests of the estate.

The proposed revised section is intended to permit greater flexibility in administering estates and to provide a procedure for realizing upon the full value of properties when operated in a single project. 

Procedural provisions: If the assets to be sold as a unit include any real property, the sale would be required to be made in the manner provided for the sale of real property and subject to limitations appli cable to such sales. In this respect the provisions of present Section 754.5 are retained.

If the assets to be sold as a unit do not include any real property, then the sale is to be made in the manner provided for the sale of personal property.

Also under the revised procedure property could be appraised as a unit, although such form of appraisal is not essential to the permissible sale procedure.

ed. ar Ter אד Provisions for minimum selling price in the present section are retained in modified form. Thus, following the present section no private sale as a unit may be made for less than 90 percent of the sum of the appraised values of the personal property and the sum of the appraised values of the real property, appraised separately; however, provisions are added that if the property is appraised as a unit, no private sale may be made for less than 90 percent of the (single) appraised value. 

PROBATE CODE 1000, 1001, 1002, 1003 

(Bill No. S.B. 387) (7) Proceedings for Preliminary Distribution in Estates 

In probate procedure two methods of making distribution of the decedent's estate, before the estate is ready to be closed, are presently provided. At any time after four months from the issuing of letters testamentary or of administration a petition for partial distribution may be filed, and upon a proper showing and the giving of bond such partial distribution may be ordered (Prob. C. Sections 1000-1003). A petition. for ratable distribution, on the other hand, may be filed when the time for filing or presenting claims has expired and other conditions exist. (Prob. 

C. 1010-1013.) These procedures are to a degree overlapping.

The purpose of Senate Bill 387 is to eliminate these related procedures and to set up a single proceeding which, though not expressly designated as such, is to be known as a procedure for preliminary distribution. The principal changes may be summarized as follows: (1) New Probate Code Section 1000 provides that preliminary distribution shall be "upon bond" or "with or without bond, as the court determines, when the time for filing or presenting claims has expired" and other showing has been made such as the payment or securing of all uncontested claims. (Also see new Section 1001.) These provisions are patterned after the procedure under present Section 1001, relating to partial distribution, rather than Sections 1010-1012, relating to ratable distribution which do not provide for a bond. The amount of the bond (or bonds) to be received would be designated by the court and the bond procedure is similar to that now found in present Section 1001. (2) The earliest period within which an application for "preliminary distribution" may be made is designated as four months after first publication of notice to creditors. This compares generally with the period of four months after letters testamentary or letters of administration have been issued, under the present partial distribution procedure. (See new 

Section 1000; present Section 1000.) (3) Present Probate Code Section 1002, relating to procedure in the probate court for payment of money under a bond given by a distributee on partial distribution (under present practice) would be repealed. (4) Provisions now found in Section 1013 relating to ratable distribution and the costs of the proceeding are adopted, but in modified form. 

Thus, under new Section 1002 if the executor or administrator makes the application, the cost of the proceeding is to be paid by the estate, except when a partition is necessary. If, however, the executor or administrator is not the applicant the costs must be paid by the applicant, or if more than one, must be apportioned among them. (See new Section 1002.) 

Compare present Section 1002 providing that costs of proceedings for a

partial distribution must be paid by the applicant, or if more than one, must be apportioned between them. 

It is to be noted that under new Section 1000 it is provided that the petition may be for payment "on account of legacies." 

The provisions of the new article are intended to be a new procedure and, while retaining certain provisions of existing law, are not intended to be a mere restatement thereof. For this reason the changes noted above are not intended as all inclusive. In general effect the proposal is intended to provide for a more flexible procedure, to simplify the procedure law and to vest the probate court with authority to make such orders as may be appropriate in a particular case. 

Reference to the new proceeding of "preliminary distribution" is to be added to Probate Code Section 1200 (see A. B. 1407 as amended). 

Provisions of Probate Code Section 1240 relating to appeals are believed adequate in present form and no amendment thereof is suggested at this time.

PROBATE CODE 1080 (Bill No. S.B. 385) (8) Right of Personal Representative to Bring Proceeding for Determination of 

Heirship

Senate Bill 385 extends to an administrator or executor the right to bring a proceeding for determination of heirship under Section 1080 of the Probate Code. 

Frequently it is desirable, for inheritance tax and other purposes, that the personal representative be in a position to have determined, before distribution of the estate, what property in an estate is separate or community or the proportions in which an estate is to be distributed. Other questions may involve the construction or validity of will provisions. 

Section 1080, it is believed, provides a suitable procedure whereby these matters, among others, may be determined in advance of distribution upon petition of the personal representative. Section 1080 provides for notice to known heirs, beneficiaries of the will and others interested. 

Upon occasions, a petition for instructions under Probate Code 588 has been filed by a personal representative, in order to obtain determination of matters of the type outlined. The effect of such procedure is not clearly recognized by case law. Two appellate court decisions have held such proceedings not controlling on issues of heirship. Estate of Thramm, 67 C.A. 2d 657 (1945); Estate of Clippinger, 75 C.A. 2d 426 (1946); cf. 

Estate of Buck, 32 C. 2d 372 (1948).

The personal representative in his petition under Probate Code 1080 as proposed would show the "reasons" for the filing of the petition by him, as he of course has no "claim to the estate or part thereof" within the meaning of Section 1080. 

It may be noted that under present law the executor or administrator does not appear to be more than a nominal "party" in proceedings to determine heirship, as he occupies a neutral position. It has been held that an executor cannot by answer set up any interest of his own under 

Probate Code 1080. Estate of Lynn, 109 C. A. 2d 468 (1952). Similarly, expenses of contesting heirship claims on the merits have been disallowed as an expense of administration. See Estate of Friedman, 176

וד 

Cal. 226 (1917); Estate of Lynn, supra at p. 473. Also under the present at law, it has been declared that the personal representative appears to have no standing to appeal. See Estate of Lynn, supra at p. 474. 

PROBATE CODE 1124, 1125, 1125.1, 1126, 1127 

(Bill No. S.B. 466) (9) Testamentary Trusts-Vacancy in Trusteeship-Bonds-Notice Procedure Senate Bill 466 amends Sections 1124-1127 of the Probate Code relat ing to testamentary trusts in various respects and represents a substantial revision in the respects covered. These are: (1) express authorization giving the probate court authority to act in the appointment of a successor trustee when the will contains provisions therefor; (2) revised provisions for notice of an application for the appointment of a so-called successor trustee whether before or after distribution of the estate in probate; (3) provisions with respect of bonds of original and successor trustees. In addition, a minor change is made in Section 1124 relating to notice of declination by a trustee. 

(1) Authority of Probate Court: Successor Trustees. Present Probate 

Code sections do not provide for the situation where a decedent by his will gives directions as to or makes designations for succession in the trusteeship. Frequently, such matters are provided for at a time remote from the time when the vacancy occurs.

It is not clear from case law to date whether, in the instance of an original trustee named in a will, the probate court may refuse distribution, except upon proof of disqualification. It has been said in one case, upon appellate review, that the court has large discretion in such matters and that the real question is not whether the court would have been justified in refusing to make the appointment but whether a disqualification so clearly appears that it must be said that the court abused its discretion. (Estate of Brown, 22 C. A. (2d) 480 (1937), at p. 485.) Certain language in a comparatively recent decision supports the view that the court has discretion to refuse to recognize the testator's designation of a testamentary trustee (Estate of Barter, 30 C. 2d 549 (1947), at p. 

557.). The purpose of Senate Bill 466 is not to deal with that situation, but to establish expressly the probate court's power to declare a vacancy, even though there are will provisions on the subject. Compare the wording of general section, Civil Code 2287, as to the power of the superior court to fill the vacancy when the "declaration of trust does not provide a practical method of appointment" and to indicated preferences. 

In any proceeding under Probate Code 1125 or 1126, the probate court would be required to follow the testator's directions unless it finds "for good cause" that the best interests of the estate require a different appointment or order of appointment. 

(2)- Notice Provisions. Notice procedure now varies according to whether appointment of a successor trustee is sought before or after distribution by the probate court. In the former case, moreover, present requirements have been criticized as uncertain. Section 1125 requires notice to all parties interested in the trust, "to be given as required upon the probate of a will." Senate Bill 466 proposes a uniform notice procedure based upon information to be set out in the petition. This would consist of the names and addresses of all living beneficiaries and of all

trustees named in the will or appointed who have not declined to act or resigned, so far as the same are known to the petitioner. Notice would be given by mail and as required by Section 1200. As noted under Item 

11, infra, it is proposed by another State Bar bill (Assembly Bill 1407) to amend provisions of Section 1200 to include as an application covered thereby any petition for the appointment of a trustee, deleting the words "after distribution.' (3) Bond Provisions. It would be provided that if a person is named in a will, as original trustee or as successor trustee, no bond shall be required "unless specified in the will or, for good cause, prescribed by the court." The power of the court to require a bond is generally but not precisely comparable to provisions contained in Probate Code Section 543, relating to bonds of executors. 

Provisions would also be added permitting any trustee under a testamentary trust to have his bond reduced in the manner provided by Section 541.1 of the Probate Code pertaining to the deposit of funds or securities in a bank or trust company subject to court control. 

Declination of trustee: Probate Code Section 1124 would be made expressly applicable to any person appointed as trustee by the court as well as to a trustee named in the will. Additionally, the copy of the written declination could be mailed either to the personal representative "or his attorney." Present provisions requiring mailing to the personal representative at his place of residence if known, otherwise to him at the county seat would be deleted.

NOTE: Senate Bill 466 in amended form should be consulted, particularly as to "successor trustee" language. 

PROBATE CODE 1132, 1133, 1134, 1135, 1136 

(Bill No. S.B. 386) 

(10) Procedure for Transfer of California Trust Assets to Domiciliary Trustee

Frequently property is held in trust in California under a testamentary trust created by the will of a nonresident decedent. In such cases both the California trustee and the trustee in the state of domicile administer the trust created by the will. It is not unusual that trust assets in California in such cases are not large enough to warrant, economically, continuation of the trust in this State, but the consolidation of the assets with those in the hands of the domiciliary trustee would provide a corpus subject to more effective management. Or, the corpus in California may be so small that, in the case of an individual trustee, surety bond premiums and other trust charges may eventually consume the principal. 

The authority of the probate court in this State to authorize the transfer of assets of such a testamentary trust to the domiciliary trustee in another state is not provided for in the Probate Code. The purpose of 

Senate Bill 386, adding proposed Probate Code Sections 1132-1135, therefore, is to provide a procedure to effect such a transfer where, upon a hearing, it appears that it would be wise so to do, having consideration for the economical and convenient administration of the trust and the interests of the beneficiaries.

The substantial rights of residents of this State, moreover, must be considered, and the court, if any, having jurisdiction of the domiciliary

In any event, the local assets must be capable of transfer and be of a value not in excess of $7,500. 

(11) Probate-Notices PROBATE CODE 1200 (Bill No. A.B. 1407) 

Section 1200 of the Probate Code provides for the setting of matters for hearing and for the notice required to be given in the case of certain types of petitions, accounts and reports. It also applies where no other method of bringing the matter on for hearing and for giving notice thereof is provided by law or by the court. 

Assembly Bill 1407 (in amended form) would change Section 1200 as follows:

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 437 (Bill No. A.B. 1279) (14) Extension of Motion for Summary Judgment Procedure 

In 1927 California, following the lead of England and a number of American jurisdictions first provided for a summary judgment, though it was limited to the municipal court and to certain actions (C. C. P. 

831 (d) as amended, 1927). In 1933 the remedy was extended to like actions in the superior courts (C. C. P. 437 (c) as added, 1933); in 1937 the remedy was made available in Class A justices' courts. In 1951 the remedy was again limited to superior and municipal courts upon the adoption of the inferior court reorganization (Sec. 437c, as amended, 1951). For a more detailed history see McCabe, Summary Judgment (1938) 11 So. Cal. L. Rev. 436, 438.

The purpose of Assembly Bill No. 1279, amending Section 437c of the Code of Civil Procedure, is to extend the summary judgment procedure to any civil action or proceeding in superior and municipal courts; at present it may be used, generally, only where the action is upon a debt or liquidated demand; to recover an unliquidated sum of money on a contract, express or implied; for specific performance of a written contract for the purchase of property; and for an accounting under a written contract or for the recovery of possession of specific property.

There are many types of actions and proceedings in which the motion is not permissible under the statute. For example, suits for injunctive relief, declaratory judgment actions, stockholders' derivative suits, mandamus or certiorari to review the order of a quasi-judicial body, and the many types of special proceedings permitted by our codes. 

Chief Justice Arthur T. Vanderbilt of New Jersey in the volume "Minimum Standards of Judicial Administration," 1949, has this to say about summary judgment procedure (p. 219): "The summary judgment procedure was developed to save time, effort, and expense, since through its use a final judgment might be entered immediately on a showing that there were no issues of fact requiring a formal trial. Although the early provisions for summary judgment demonstrated the utility of this device, its applicability was originally limited to a few actions only. Gradually the scope for its use was extended to an increasing number of designated actions, but it was not until the adoption of Rule 56 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure that summary judgment procedure was anywhere made available without restriction to particular actions. 

Twenty-eight of the jurisdictions of the United States provide for some form of summary judgment procedure. In seven of the states in contrast to the liberal federal rule, use of the procedure is severely limited: Alabama, Arkansas, Kentucky, Missouri, Oklahoma, Texas, and Washington. In three states the motion is limited to actions on

contracts: Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and West Virginia. Eight een jurisdictions have a more liberal form of summary judgment procedure. In seven of these, use of the procedure is limited either to certain parties or to certain actions: California, Connecticut, Illinois, Iowa, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Virginia. A liberal rule patterned usually on the federal rule prevails in the remaining 11 jurisdictions: Arizona, Colorado, Delaware, District of Columbia, Maryland, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North Dakota, Ver mont, and Wisconsin."

The theory of the motion is that where the affidavit of the plaintiff and the countering affidavit of the defendant show that there is no triable issue of fact, and the case may be decided as a matter of law, then, and then only, may the motion be granted. Appellate court decisions in Cali fornia appear to give careful interpretation to present Section 437c, so as to require a trial on the merits if there are any facts presented by affidavits which give rise to a triable issue of fact. The court is not to decide the case on conflicting affidavits or to determine the facts from them (Coyne v. Krempels, 36 Cal. 2d 257 (1950); Arnold v. Hibernia 

S. & L. Soc., 23 Cal. (2d) 741 (1944); Eagle Oil & Ref. Co. v. Prentice, 19 Cal. (2d) 553 (1942); Walsh v. Walsh, 18 Cal (2d) 439 (1941); see comment (1948) 36 Cal. L. Rev. 465, 469 and (1940) 13 So. Cal. L. Rev. 523).

For comments upon the federal practice particularly, including a warning as to the over-use of summary judgment dispositions, see Yank wich, Summary Judgment Under Federal Practice, 40 Cal. L. Rev. (June, 1952) 204-224. Additionally, adoption of Assembly Bill No. 1279 will eliminate present areas of uncertainty which result from the fact that a "speaking" motion to dismiss has been held proper and been granted in some instances, without express statutory provision therefor 

See Cunha v. Anglo California Bank, 34 C. A. 2d 383 (1939). In federa! practice, it is provided by F. R. C. P. 12(b) that such a motion when supported by affidavit is to be treated as a motion for summary judg ment.

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 601 (Bill No. S.B. 521) (16) Peremptory Challenge of Jurors 

66 It is now provided by Section 601 of the Code of Civil Procedure that each side' is entitled to six peremptory challenges to jurors and that if no peremptory challenges are taken until the panel is full, they must be taken by the sides alternately, commencing with the plaintiff. 

The wording as to "alternate taking" of such challenges has been in Section 601 almost since its adoption in 1872. However, the case law to date does not appear to be decisive on the effect of "passing" the jury as then constituted. Such a passing might in theory be held to preclude the exercise of a peremptory challenge to any person who was on the jury at the time it was passed. Such a view appears inconsistent with 

Silcox v. Lang, 78 C. 118 (1889), which recognizes that the "passing" is only a passing of the jury "as then constituted."

While case law may be found declaring that the alternative order of Section 601 and Penal Code 1088 (in similar but not identical language) is to be followed (for example, Vance v. Richardson, 110 Cal. 414, 416 (1895); Jackson v. Superior Court, 10 Cal. 2d 350, 358 (1937)), other cases have indicated the matter is not vital; and that it is within the discretion of the trial court (Vallejo, etc., R. R. Co. v. Reed Orchard Co., 169 Cal. 545, 559 (1915); Jones v. Southern Pacific Co., 74 C. A. 10, 37 (1925)) or that no prejudice results where the party (or side) was in fact only allowed to exercise the number of peremptory challenges allowed by statute (Switzler v. A. T. & S. Fe Ry., 104 C. A. 138, 151 (1930)).

No case has come to attention in which the appellate courts have adopted the rule, followed in some other jurisdictions, that the "passing" of the jury is the equivalent of the exercise of a challenge, on the theory

that the alleged waiver of the challenge exhausts the challenge. See for example, State v. Sitts, 26 N.W. (2d) 187 (S. Dak.-1947). The Attorney General of California in 10 Ops. Att'y Gen'l, p. 79 (1947) expressed the opinion that such a "charge" rule is not the law in this State under 

Penal Code Section 1088. 

As a result of the uncertainty in the law, some divergence of practice has grown up. It is stated that even in the same court the rule to be followed may depend upon the views of the particular judge, some following the "charge" rule and others following the view based upon the reasoning of Silcox v. Lang that the mere passing of a jury as then constituted is not a waiver of the right to challenge peremptorily. 

Senate Bill 521 amends Code of Civil Procedure 601, to resolve the conflict which now apparently exists by adopting the practice understood to be followed in the great majority of courts; that is, of allowing the jury to be temporarily "passed" without the loss of a challenge.

While peremptory challenges may, in some instances, be saved and exercised at a late stage in the jury selection process under the amendment proposed, neither side may exercise any more peremptory challenges than the other. Moreover, two factors may act as a practical deterrent : First, a side passing its peremptory challenge runs the risk that the opposite side will then pass. In such case, the jury is then sworn (subject to the right of the court, for good cause, to order otherwise). Second, the accumulation of peremptory challenges for later use is usually not considered tactically sound.

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 647 (Bill No. S.B. 391) (17) Requirement of Exceptions in Civil Matters 

The purpose of Senate Bill 391 is to remedy a situation arising from the present wording of Section 647 of the Code of Civil Procedure. That section, which prescribes the instances in which an exception must be taken after a trial court's ruling in civil cases, has been amended in substance only once since 1909. As to certain classes of rulings and orders, generally described, and as to other rulings and orders, particularly listed, it is provided that an exception is automatically deemed to have been taken. But these provisions have been characterized as arbitrary and a snare for the unwary. (See Grossblatt v. Wright, 108 C. A. 2d 475, 480 (1951)). Thus, if the rather complex provisions of the section are not kept in mind, the record in the trial court may later be found to be such that a claimed error of substantial nature is not subject to review on an appeal.

Recent decisions have held, for example, that a trial court's ruling upon the claimed right to a trial by jury or by the court is not reviewable, in the absence of an exception made following the ruling. Allin v. International, etc., Stage Employees, 113 C. A. 2d 135 (1952); Snidow v. Hill, 100 C. A. 2d 31, 36 (1950); Glogau v. Hagan, 107 C. A., 2d 313 (1951); 

Boone v. Hall, 100 C. A. 2d 738, 741 (1950); also see earlier decision of 

Supreme Court in Estate of Magerl, 201 Cal. 162 (1927). Many other orders and rulings are made in the course of civil litigation that fall into like category of rulings not deemed excepted to. On the other hand, such rulings as an order allowing or refusing to allow an amendment to a

pleading (as distinguished from an order on the filing of a supplemental pleading) and an order refusing a continuance (as distinguished from an order granting a continuance) are now deemed excepted to by the provi- 

Isions of Section 647. 

The purpose of Senate Bill 391, amending Section 647, is to preserve the automatic exception rule as now set out in that section, with respect to orders and rulings therein described; and to extend it, by general provision, to “all other orders, rulings, actions or decisions." These = latter, however, would be deemed excepted to only "if the party, at the time when the order, ruling, action or decision is sought or made, or within a reasonable time thereafter, makes known his position thereon, by objection or otherwise." Thus, in cases to which the amendment would be applicable and for the purpose of meeting the "exception" requirement, it is sufficient that the party litigant make known his position, by objection or otherwise, at one of the times designated. In such cases, he is therefore required to act affirmatively in the matter or issue before the court.

It may be noted that Rule 46 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure states a somewhat similar principle to that now proposed but in different terms and with somewhat different effect. It is provided by Rule 46 that formal exceptions are unnecessary but that "for all purposes for which an exception has heretofore been necessary," it is sufficient that a party make known to the court, at the time the ruling or order is made or sought, the action which he desires the court to take or his objection to the action of the courts and his grounds therefor. It is further provided that if a party has no opportunity to object to a ruling or order at the time it is made, the absence of an objection does not thereafter prejudice him. In the old federal court practice, however, no provision of the breadth of Section 647, expressing "deemed" or automatic exceptions, was found. Moreover, the present federal practice in respect to "objections," and to "motion" procedure, is not identical with that in our State courts. Adoption of the federal rule, verbatim or in some modified form, it is believed, would give rise to many questions and lead to uncertainties. It accordingly has not been followed. 

The time for making known a party's position, in cases within the amendment, is taken from the federal rule, with the additional provision that the position may be made known "within a reasonable time thereafter," i.e., after the order, ruling, action or decision. This is intended to eliminate technical questions of timeliness. What is a "reasonable time thereafter" must necessarily depend upon the circumstances of each situation and the status of the proceedings.

The principal but not sole reason for Assembly Bill 1260 is to resolve a situation which occurs in counties where superior court trial calendars are congested, in connection with appeals from justice courts in civil actions. Under the present wording of Section 981a of the Code of Civil

It is believed that the authority of the court, under rules or inherent power, to deal with appeals from justice courts on questions of law alone, is not affected.

When time limits depend upon the motion itself, it is usually necessary to show that the motion has been orally made to the court (Estate of 

Corcofingas, 24 C. 2d 517 (1944)) unless particular circumstances are sufficient to establish substantial compliance. (Socol v. King, 34 C. 2d 

292 (1949)). But it is often difficult to determine whether the oral motion has been made or the requirements have been substantially complied with. The minutes of the court do not always reflect the proceedings in detail. A motion may be continued for an entirely proper reason and the minutes will so show; counsel may fail at the same time to make the oral motion to the court or to have the minutes show it. In smaller counties, absence of the regular judge on assignment may preclude the oral motion unless an outside judge is sitting or is specially assigned. In earlier cases under Code of Civil Procedure 473 the device of an ex parte oral motion or order to show cause was resorted to and sustained, for the purpose of complying with the jurisdictional time limits. While that method appears no longer permissible, it is indicative of the problems.

To avoid jurisdiction being dependent upon the making and proof of an oral motion, and in the interest of uniformity in practice, Assembly Bill 1510 (amended) would add a new general section (C. C. P. 1005.5). 

The proposed section would provide in substance, that a motion upon all the grounds stated in the written notice is deemed to have been made and to be pending before the court, upon its due service and filing. 

It is intended by the section that the notice of motion, under such conditions, shall be sufficient to comply with applicable time limitations, even though the matter is not brought to the court's attention until later.

The section, it is believed, is in harmony with the various sections now having specific provisions to similar effect. However, to avoid questions of interpretation, and to make clear that the new trial procedure is governed by its own provisions (which include requirements for the filing of affidavits and counter-affidavits within a limited time), such proceedings would be expressly excluded from the operation of the proposed general section.

It is not intended that a hearing on the motion is to be dispensed with by reason of the section.

It is to be noted that no language has been incorporated expressly excluding application of the proposed section to motions normally made orally, where no notice is required; for example, motions during trial. 

Placement and contents of the proposed section, it is believed, sufficiently preclude the possibility of extension to that type of motion. 

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 1881(2) (Bill No. A.B. 572) (24) Evidence-Attorney-Client Privilege 

The statutory provisions defining the attorney-client privilege (C. C. P. 

1881(2)) are general in terms and have not been amended since 1893. In two recent decisions of importance, the section has been applied and the privilege sustained. Thus, in City and County of San Francisco v. Superior Court, 37 C. 2d 227, 236 (1951), it was held that a physician who examined an injured party at the request of and as agent for the person's attorney, to aid the attorney in preparation for a proposed law suit could not be examined by the adverse party to the litigation, where a physician did not testify. Also see In re Ochse, 38 C. 2d 230 (1951), holding C. C. P. 

1881 (2) applicable where the attorney for the accused in a criminal case sought an examination of his client by a psychiatrist. 

It is also to be noted that since the adoption of the Federal Rules and the decision in Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U. S. 495 (1947), there has been much litigation in the federal courts on the question of examination of papers, reports, statements and other documentary material in the hands of the adverse party or his attorney. It has long been the rule in England that reports prepared for the attorney are privileged, as, of course, is the attorney's own work product. See 4 Moore's Federal Practice, 2 Ed. 10901098. Various state courts in decisions prior to the impact of the Federal 

Rules refused inspection or requests for production in various types of situations either on the ground of privilege or because the material was not admissible in evidence.

The purpose of Assembly Bill 572 is to amend C. C. P. 1881 (2) in favor of the privilege and generally to adopt the views taken in England on the attorney-client privilege; and to prescribe a definite rule by statute which will enable clients and their attorneys, as well as their aides, to know what is privileged and what is not.

Three amendments are proposed to subsection (2) of Section 1881. (1) The class of persons who may not be examined without the client's consent is enlarged from the attorney's secretary, stenographer or clerk to "any other person employed or engaged by such attorney in connection with the client's business." The limitation, however, would be confined to knowledge which has been acquired in such employment or engagement.

(2) The problem of the presence of third persons at conferences between client and attorney would be covered. Frequently, it is pointed out, the client will bring with him some member of his family or business friend or advisor to be present and take part in a conference between the attorney and client. The presence of such third person, even though present in a confidential capacity, might technically destroy the privileged character of the conference. Thus, a person such as an accountant or engineer is frequently called upon to be present at business conferences of a confidential nature between the attorney and client. (3) An attorney not only prepares documents himself but in order to make a proper appraisal of a matter or to prepare for trial obtains reports such as investigator's reports, appraiser's reports, medical and scientific reports, as well as witness' statements. The "working papers" of an attorney including but not by way of limitation, documents such as those mentioned would be privileged.

However, the form of the amendments and the extent of the privilege so proposed are to receive further study. 

NOTE: In Holm v. Superior Court-114 A. C. A. 889 (1952)-hearing granted by 

Supreme Court-it was held that an employee's accident report, and a witness statement and photographs taken by investigators employed by a city later turned over to the attorney for the city for use in litigation were subject to inspection under C. C. P. 1000.

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 2084, 2084.1, 2084.2, 2085, 2086, 2090 (Bill No. S.B. 832) (25) Proceedings for Perpetuation of Testimony 

Senate Bill 832 makes various changes in the procedure now provided by Chapter 2, Title 6, Part 4 of the Code of Civil Procedure (Sections 

2083-2089) relating to the perpetuation of testimony.

Section 2084 (amended) and Section 2084.1 (new). Apart from the division of the subject matter by section renumbering, principal changes are brought about by first, addition of provisions expressly providing for situations where the adverse party expectant resides out of State; second, inclusion of provisions for a deposition upon oral interrogatories when taken out of the State (see amended Section 2086); and third, expansion of procedural provisions on notice and court orders. 

Notice of the deposition is to be personally served where the adverse party expectant resides within the State and taking is upon oral interrogatories. In cases where the deposition is to be upon written interrogatories and the adverse party expectant is a resident of this State, notice of settlement of written interrogatories is to be personally served. In the latter case, provision is made for a further notice. In the order settling written interrogatories the judge is to prescribe the notice of time and place for the taking of such deposition and the manner of giving such notice.

When, however, an adverse party expectant resides out of the State, then the pertinent original notice is to be given by publication in the manner and for the time provided by law for summons, subject to the proviso that the judge may shorten the period of publication for good cause shown.

When the expectant adverse parties are unknown, the pertinent original notices are by publication, as in the case of the nonresident "party" but with a slight variation in language, as the requirement for mailing copies prescribed by the general law (C. C. P. Sec. 413) would not apply. 

If in this situation interrogation is to be by written questions, the judge has power to dispense with further notice if no one other than the applicant has appeared. and It is to be noted that this procedure is more explicit than that now provided and covers additional situations. Section 2084 now requires personal service of the notice where the parties expectant are known and reside in this State; if unknown, service is required to be made by publication for the same period required for the publication of a summons, in addition the notice is now required to be served on the county clerk where the property to be affected by the evidence is situated or the judge making the order resides. The latter provisions for service on the county clerk are deleted. However, Section 2084.1 retains present provisions that the judge's order shall designate the clerk of the county to whom the deposition must be returned.

Section 2084.2 (new). This section confers authority upon the judge signing the order allowing the examination to impose such reasonable conditions in addition to those prescribed in the chapter "as in his discretion will provide reasonable protection to such party expectant or witness." The wording is prefaced by the language: "In order to protect the party expectant and any witness from abuse of process." The power to order a deposition to be sealed subject to further order of the court is expressly included.

The purpose of these provisions is to vest in the trial judge control of the court's process and the proceeding. The words "abuse of process" are included to indicate that the provisions are not to be used to deny the right conferred by statute. Such provisions, it is believed, are generally analogous to the power of the court to control depositions, including those to perpetuate testimony, given by Federal Rules of Civil Procedure Section 30 (b) and (d). The federal rules, however, are not intended to be adopted as such. 

Section 2084.2 is stated in general terms. Its application in our state court practice, it is believed, must be left to judicial decision upon the various factual situations that arise. However, one application (by way of illustration only) may arise where the adverse party expectant is a nonresident.

It is to be noted that where a subpena is not requested, present law requires no more than the showing prescribed by present Section 2084 for the proceeding to be maintained. See Kutner-Goldstein Co. v. Superior 

Court, 212 Cal. 341 (1931); Tone v. Superior Court, 111 C. A. 2d 110 (1952); MacLeod v. Superior Court, 115 A. C. A. 217 (1953). 

It appears clear that while under existing law there is a duty on the trial court to enforce the statutory right to a deposition and to compel a witness to testify (Brown v. Superior Court, 34 Cal. 2d 559 (1949),) some degree of control has been exercised. For example, it has been held that a question asked is subject to objection on legal grounds (Brown v. 

Superior Court, supra, at page 566); and that questions and documents relating to issues which cannot be litigated are properly removed from the proceeding. (Malone v. Superior Court, 40 A.C, 548 (1953)). While

these decisions do not go to the matter of "reasonable conditions'' they indicate that the statutory right of examination is not an absolute right of the applicant. (Cf. Brown v. Superior Court, supra, at page 566concurring opinion; Hays v. Superior Court, 16 Cal. 2d 260 (1940) at page 268 dissenting opinion. Also see Palomar Refining Co. v. Prentice, 57 C.A. 2d 954 (1943)—change of place of taking deposition within county.)

Section 2086 (amended). This section is revised to permit a deposition to be taken upon oral interrogatories out of this State. It is to be noted that under Civil Code Procedure 2025, added in 1903, this type of deposition may be taken in the case of a pending action. It is believed that, upon a showing of hardship, the trial judge would have power, under proposed Section 2084.2 to impose conditions upon the taking of the deposition outside of the State, so that the oral deposition procedure on such cases would not operate oppressively. 

Section 2090 (new)--Subpena duces tecum. This new section deals. 

with the procedure, and the showing required to be made, for the issuance of a subpena duces tecum in a perpetuation proceeding. Its purpose is to adopt the general principles announced in Kutner-Goldstein Co. v. 

Superior Court, 212 Cal. 341 (1931) at page 346. (Compare Strauss v. 

Superior Court, 36 C. 2d 396 (1950).) The wording largely follows, but with important variations, Civil Code Procedure Section 1985. 

It is required that a showing for the issuance of the subpena duces tecum be made to the court. The court is to issue the order for the subpena, in contrast with the procedure of filing an affidavit (C.C.P. 1985) and obtaining a subpena from the clerk (C.C.P. 1986). Thus, the court at the outset scrutinizes the showing. It is intended, however, that a motion for an order quashing a subpena duces tecum may be made in appropriate cases (see procedure in Strauss v. Superior Court, 36 C. 2d 

396 (1950)). Court scrutiny at the outset may tend to avoid delay and the necessity of such a motion.

It is to be recognized that a perpetuation proceeding may be used as a pre-suit discovery proceeding. It has been held, for example, that disclosure of insurance coverage may be obtained by this method. Superior Insurance Co. v. Superior Court, 37 C. 2d 749 (1951)-majority opinion; Demaree v. Superior Court, 10 C. 2d 99 (1937). It is not necessary, under present case law, that the petition allege a cause of action. 

Brown v. Superior Court, 34 C. 2d 559 (1949); Malone v. Superior Court, 40 A.C. 548 (1953); also see Note: Petition to Perpetuate Testimony as a Pre-suit Discovery Device, 3 Stanford Law Review 530-536 (1951). 

Therefore, the showing to be made for issuance of subpena duces tecum must necessarily be less precise than that embodied in Civil Code Procedure 1985, 1986 as to materiality, description and control. 

Proposed Section 2090, therefore, requires the petition to allege the nature of the anticipated "action or matter"; and that the books, documents or other things desired to be produced "will be material to the issues expected to be involved." Also, the word "exact" contained in 

Civil Code Procedure 1985 and describing the documents, etc., has been eliminated. 

It is also to be recognized that the applicant may not at the outset have sufficient information to make the showing required for the subpena duces tecum under proposed Section 2090. Frequently, however, informa-

tion is developed by oral examination (see, for example, factual situation in Strauss v. Superior Court, supra, where a second subpena was issued). 

Proposed Section 2090 therefore provides that the showing in question may be made by "verified supplemental petition" as well as upon original petition. The proposed procedure is intended to eliminate the "dragnet" or omnibus subpena criticized in the dissenting opinion in Strauss v. Superior Court, supra, and to substitute a sufficiently flexible procedure to insure the production of documentary evidence in this type of proceeding.

CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE 2105 (Bill No. S.B. 464) (26) Admissions-Requests for, in Civil Actions and Proceedings Senate Bill 464 would add Section 2105 to the Code of Civil Pro cedure. In brief, it provides for addition of a procedure whereby a party to a civil action or proceeding may serve upon another party a written request for the admission by the latter of (1) the genuineness of relevant documents or (2) the truth of any relevant matters of fact set forth in the request. The purpose of the procedure is to shorten trials and to save the expense and time attendant upon proof of matters which may be disposed of in advance. In many instances formal requirements of proof may be dispensed with, although that is not the sole purpose of the section. 

A similar procedure exists in the federal courts under Rules 36 (a), 36 (b), and 37 (c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure. It is understood that the federal practice has worked satisfactorily and for this reason, it is contended, the proposal would be a desirable addition to state court practice.

If the party from whom the admission is sought does not reply as required, each of the matters of which an admission is requested is deemed admitted. If he objects he may (1) deny specifically under oath the matters of which an admission is requested or give the reasons in detail why he cannot truthfully admit or deny them, or (2) he may "object" to the request on the ground that some or all of the requested admissions are privileged or irrelevant or that the request is otherwise improper; in the event of such "objection" a prompt hearing by the court must be requested by him.

It is further provided that an admission under this procedure is for the pending action or proceeding and may not be used against the party in any other action or proceeding.

If the party requests admissions of matters which are denied and nevertheless on the trial proves the genuineness of the document in question or the truth of the controverted fact, he may apply to the court for an order requiring the other party to reimburse him for his reasonable expenses incurred in making such proof, including reasonable attorney's fees. Unlike the federal practice, an application for such an award must be made in the same action or proceeding. If the court finds that there were no good reasons for the denial and the admissions sought were of substantial importance, it is to make an allowance of costs and attorney's fees. In the federal practice, the party denying the request appears to have the burden of showing that there were good reasons

for the denial or that the admissions sought were of no substantial importance, to avoid the award for expenses of proof. This, it is believed, places an undue burden or hazard on the denying party. 

It is to be noted that present Sections 447, 448, and 449 of the Code of Civil Procedure provide for the admission, under certain circumstances, of the genuineness of a written instrument when a copy thereof is contained in or attached to the complaint or answer. These sections, however, apply only when the action is brought, or the defense is founded upon the written instrument. They therefore have limited scope and application, in comparison with the procedure now proposed. 

CIVIL CODE 137.3 (Bill No. A.B. 2438) (27) Procedure for Allowing Attorneys' Fees and Costs in Domestic Relations Matters 

The purpose of Assembly Bill 2438, amending Civil Code 137.3, is to provide in more detail for the procedure when applications are made for allowance for attorneys' fees and costs in domestic relations matters and, specifically, to empower the court to make appropriate orders without being restricted to a fixed rule as to the time when the application must be made. Under the present rule declared by case law the court may not make an allowance for past services when application is not made until they have fully performed. See Warner v. Warner, 105 C. A. 

2d 763 (1951). The rule has also been broadly stated in general terms as prohibiting an allowance for past services. See Warner v. Warner, 34

C. 2d 838 (1950). As a result, unless a stipulation is entered into or there is some other special order the court is compelled to speculate in advance in making the allowance. See Warner v. Warner, 34 C. 2d 838 (1950).

The amendments proposed authorize the court to make allowance for past services and costs theretofore incurred and permit the court to augment or modify the original award. Compare on orders for additional allowance, Rose v. Rose, 109 C. 544 (1895); DeVall v. DeVall, 102 C. A. 2d 53 (1951); and on stipulation for award to be determined by, or at the time of the judgment, Farrer v. Farrer, 41 C. A. 452 (1919); 

Brockmiller v. Brockmiller, 57 C. A. 2d 623 (1943). It is to be noted that the word "modify" is included. See Warner v. Warner, 34 C. 2d 838, 841. 

It would be necessary for the pleading to request the relief, i.e., pray for an allowance for attorney's fees and costs; application by order to show cause or motion would also be required, although as indicated such motion or order to show cause would not be required to precede the services or incurring of costs. It is to be noted also that the section distinguishes between applications for allowances before entry of judgment in the trial court and after such time. Separate treatment is given in order that the matter of services and costs prior to entry of judgment may be determined by the judgment itself (or prior thereto), and will not be continued, or be raised for the first time, thereafter. 

In respect to services rendered after the entry of judgment by order to show cause or motion the court may make an allowance and thereafter

upon application augment or modify any award so made. In context, the requirement for relief in the pleading does not appear to apply with equal force to applications subsequent to judgment (particularly as to later enforcement proceedings).

Assembly Bill 2438 does not change the basis for allowances, namely, amounts "reasonably necessary" to maintain or defend the action or proceeding.

The amendments will eliminate the necessity for speculation in advance, or the necessity of following the present practice of stipulating that the amounts be fixed at the time of trial. Sometimes necessary steps are not taken or are overlooked and the court must then deny awards on what amount to purely technical grounds. It is believed that the new procedure will tend to decrease the volume of orders to show cause which is now substantial and which require considerable time of the courts, parties and counsel.

The bill makes the same procedure applicable to situations in annulment cases where allowances are now authorized by Section 87 of the same code.

CORPORATIONS CODE 3004 (Bill No. A.B. 538) (34) Corporations-Director's Right of Inspection 

Assembly Bill 538 relates to a situation which occurs when a director desires to inform himself of matters pertaining to the affairs of the corporation of which he is a director (or of a subsidiary corporation). 

It is provided in part by Corporations Code Section 3004 that a director "shall have the absolute right at any reasonable time to inspect all books, records, documents of any kind, and the physical property of the corporation." Unlike the right of inspection of records conferred upon a shareholder by Section 3003 of the Corporations Code, Section 3004 does not expressly specify that the director may exercise his right through an agent nor that he may make extracts of matters so inspected. 

The purpose of Assembly Bill 538 is to remove the distinction which now exists in the statutory law in these respects and to recognize that the director's right to assistance is at least equal to that of a shareholder. 

The words "such inspection by a director may be made in person or by agent or attorney, and the right of inspection includes the right to make extracts" would be added to Section 3004, conforming to corresponding language in Section 3003.

Of these two sections the late Professor Henry W. Ballantine, draftsman of the General Corporation Law (1931), states (Ballantine and 

Sterling, California Corporation Laws, 1949, p. 332): "Section 3004 does not expressly declare the incidental directors' right to the aid of agents or attorneys in making an inspection or the right to make extracts from the records which Section 3003 expressly gives to a shareholder. Such right is, however, generally given to directors at common law in making their examination of the records in order to exercise their privilege and perform their duty effectively. "In an impolitic decision in Dandini v. Superior Court (38 C. A. 

2d 32 (1940)) the District Court of Appeal interpreted former Sections 355 and 356 of the Civil Code together and drew an inference that Section 356 impliedly denied directors the right to employ agents or attorneys by reason of the failure to make an express grant such as that in the section relating to inspection by a shareholder. The court did intimate that a director could summon assistance if necessary in order to enjoy the right given him, if refused by the corporation, by a special court order. In the Dandini case the director was a woman. She appeared at the office of the corporation accompanied by an attorney and an accountant and the corporate officers refused the attorney and accountant access to the books. This refusal was upheld by the District Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court denied a rehearing. This unfortunate misinterpretation denying a director the incidental rights expressly afforded to a shareholder needs to be corrected by legislation. "As the Delaware court points out in the case already cited (Note 

-citing State v. Mo.-Kan. Pipeline Co. (Del.) 36 Atl. (2d) ----) 

the law casts a duty upon directors to acquire knowledge of corporate affairs and a director may have occasion to examine the books by himself or by an attorney or public accountant to discover

dishonesty and mismanagement. There is no question of any delegation of authority or duty but merely of employment of competent assistance by one qualified to make the examination. It seems clear that the legislature intended no such restrictive effect in enacting Civil Code Section 356 as the courts have read into it. There should be no artificial requirement that the director as a fiduciary should personally conduct the examination. The propriety of his selection of agents and assistants should, however, be subject to judicial control, as in case of shareholders if any valid objection to the representatives selected by him is shown. "It is probable that the right of a shareholder to inspect the property of a corporation and to check up its cash or securities with its books can be asserted only in connection with a court investigation. The right of a director both as to inspection of the records and also as to examination of the corporate property is superior to that of a shareholder." 

Also see comment in 28 California Law Review 636 (1940).

The amendment proposed by Assembly Bill 538 will change the rule. of the Dandini case cited.

The state tax authorities have apparently felt constrained to rule that such "grandchildren by adop tion, so to speak, are "class D transferees. "The latter class includes persons not related. As a result, it has been stated by example, when a bequest of $5,000 is left to each of four grandchildren, the grandchild who has an adopted status is subject to a state inheritance tax while the natural grandchildren pay no tax by reason of their exemption under the present law. This result has been criticized as unfair and discriminatory under modern conditions where adoptions are common and where efforts are made to treat adopted and natural children alike and without distinction. 

Assembly Bill 1472 would add a new subdivision (e) to Section 13307, containing provisions which would place in "class A" "any transferee whose relationship to the transferor is that of a person adopted in conformity with the laws of this State by any lineal issue or child mentioned in this section, provided such person was under the age of 21 years at the time of such adoption." A corresponding change is proposed in Section 15110, defining a "class A donee," for the purpose of the California Gift Tax Act.

VEHICLE CODE 332.01 (Bill No. A.B. 2437) (37) Vehicle Code-Manner of Giving Notice The purpose of Assembly Bill 2437, adding Vehicle Code Section 

332.01, is to require that all notices of the revocation or suspension of driving privileges or the refusal of a driver's license or permit to drive by the Department of Motor Vehicles, be sent by registered mail with return receipt requested or be personally served to the person to be notified.

Present provisions of the Vehicle Code (Section 10) permit notice to be given by ordinary mail and provide that the giving of notice by mail is complete upon expiration of four days after the deposit of the notice in the United States mail.

Situations have arisen under Section 332 of the Vehicle Code, defining the comparatively serious offense of driving a motor vehicle when the operator's or chauffeur's license has been revoked or suspended or when a license has been refused. It is an element of such offense under the 1949 amendment (1949 Stats. Ch. 273) that the act be done with "knowledge” of the revocation, etc. In an opinion by the Attorney General (15 Õps. 

Att'y. Gen. 255), it was ruled that the mailing of a notice by the Department of Motor Vehicles or an employee thereof under Section 10 was sufficient to satisfy the requirement of "knowledge" in a prosecution for violation of Section 332, whether or not it was shown that the person charged had actually received the notice. 

Assembly Bill 2437 would provide more adequate likelihood that notice has been received as to the important actions of the department mentioned and among other things, preclude the possibility of a severe penalty being imposed without adequate proof of knowledge under the interpretation of Section 332 apparently presently tenable.

It would be provided that a returned receipt purporting to be signed by the addressee creates a presumption that the notice was received. 

WELFARE AND INSTITUTIONS CODE 721 (Bill No. A.B. 2263) (38) Proceeding to Declare Child Free From Parental Control 

Section 721 of the Welfare and Institutions Code presently provides for the filing of petitions thereunder in cases arising under either Section 700 or Section 701 of the same code. The basis for petitions under these two sections is widely different. A petition under Section 700 of the Welfare and Institutions Code is one to have a child declared a ward of the Juvenile Court. On the other hand, Section 701 sets out the grounds on which an action to declare child free from parental "control' may be brought. These include what are commonly known as an abandonment proceeding, the usual case being where a parent has failed to support or communicate with a child for over one year. The petition under Section 701 is a proceeding to establish the legal status of a child with respect to a parent who is already out of the picture. It is not an action which envisages any care, custody or control by the Juvenile Court, as such, and is more analogous to other private litigation affecting the status of a child, such as a divorce action, annulment or paternity action. It is the custom in most counties for such petitions, after they are filed, to be investigated by the Probation Department before judicial action by the court.

Assembly Bill 2263 would amend Section 721, to limit the cases in which the probation officer is required to make an investigation before the petition may be filed and to approve the same (unless the juvenile court orders otherwise), to petitions under Section 700. 

It has been stated that as the law now stands, a proposed proceeding under Section 701 must be explained to the satisfaction of the probation officer and then it is again investigated and considered by the judge before final action. This, it is contended, not only entails duplication, but it has the effect of practically creating a probation officer as a judicial officer, to determine whether or not the petition may be filed. 

BUSINESS AND PROFESSIONS CODE 6060 (Bills Nos. A.B. 1276 and S.B. 651) (39) Requirements for Admission to Practice Law 

Among other requirements for admission to the practice of law are the following: (1) an applicant shall have completed at least two years of college work or have reached the age of 25, before beginning the study of law (B. & P. Code 6060 (e)); and (2) such applicant shall have graduated from an accredited law school, requiring full or part time study for three or four years, respectively or "have studied law diligently and in good faith for at least four years." (B. & P. Code 6060 (g).) In addition, preliminary examinations may be required of students in nonaccredited law schools. (B. & P. Code 6060 (i).) 

Assembly Bill 1276 and Senate Bill 651 (companion bills) in original form provided (1) that persons over the age of 25 years who had not completed at least two years of college before commencing the study of

law should have in point of intellectual competency the equivalent of two years of college, and (2) for the ultimate requirement of graduation from a "full time" or "part time" law school accredited by the Committee of Bar Examiners, such requirement to be effective after 

January 1, 1960.

Subsequently the proposed measure, after hearings, was amended, particularly in respect to the requirements for law study and for exemption from the preliminary examination. As amended, the measure would not make graduation from an accredited law school a requisite, various specific law study requirements being incorporated instead. All law students except those studying in an accredited law school and who had completed at least two years of college work prior to matriculating therein would be required to take a first-year examination given by the Committee of Bar Examiners at the end of their first year of law study. 

However, as the subject matter is detailed, reference should be made to the amended bill (A. B. 1276).

BUSINESS AND PROFESSIONS CODE 6101 (Bill No. A.B. 1280) (40) Plea of Nolo Contendere as a "Conviction" Under the State Bar Act 

Under Section 6101 of the Business and Professions Code it is provided that the "conviction" of a felony or misdemeanor, involving moral turpitude, constitutes a cause for the disbarment of an attorney. 

Section 6101 in present form provides in part that a plea or verdict of guilty is deemed to be a conviction within the meaning of the section. 

It has been held that the section covers convictions in the federal courts, as well as in the state courts. In some situations, an attorney accused of a crime may enter a plea of nolo contendere, which plea is recognized in the federal courts.

It is the purpose of Assembly Bill 1280 to include the plea of nolo contendere along with the plea or verdict of guilty, as a "conviction" 

within the meaning of Section 6101. 

The amendment does not change the requirement that the offense must be one involving moral turpitude.

BUSINESS AND PROFESSIONS CODE 6140 AND 6141 (Bills Nos. A.B. 1276 and S.B. 651) (41) State Bar Membership Fees 

Assembly Bill 1276 and Senate Bill 651 (companion measures) would amend Business and Professions Code Sections 6140 and 6141, to in crease the statutory maximum of annual active and inactive membership fees of the State Bar (from $15 to $25 for active members; and from $2 to $5 for inactive members).
