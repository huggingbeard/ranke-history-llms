FOREWORD 

This memorandum is the first in a series of six detailed reports to the State Board of Control. 

These memoranda are intended for the internal use of the board in arriving at the policy decisions necessary to implant the administrative reorganization of the important functions for which it is responsible. This volume is concerned with three topics which are basic to the overall operation of the board and the administrative machinery under it; namely:

General Administration Organization Personnel Administration 

Each of these topics is presented as a separate part in this memorandum. 

Part I, General Administration, is concerned with the historical development of the department, a review of its overall statutory authority and responsibility and an examination of the department's relations with using agencies and vendors.

Part III, Personnel Administration, analyzes such problems as recruiting, promotional opportunity, working conditions, termination policies and other aspects of personnel administration. It is more detailed than 

Parts I and II because at present the department has no personnel technicians, and those employees with part time personnel functions have indicated a desire to have a detailed program presented to them.

SUMMARY 

The basic non-operational problems which confront the State Board of Control in its day-to-day functioning are reflected in this memorandum. 

These problems are so fundamental that their existence has negatively influenced all aspects of the department's operation. Most of them are directly traceable to out-of-date and inadequate laws, poorly defined or non-existent policies, or lack of fully trained and experienced departmental staff. It will be necessary for the department to solve these basic problems systematically in order to lay a sound management foundation for the improvement of the purchasing and building engineering functions. 

Some of the most important problem areas are: 

In the area of general administration: 

Existing laws do not reflect a clear definition of the departmental authority and responsibility. This makes it difficult for the department to determine its ultimate objectives and develop the necessary policies and procedures to achieve those ends. 

The department has not developed modern systems and procedures, and it lacks an aggressive program aimed at stimulating using agency cooperation. 

The department has not developed and published specific, up-to-date, and consistent information on the policies and procedures to be followed in doing business with the state and what can be expected in the way of administrative action by the department and/or the board. 

In the realm of organization: 

The present departmental organizational pattern as reflected in the present appropriation bill and in existing statutes is not conducive to the most effective organization of the department.

vi Some functions are over staffed and some are understaffed. 

Most of the departmental divisions operate with a staffing pattern which is not commensurate with the most efficient performance of their legal responsibilities. 

Departmental executives do not have responsible subordinates to whom functions can be delegated. In addition no secondary supervisory employees in important areas has resulted in inadequate audits of performance. - Because of the lack of competent executive staff, the board has not exercised the delegation of administrative authority to its staff permitted by law. 

In personnel administration: 

The board has issued few formal personnel policies, and these have never been consolidated and brought up-to-date in a personnel manual.

There are few established personnel practices, and there are no departmental personnel primarily concerned with this function. Consequently such basic practices as recruitment, application screening, promotional opportunities, compensation programs, time policies, grievance and terminal policies, retirement programs, and personnel records all disclose a lack of sound management. - There is no systematic program for the discovery and elimination of personnel problems or the development of employees. 

In-service training is not formalized and depends largely on the attitude of the division chief. - Lines of authority and responsibility have never been clearly defined and such basic personnel administration tools as job descriptions, an employee rating system, compensation plans, etc., do not exist.

Obviously any department which directly influences the expenditure of over $90,000,000 a year of tax monies and which has jurisdiction over key programs which affect the operation of all state agencies cannot operate effectively with problems such as those listed above. To modernize the operation of the State Board of Control so that it may provide maximum service to all state agencies for the tax dollars

vii expended, the following recommendations are made:

PART I GENERAL ADMINISTRATION

GENERAL ADMINISTRATION HISTORICAL BACKGROUND: The State Board of Control was created by an act of the 36th Legislature in 1919 and was formally organized in 1920. 

The act consolidated 21 independent agencies with widely diverse governmental service functions of which the two most important were purchasing and management of institutions. It also provided that all laws applicable and relating to the governing of the asylums, institutions and departments would be administered by the State Board of Control. For its time this act could be described as a sweeping legislative move to decrease the number of separate agencies and effect a coordination of effort on the part of those with similar functions. 

The board itself was to be composed of three citizens of the state, appointed by the Governor with the approval of the Senate for six-year overlapping terms. The Governor was given the authority to remove any board member for "good cause." The board was considered a full-time administrative board.

By 1933, when the Griffenhagen Study was completed, the activities of the Board of Control fell into three distinct and largely unrelated fields: financial administration including budgeting and purchasing; public welfare including all eleemosynary institutions and child welfare functions; and management of public buildings and grounds. The Griffenhagen Study concluded that:

The Board has attempted to handle many types of technical problems including purchasing, construction, budgeting, auditing and institutional management, but in the selection of its staff 1

it has not recognized the importance of technical qualifications ... The Board as an administrative agency is slow and indecisive. It is necessary to delay decisions on all important matters pending meetings of the Board. Different members of the Board often gave conflicting instructions to employees. 

There is little planning for the future, since the Board seldom finds it possible to reach an agreement on any question until a 1 decision can no longer be delayed .

An examination of the minutes of the meetings of the State Board of Control clearly confirms the findings of the Griffenhagen staff. Usually each of the three board members accepted responsibility for a functional area of operation and administered it independently of the other two board Employees in the department developed loyalty, not to the demembers. partment, but to individual board members. 

Frequent prolonged disagreement between board members plus the lack of formal administrative policies and procedures further increased administrative problems. Most problems brought before the board were decided without regard to established policy. In many instances, this gave rise to conflicting judgments and contradictory decisions. 

Well qualified and dedicated persons appointed to the board often found themselves handicapped in their efforts to strengthen the department administratively and develop formal policies. The chief obstacles to such efforts were: 1 

Responsibility of the department for widely diverse and exceedingly complicated functions. - Absence of clear delineation of authority and responsibility in the basic laws establishing the department. 

Griffenhagen and Associates, The Government of the State of Texas, Part II Administration; the Fiscal Agencies, January 10, 1933, pp. 34-39.

Lack of trained and experienced technicians and administrators in key positions. - The political nature of the appointment itself and the frequent changes in the composition of the board. - The hostility or apathy of one or both of the other board members.

Moreover, the department itself was beset with problems. Since it lacked cohesive executive direction, other agencies of Texas government became confused as to its role. The name itself was misleading since many of the functions for which the department was responsible were service rather than control functions. Vendors were never certain of how to do business with the state since policies were informal and subject to unlimited administrative discretion. The Legislature became increasingly dissatisfied with the operation of the department and gradually transferred key functions to other governmental jurisdictions. Between 1933 and 1953 the administration of all the eleemosynary institutions, all child welfare activities, all budget functions and some service functions (such as a central mail service) were taken from the department's jurisdiction. 

Legislative dissatisfaction reached a peak in the 1953 session and resulted in a revision of the statutes changing the full-time administrative board into a three member part-time board with a full-time executive director serving as department head. This was a major step forward in modernizing the organization of the department. By this time, and continuing to the present, the major functions of the department fell into three categories with "service" being the ultimate goal of each. These categories are:

Central administrative services Purchasing

Building engineering and building management In addition, the department continues to perform several miscellaneous functions, many of which are no longer significant or germane to its primary responsibilities. they are minor.

RELATIONS WITH OTHER STATE AGENCIES 

There has been a gradual improvement in the attitude of state agencies toward the State Board of Control since the departmental reorganization of 1953. This can be credited in part to the series of board-sponsored meetings of purchasing agents of using agencies during 1954 and 1955, to the publishing of a purchasing manual, and to the establishment of executive continuity in the office of executive director.

Despite these improvements, there is still considerable lack of understanding of both the role of the State Board of Control and the actual quality of its performances. Some of this misunderstanding is directly traceable to confusing and inadequate laws, and some to the lack of well defined, published departmental policy. Most, however, is generated by the using agencies' inadequate performance in those aspects of the purchasing or building engineering function for which they are responsible. Many of the operational problems for which the State Board of Control is blamed result from failures by using agencies, some of which have displayed a tendency to "pass the buck" - blaming the Board of Control.

WITH VENDORS One of the continuous problems of the State Board of Control is that of winning and keeping the confidence of Texas vendors. 

As a necessary

part of this study, the attitude of vendors who do business with the state, as well as some who do not, was explored. In general these vendors are dissatisfied with the operation of the department. Some of their dissatisfaction stems from the inadequate performance of functions for which the department is directly responsible, but a substantial portion of their discontent is traceable to certain obsolete administrative practices of other state agencies. Those vendors who sold to the state regularly could pin point the area of default and the agency responsible. Those who dealt with the state infrequently had more difficulty in locating the source of the problems and tended to blame the 

State Board of Control. 

In the areas for which the State Board of Control is responsible the most frequently cited problems are: - Lack of specific, up-to-date, and consistent information on what procedures and policies must be followed to do business with the state, and what can be expected in the way of administrative action by the department and/or the board. - Lack of trained and experienced technicians to perform adequately those departmental functions requiring technical judgment and discretion.

In the area of other state agency responsibility the most frequently cited problems are: 

Constant flow of small, repetitive orders which makes doing business with the state uneconomic. - Slow payment; sometimes more than 60 days after the items. are delivered. Inability on the part of agency personnel to predict needs. accurately, provide logical specifications, or handle the pre- or post-purchasing functions efficiently.

These problem areas give rise to the thought on the part of many vendors that the State Board of Control is maladministered and inefficient, and in some extreme cases the honesty of the departmental staff is questioned. These thoughts are not new. In fact, as far as can be ascertained, to a greater or lesser extent the department has been held responsible for these shortcomings for many years. Consequently many highly competent vendors do not bid for state business. In some areas of state purchasing, such as the purchase of printing, the number of vendors bidding on contracts is just a fraction of what it should be in a state of this size.

Fortunately, in the last two years there has been some improvement in the attitude of vendors toward the department. The board itself has been conscientiously trying to improve the situation. However, much remains to be done.

The key problem which needs the immediate attention of the board is that of developing and publishing specific, up-to-date, and consistent information on the policies and procedures to be followed in doing business with the state, and what can be expected in the way of administrative action by the department and/or board. To bring this about the board must demonstrate consistency as well as fairness in all its decisions.

PART II ORGANIZATION

ORGANIZATION HISTORICAL DEVELOPMENT 

For the first 20 years of the State Board of Control's operation internal organization was fluid, depending largely upon the personality of the individual board members and their length of tenure. Supervision of important functions such as purchasing was decentralized, with each of the board members directly responsible for certain operational phases. 

Executive direction was diffused among the three board members with the result that employees developed loyalty to individual board members and not to the department as a whole. As far as can be ascertained, the internal organization of the department prior to 1953 was never formalized except as it was reflected in the appropriation bills or statutes. 

The reorganization of the board in 1953 centralized executive direction in one position with the three member board transformed into a part-time policy making body. This, however, is only the first step in the reorganization of the department. The following major problems remain to be solved in order to permit the most efficient organization: 

The present departmental organization pattern as reflected in the appropriation bill and other statutes is not conducive to the most effective organization of the department. For example: 

Overly specific statutory qualifications for departmental executive personnel limit the number of applicants for key positions and could cause competent persons to be excluded.

Some of the position titles contained in these laws are not descriptive of the true function of the positions and in some instances are obsolete. 13

Some of the present divisions, as established by law, should be abolished or consolidated with other divisions. 

Some functions are overstaffed, and some are understaffed. Most of the divisions operate with a staffing pattern which is not commensurate with the most efficient performance of their legal responsibilities. For example: 

More emphasis has been placed on low salaried clerical and stenographic personnel than on obtaining qualified executives. Despite the fact that this department is responsible for the efficient expending of over $90,000,000 in tax monies annually, there is only one position in the department with annual compensation over $7,200. 

Departmental executives do not have responsible subordinates to whom functions can be delegated. In addition, a lack of secondary supervisory employees in important areas has resulted in inadequate audits of performance. 

Key executives are so concerned with daily work routine that they have not developed and formalized policy, suggested more efficient programs, developed data which allow a close. audit of operations, or properly trained new employees in their divisions. - Because of the lack of competent executive staff, the board has not exercised the delegation of authority to its staff permitted by law. Much board activity concerns routine matters below the policy level. Moreover, some of the executive staff are hesitant to make decisions, referring all administrative problems to the board for final decision. This, of course, is a direct outgrowth of the system which existed prior to 1953. To date the board has not encouraged or forced these employees to make routine decisions on their own.

The solutions to these major organizational problems involve a combination of statutory changes, new and better defined policies, and better trained and experienced personnel. An initial step in solving these problems is a reorganization of the department by grouping functional areas of operation logically and pin pointing individual authority and re sponsibility.

At best any job requirement is arbitrary and must be tempered as the duties of the position change. It should, however, be understood that there are two distinct types of specifications development. The first covers equipment and specialties purchased on a non-repetitive basis which do not lend themselves to standard specifications: for example, special types of medical equipment or automobiles, etc. Specifications for these items are sometimes written in by the purchaser and are based on his experience and technical knowledge plus any necessary adaptations to conform to the user's requirements. Other times, the purchaser may choose to use an acceptable brands list. 

The second level of specifications development covers those items which lend themselves to standard specifications and are used on a recurring basis such as laundry and janitorial supplies, office equipment and supplies, fuel oil, gasoline, tires, etc. To be usable these specifications must reflect a knowledge of the needs of the using agency, product use, product research, market analysis and perhaps testing by a laboratory. 

Obviously, the development of specifications of this type requires time, considerable technical knowledge above that of just purchasing, and the accumulation of many different types of knowledge. While the purchaser can assist in advising on particular aspects of this type of specifications development, the time and technical knowledge required is greater than that available to any individual purchaser. 

It should be constantly borne in mind that the chief function of a purchaser is to purchase. Good usable specifications are an important tool in purchasing. To expect a purchaser to perform his chief function and also develop this purchasing tool is not sound. If the development

of all specifications is left entirely up to the individual purchasers, the result would of necessity neither be sound nor developed rapidly enough for effective use.

PART III PERSONNEL ADMINISTRATION

PERSONNEL ADMINISTRATION 

Among major state agencies the Board of Control may be considered comparatively small in terms of staff. There are approximately 175 full-time and ten part-time employees. Nevertheless, a wide range of occupations demanding diversified skills and experience are represented in this department.

No position is designated for full-time personnel management and employee relations. However, the chief clerk spends part of his time in personnel functions and informally is called the personnel manager. 

His secretary assists in screening applicants and maintains central personnel files for the department; actually, however, little of her time is spent in personnel activities. 

LEGISLATIVE PERSONNEL POLICY 

Basic personnel policies established by the Legislature for all state agencies include the following: - Working hours: All Board of Control employees are officially on a 40 hour, 5 day work week. The offices are kept open 

Saturday mornings by a skeleton force which is allowed compensatory time off during the week.

BOARD PERSONNEL POLICY 

Within this statutory framework the board has authority to establish personnel policies and procedures for the department. It has exercised this authority in particular situations, and the action has been recorded in board minutes. However, the board has issued very few general policies affecting departmental personnel administration. General policy decisions are forgotten or become out-of-date inasmuch as the department does not maintain, revise periodically and distribute a personnel manual. 

The board did approve a personnel manual in June, 1952, which was mainly a compilation of state laws pertaining to state employees. It did not include all previous personnel policies, and all board personnel policy passed after the manual was issued have not been added. Thus, many of the directives are either out-of-cate or no longer followed by department employees.

Since personnel policies have never been consolidated and published, the present board has not had an opportunity to review policy now in force and, with the help of its employees, institute necessary revisions and additions. EMPLOYEE PRACTICES 

To date, few formal personnel practices have been established by the board or developed and implemented by the department. Officially, the chief clerk and his secretary are considered the personnel section for the department, but their primary responsibilities leave little time to devote to personnel work.

CLASSIFY EMPLOYEE POSITIONS: 

Most departments both add and drop responsibilities from time to time, as well as redefine programs which they administer. Such fluctuation in departmental operation demands a mechanism more flexible and thorough than legislative line item job titles to keep jobs stabilized. Accurate and up-to-date job descriptions which indicate clearly both the duties and the line of command within the department or division, should be written and utilized. 

Without the benefit of such descriptions the Board of Control now has

The stenographic positions were created for advancement of typists instead of establishing several levels of advancement within the typist classification.

In still other positions, employees who are performing essentially different functions have all been grouped under one job title. 

The job title of junior claims auditor may be found in three different Divisions, Printing, Contract and Claims. 

Although basically the work performed is similar, daily routine, subject matter, working conditions, responsibilities and scope of activities vary considerably between the divisions. Yet, there has been no attempt to vary status and pay of the personnel involved. 

A third problem fostered by the lack of job descriptions and job

classification concerns uneven workloads and lack of coordination in functions noted in certain areas of the department. This problem seems to exist particularly in the Purchasing Division. - Purchasers or buyers have been arbitrarily assigned purchasing areas as their responsibility. These areas have not been measured by formal methods to determine the scope of duties and responsibilities which must be assumed in each one of them. Thus, it is impossible to measure and in some instances determine inequity in workload distribution among the purchasers. - In view of this situation, the status and salary structure under which the purchasers operate cannot be supported as realistic. Of the 11 purchasers below the level of chief purchaser, one has separate division chief status at a salary of $5,760, one has assistant chief status in the Purchasing Division at $5,500, one is a purchaser at $5,500 and nine are purchasers at $4,740. Not one of these salaries can be supported adequately, as high, low, or about right, in terms of the position's relative value in the department. 

Although many of the assigned purchasing areas overlap in terms of related purchases, there is indication that purchasers do not coordinate their functions with other purchasers but operate their own areas on an isolated basis. Several reasons may be given for such lack of coordination. However, a primary reason stems from the lack of adequate definition of duties and responsibilities of the individual purchasers and the purchasers as a group.

Such problems as apply to the purchasers may also be extended to buyer secretaries who are assigned to the various purchasers. This is apparently true because the workload of the secretaries is directly related to the demands of the purchasers to whom they are assigned. 

Variations in secretarial workload also seem to relate to the ability of the secretaries to assume duties involved in the purchasing areas of their purchasers. Yet all secretaries have equal, static salaries and status. - Finally, variation in assignment of secretarial assistance to the purchasers is not based on formalized methods of workload assignment. No written justification is available

for assignment of one secretary and half the working time of another to certain purchasers, one secretary to others and a secretary half-time to the remaining purchasers.

In such situations as those described, workload variation may well relate to employee initiative and drive and at the same time not relate to status or salaries paid. In turn, this can lead to reduction of drive and initiative among the employees, qualities vital to the success of any organizational performance. 

Finally, absence of job description and classification produces an organizational problem in that key job positions are not properly related to each other in terms of value and importance to the department. 

RECOMMENDATION NO. 26: 

It is recommended that description of the duties, responsibilities and requirements of each position in the Board of Control be determined and incorporated in written job manual for departmental use. 

Cooperation and assistance of all employees will be necessary for the proposed chief of Administrative Services, his staff, and the proposed operations committee to accomplish this first phase of employee development. Appendix A shows a typical job description. It is suggested this format be used to create descriptions for all jobs in the department. 

Such descriptions should be used in adequate analysis of the jobs and considerable discussion with individual employees and their immediate supervisors.

Once these descriptions have been completed and approved, they should be reviewed periodically and revised to fit the changing needs of the department. It is probable that such revision will occasionally

require reclassification of certain jobs in the classification system the board chooses to adopt. This periodic revision would help eliminate the present problem of unstable positions cited earlier in this section. 

RECOMMENDATION NO. 27: 

It is recommended that the job descriptions become the basis for evaluating the positions in the department and establishing the relative value of each position.

An example of a factor-comparison evaluation system in chart form has been given to the department for its consideration. 

It represents one of many possible mechanisms for setting up an evaluation system. Although some of the factors listed on the chart may not be applicable, the system itself can be tailored to the department's needs. The Operations 

Committee should serve as a personnel committee by assisting in establishing the proper factors and their relative point values. Each job should then be measured in terms of the factors and assigned the point values. 

The grade of the job would be determined by the total points of the job. 

It is recognized that the department does not have the trained personnel staff or available advisory personnel assistance from an outside state agency to guide and help implement this technical phase of personnel administration. However, departmental need for job evaluation already exists. The board and the Legislature should give serious consideration to the problem and review possible means for remedying the situation. 

RECRUIT, PLACE AND ESTABLISH EMPLOYEES: Although job description and classification should be used when hiring and processing new employees, normally adequate recruitment and screening standards can be established

before a classification system is in operation. 

In addition to recruitment and screening standards, basic standards of performance should be established for a specified trial period. new employee should meet these standards satisfactorily before he is considered a permanent employee. Such standards should relate directly to the recruitment standards established for each position. For example, if an executive secretarial applicant is required to have public relations experience, she should be required to demonstrate this ability satisfactorily during the trial period following her employment. 

If it is obvious that an employee has been misplaced according to ability and desire during his trial period, then he should be offered an opportunity to transfer into a position for which he is qualified. He should then undergo a second trial period for the new position. 

The department will probably be able to initiate a recruitment, screening and placement plan before the rest of the employee development program can be realized. In view of this, it is hoped that recruitment and screening standards will not be used as substitutes for complete written job descriptions and requirements. necessity of revising such standards later to reflect accurately complete job descriptions and requirements should be kept in mind.

MEASURE EMPLOYEE EFFECTIVENESS: At present, the department uses no formal method or device to measure employee ability, potential or effectiveness. This is understandable because this is one of the most difficult phases of personnel administration. Industry has found that most rating methods available are at least partially inadequate.

Nevertheless, many advantages accrue from attempts to rate employees on an objective basis. They include: - A written record or evaluation of an employee's past perform- Periodic rating of employees offer an opportunity for supervisors to review progress or lack of it. - Indications of personality or performance problems for particular employees. Persistent failings usually come to light in a rating system. This offers the supervisor an opportunity to direct special training or personal effort in areas where his employees will benefit the most. - A reasonably objective picture of the employee's abilities and weaknesses which can be used as one of the measures by which change-in-status is granted. Without a rating system, supervisors must depend on their own subjective, on-the-spot judgment in granting promotions, demotions or transfers. - An objective appraisal through which employees with outstanding leadership ability and executive qualifications may be readily recognized. Supervisors may then offer such employees training and exploratory opportunities which will help them expand and mature their leadership potential. 

One of the major problems in establishing a personnel rating or appraisal system is concerned with the factor of subjectivity. Although a rating system's purpose is objective measurement, this is never quite accomplished, because supervisors must still rate other employees. 

Objectivity is encouraged by the formal mechanics of most systems, but supervisors or other employees who rate an individual employee must base their ratings on what they think is true of the individual. Sometimes such ratings may even reflect what they wish were true of an individual. 

Therefore, there may be danger in depending on the rating system alone to measure an employee's eligibility for promotion. The results may be more fair if the employee's rating is weighted by other factors such as longevity, test results, experience, education, etc.

RECOMMENDATION NO. 28: 

It is recommended that the department review methods of employee rating used by industry, other state agencies and local business firms and 1 determine a system which could be used for departmental employees. 

Once a system is devised for the department, the chief of Administrative Services should be available along with the executive director to review rating results with supervisors. Top level guidance should be given in pointing out and remedying trouble areas indicated in the ratings. Also, supervisors should be encouraged to be as objective as possible in completing the rating sheets. Possibly some objective standards should be established for employees rating other employees so that ratings may be as uniform as possible between divisions. 

EXPAND EMPLOYEE ABILITIES: A program designed to encourage pro fessional growth of employees must have two basic prerequisites. There must be opportunity for an employee to learn, and there must be sufficient incentive for him to do so. Unfortunately, the department has had little to offer in either category.

Beyond basic in-service training by division supervisors and occasional attendance of selected individuals at conventions, the department does not have a training program. Potentially outstanding employees have no opportunity to take courses connected with their field of work, or to get supervisory training for leadership preparation or any form of training which might help expand their scope of knowledge The League staff is able to furnish materials which could be of assistance in establishing a rating system.

and, thus, their value to the department. This problem is noted particularly in the Purchasing Division where the value of an individual can be measured at least partially by his scope of knowledge in the state purchasing field.

In addition to training, another technique commonly used for expanding an employee's scope is to delegate higher job functions to him. 

Assignment of exploratory job functions allows an employee not only to become acquainted with duties and responsibilities of positions above him but also gives him and the department opportunity to measure his potential abilities. Again, this technique was not used in the Purchasing Division. 

Employee incentives, the second prerequisite to a program of improving employee abilities, are a basic problem for the department because of limited salary increases and promotional opportunity. Other incentive factors include departmental loyalty, special recognition and personal sense of achievement. Only the latter was evidenced in the department. 

In some divisions division loyalty was apparent, but generally a strong feeling of department loyalty seemed to be displaced by loyalty to individuals or the state government in general. Individual loyalty is understandable since the department is widely diversified in functions and not well-coordinated.

Periodic change in board composition also plays a part in weakening departmental unity and coordination. 

Special recognition such as letters of commendation in the personnel files, employee of the year awards, and departmental notification of outstanding achievements of particular divisions are not used in the

department. Although such methods play only a minor part in supplying employee incentive and initiative, they usually are worthwhile in terms of the minimal effort required to put them in effect. 

This phase of employee development, expansion of employee ability, is one which can be initiated long before several of the other phases can be realized. However, there is danger in developing this phase prematurely because the department may not be ready to utilize the trained, motivated employee's services in a higher capacity. This could lead to unhealthy turnover and excessive loss of well trained employees to agencies or private concerns which can offer them better opportunities for advancement.

It should be noted here that an agency with a complete employee development program may also lose well-trained employees. However, this normally occurs after such employees have progressed to the higher job classifications offered by the agency, and then eventually become underutilized and underpaid. This can be termed healthy turnover, because there are employees ready to move up in their places, who would also become dissatisfied if no vacancies occurred. Essentially, a complete development plan means dynamic movement of the employees at all levels. 

If this arrangement is working successfully, reasonable turnover at the top levels should help and not hurt the organization. 

RECOMMENDATION NO. 29: 

It is recommended that the Board of Control determine feasible training and incentive plans designed to help employees broaden their base of knowledge in the fields in which they are employed.

Basic training opportunities and incentive mechanisms should provide the short range benefit of improving employee morale and interest in departmental goals. It is recognized that the major benefits cannot be realized until the department is capable of initiating and maintaining a full employee development plan. 

ADVANCE EMPLOYEE STATUS: The final step involves creation of promotion and compensation plans. These personnel techniques are essential in achieving a successful development program because there must be opportunity for a well-trained employee to advance. This phase is last because it cannot be made workable until the rest of the development program is well under way. In addition, an agency must be able to justify a request to the Legislature for budgetary changes needed to install a compensation plan.

Earlier in the report problems concerning compensation and promotional opportunity were discussed. At that point recommendations for improvement were deferred. The League staff feels strongly that recommendations on salary administration or promotional patterns would be premature if they preceded recommendations on job classification and essential organizational changes.

Once recommendations on job classifications and organization are accomplished, then the following recommendations may be considered.

RECOMMENDATION NO. 30: 

It is recommended that: - the department devise a compensation plan¹ complete with pay grades and step increases which relates directly to the recommended job classification point system. This plan should be devised and maintained, with board approval, by the department's Personnel Committee which in turn would receive technical assistance from the chief of Administrative Services and his personnel staff. - a merit system be devised for compensation step increases based on employee ratings, outstanding achievement, length of service and in-service training. - lines of promotion be established by the committee, with board approval, based on the reorganization and job classification recommendations.

APPENDIX A CHIEF, PURCHASING DIVISION BASIC FUNCTION 

The chief of the Purchasing Division shall be responsible to the executive director for the direction and coordination of all purchasing functions and related activities assigned to the Purchasing Division by law, board policy, and executive order. The chief shall assume all other duties assigned to him in his capacity as division chief which shall include attendance at committee meetings and public affairs as directed. 

DUTIES AND RESPONSIBILITIES 

In order to perform this basic function, the chief purchaser shall assume administrative command of the division, subject to board policy and executive order. Although he may delegate some or part of his individual duties, he shall maintain his authority over them and be responsible for their performance. He shall: 

Determine and define the purchasing function as established or limited by law, board policy and executive order. He shall then organize his division so as to perform the purchasing function most effectively, subject to approval of the executive director.

Organize and give overall direction to the requisitioning, procurement, and inspection of all items purchased by the division for state agencies.

Assign duties and responsibilities to each immediate subordinate which shall include assignment of purchasing categories to the senior purchasers. 76

Instruct his subordinates on existing statutory and departmental policy which affects the Purchasing Division's operation. He should be available to assist subordinates in solving difficult or extraordinary problems which may arise from time to time in the purchasing process and recommend to the executive director changes in policy which such problems may suggest.

Work closely with his subordinates to plan, devise, establish and follow procedures for internal division administration which shall include techniques and methods for reporting, awarding of bids, in-service or on-the-job training for purchasing personnel, qualification of vendors and investigation of commodities purchased. - Determine finally whether items should be purchased under contract or on the open market. 

Meet and maintain good relations with vendors who are potential or actual suppliers for the state in order that problems may be mutually understood and efforts made to solve them. - Devise and maintain adequate standards of performance for vendors and discipline those who fail to meet such standards in whatever manner is deemed necessary and in the best interest of the state. 

Maintain a cooperative, workable relationship with representatives of using agencies for the purpose of exploring purchasing problems and coordinating effort to eliminate them wherever possible.

Encourage subordinates to maintain a current knowledge of commodity markets, marketing practices, and commodity pricing methods and differentials, as well as the availability of materials, acceptability of substitutes and other information which serves to improve a purchasing operation. Distribute current purchasing publications among personnel for their use. - Review personnel and equipment needs of the division periodically. This information shall be used as an aid in the preparation of a well-supported divisional budget request each fiscal period. 

Approve the employment, reassignment, promotion, demotion, or discharge of subordinate employees when such action is deemed to be in the best interest of the department.

Correspond with vendors, using agencies, service agencies, and the public as often as necessary to operate the division in an efficient manner.

ORGANIZATIONAL RELATIONSHIPS 

The chief of the Purchasing Division shall report and be responsible to the executive director. He shall report to the board upon request of the executive director but only in the capacity of staff assistant to the executive director.

The chief shall appoint his immediate subordinates. He shall have final approval of all appointments made in positions below his immediate subordinates. 

The chief shall be a member of the department operations committee and the retirement review committee.

He shall also attend the standards advisory committee upon request of the executive director.

MINIMUM JOB REQUIREMENTS 

Applicants for the position of chief of the Purchasing Division must meet all of the following basic requirements: 

· Have ability to administer a large scale governmental purchasing operation. His training and experience in the field of purchasing shall include successful experience as a purchaser either in government or business on a level comparable to the senior purchaser level in the department. 

Preference shall be given to those eligible applicants who are college graduates with specialized training in purchasing, management, public administration or business administration.

Have complete knowledge of modern principles and methods of large scale purchasing, particularly governmental purchasing; be well versed in modern business practices as they apply to the purchasing function. 

Have advanced general knowledge of the functions of Texas government and specific knowledge of Texas laws which apply to state purchasing. - Be able to use tact and diplomacy in cooperating and associating with state officials, business representatives and the public at large. 

Have physical and mental ability necessary to perform supervisory functions in a manner satisfactory both to his superiors and his subordinates. - Be physically active, resourceful, financially responsible, and a person of basic integrity and initiative.
