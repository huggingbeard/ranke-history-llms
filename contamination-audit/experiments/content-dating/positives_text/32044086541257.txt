In considering these, one is not justified in selecting only a few of these facts and from them drawing a generalization purporting to cover all the facts. In forming a theory on m- prefixed words, the only logical method is to consider all the m- prefixed words available. 

In this article all the m- prefixed words in Jäschke's and Das' dictionaries are considered. And the conclusion is that no one generalization will cover all of them, but that they fall into several different categories.

The use of mi- prefixed to parts of the body in languages as far separated geographically and linguistically as Empeo in the Naga Hills and Magari in the Himalayas, the first belonging to the Kuki-Chin linguistic group and the second to the West Central Himalayan, is significant. SMALLER CATEGORIES 

There are a number of smaller categories of words with m- prefix. m- occurs with some words for color or highly colored objects; mdog "color", mts 'ur (also ts'ur) "coloring matter, pigment", mt'in "indigo", mt'on-ka "azure, sky blue", mts'al (Csoma also ts'al) "vermillion", There is some indication that m- was used with pointed weapons: mda "arrow", mdun "lance, spear", mts'on "any pointed or cutting instrument" (but also "forefinger"). 

One category of words has to do with the sky: mk 'a "heaven", mdan "yesterday evening", mts 'án-mo "night". In

Burmese there is a word muig" "sky" which Judson compares with Pali meg'a. Despite its peculiar appearance, however, the word seems to be a native word. In many languages closely related to Burmese we find what seems to be this same word as the first part of a compound denoting certain heavenly phenomena. 

In Maru, by Hillier in Scott's Gazetteer of Upper Burma and the 

Shan States, vol. 1, pt. 1, p. 667, is given muk-u "rain, mūkkūm "thunder", mük-kōng "sky", muk-tap "cloud", muk-hsai "night, dark" On p. 666 for Atci, Davies has the word mao prefixed in the compounds for "thunder", lightning", "sky", "dark". In Lahu, given by Scott p. 676, we find mö- prefixed in words for "rain", "wind", "thunder", "sky", "night", and "dark", and ma- in "lightning" and "light". It is not, however, certain that Tibetan was originally a monosyllabic language. CONCLUSION 

Previous theories which attempt to solve the problem by means of a generalization covering all m- prefixed words in Tibetan are unsatisfactory since they have only considered part of the material and no one generalization will cover all the instances in which m- occurs.
