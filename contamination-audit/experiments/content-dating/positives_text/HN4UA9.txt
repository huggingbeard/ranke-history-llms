The Continued Study Unit 

This is a new educational tool. It represents the cement that binds together the diverse elements or topics which together comprise the Social Science fabric of our educational program. 

These Units must not be thought of as isolated topics which have no connection with the subject matter of textbooks. They do, however, represent a correlated, integrated, and cumulative program. They represent a study of the whole social science field which has resulted for the first time in a solution of the problems involved. 

No textbook can possibly give all that is known about any character, any event, or situation, and yet much more information is needed on every such topic than the textbook affords. In place of printing many paper-bound booklets on various subjects, this program has resulted in a survey of the factual material needed to supplement the textbooks in history, geography, civics and government, and allied topics. Over five hundred topics have been selected as an objective- not that they may all be published in the immediate future. 

Each of the topics selected will be tied into subject material in the textbooks. In this way, additional material on a subject may be added from time to time. A fuller knowledge of the subject under discussion in the textbook is thus made possible. Herein is offered not only factual material correlated with the textbooks, but supplementary reading that is automatically directed to a constructive end. The pupil may be daily exposed to what is really research material, without the labor of digging it out of reference books written for adults. 

Another advantage of the Continued Study Unit idea is the possibility of keeping textbooks up-to-date, as well as the opportunity of securing Units on new and modern problems as they arise. Millions of years before the land portions of the globe had taken on their present forms, or even before many places had become habitable, the earth was peopled by huge animals. the prehistoric monsters; indeed some of them would probably have been able to eat an entire horse at a single meal.

Whole skeletons have been dug up from deep in the earth, and these have been carefully cleaned and mounted in many of the public museums. They make an interesting sight and one that should be viewed by everyone who would like to know something about prehistoric animal life. 

Let us think of ourselves as visiting one of these natural history museums. We go to the great building, enter it, and hurriedly look around. 

Immediately, we see a great variety of skeletons all around us. 

Some are long and slim with towering necks and heads reaching far above our heads; others are short and stubby, with massive bones as thick as gate posts. Some of the huge skeletons have to be supported on strong iron rods, lest they collapse of their own weight.

The Diplodocus: One of the huge skeletons placed in the middle of the long room bears a sign upon which is printed the word "diplodocus," (di-plŏd'ô-kus). The diplodocus measures eighty feet from head to tail tip, and is fourteen feet high at the hips. This makes it easily the largest land animal that ever lived. 

We stand a moment looking at the skeleton in wonderment. Its ugly head, up at the end of a long, snaky neck, is level with the second floor gallery. We realize that if the diplodocus were alive he could just by raising his head, but he will not do so, for he has been dead a hundred and fifty million years. 

How was this huge skeleton preserved for us, and what is its story? 

We shall learn that, presently; let us, for the moment, inspect the creature more closely.

We see that the body is long and narrow, and despite its huge size, that the animal is shaped much like a present-day lizard. The head seems small in proportion to the rest of the animal, and is so flattened at the top that it does not seem that there could have been a very large brain in the skull. The curator of the museum, who comes along, gives us some information about this: "The diplodocus had a brain only four inches long and two inches high," he said. "That small organ had to control the movements of an animal weighing seventy thousand pounds." 

We look upward again at the small head, and then downward, along the enormous body, and do a little rapid figuring. This creature weighed seventy thousand pounds, and he had but a half pound of brain. Suppose our brains were made in the same proportion to our body weight. We should have to get along on a brain about the size of a soup bean! "We could not expect him to be a very intelligent creature," we tell the curator.

The Three-toed Lizard: We move along the gallery, pausing to look at a flat slab of rock, which is marked with rows of impressions in groups of threes, like great bird tracks. Running down between the rows are grooves, looking like the marks a stick makes when it is dragged through mud. 

An attendant tells us they are the tracks of a three-toed prehistoric lizard, and that the grooves were made by the dragging of the animal's tail through the mud. 

He says the tracks are a hundred and fifty million years old, and that they were made when a dinosaur walked along a muddy beach. The tracks were pressed into the mud, just the same as when our dog, "Blackie," romped through the fresh concrete of our neighbor's new walk. The mud hardened, just as the concrete did, preserving for us a record of the feet that tracked it up. From these tracks we learn that some prehistoric lizards stood upright on their hind legs like kangaroos, and did not use the front legs in walking.

We shall learn other interesting facts about the lives of these creatures, but let us look around now for a few moments at some of the other skeletons.

The Tyrannosaurus rex: Over be yond the diplodocus is another enormous skeleton. It does not tower up to the second floor gallery as did the one we just looked at, but we can see at a glance that this creature must have been a very terrible one in its life-time. 

For one thing, it has huge teeth, much larger than those of the tall diplodocus. That means it preyed on living things-that it was a flesheater instead of an eater of plants and small insect-like creatures.

There we have it, "King of the tyrant lizards." You see how a knowledge of Latin helps us to understand many words. 

The tyrant lizard does not tower aloft like the diplodocus; he is only of middle height among the beasts of his time; but he is thick-set and powerful. If the prehistoric monsters had formed a football team this tyrant lizard would have been a guard. He would have provided the weight needed to crush an opposing side. We may well believe that Tyrannosaurus rex caused panic everywhere he went. Even reduced to a skeleton, with the light shining through his empty ribs, we shudder a little at sight of him.

The Three-horned Lizard: We move along, stopping to look at another skeleton which has three horns on its huge face. This animal must have looked a little like a hippopotamus in its life-time.

This animal, however, was much bigger than a hippopotamus, and much stronger and uglier. Indeed, he was so startling of appearance, with his three huge horns, his sharp cutting beak, and his great frill of bone running around his neck like a cape, that we wonder if, sometimes, when the Three-horned Lizard saw his reflection looking up at him from a quiet pool, whether he did not almost scare himself!

The Roof-back Lizard: Farther along we see a restored model of an animal that is even more startling of appearance, than the Threehorned Lizard. It is a Stegosaurus, (stěg”i-sốrus) or Roof-back Lizard, a forty-footer, with a double row of enormous bony plates set up edge-wise all along the neck and back, almost down to the end of the tail. At the end of the tail are four huge bony spikes, like the long, sharp tusks of an elephant.

The powerful tail of this animal, armed with those four long spikes, was no doubt a weapon to defend itself against its enemies. It is not likely that any but the most powerful enemy could have withstood a blow from a weapon like that armored tail.

The Nose-horned Lizard: Farther along we see another fearsome lizard of the past. It is the Nosehorned Lizard, or Ceratosaurus, (sĕr-ȧ"tó-sô'rus). It stands nearly twenty feet high, in its erect position, like a kangaroo; it is covered all over with plates of bone, called "scutes." 

We do not know whether this creature was fixed up for some kind of fancy party, or whether Nature intended him to be the final word in ugliness. At any rate, we think this armored horror used up a great deal of energy carrying that house of bone around. We cannot help but feel that those plates must have gotten uncomfortably hot in the summer sun.

We can well imagine he might have liked to shed that heavy shell at times; but a glance at his makeup shows that he could not have done so; he was born in a prison and he had to live in it until he died.

1. A Prehistoric Submarine 

Having seen these giant skeletons, let us take a brief view of the living animals, as science rolls back the curtains of time, and shows us what life looked like a hundred and fifty million years ago.

We will imagine ourselves visiting a region where a great river joins the sea. Here it is unbearably hot. Although a breeze is blowing, it brings no relief, for the air is laden with the vapors of the marsh, and smells with rank seaweed and rotted pulp of sponges cast ashore at high tide. 

The burning sun beats down on the muddy waters, and we see on one side the mighty sea, on the other side the wide reaches of the river, pouring down everlastingly from the mainland.

There are no sails on this vasty deep, and no smoke plumes from ships. Not for millions of years will such things appear. The same wave-crests, however, break over into white-caps; the same swirl of water marks where a shark dashes savagely into a school of mackerel.

These things have existed since time was young. 

Toward the left, we see the opposite shore of the river. It is tropic green, with massed tree ferns and strange palm trees matted into a tangle that from this distance seems solid. The trees appear to trample each other in a merciless struggle to reach the sunlight and air. Only here and there is a narrow opening, cut through the greenery, like an alley-way, where the prehistoric beasts have made paths leading down to the water-hole, where they drink and carry on the never-ending search for food.

Old Diplodocus Again: Even as we look something is stirring in the jungle, something so heavy that its tread shakes the marshy ground, and we can hear trees and shrubbery snapping before it like weeds in a gale. 

Ah! It is coming into view. It is an unbelievably tall animal, a kind of long-necked, nightmarishlooking beast, that stands up on great long legs like a giraffe; but it is far larger and more fearsome than any giraffe. 

The thing moves completely into view now, and still carrying its

neck and head high above the ground, lumbers across the muddy strip of shore dividing the green from the water.

It is a diplodocus, the giant of prehistoric reptiles already mentioned. Eighty feet long, weighing thirty-five tons, this huge lizard could stand alongside a three-story house, if there were any houses, and without any effort, thrust its awful head through a top-floor window.

The head is small compared with the size of the body; little cold reptile eyes are set into the bony plates at the side of the head. The ear holes are so small we cannot see them. 

The front legs are smaller than the hind legs, which are of such size that this beast probably could give the greatest kick the world has ever known. The tail is alarmingly long, for its weight must balance the weight of the long neck. Otherwise the beast might be pulled over on his face by the great weight of the long neck and head. 

We cannot see why Nature gave him such a long neck, but Nature always has a reason for her doings, and soon we shall understand why she made him that way.

The diplodocus is a devourer of soft food, sponges, slimy jellyfish, and all the other creeping, ugly things that live in the black ooze of the river bed. 

Now he wades out boldly along the flat bottom of the river. It is a wide estuary, where the bottom drops off very slowly.

Despite his size, life was full of dangers for the diplodocus. His tiny brain could not handle the problems he met, and sooner or later he had to give up the stage for smaller animals who were better fitted for the harsh struggle of existence. 

2. Reptiles That Flew 

How many of us, of a summer evening, have watched the bats hurtling through the gloom, crossing and recrossing overhead, and shuddered a little every time one came close to our heads? 

Why do we shrink away from this most harmless of Nature's children? Is it merely because we dislike the idea of speed—the fear that one of these living missiles might hit us? We think not, for the nighthawks fly in the dark, too, and we do not shudder away from them.

Nor do we shrink from the hawkmoths that visit the petunias in the garden, or even from the booming beetles. It is only these swooping

bats, with their flapping, leathery wings, that give us that creepy feeling. We do not like their pointed wings, their claws, or their blinding speed.

We wonder if this feeling may not be a stirring of a deeply-rooted instinct, some inborn dislike, like that we feel when a snake crosses our path.

The Pterodactyls: In prehistoric times, too, there were things that flew in the night on flapping wings, and with blinding speed, and they too had claws on their wings like bats; but they were not small, as are our bats. They were not even bats at all, but flying reptiles, sometimes nearly twenty feet from wing-tip to wing-tip, and with long, sharp, thick-growing teeth, sharp as needles. Instead of being warmblooded harmless creatures, as are our bats, they were cold-blooded flying monsters, savagely on the look-out for something to kill and eat.

The reptiles that flew are called pterodactyls (tĕr"ô-dǎk'tils) by scientists. This long word, like many another scientific term, can be broken up into very simple words. They are: pteron, the Greek word for wing, and dactyl, meaning finger. That is, "finger-wings," meaning there was a kind of hand on each wing, from which strong, swift-moving fingers reached out. 

We think the name "flying dragon" used by the English is a better name for these monsters than any other. 

We cannot say with certainty if our remote ancestors knew the flying dragon or not. Some scientists think that human beings were created at a later date, others believe that our earliest relatives had a chance to see them, and to look upon them with horror and fear.

We do know that very old civilizations such as the Chinese, have used the dragon as a sign of evil in their religion and art since very old times. Indeed these people still make figures of dragons, which they burn at certain festivals, as a sign. that the spirit of evil has been destroyed. 

Flitting across the night, their large, bony jaws studded with teeth, and their slender wings marked briefly against the full moon, or for the moment blotting it out altogether, the flying dragons would have sent chills down the spine of anyone who saw them.

These strong-winged reptiles had the power to swoop down out of the air without warning. No small animal was safe from their strong claws or their merciless teeth. Even a child could have been snatched up by a creature so powerful.

We can well imagine a prehistoric mother, snatching her child back into the cave at sight of a flying dragon. The prehistoric folk would have dived into their caves the same way the victims of a war-torn

This power of flight enabled the flying dragons to develop an altogether different set of habits from the other prehistoric animals. CHAPTER II The Amphibians 

An amphibian (am-fib'i-ăn) is a creature living on both land and in water. We are accustomed to consider frogs, toads, salamanders and other amphibians as rather small, harmless creatures which would prefer not to be noticed. Many of them are ugly and repulsive, particularly salamanders and waterdogs, but none of them, in spite of common beliefs, are either poisonous or capable of inflicting a severe bite.

In prehistoric times, however, many amphibians reached a great size, comparable to an alligator, and they were equipped with fierce teeth which could seize and rip to pieces animals much larger than themselves. 

Conditions in prehistoric times (especially in that period known as the Triassic Epoch) favored the development of the amphibians. The climate was warm and moist, and the plant life consisted of tree ferns of queer proportions, and of palmlike plants which now grow only in tropical and subtropical regions. 

In those prehistoric swamp lands, somewhat like the Everglades of Florida, great numbers of amphibians, both small and large, lived and feasted on the abundant animal life.

The Labyrinthodons (lăb-i-rin'tho-dons). The fiercest members of the amphibian world have disappeared without leaving any living relatives. These were the members of the lost order of Labyrinthodons.

This cumbersome word refers to the structure of the teeth, in which the enamel was folded in upon the dentine in a complicated pattern, reminding us of the structure of certain curly-grained pieces of wood. 

The Labyrinthodons were ugly, four-legged creatures, somewhat like crocodiles in form, but thicker in proportion, and with larger jaws. 

Many of them had swollen stomachs, indicating gluttonous habits, and humped backs, which added to their repulsive appearance. The tail was usually somewhat short, and the head, which was nearly all jaw, was sometimes nearly a third of the body length.

We cannot say whether the Labyrinthodons were swift and agile in capturing their prey or not. From their squat, stubby make-up we should think they were rather sluggish. However, there is no mistaking the murderous power of those tooth-filled jaws, once they got hold of something. They would crush victim to a pulp in a single bite, (10)

Dinosaurs (di'no-sôr). The dinosaurs (deinos, terrible, mighty; sauros, lizard) were a class of reptiles which were first created something like three hundred million years ago. They held undisputed sway over the earth for a period of perhaps two hundred and fifty million years, and then appear to have gradually died out. We know of these reptiles only through our studies of fossil bones in the rocks, and estimates of their age, made by geologists who have studied the processes by which these rocks were formed.

The dinosaurs have no close living relatives; the present-day crocodiles and alligators are probably the closest to them in body structure. The habits of crocodiles and alligators, however, are very different from those of the land-dwelling dinosaurs. To find reptiles with habits more nearly resembling those of the dinosaurs we must look to the giant Dragon Lizards of the

Many of the dinosaurs were sluggish, grazing animals, living upon the rank grasses of the marshland, and never troubling other animals, if they could help it. They furnished meat for the fierce flesheaters, just as deer and bison furnish meat for wolves and wild-cats today. 

Some dinosaurs were browsers, living on the tender leaves of trees. 

The Iguanodon is representative of this group. This huge reptile learned to get up on its hind legs, too, in order to get at the tender top leaves of trees, but it was not so well-fitted for running about on its hind legs as were the erect flesheaters.

Although these herbivorous animals did not attack other animals for food, nevertheless, some of them were well protected against assault. 

They carried armor, in the form of thick plates covering the whole body, and also spikes and sharp prongs, with which they could gore and mangle anything that attacked them.

A peculiarity of the dinosaurs was the possession of a second brain, or nerve-center, located in a cavity of the spine near the tail.

CHAPTER III Life in the Ancient Seas 

During a large part of the period known as the "Age of Reptiles," much of the eastern half of the 

North American continent was under the ocean, with long arms of shallow water reaching down from the polar regions into the Canadian provinces, Kansas, and Texas. 

This resulted in the formation of huge deposits of shale rock, which were originally layers of sand bought down by the great rivers. and deposited near the shores.

There the tides flowed in and out and buried many interesting things which gradually were turned to stone. In these shales are found fossils of giant sea-shells, star-fish, whelks, bivalves, and a great variety of corals, some of which have become utterly extinct.

Even more interesting than the shale beds are the deposits of chalk found here and there, notably in 

Kansas. The remains from the chalk beds of Kansas are among the most spectacular known to science.

This chalk is in reality a form of lime, and is composed of the shells of countless shell-fish, corals, and marine animal skeletons, some of which are almost too small to see with the naked eye. From these tiny forms, known as diatoms (di'ă-toms), the petrified forms range up to some of the largest prehistoric monsters known. In the region around Chicago we have a fine picture of the kind of life that lived in the sea one hundred fifty million years ago. 

Huge spiral sea shells, covering the bodies of soft worm-like creatures, and multitudes of sea-scorpions lived along the sands; farther out the polyps built vast reefs of coral, in which swam strange, brightly colored reef-fishes, not unlike those of the tropic seas of today. In the waters of the open sea were pikes, gar fishes, and the everlastingly hungry sharks, always on the lookout for something to seize and devour. 

Sharks are among the oldest forms of life. Even in the fossils of the most ancient seas we find remains of them. Always they have had savage teeth, huge fins capable of driving their great great bodies through the water at high speed, and heavy tails that could smash a lesser creature to a pulp by a mere lashing movement. One of the sharks of the ancient seas had jaws six feet wide. Coming toward one head-on, it would have had the appearance of a tooth-margined cave.

The Pteranodon: In Kansas are found skeletons of the Pteranodon (té-rǎn'ò-don), the largest flying dragon, expanding to twenty feet across the wings. This huge pterodactyl must have had vast powers of flight, else it would not have ventured so far to sea. The exact manner in which the wings of these flying dragons operated is a mystery to scientists. It is hard to understand how such monsters managed to get themselves launched into flight. It is known that many of our large birds have to take a run, like an airplane, before they can take off. Swans, geese, large pheasants, even the albatross, all have to take this short run. It would seem that the pterodactyl would not have dared to alight on the water, or it would never get into the air again. Although prehistoric skeletons have been discovered in nearly every portion of the earth, a few places have been unusually rich in this kind of material. These places fall into three main groups: peat or coal deposits, marking the locations of ancient swamps; rocky layers of sandstone or limestone which once were covered by water; beds of pitch or asphalt. To these must be added four sources of lesser importance: insects preserved in amber; cave-floor remains and cave pictures; remains preserved by copper salts, and bodies found in the polar ice.

Sandstone deposits have given us very fine fossils of the giant dinosaurs, especially the sandstones of 

Wyoming, and of Saskatchewan and 

Alberta, Canada. These were shore areas, two hundred million years ago, at which time the sea extended down from the Arctic regions, and covered more than fifty per cent of the present land area of North America.

Swarming with plant and animal life, these shallow seas, and the bordering swamp areas, have given us a collection of monsters of prehistoric times not equalled anywhere else on the globe. 

The largest of these monsters were the long-necked, long-legged dinosaurs, represented by the Diplodocus, the Apatosaurus (ǎp"ȧ-tosô'rus), and the Brontosaurus.

These great animals ranged upward of seventy feet, and weighed eighty thousand pounds or more.

The Brontosaurus was probably a rather tame creature, blundering around the lowlands, between clumps of palm-like cycads (ferns) or wading in the shallow water off shore, searching for the small creatures on which he lived. His small mouth was provided with thin, pencil-like teeth which could not have been of much use for catching anything, or even chewing it.

Another prehistoric Wyoming animal that was amazingly ahead of his time, in the scheme of Creation, was the Stork dinosaur. This tall, graceful reptile of the plains was stream-lined to the last detail of his slim, bird-like figure. It is probable he could have surpassed a greyhound in a test of speed.

The Chalk Beds of Kansas: Next to the Wyoming and Canadian deposits, the Kansas chalk is probably the most productive source of American fossils. This region also was under the ancient sea, but it was much farther from the shore, so that sand and surface dirt from the land was not washed into it by flood waters.

The climate was tropical in the Kansas region at that time, and huge masses of corals and other marine shells, much like the Great Barrier Reef of Australia, were formed in the shallow seas. This material in time formed limestone or chalk, which became covered with earth during later processes of the earth's formation. 

In the Kansas chalk are found the greatest of the flying dragons: the Pteranodon. This flying monster, which spread twenty feet across the wings, surpassed any winged creature of modern times, and probably in any time.

As has been explained elsewhere, the Pteranodon would have been compelled to remain on the wing during all its long journey over the sea, for it was not fitted for alighting upon the waters, nor for taking off again, should it accidentally fall into the water. With its long wings. it would flap about helplessly, and no doubt usually attracted the attention of the destroying Mosasaurus, or Killer-whale, which seized it and ate it.

The ball was studded with sharp spikes in such a manner that when the tail was lashed from side to side, it was almost certain to give a knock-out blow to whatever animal molested its owner.

The Glyptodon was so well-protected, both by his suit of armor and by his war-club tail, that we can scarcely see how he lost out in the struggle for existence. However, he may have fallen a victim to the period of glacial cold which came up over South America in prehistoric times, just as the glacier came down over the United States.

European Prehistoric Remains: 

The European prehistoric remains have been known for a longer time than those of any other land. In fact, the European fossils formed the basis of almost all scientific works on the subject until about fifty years ago.

The European fossils rank first in the production of such remains. as the Iguanodon (i-gwan'ô-don) (teeth like iguana lizard). This huge plant-eating Dinosaur stood eighteen feet high and was thirty

feet long. It was capable of rising up on its hind legs, in order to get at the tender leaves of trees, in the same manner as did the ground sloth of South America. However, the Iguanodon probably did not spend all its time in this position, as did some of the flesh-eating dinosaurs. The front legs are rather well-developed in this animal, and this would not be the case were they not used a great deal, as in walking.

The Iguanodon was found most abundantly in certain coal pits in 

Belgium. There the remains have been found in a fine state of preservation, just as they were deposited in the mire of the swamp when the coal bed was a region of lush plant growth in prehistoric times, and the

Another interesting European locality is that of the Solenhofen slate quarries in Bavaria. Here is located a very fine-grained variety of slate that was once the earthy matter at the bottom of a prehistoric lagoon.

Preserved in this slate are the remains of prehistoric birds, such as the Archaeopteryx (är"ke-op'tĕrĭks), and the smallest known dinosaurs. 

The Archaeopteryx does not closely resemble any bird of present times. It has no bill, but hard jaws, studded with teeth. The eyes are large, like those of reptiles, and it has three fingers along the front wings, reminding us of a Pterodactyl. The tail is feathered like the body, but the spine goes right down into it, as in reptiles. The feet are like those of birds, but the wings are longer in proportion to their width than are those of most birds of present times. The tips are rounded, rather than pointed. 

A tiny dinosaur was found at Solenhofen, which has been previously mentioned. It is the size of a hen, and is remarkable for its birdlike frailty. Strangely enough, this small, birdlike dinosaur had another dinosaur inside it, indicating not only that it was a flesh-eater, but that it had taken on a good meal just before disaster overtook it. 

The hairy mammoths found in northern Europe and also in the Siberian ice have been described on another page, as have also the cave pictures of the woolly rhinoceros, the cave-bear, the sabre-tooth and others.

The cave pictures, of great interest to archeologists, are particularly fine in certain regions of France,

where the remote ancestors of present-day Europeans pictured wild life as they saw it. In France some of the caves have been lighted with electricity, and are open to tourists. Visitors who come here obtain a remarkable glimpse into the prehistoric past, although their view is not the same as that of prehistoric man. Prehistoric man, however, had no electric lights to make the interior of the caves as bright as daylight. All he had was a smoking torch, and it is a remarkable thing that he was able to make these fine pictures under such handicaps. However, hardships often bring out the best in a person, and the Cave-man of a hundred thousand years ago seems to have been no exception.

Prehistoric Remains in Africa: 

Africa has not produced so many fossils as have some of the other continents, probably because so little exploration has been done. Most of this continent is still a rank tropical jungle, and thousands of square miles have no quarries, mines, or other excavations which would give explorers an opportunity to see what lies under the surface. 

However, remains of prehistoric elephants have been found in

Egypt, and these are remarkable for their small size, and for the absence of a trunk. Apparently, the prehistoric elephants of Africa were animals somewhat like the tapir, with a mouth not very different from other animals. However, the trunk shows in a limited degree on some of them, and the bones are of the same general type as those of elephants of modern times. 

It is worthy of note, also, that tusks did not always appear as in the elephants of today. Sometimes they are in the upper jaw, sometimes in the lower, sometimes in both. Wherever they occur, they were probably used for fighting, and also for rooting up sod and small trees. 

Then, in South Africa, in the sandstone rocks known as the "Karoo beds," peculiar animals are found, which resemble mammals, or warm-blooded animals. However, the resemblance is only an outward appearance, for they are not mammals, but reptiles, or perhaps it might be better to say they are somewhere between the two. One of these is the Cynognathus (sinog'nȧ-thus) (dog jaws), a fierce flesh-eater, with long jaws, huge dog teeth, and for all its reptilian nature, a body strangely resembling some of the more lowly of presentday animals of the African jungle. 

Then, there was the Trinaxodon (tri-naks'ō-don), a reptile which approaches the egg-laying mammals, of which the present-day Australian water-mole is one. 

Fossil Life in Asia: Asia, too, has not been investigated to any great extent, for the country is largely populated by warring tribes, which render the peaceful expeditions of

scientific men a dangerous task. 

However, a recent trip to the Gobi desert made by Dr. Roy Chapman

Andrews, yielded some remarkable finds which are described elsewhere. 

The discovery of dinosaur eggs in 

Asia by Dr. Andrews confirms the finding of eggs previously in France, which were believed to be dinosaur eggs, but which could not certainly be identified as such. The Protoceratops which laid the eggs in Asia was a medium-sized dinosaur of the flesh-eating group, and had a peculiar, beak-like mouth with hard, sharp lips, much like the fierce snapping-turtles of our own times. 

Dr. Andrews found also the Baluchitherium (ba-loo"chi-the'ri-um), an enormous rhinoceros-like animal in Asia, and there can be no doubt that when the region of the Gobi is more fully explored, a great many new forms will come to light. If, as scientists believe and the Scriptures confirm, life was first created in Asia, we may expect to find a great treasure of prehistoric material there, once the land is fully explored.

CHAPTER VI Warm-blooded or Mammal Prehistoric Life 

Warm-blooded animals, called mammals, appeared on earth at a later date than did the cold-blooded reptiles. Some of the earlier types of these mammals laid eggs, much like the dinosaurs and other reptiles, but the higher types of mammals all bear living young. All of them nurse their young, and all have a backbone, a heart, and circulation of blood. 

As the earlier reptiles were mainly huge, clumsy creatures, later followed by smaller, more efficient ones, so the mammals of the earlier periods were for the most part clumsy types, and many of them were very large.

We have been particularly fortunate in recovering the bones of many of these early mammals, and from these remains we learn that a great many of the early mammal forms have passed away completely. 

A number of these failures will be described, but these will be only a sampling of the hundreds of mammals which lived on earth, long ago. 

Among the very earliest of mammals to be created were the marsupials (mär-su'pi-ăls), a class of animals which differs from all others in the fact that the young are born while still very tiny. Even the young of such large animals as the kangaroos are hardly larger than a man's finger.

These baby animals are so small and tender that they could not survive had Nature not made special provision for their care. 

This is done by transferring them into a large pouch which the mother carries on her under side, along the abdomen. There the babies nurse and grow for many months, until finally they are strong enough to climb out of the pouch and shift for themselves.

Only one pouch-animal is still left in the United States: that is the lowly opossum of the eastern woodlands. In prehistoric times, however, a great variety of marsupials lived in what is now the United 

States. There were kangaroos ten feet high, and with a long enough leap to carry them across a small river, and giant wombats, many times larger than the small wombats now living in Australia.

Dromatherium: 

Some very important marsupial fossils have been found in the coal fields of Chatham, North Carolina. The most interesting of these was the Dromatherium (drō-mă-the'ri-ům), which is not only the oldest marsupial, but also the oldest mammal known to science. It would seem probable that the Dromatherium lived while the (35)

cold-blooded dinosaurs were still lords of creation, and perhaps furnished food to some of them. 

Marsupials were distributed all over the world in prehistoric times, but then, as now, the greatest variety of them seems to have lived in Australia. There were representatives of all the living varieties of marsupials, the bandicoot rats, the squirrel-like phalangers, the savage, flesh-eating Zebra-wolf, the planteating kangaroos, and wombats; in addition there were many that have no living relatives.

Diprotodon: Among the types without a living thing to represent them was the Diprotodon (di-prō'to-don), a marsupial which surpassed the rhinoceros in size. 

Compared with it, the largest living marsupial, the kangaroo, seems small and weak. The Diprotodon took the place of the elephant among marsupials; it was a plant eater, like the elephant, and it was undoubtedly the largest animal that lived in the Australian continent. 

Despite its size it was a misfit, and like all misfits, in time went into 

Nature's discard.

The marsupials, however, were not the only warm-blooded animals which produced their misfits. Many of the other classes of animals also failed to survive, and among them. were a few types which show a definite relationship to certain animals of today. The great order of Ungulates (ung'gu-låts), which included the hoofed animals, such as cattle, deer and horses, also had their types which could not make the grade. 

Dinotherium: One huge misfit was the Dinotherium (di"no-the'rĭ-ŭm) of Europe. This elephant-like animal had drooping tusks growing from the lower jaw, resembling the tusks of a walrus. It was 18 feet long and no doubt weighed more than any elephant of our times. The 

Dinotherium probably perished in that era of cold when the glaciers came down from the North and covered most of Europe. Some animals. of that time, such as the rhinoceros and the mammoth, developed great coats of hair, by which they were able to survive the intense cold, but the Dinotherium evidently did not have it in his make-up to do so, and for that reason failed to survive.

Elotherium: In our country lived the Elotherium (ĕl-ô-thē'rĭ-ům), which has no living relatives. It was a great, pig-like animal as large as a moose, but it had no horns. The 

Elotherium had a head three feet long, great projecting jaws, and large, heavy canine teeth, somewhat like those of a dog. The Elotherium was another of Nature's failures; she got rid of him ages ago, and permitted only moderate-sized hogs to survive.

Moropus: Perhaps the strangest of all the misfits was the Moropus (mor'ô-pus) of Europe. If anyone were to find the legs and body of a Moropus in separate 37 places, he would never suspect that the two sets of fossils belonged to the same animal. 

The Moropus has taught modern scientists to be extremely careful in reconstructing the model of a dead animal from one of its parts. Formerly there were men who thought they needed only a few parts to remake the entire animal. Now they know that a complete skeleton is essential before they can build a model with any certainty of its being a faithful likeness of the living animal.

The animals thus far mentioned in this chapter were grazing or browsing animals. They lived on green plant life, and not on flesh. It must not be thought, however, that all of the early mammals were of this group, for there were fierce flesh-eaters among mammals, then, as now, and some of them, as the sabre-toothed cat, were perhaps as fierce as any animal we have today. 

Hyaenodon: At the same time the Elotheres and the Titanotheres of grass and leaves, there lived also strode about devouring great masses a class of animals called Hyaenodons (hi-ĕn'ô-dons) (hyena-toothed).

This animal was about the size of a leopard, and was fitted for running down and tackling its prey. It is believed that they followed the herds of grazing animals, such as 

Uintatheres and Titanotheres, and snatched off a tender calf whenever the opportunity offered.

The cause of the disappearance of the Hyaenodon cannot be stated.

It may have perished in the era of great cold, when many of the animals upon which it preyed also perished; or it may have fallen a victim to some kind of disease, such as the distemper which sometimes affects animals today. Whatever the reason, it happened before prehistoric man entered America, for no remains of Hyaenodons have been found, either in prehistoric tombs, or in the cave-floor remains where primitive man threw the leavings from his dinner. 

Bison: Another prehistoric animal which has been found in the cave-floor deposits, showing that prehistoric man used it for food in 

America, was the giant bison. The giant bison lived in the southwestern part of the country, mainly in 

California, and in New Mexico. It was much larger than the presentday bison, and had wide, sharp horns, measuring six feet across the tips.

The exact manner in which the prehistoric men of New Mexico, the mysterious "Folsom people," hunted this animal, is open to question. It is known that they possessed the atlatl, or throwing stick, for hurling arrows and spears; but it is not known whether the bow had been invented at that time or not. Many scientists believe the throwing stick alone was used. Whatever the weapon, primitive man was very successful in capturing this great animal, for a considerable number of the bones have been found in the cave-homes of the "Folsom people" in New Mexico. It is quite probable that the cause of the extermination of this noble animal is to be traced to the hunting of these people, just as the horse of Europe was probably killed off by Stoneage men.

CHAPTER VII Hunters of Fossils 

Although the scientist and explorer are usually thought of first when fossils are mentioned, these persons are by no means the only ones who are interested in fossils. 

Neither are they the first hunters of such objects.

To find the first fossil hunters we have to go back into the remote past, back into prehistoric times, before there were civilizations as we know them.

The prehistoric men gathered fossils, for we find them in the most ancient graves. In fact, some of the graves may be almost as old as the fossils found in them.

It is known that the ancient Chinese, as far back as history goes, collected fossils, not merely as objects of interest, but for the purpose of grinding them into medicines. They would get the petrified sea-shells, bones of prehistoric monsters, and other material, and grind them into powder in a kind of hollow vessel known as a "mortar." 

The club used for grinding would be made of bone or hard wood, and is known as a "pestle." The mortar and pestle method of grinding medicines is still used by druggists for mixing medicines on short notice.

We are likely to look upon the 

Chinese as sunk in superstition, and to ridicule their attempts to make medicine out of such things as fossils. Let us, however, consider this fact in civilized Europe, only two or three hundred years ago, the druggists were grinding Egyptian mummies and putting the powder into their medicines! The practice is mentioned in Shakespeare and in 

Roger Bacon's works.

A mummy is not a fossil, but a human body which has been soaked in spices and brine and cured, somewhat like a ham. It does not even have the virtue of being an alkaline earth. The lime of the ground-up fossil might possibly help the patient a little by sweetening the acid in his stomach, or aiding in the treatment of rickets, but no such virtue could be claimed for the powder of a ground-up mummy.

Fossils were also collected by the prehistoric Mound Builders,¹ for these objects have been found in the tombs of these strange people. 

Some scientists think these ancient people merely collected the fossil objects because of some peculiarity of shape, and that primitive man could not have understood what they were, or how they were formed.

It is an interesting fact that the sandstone slab, which bears this fossil design, also bears little wave marks. These are the ripple marks formed when the wind blew gently upon the shallow water, and little lapping wavelets caressed the muddy shore, and moulded the sand into a pattern exactly the same as may be seen at the water's edge today.

Replacement depends upon the bones being soaked in water which is laden with minerals. As the bone cells break down the cavities are filled with the mineralized water. 

The minerals are deposited in the bone and form solid crystals which exactly imitate the shape of the original bone. Thus, in time, we have a cast of the bone, which is so hard that it rings like glass, and it must be handled with care, lest it shatter.

As the petrifying process usually is not uniform, some parts receiving a better supply of the mineral-laden water than others, some parts usually will not petrify. Then, as the explorer finds the fossil, some toes may be missing, or some ribs, perhaps even the head. These parts must then be replaced from other skeletons before the animal is mounted in the museum for public display.

This work is done by experienced scientists who have made a long study of the bony structures of the animal, and are not likely to make a mistake. It is readily seen that this sort of work could not be trusted to any but the most skilled.

Copper as a Preservative: Copper has as yet preserved no unusual animal remains, so far as is known. 

A number of human and other perishable objects of the far past have been found preserved by copper, however, and for this reason the subject will be discussed. There is a distinct possibility that important animal matter may be found preserved by copper, or rather by the bright green, poisonous salts which form on the copper and prevent decay in anything which they touch.

In the American Museum of Natural History lies the body of an Indian who has been dead for hundreds, perhaps thousands of years.

The body is not a skeleton, but a complete corpse, clothing and all. 

It is the body of an Indian who was mining copper in South America, probably during the time of the prehistoric Incas.

He had crawled back into a narrow passageway in the copper mine, carrying his leather pouch and stone mauls and was busy quarrying out chunks of the red metal when the roof of the mine caved down on him and killed him. As

he lies in the museum, stained green but perfectly preserved, we can see the flattening of his chest, caused by the load of metallic rock, and the red stain of the hardened blood which poured from his crushed lungs, causing his death.

Even his hair is intact, and the finger nails, dusted with green, still show some of the natural rosy polish, peeping through the green dust. 

His moccasins, wooden-handled wooden-handled stone hammer bound with leather thong, and the buckskin garments are all intact. 

Copper has preserved wonderful relics of the Mound Builders. Thus, the ancient nobles of that race wore large copper breast-plates which were placed upon the chest after death. Whatever portions of the clothing and burial shrouds touched the copper breast-plates did not decay, but were preserved for us.

Thus we have gotten fragments of finely woven cloth, produced on looms that crumbled to dust thousands of years ago. Fragile musical instruments of cane tubing, and royal crowns decked with deerantlers are other things preserved by the copper.

All who have access to prehistoric copper deposits, or to to caves in which the metal occurs, should be on the lookout for prehistoric remains. Perhaps we may sometime find the actual body of a prehistoric reptile, a dinosaur or a pterodactyl, or perhaps a flesh-eating amphibian which somehow got into a copperfilled cavern and has been preserved by the metal in the same way as was the dead Inca in the New York museum.

The story of prehistoric animal life has, in the past, been confined to a few specialists and museum workers. These men have kept it largely to themselves, refusing to give the prehistoric animals names which would be of interest to the nontechnical reader. But a new approach to the subject is being made in recent years, and some of the long names, written in dead languages, are being translated so that all may understand their meaning.

A much greater interest is being awakened, and many more people are looking for fossils. Many who take outdoor hikes, boy and girl scouts, camera clubs, and nature lovers of all sorts are beginning to help piece together the fascinating puzzle of the prehistoric past. This increased interest is bound to result in important discoveries as time moves along.

Prehistoric Mysteries: Although much of the life of prehistoric times is now an open book to us, there are still a number of mysteries, connected with the animal life of that era, which are wholly or partly unexplained.

One of these concerns the disappearance of the horse from America. When the white men first explored the Western World they found no horses anywhere within its borders. So far as they could

Later, when roads were dug through rocky ground and quarries were sunk in the earth, fossil bones of horses began to appear.

It was then plain that horses had once lived in North America. Later, when human bones were found along with the horse bones, it became evident that horses had lived here in rather recent times, as geologists measure time: that is, perhaps not more than fifty or a hundred thousand years ago. 

The fact that horses had once been here, but now had disappeared, caused the question to be raised: "Why had the horses disappeared?"

It was a question that was not easily answered. It could not be said that horses had perished for lack of suitable food, or that a changing climate had wiped them out, because other grazing animals, with similar habits, such as the bison and deer, had thrived and multiplied from prehistoric times right down to the present. Neither could it be said that horses had been exterminated by the early human occupants of the land, as they may have been destroyed in Europe, for the mysterious "Folsom" people, who lived in western North America ages ago, had hunted the giant bison and the mastodon, but only a few horse bones were found in their cave shelters. Horse meat certainly was not their main article of diet, for no huge piles of horse bones appear here, as is the case in France. 

No completely satisfactory answer to the disappearance of the horse in 

America has been found. The most reasonable suggestion is that some disease, such as the tsetse (tsět'se) fly of Africa, got into the herds and wiped them out before they could develop a resistance to it. 

This explanation, however, is given only for want of a better one, and it is probably not the true one, for, so far as is known, no disease ever wipes out an animal or human colony completely. No matter how deadly the disease, or how contagious, there are always some individuals who either have immunity to it, or who manage to recover from the attack, and these live to start a newer, hardier race. Until more data can be gathered it is best. to list the disappearance of the horse in America as an unsolved mystery.

One of the mysteries of reptiles is connected with that organ at the top of the brain, which is known as the "pineal eye." cells. The nerves go down into the brain, through a hole in the skull.

Another thing which is hard to explain is the disappearance of the savage mosasaur, that thirty-foot killer-reptile of the ocean deeps.

We are pretty safe in explaining the disappearance of the land reptiles as due to changing climatic conditions, and to changes in the food supply. Thus, the hot palmetto swamps would give way to colder areas, where an entirely different type of plant life would graw. This would favor the warmblooded grazing animals, such as deer, bison, and elephants, while the dinosaurs, used to tropical plants, would perish. 

No such changes, however, can be said of the sea. Scientists are agreed that the sea has changed least of any portion of the earth. 

They point to coral polyps, sponges and anemones, and even sharks and garfish as being practically unchanged since most ancient times. 

It would appear that the destruction of the mosasaur could not have come from lack of proper food. Always the sea has held an abundance of fish and turtles and other living creatures which the terrible mosasaur was so well-fitted to seize and devour.

There is a possibility that some parasite might have attacked the eggs of the mosasaur, or even devoured the helpless young. Somewhere there was a weak link in the mosasaur's chain of life, and he perished because of it, leaving us a mystery to explain as best we can. 

Still another prehistoric mystery is connected with the digestive apparatus of the enormous dinosaurs. 

We have called attention previously to the huge teeth of some of these reptiles. These teeth, which are in reality close-set, curving fangs, have never been surpassed in any other animal on earth. 

In the mighty Tyrannosaurus, the teeth are a foot long; they resemble great steel spikes, round and sharppointed. Giant sharks and killer whales also had very large teeth, but if we examine these teeth we see they were all of the same pattern. They are simply huge, pointed

For seizing and tearing up meat these spikelike teeth would be excellent, but for grinding it up and making it suitable for digestion they would be unfitted.

In the higher animals, this grinding of food is performed by the back teeth, whence these teeth are called "molars" from the Latin molere, "to grind." Most warmblooded animals have them, at least in one jaw. Dinosaurs, however, had no such grinding apparatus. 

We are naturally impelled to ask: "How did the meat-eating dinosaurs grind up their food?"

When we examine the grass-eaters and those that browsed on trees and at the bottoms of rivers, we are still less able to understand the working of their digestive mechanisms. These animals, especially the eighty-foot Diplodocus, had narrow teeth like pencils. They must have gulped their food whole, trusting that their stomachs would know how to handle it when it got there.

Some persons believe the dinosaurs swallowed stones which ground their food up, as the gizzard stones of a fowl grind up food at the present time. They point to certain highly polished stones, worn almost round, which have been found in the Wyoming desert, associated with dinosaur remains. They think the dinosaurs had gizzard stones before birds did.

This explanation has not yet been accepted by scientists; however, the men who know about these things can offer no better explanation at the present time. Perhaps the inner workings of the dinosaur's stomach may forever remain a mystery.

It has been noted that some animals are much better fitted to survive than are others. That is because some of them are more intelligent, or more speedy, or better. able to change their habits in accordance with changing climatic conditions.

Thus, a large part of the North American continent was under the sea in prehistoric times, and favored the increase of certain water animals, and of the amphibians, those peculiar creatures related to frogs and salamanders, which can live either in water or on land.

Then, as the land gradually rose from the sea, forming first the Allegheny Mountains and later the Rockies, the drier, colder climate produced great changes which the amphibians could not withstand. It may be said, therefore, that they fell victims of the harsher climatic conditions and perhaps to a dwindling supply of the food to which they had become accustomed. 

There were some places where these severe changes did not come about, with the result that the older, more backward kinds of animals were left almost as they had been for millions of years. 

A place where this occurred was the island continent of Australia.

Here we find a land which has been free of violent climatic or geological changes for a very long time. As a consequence, the animal life of this region takes us farther back into the past than is the case anywhere else on earth.

Most of the animals on the continent of Australia are marsupials, a kind of creature which differs from other warm-blooded animals in having a pouch on the under side of the abdomen. This pouch, which proved to be an inefficient method of caring for the young, has long since gone into the discard in nearly every portion of the earth except 

Australia. The marsupial animals are much less able to take care of themselves in a world peopled with fierce flesh-eating animals than are the other animals which inhabit the jungle lands. It is only because the island continent of Australia lacks the fierce hunting animals of Africa and Asia, that these animals have been able to survive. Were they gathered up and turned loose in Africa the lions and leopards would make short work of them.

It is worthy of note that when the white settlers came to Australia they introduced the rabbit, and that these animals soon multiplied to such an extent that they threatened to devour all the farmers' crops.

to keep them out, and frequent mass hunts were resorted to, lest the rabbit population get out of control. This indicates the unusually favorable conditions for small animals that exist in Australia, which have preserved animals for us which otherwise would be known to us only as fossils in the rocks. 

The animals of Australia are not limited to the pouch-bearing marsupials, but there are some that are even more lowly. These are the most primitive of all warm-blooded animals, the egg-laying mammals. 

One of these is the duck-billed water mole.

The duck-bill, when first discovered in Australia and taken from there to England as a stuffed specimen, caused the owner to be subjected to scorn and ridicule. Men of learning refused to believe there could be any such animal as the duck-billed water mole. The owner was accused of taking parts of several wild animals and cleverly combining them to produce a monstrous fake.

The duck-bill is an animal about the size of a small cat. It has a bill like a duck, and webbed feet, but the body is covered with soft warm fur, like an otter. Its blood is hardly warm-almost on the border-line with reptiles and, strangest of all, it lays eggs like a reptile.

Still another of the backward 

Australian animals is the spiny anteater, which must not be confused with the ant-bear or armadillo of the American tropics. This lowly creature resembles a large hedgehog, in that it is covered all over with spines; however, the spines are larger than those of the porcupine and the head is very different. Instead of having strong teeth and jaws, the face is drawn forward into a long, rubbery snout, which ends. in a very narrow tip. The long, worm-like tongue, covered all over with slimy glue, darts out like a striking snake, and picks up ants, and in this manner the animal somehow manages to feed itself. 

Like the duck-bill, the spiny anteater lays eggs. 

These backward animals may be called "living fossils," for all their relatives have been dead for millions of years. Were it not for these lone left-overs of a forgotten age, we should know nothing of such animals, except what we could learn from studying skeletons buried in the rocks.

Horses: The period when the horse first came under man's control is clouded in the prehistoric past. 

Horses appear in the earliest records of man about four thousand years ago. At that time, in Syria and Egypt, they were used in the waging of war, and in all probability were the property of the chiefs and nobles.

They were used for dragging war chariots and were also ridden by leaders in battle, while the ordinary soldiers went afoot. 

Before the horse was brought into full control, there must have been a long period of partial domesticity, while man was struggling to break the spirit of this proud animal of the plains, and to compel him to carry or drag a load. 

In nature, only one wild horse now exists. There are so-called "wild horses" in the southwestern parts of the United States, but these are merely the wild descendants of the horses of the Spaniards who came into this country under Coronado, Ferdinand de Soto, and Garcilasso de La Vega. They are domestic horses who have "gone back." 

The real wild horse is called "Prjevalsky's Horse," and lives in the desert region of central Asia, near Zaisan, where it was discovered in 1881 by the Russian whose name it bears. It is a shaggy little horse, with an erect mane, and a darker stripe down the back. Small herds of from five to ten live in the rocky barrens of the Gobi desert, under the leadership of the old males.

This horse may have been the ancestor of some of the modern horses. It is not believed, however, that it was the ancestor of all present-day horses. There is too much difference in the various breeds of horses, both in size and speed, to permit us to believe that all of them came from the same ancestor. 

Some scientists trace two distinct types, referring the light-weight, spirited race horses to one type, the heavy, plodding draft horses to the other. These two types, they believe, represent two distinct kinds. of prehistoric wild horses. Other scientists trace three types, the third being the one that gave rise to the 

Shetland ponies and other small horses. Whichever theory is true, there are no wild horses in Europe, and have not been since the close of the Stone Age.

There were, however, plenty of wild horses in Europe thirty or forty thousand years ago. Literally, thousands of horse bones have been found in the refuse heaps outside the cave homes of the early inhabitants of France and Spain, where they were thrown after the occupants ate the meat from them. 

There can be no question that these horse bones represent food, for the bones were split open to remove the marrow, and frequently bear marks of the flint

knives the Stone Age men used in their butchering operations.

It is even believed that horses were the favorite article of diet among some of these people, and in- Ideed there are still backward sections of Europe where horse meat is not only a legal article of diet, but is much used. Butcher shops which sell horse meat in some parts of

France are compelled by law to display a horse's head at the door. It is interesting to note that many of the horse bones bound on the floors of the French caves are the bones of foals, showing that the occupants. liked the tender meat of the younger horses whenever they could get it.

By the time history opened, however, and the Bronze Age was ushered in, there were no longer any wild horses in Europe, and the cause of their extermination was probably man's incessant search for food. A similar thing very nearly happened in the United States with reference to the bison, which once roamed the western plains in countless hordes, but was reduced to a few hundred head by hunters, and would have been wiped out altogether were it not for the government setting aside preserves for their protection.

Students of the history of the horse note a peculiar thing. Mankind used the horse for thousands of years for riding, and for war, before he commenced to use it for labor. In Bible records, and in other ancient accounts, it is the ox that does the work, not the horse.

Even down to the present time in backward countries the ox still pulls the plow and draws the load. 

England and the United States have used the horse the longest for this purpose, but even in these countries the breeding of work-horses did not become general until a little more than a century ago. 

Going back into the prehistoric times we find a great variety of horses, some of them very different from the horses of present times.

The most interesting of these prehistoric horses and the greatest variety of them are found in the fossil beds of the United States. 

The smallest and earliest of the prehistoric horses is named Eohippus (e”-hipus) (dawn-horse). 

Eohippus was only about a foot high, yet the tiny skeleton is practically the same as that possessed by the larger horses of our time.

There is, however, one striking difference. Eohippus has four hoofed toes on the fore feet and three hoofed toes on the hind feet, instead of a single large hoof on each foot as do the horses of the present time. 

Next in size was Orohippus (or"ò-hip'us) (mountain-horse).

Orohippus was larger, about the size of a good-sized fox. Like Eohippus, Orohippus had four hoofs on the front feet and three on the hind feet.

Other Prehistoric Domestic An imals: Long before man had begun to write history, or was even capable of writing the simplest messages, he had turned his mind to the problem of taming wild animals. It cannot be stated definitely whether his first purpose in doing this was to obtain a food supply, or whether he wanted animal aid for working and carrying burdens.

It is probable, however, different ideas prevailed in different regions. 

Thus, a shepherd or farmer race would think of raising sheep for wool, cattle for milk, oxen for drawing loads. On the other hand, a warrior race, interested in raiding and looting, would domesticate the horse, the war elephant, and fierce dogs for attacking the enemy.

Britons were tilling the fields, raising sheep and oxen, and conducting, under a system of kings and princes, a civilization that was already old in the time of Julius

Digging into the archeological remains of the island showed that beyond the Britons were other prehistoric peoples, known as the "Beaker Folk" from the many drinking beakers found in their tombs, and these people, too, were no strangers to the idea of raising cattle and sheep for man's use.

In the Western World, the Indians and Mound Builders tamed animals to a much lesser degree than did the people of Europe. We find no remains of domestic horses, for, as has been said, the horses had disappeared before glacial times, when people were still living in the hunting and fishing stage of existence. 

In South America the llama, a small animal related to the camel, was used by the highly civilized Incas for bearing their loads. This animal is descended from the wild guanaco (gwä-nä'kō), but has been much improved by contact with man. It is not only used for drawing and carrying loads, but also is an important source of wool and other animal products, and has been since remote prehistoric times.

Among the Mound Builders the chief animal was the dog, and it held a high place in their regard, if we are to judge from the finely carved heads of such animals which have been found in the ancient mounds. The Mound Builder dog was a large animal much like a collie, and performed his chief use in the matter of defending the towns and forts against enemies. If a hostile force tried to sneak up on a town, under cover of darkness or fog, the watch-dogs would hear them and howl, long before even the keen ears of the Indian sentries knew that danger was near.

The Mound Builder carving of dogs, though sometimes gruesome, is very lifelike. Thus, one of the notable tobacco pipes taken from an ancient Ohio mound shows one of these large collie-like dogs gnawing a human head—presumably the head of an enemy, taken as a trophy in battle. 

It is not believed that the dog was a regular article of food with these people. However, in time of starvation, when the Spring was late, or disaster happened to stored food, the dogs had to be sacrificed to save the humans. Then, every part of the animal was used for

food; even the bones were split for the marrow, and the skull opened to obtain the brain. This latter has been a source of worry to archaeologists, who have found the bones so broken up as to make it extremely difficult to obtain a complete skeleton.

In the region of Arizona and New 

Mexico, thousands of years ago, there lived a race of Indians called "Basket Makers," from their great skill in the making of baskets and woven ware. Among these Indians dogs were very highly regarded, perhaps even more so than are fine dogs in our own time. Thus, R. V. 

D. Magoffin and Emily C. Davis, in the Romance of Archaeology, tell of the finding of the mummies of two dogs along with the burials of important chiefs. One of the dogs is a little spotted spaniel, looking little different from our own cocker spaniel, the other is a yellow plains dog, probably similar to the collielike dog of the Mound Builders.

These dogs were valuable pets, or they would not have been given such careful burial; they are very old, perhaps older than the pyramids. 

The wild, prehistoric ancestor of the dog is unknown. Wild dogs occur in the jungle, but these are 

European dogs which have gone back. Most scientists now believe that dogs had many different ancestors. Perhaps some were jackals which came to the refuse heaps of prehistoric man, finding food easier to obtain there than hunting for it. 

Some dogs probably originated as pup wolves or foxes, brought home by the hunters. Thousands of years before civilization emerged from savagery, the various kinds of dogs had become mixed, so that now it is no longer possible to tell what the original forms were. 

Another important object of prehistoric man's domestic efforts was the wild turkey of the southern

United States. Among the Pueblo 

Indians this bird was tamed and held as important a place among these people as does the hog among present-day meat packers. 

The wild turkey was not only a source of meat, but the strong bones were used for needles, bone awls, and a great many other small tools and ornaments were made of them, while the feathers were woven into feather-cloth, from which garments and blankets were made. 

In the Polar regions the reindeer was domesticated by the Laplanders and other wild tribes, in the very remote prehistoric past. These strong animals were not only used to draw heavy loads, but they took the place of the cow and sheep as well, by furnishing leather, meat, and every animal product by which people live and protect themselves against the cold.

In Oriental countries the water buffalo was brought into domestic use in prehistoric times, and this stubborn, almost untameable beast was responsible for man's tilling of

the fields before the ox. Elephants were used in prehistoric times for work and in war, and in prehistoric times there are even accounts of lions and of great dogs, especially bred for war.

Other lesser uses of wild animals tamed for man's use, both in prehistoric and modern times, are the use of ferrets for driving small game from underground burrows, cormorants used by the Japanese for fishing, and of hawks and falcons in sport. Babylonian and Hittite kings interested themselves in hawking and hunting long before they gave any thought to better conditions among the common people. Wall carvings in the ancient palaces identify these sports with the prehistoric nobility in many lands. 

Lastly, we must not forget the complete domestication of bees and silk-worms. For thousands of years before the dawn of history man's only sugar was honey, while silk, although it was replaced by other materials among prehistoric Americans, has ever been a valuable article in the Old World, particularly in the Orient. 

The Message of the Rocks: Once, years ago, as a half-grown boy, the writer came upon a little old man industriously chipping at a rocky cliff-side with a chisel and hammer. "What are you doing?" I asked, with that curiosity that seems to be part of the make-up of every growing youngster. "Digging out a fossil," he said. "It is the petrified body of a fish that lived millions of years ago. It died, dropped down into the muddy bottom and turned to stone." "Do you mean the rock was once mud?" I asked unbelievingly. "Yes, it was part of the shore of a great sea, which covered the whole eastern part of the country. The fish lived where we are standing now." "How did it get to be dry land?" "There have been many changes in the earth's surface since time. began," the old man explained patiently. "The inner parts cooled and shrank, and the outer crust was broken up. Some parts sank down, making seas and lakes; other parts raised up making mountains and high lands."

"Are there still fish like this?" 

I wanted to know. "No, there are none here, now, but in Europe there are some close relatives of this fish." "But how did they get into Europe?"

The Oldest Eggs in the World: 

Much of the knowledge of the long past is found where it would be least expected. A place of this type is the Gobi desert of central Asia.

The Gobi desert is a barren region almost unpeopled of living things and extending over an area of hundreds of square miles. In summer it is a place of rocks and dust storms and blinding sunshine. In winter the the marrow-chilling cold stalks down out of the north and turns it into a howling wasteland of wintry gales. The tumbled rocks are cut into strange shapes by the driving sand. Sometimes they take the forms of animals, like those in our own "Garden of the Gods" in Colorado. The Gobi is a place empty of life and anyone who travels it might think that it were one place on earth the Creator never intended should be a home for anyone.

Scientists, however, looked at this place and noted facts that ordinary folk did not. They saw fossils peeping from the rocks, and tracks of prehistoric beasts, and other signs which seemed to show it had not always been a desert. On the contrary many scientists thought the 

Gobi might once have swarmed with life.

Among the persons who held this view was Roy Chapman Andrews, one of the greatest living authorities on prehistoric animal life. He determined to go deep into this

desert and look for the prehistoric animal remains he felt sure were there. 

Fitting out a search party he went out into the Gobi and for months was lost to civilization. All the time he was exploring the desert country, making notes, taking photographs, and seeking the remains of prehistoric animals. Finally he brought his toil-worn party out, and a sensational find was announced. 

They had found the eggs of a dinosaur!

These eggs, which had been laid by one of the smaller prehistoric lizards, millions of years ago, were nearly as long as an ostrich egg, but narrower, and not pointed at one end, as are bird eggs. They had been laid in a carefully prepared group in a hollow of the sands. The careful placing of the eggs was taken to mean that the mother lizards were accustomed to sit upon their eggs, and not just merely leave them for the sun to hatch, as is common with some reptiles of today. 

But some disaster overtook these eggs. They had been abandoned and failed to hatch, and by almost. miraculous good fortune no harm came to them in the ensuing millions of years. 

No one can say exactly why the eggs were abandoned, or what happened to preserve them. The mother may have wandered away to a water hole and been killed by a tyrannosaur, or she may have been trapped in a bog, or fallen down one of the earthquake fissures common in some parts of Asia. 

Whatever happened, she did not come back, and the eggs she left became covered with sand, perhaps by a sudden sandstorm whirling across the plains. This sand settled down and made a hard shield over the eggs. More and more sand covered them, until in time they were buried hundreds of feet deep.

Nature does not always pile material in one place, and take it away from another. She is continually changing her plans, shifting from one form of action to another. What is a high place now may be worn into a deep valley a million years hence. The covering that had so faithfully protected the dinosaur eggs was finally dissolved by sun and frost and wind, and the eggs were once more exposed to view. 

Then, Roy Chapman Andrews found them, and brought them home to us, and anyone who will take the trouble to go to the American Museum of Natural History in 

New York can look at them and learn for himself just what kind of eggs the reptile monsters of many million years ago laid.

A Tomb in the Ice: For many years it has been known that huge elephants, called mastodons and mammoths, lived in the remote past. all over North America, and also in Europe and Asia. The great tusks were found in cold bogs, and

under peat swamps, and sometimes whole skeletons were unearthed. 

These prehistoric elephants were taller than any of the elephants of our time. Still, in their general appearance they resembled modern elephants, particularly those of India. Scientists could make pictures and models of them by using the modern elephant as a guide. It was felt that their likenesses were fairly true. 

One detail that gave them trouble, however, was in the amount of hair they should show on a mammoth. Some students of natural history believed the mammoth had been like the wild elephant of present times, that is, with a naked skin. Others pointed to rock pictures made by men one hundred thousand years ago, showing the mammoth with hair so long it almost dragged the ground. These people believed that prehistoric man drew his pictures from life, and that the pictures showing the mammoth with thick, long hair were the true ones. 

Mammoth remains had been found most abundantly in the cold bogs of Russia, and the ivory tusks gathered from them had been sold in the markets of the world for a long time; but the bodies of these mammoths were not sufficiently well preserved to tell much. about what the skin and hair looked like. The problem of what the mammoth looked like in life remained a disputed one until the year 1799.

In that year, a fisherman named 

Schumachoff, on the cold Lena 

River in Siberia was moving slowly across the ice, stepping carefully, lest he fall into a crevasse (kre-vǎs'). 

Ice fields are hardly ever solid, but are crossed and recrossed by deep fissures made by storms which rock the ice fields and break long cracks across them. These cracks are called crevasses. The crevasse is a death trap to man and beast alike; rarely does a living creature, falling down one of these deep fissures in the ice, climb out again to remember his experience.

As this fisherman journeyed along, looking carefully ahead, he saw something that stopped him in his tracks. The thing he saw was like a huge animal, but it was an animal such as he had never seen before.

It was larger than a seal or even a walrus, hairy all over, and withal it had a terrifying appearance, as though it might suddenly charge at him in rage. Frightened half out of his wits, he turned and ran back to the village whence he had come, never stopping until he was among friends. 

He told them what he had seen, but they showed little inclination to believe him. Some thought that his imagination was running away with him, or that the cold had touched his wits. It was some little time before he could persuade any-

one to go back with him to the ice cliffs, to see what he had found.

Finally, however, he managed to find a few brave souls who would accompany him, and he started to lead them back to the ice cliffs.

When the little party reached the place where the large animal had been seen, they suddenly stopped in their tracks, even as had Schumachoff, for there was a huge mammoth, thinly veiled in the ice, and seemingly looking right at them!

It looked so real and so terrifying that it was some moments before they could make up their minds as to whether it was a living animal or one that had been dead for thousands of years.

Finally, they realized that it was only a dead animal, which had been preserved in the ice and was therefore harmless, and then they went forward and began hacking away the thin ice still covering him. 

They found a perfectly preserved mammoth, clad in thick, long hair, mixed with wool, that hung down almost to the ground. It looked just like the rock pictures of mammoths carved by prehistoric man in the forgotten long ago.

They cut him apart and removed the skin. Inside, the meat was so well-preserved that it was devoured by wolves and bears and dogs. So far as could be determined, it had not been damaged in any way by its long stay in the ice. It is probable that this mammoth had been frozen solid for at least a hundred thousand years, perhaps the longest. record of refrigeration known to man. 

How did the mammoth get there? 

In all probability he fell down a crevasse while out looking for green grasses, or for the reindeer moss on which these animals lived. Falling down a deep crevasse, he would be powerless to save himself. He could not climb up the steep walls, for the ice was slippery and there was no foothold; all he could do would be to claw the ice and trumpet his rage and misery, while he remained trapped. 

In time starvation would overtake him and he would die. Then, the falling snow would sift down on him, filling up the crevasse, and covering him in his great white grave. More and more snow would fall, and the snow would become packed about him and make solid ice, until finally hundreds of feet of it would seal him in. 

There, unknown to living things, he would stand in the ice alone. 

He would wait unchanged, while one act after another hurried across the stage of human history. The cave-man would be followed by the farmer and the shepherd; great cities would arise, then mighty nations and empires, until finally, a hundred thousand years after disaster overtook him, the sun of a warmer climate would melt away the ice and set him free. Insects of the Past: The books dealing with prehistoric animal life place much emphasis upon the great reptiles and fossil fish. We might perhaps be inclined to believe. that these large animals were almost the only forms of prehistoric life about which we have any knowledge. Such is not the case, as was explained to the writer years ago, during a visit to a natural history museum.

The curator pointed out that not only is a great deal known about the insect life of that remote time, but that we have preserved specimens of even such frail creatures as midges and mosquitoes. "Look at this," he said, producing a lump of clear, honey-colored material from a cabinet.

The stuff he was holding out for my inspection was amber-a kind of petrified rosin, almost as clear as glass. 

Amber was formed in prehistoric times upon the bark of pine and fir trees. These trees bled profusely from wounds, and the dried sap collected in great balls upon the bark, just like turpentine hardens to make waxy balls upon the pine trees of our own time.

Insects were attracted to it in great numbers, and many were trapped in it, as flies are entangled in sticky fly-paper. Their bodies sank down into the liquid, which soon hardened, protecting even the they remained unchanged for millions of years, until curious scientists came along and broke up the amber and found in it these preserved insects. "Are these insects the same as we have now?" I asked. "Some of them are quite similar," he said. "Others belong to groups now extinct. The insects have changed perhaps less than other forms of life." The Caves: We have seen how prehistoric knowledge comes to us from the bodies of animals found frozen in bogs, and from skeletons imbedded in the rocks. There is another source of information available to us, which is in some ways even more interesting than the fossils. That is the caves.

Prehistoric man lived in caves, especially in northern regions, where the climate was cold. Caves gave him protection from the cold, which was ofttimes bitterly severe, especially during glacial times. 

Caves also protected him from wild beasts, although he frequently had to drive out the cave beasts before he could himself take possession. 

Cave beasts included the huge cave bear, with teeth larger than a man's fingers, the fierce sabretooth cat, whose eye-teeth reached down either side of the lower jaw, like hanging tusks, the cave hyena, and other hardly less dangerous animals.

That man fought and conquered these creatures we know from finding their remains in the ashes of his camp-fires and from trinkets and charms carved from the teeth and bones of these animals. the Doubtless, man inhabited caves for thousands of years before the discovery of fire; but not much of this earlier period of his existence is known, for without light to guide him he was unable to go back and such remains as he left in the shallower parts have been destroyed by the weather.

After man learned the use of fire it was a different story. He could make torches of resinous pine wood and go back into the dark depths of the caves. In some of the deep galleries stone lamps have been found which must have used a wick and burned oil, perhaps bear fat. 

Back in those deep caves man turned his attention to decorating the walls of the rooms. There were always artists among men, ever since the Creator made the human race. These early artists pictured the wild animals they saw the bison, the mammoth, the cave bear, and other extinct animals. In one cave in the Pyrenees Mountains there are even bison carved in clay. 

They are the oldest statues in the world, thought to be perhaps one hundred thousand years old. 

Some persons ask: "Why did early man make these pictures?

Were they for magic or for religious purposes?" The writer believes that they were not necessarily made for either purpose. Some, no doubt, were magical; others were perhaps just works of art. 

There is no reason why artists of that remote time should not make pictures for enjoyment, just as artists do today. In all times there have been persons who thought of beautiful things, and wished to share their thoughts with others,

and that is why we have art, and poetry, and music.

The writer has himself had some personal experience in exploring ancient ruins, and has been impressed with the intelligence and artistic skill shown by these ancient people in making their ornaments and weapons. No matter how far back we go we find three things that raised human beings above the level of the animals: a belief in a life beyond the grave, a desire of man to worship his Creator, and some kind of code of right and wrong. We want to bear these facts in mind when we study these records of ancient man, else we are apt to place an entirely wrong meaning on some of his works. 

Some of the things discovered in caves are almost beyond belief. 

Thus, in New Mexico, near Albuquerque, is a place called "Sandia 

Cave," in which prehistoric flints, and animal and human bones are found buried in solid rock. Some of these bones are those of the giant ground sloth, the prehistoric horse, and the mastodon.

People lived there thousands upon thousands of years ago, and after they were gone their flints were left behind. Then, dripping water, soaked with lime from the limestone roof, fell upon the relics and slowly covered them with a rock crust. It takes a very long time for rock to be built out of water in this manner; sometimes an inch will not form in a hundred years. When a thick layer is found we know that hundreds of thousands of years have passed since the cave-dwellers laid down their tools. 

Arrowheads found deep down in the rock of the cave floor are in many cases the same as those found at Folsom, a city in the same state.

They are different from any known 

Indian arrowheads. No scientist can trace any connection between these arrowheads and those of the 

Red Man. 

The Folsom arrowheads are the highest possible type of workmanship in flint. They are, in fact, the most efficient type of stone weapon that has ever been made on earth. 

We are thrilled when we find these "Folsom" points along with the bones of the ground sloth and 

Taylor's bison, which have been dead for hundreds of thousands of years. We realize that although the other things these people left have been lost, the one fact that they were able to make spear-points, knives, and arrowheads of this masterful pattern indicates that they were highly skilled and intelligent. 

There is no reason to believe that ancient man, whatever may be written about him by certain individuals, was in any way inferior to the persons of our time in the important qualities of common sense, ability to provide himself with the necessities of life, and to handle himself with skill and courage in time of danger. They lived in towns and villages, possessed tem-

ples of worship, showed respect for their leaders, and in many ways were much above many of the savage peoples still upon the earth.

In the matter of team-work and of acting together for the common good, primitive man perhaps excelled the people of today. Thus, there were many animals too large for the individual hunter to tackle.

These included such animals as the cave-bear, the sabre-toothed cat, and the mammoth. Such animals were hunted by groups of men, and there have been skeletons of these great animals found with many arrowheads scattered around them, proving that many hunters acted together to bring the beast down. 

Not all the rock carvings are understood. Some have to do with magic and others were made by the Indians. Many appear crude. Perhaps the crude ones were made by children. Others are very mysterious. 

One of these rock mysteries is found in a cave in Arizona. It consists of a carved figure of an animal that looks like a dinosaur a Tyrannnosaur or one of the three-toed lizards that walked on their hind legs like a kangaroo. We cannot be sure that the artist really meant to portray a dinosaur. We cannot be sure even that these great reptiles existed during man's stay on earth; but we should like to know how this ancient artist made such a remarkable picture if he had not actually seen something resembling a dinosaur.

CHAPTER X Why Animals Became Extinct and Their Importance

We have seen that the earth was once very different than at present, with many animals that once swarmed on its surface, now being entirely extinct. The question naturally arises: "Why did these animals perish?" 

One of the most important reasons is to be found in changing climatic conditions. Long wet periods favor the growth of certain types of animals. Later, when the dry periods set in, the wet weather animals perish, and other kinds are favored.

We can get an idea of how this would work were we to take the animals that live in the swampy everglades of Florida and turn them loose in the "dust bowl" of the western United States. Not one would survive.

The same would result if we gathered up a collection of desert animals and turned them loose in the swamp. Horned toads and Gila monster lizards could not live in the mangrove swamps of Florida.

Neither could the manatees, alligators and pelicans of Florida live in the western desert. 

When we realize that these periods of extreme wet and cold were not confined to small localities, but covered whole continents, we can readily see how whole groups of animals might perish through failure to change their lives with changing conditions. 

Another thing that influenced animal life was the glacial period when the ice came down out of the north and covered much of the northern half of the world.

The effects of the ice have not been thoroughly been thoroughly studied in the 

United States as yet, but in Europe we know it wiped out many large prehistoric wild animals, including the cave bear. 

During that period of mortal cold only those animals which could develop thick, warm hair were able to survive. Hence we find in the glacial remains of that time skeletons of the woolly rhinocerous, the hairy mammoth, and other cold weather animals. Man himself had a difficult time surviving in that period of extreme cold. He went back into the deepest caves and it was only by covering himself with warm skins and huddling over the fires that he was enabled to hang on till the earth warmed up again. Going back still farther in the scale of time we find the great reptiles, and here there were other causes operating to cause the destruction of many kinds of animals. (66)

67 Some of the reasons for the perishing of these reptiles have already been mentioned. Many of these animals had such great blundering bodies that they were more likely to become mired in a swamp or to fall over a cliff than to survive, as we witnessed in our study of the Tyrannosaurs and the armored lizards.

Examples of creatures that failed to survive are found in much more recent times. There was a bird on the island of Madagascar, not far from the coast of Africa, called the "dodo." It was related to the pigeon, but grew as large as a goose. 

The dodo in his island paradise took life very easy, so easy in fact that he quit flying, probably because it wasn't worth the trouble. 

He could get all the food he wanted in that land of plenty, without having to leave the ground. As a result he lost his wing-power and became as helpless as an over-fed pig. 

When sailors came ashore on

Madagascar looking for food, they had no trouble killing these dodos with clubs and every single one of them perished. 

Another example of a handicapped creature is to be found in the Irish elk. The Irish elk had enormous horns, in fact its antlers were the largest of any deer. They were so huge that they hampered the animal at every move. They caught in trees, and weighed him down so he was unable to get away from his enemies. Many persons believe that the Irish elk perished because of his huge antlers. At any rate, there are no more of them left alive, although their great antlers are still found occasionally in the

Uses of Prehistoric Animal Prod ucts to Man: No study of prehistoric animals would be complete without some notes upon the uses to which prehistoric animals and their products were put by mankind. 

Animals, of course, served our ancestors for food from of old. In the beginning man was forced to turn to the animals for food, as this type of food was the most readily obtainable. Not for thousands of years did man learn agriculture and the raising of crops from the soil. 

Aside from a few fruits and nuts snatched from the trees in certain seasons, man's original diet probably consisted almost entirely of meat.

Later, he learned how to develop and cultivate the grains, such as corn, rice and wheat, and with these advances meat from the wild animals became less and less important. There were many other uses to which early man put the animals. 

First among these was the preparation of animal skins for leather.

Other materials that the prehistoric animals furnished to man were bone and antler, out of which he made valuable tools of many kinds. 

Out of bone were made fish hooks, harpoon heads, chipping tools for making arrowheads of flint, chisels, hammers, and scrapers. Even bows were made of bone in many regions, particularly in the northern regions where wood was scarce.

Teeth served as ornaments and were often beautifully carved.

Among the prehistoric races of America teeth of such ferocious animals as the grizzly bear were hung about the neck as trophies of the hunt. The brave man who killed the grizzly bear hung the bear's huge teeth around his neck and thereby proved his bravery to all who saw him. It was a badge of courage, similar to the decorations now awarded heroic soldiers after a battle

Sometimes, particularly among the prehistoric Mound Builders, bear teeth were elaborately carved and sometimes they were inlaid with priceless pearls.

A peculiar use of bone, in the case of certain prehistoric Americans, was connected with the musical art. Thus, the Mound Builders had a form of the Pan pipe, which was in reality a simple form of the organ, blown with the lips. Sometimes the tubes of the Pan pipe were made of bone and sometimes of hollow reeds of cane and bamboo.

The shields of many a prehistoric people were made of thick animal hide. Hides were also used for baskets and bags, and for bottles to hold liquids. Even down to Bible times the skin bottle was used. In fact, in the Holy Bible when a bottle is mentioned, it almost always means a goat-skin with the holes tied shut so it can be used for holding water or wine. 

Animals have been used to haul carts and sleds and to lighten man's load from the earliest times. Not only was the ox and horse used, but also such animals as the reindeer, the ass, goat, water-buffalo, llama, vicuña (vi-kōōn'yȧ), and many others. We cannot say when the practice of making the beasts draw a burden started, but there are records of animals being so used at the very dawn of civilization. 

The dog also goes back to very old times, for no ancient civilization was without them. Just what prehistoric animal was the ancestor of the dog is not exactly known. 

Some persons think it was the wolf, others the jackal or hyena. There is a possibility that some other animal, perhaps a genuine wild prehistoric dog, was the father of all domestic dogs, and that this original dog has become extinct.

Only a few of the uses of prehistoric animals by man are listed here. Many others can be learned by studying more advanced works. 

In closing we can say that our early ancestors leaned very heavily upon the prehistoric animals in their upward march to the present form of civilization.
