PUBLIC HEALTH AND EDUCATIONAL SERVICES IN MICHIGAN* C. R. HOFFER Introduction 

Goods and services contributing to the standard of life of any group of people may be divided roughly into two classes: those that a family can secure by its own efforts and those that are made available only when many people cooperate to get the goods or service that are wanted. 

Articles of food essential for a proper diet, many modern conveniences, and personal items of various kinds may be placed in the first class. 

Their acquisition depends largely upon the ability and initiative of any particular family.

Hospital facilities, public libraries, good schools, churches, and roads are in the second class. The problem of securing these services, all essential to modern living conditions, extends beyond the finances and desires of families or individuals considered separately and involves questions of group organization and action. It is necessary to know, for example, how many people are needed in order to have a particular service, whether the people can afford it, and what are the best methods to follow in making it available. The farm family, or any family for that matter, does not stand alone in its endeavor to maintain or secure satisfactory living conditions.

Purpose and Method of Study 

The present study is a consideration of some agencies or living advantages that involve the combined effort of many persons. It is proposed, first, to ascertain, insofar as possible, the availability of certain services in the field of health and education to the people of Michigan. A second objective is to compare the availability of these services in rural and urban sections of the State, and third, to point out a few fundamental considerations which seem to be pertinent to the problem of extending these services to rural districts when it appears necessary or advisable to do so.

The method of study is primarily statistical. Data are secured from reports of State Departments, the United States Census, and commercial directories, their source being indicated when the figures are used. 

Generally speaking, they represent a cross-sectional view of the various services considered. In most cases, the unit of comparison is the county because a majority of the data do not permit comparisons for smaller areas. There are 83 counties in the state of Michigan. According to preliminary reports of the Fifteenth Census, 27 of these counties are entirely rural, that is, there is no incorporated town in them having a population of more than 2,500 people. Twelve counties are more than 75 per cent rural; 22 are 50 to 74 per cent rural, and 22 are less than 50 per cent rural.¹ 

It should be pointed out, though, that even if a county has a small percentage of rural population it may be important agriculturally. For instance, there were in 1930 a total of 48,982 farms in the 22 counties less than 50 per cent rural. This makes an average of 2,226 farms per county. In 1925, the number of people living on farms in these counties totaled 285,191.3

In the entire State, there are 169,915 farms and approximately 1,400 towns with less than 5,000 population. The acquisition and availability of living advantages for these people is a problem of importance to both urban and rural residents. The number of farms in each county is shown in Figure I.

Health Services 

Adequate health facilities within a reasonable distance of a family are an essential part of a modern standard of living. No one questions the desirability of good health. Too frequently, it is not appreciated or conserved until it is gone. The farm offers several advantages from the standpoint of health. Fresh air, out-door work, and sunshine are the heritage of farm life, yet there is need for medical care in the country. Census reports show that death rates of the rural population for certain diseases equal or exceed those of the urban population. Health defects are numerous in rural districts, as data pertaining to the work of public health nurses presented later illustrate. The services of dentists are necessary for teeth must be treated, filled, or extracted. Health education is no less important in the country than in the city. Healthy persons need to be taught to conserve health and those who are ill how to regain it. One indication of health service is the availability of agencies that safeguard health. As one looks over the figures in this table, certain facts are evident. First, there are more persons per physician, dentist, hospital bed, and public health nurse in the rural parts of the state than in the urban parts. A detailed consideration of the physicians, dentists, hospitals, and public health nurses follows. Physicians

It is not possible to state exactly how many persons there should be per physician because many circumstances will affect this ratio. 

Good roads, density of population in the area, the habits of the people in using physicians, whether or not they are general practitioners, and the practices of the physicians themselves will influence this relationship. The number that most frequently appears for the counties in this state varies from 700 to 1,000. The figures for 27 counties fall within this range. No county has less than 500 persons per physician except Washtenaw which is undoubtedly an exception due to the Medical School and Hospital at the University of Michigan. At the opposite extreme, there are two counties having over 2,500 persons per physician. These are counties without large towns in them, although the population of the smallest county is nearly 5,000 and that of the largest about 8,000. One of these counties has six unincorporated towns in it and only three incorporated places of less than 500 in population. The other has seven unincorporated places, four incorporated towns under 500 in population, and one town having a population of 795 persons.

To some extent people may go outside the county of their residence for medical services, especially if they happen to reside in the trade territory of a town located in another county, but, in general, the number of people per physician indicates fairly well the availability of resident physicians. 

Figure II shows in graphic form the number of persons per physician in various parts of the state. 

It appears from an examination of the data that the presence or absence of a town or towns in a county is more significant in determining whether physicians will be in the county than is the total population of the county. 

Few towns are without physicians. The proof of this statement is found in Table III which shows the number of physicians in incorporated towns of various sizes in Michigan up to 5,000 in population. Only 39 out of a total of 350 towns are without this service, and 34 of the 39 are towns having a population of less than 500. Custom and convenience have decreed that the physician establish his office in a town, though it is generally known that a great deal of the practice physicians in small towns have is with farm people.

If rural people do not live conveniently near a town which has one or more physicians who will serve them, two alternatives are available. They may try to get along without a physician except in cases of extreme emergency or they may co-operate in hiring one to live near them and offer his services according to some plan agreed upon in advance. In most cases, it appears that if rural people employ a physician they take what the nearby town offers and pay the prevailing charges which usually include a transportation fee in addition to charges for professional services. It may be that opinions will vary regarding what constitutes adequate medical service. Nearly everyone would agree, however, that at least two conditions are essential for adequate service: first, a physician should be near enough to reach the most remote family in a comparatively short time and second the physician should have the time to attend to patients satisfactorily. In other words, the number of physicians and their distribution are important questions. Concerning the first, it may be stated that, when the number of people per physician exceeds 1,000 to any considerable extent, there is a possibility that more physicians are needed in the area. 

Concerning distribution, it seems reasonable to believe that unless very exceptional circumstances prevail, every rural trade center having a population of 500 needs a physician and can support one. A high percentage of towns of this size do have at least one physician and some have two, (see Table III).

The figures in this Table do not give evidence that there is a striking difference in the age of physicians in towns of less than 5,000 in population. 

The percentage of physicians over 70 years old appear somewhat higher in the smaller towns, but there are too many young and middle-aged physicians in these towns to warrant the generalization that only the older physicians are found in them. It frequently happens, as a detailed examination of the original data indicate, that if a small town has two physicians one may be old and nearly ready to retire, while the other is a younger man who presumably will gradually take over the practice of the older one. Furthermore, an examination of the data shows that in the case of 55 towns below 500 in population having only one physician, 80 per cent of the physicians were under 65 years of age.

When the totals for the towns under 5,000 in population are compared with the ages of the 606 physicians selected at random from Grand Rapids and Detroit there is a distinct difference in percentage of physicians in the various age groups. 

All age groups in these two cities up to 50 years rank higher in percentage than they do in towns of less than 5,000 in population, and beyond 50 years the reverse is strikingly true. Insofar as age has anything to do with the quality of service that physicians give, it would appear that these cities have the advantage of younger men who very likely have been trained recently in medical schools. However, the experience of physicians in the smaller towns is likely to cover a longer period of time.

Dentists

It is evident from the figures in Table I that one dentist can take care of more people than one physician. He probably can give dental service to about twice as many. There are 1,044 persons for each physician in the state, but 2,465 persons for each dentist. The variation in numbers of people per dentist in the different counties is great. Three counties have over 8,000 persons per dentist while 16 counties have less than 2,500. Such variations as these figures represent suggest that demand for dental services is not well standardized. Some people go to a dentist much more frequently than others. Reference to Table II will show that there are more people per dentist, that is, proportionately fewer dentists, in the counties that are mostly rural than in counties that are largely urban. As in the case of physicians, the presence or absence of towns in a county seems to be more important in determining whether it will have dentists than is the total population of the county. Of the six counties having no dentists at all, only one has an incorporated town of over 1,000 in population. Table V shows the number of dentists in towns of various sizes.

Good dental service is essential in a modern standard of living. The extent to which people have this service is not definitely known. A strong presumption exists to the effect that a fairly large proportion of the population does not consult a dentist until it is too late; that is, until teeth have decayed beyond the point of repair and perhaps have had their damaging effect on health. When there are several thousand people per dentist, it seems logical to conclude that he would have an opportunity to do little of what might be called preventive work. Good dental service obtained frequently enough to preserve the teeth in a healthy state is a living standard that is as essential in the country as in the city. It has an important relation to health.

Some persons go to the hospital only when it is extremely necessary to do so. Others go in order to facilitate the improvement of their health even though they might get well if they stayed at home. The ratio of 500 persons per hospital bed has been suggested as a possible standard but there is no way to tell if this is the correct number. The fact that the ratio in 32 counties in the state falls somewhat below this number and that in 11 counties it is less than 250 indicates that 500 is amply high. As pointed out previously, the number of persons per hospital bed for the entire state is 292. It may be that counties having around 300 persons per hospital bed approach more nearly the most desirable number.

The fact that people living in one county may go to hospitals in another should not be overlooked. If a county is surrounded by territory that does not have hospitals, it may advantageously have more beds in proportion to its population than some counties in another section of the state where hospitals are more numerous. Crawford county appears to be a case in point. It has less than 250 persons per hospital bed, 103 to be exact, but according to the data given counties adjacent to it lack this service. Again, if a county has a town or city within its borders that is exceptional as a medical and health center, the population per hospital bed would fall considerably below 250. Calhoun county because a large sanitarium is located there.

Public Health Nursing Service 

This is the last one of the health services to be considered. Table I gives the essential facts about the number of people per public health nurse and the distribution of this service in various parts of the state. At the time these data were compiled, 1928, there were at least 337 persons in the state employed as public health nurses. Some industrial firms employed public health nurses but they were not included in this total. One hundred and forty-five of the 337 were school nurses, 61 city nurses, 49 county nurses, and 82 were designated as others.

Unquestionably, this service is more generally established in urban than in rural districts. Reference has been made previously to the fact that there are over three times as many persons per public health nurse in counties largely rural as in counties with more than 50 per cent of their population in the urban class. 

Since the work of county public health nurses most directly reaches people in small towns and rural districts, it will be considered in detail. The data for 1928 show that over half the counties in the state are without this service. Only eight of the 40 counties, 75-100 per cent rural, have county public health nurses. Five of the 20 counties, 50-74 per cent rural, have this service; while 13 of the 23 counties which are less than 50 per cent rural have one or more nurses employed in this kind of work. In addition, nine counties have public health nurses who are engaged primarily in tuberculosis work. The expense of employing a county public health nurse is not great and provision is now made by law enabling the board of supervisors in any county to establish this work at the expense of the county.¹

Library service like some others gains in effectiveness with an increase of the territorial and population unit involved. Theoretically, any community may have its library but, actually, it has been found that, if too iew people try to support it, difficulties arise when the service extends beyond a limited number of books. For this reason, the county library system has received an increasing amount of recognition and support during recent years. The essential features of such a library are: first, a central library containing several thousand volumes; second, branch libraries located at strategic points in the county; and third, a trained librarian in charge provided with an automobile equipped to carry books between the central library and the branch stations. The central library is usually one that is owned by a city or town but which through agreement with the county board of supervisors offers its services to the entire county.12 At the present time, 1930, there are five county library systems in operation. One advantage of the plan is the coordination of effort on the part of all persons interested in public libraries. Moreover, as has been pointed out, the county usually represents a territorial unit containing enough people and wealth to support a library at reasonable expense. When a county library system is organized every resident in the county may use the service. There is no discrimination or charge for use of books circulated by it. This phase of the plan is important, especially from the standpoint of farm people because a library at their trade center frequently does not loan them books without a special fee. The justification of the fee in the minds of the members of the library board is the fact that the library is supported wholly or in part by taxes from the town. Town people pay for it, therefore country folks using the library should pay for it also. The basis for this claim may be sound but in actual practice it has a deterring effect on the use of the library by people outside of the legal district supporting it. The

School Facilities 

People with high living standards demand good schools. Like other services considered in this bulletin they cannot be secured by families working independently. United effort is necessary in order to have them. Laws demand that school facilities be established in both rural and urban districts, but the extent to which this service may be developed beyond the requirements of law is largely determined by community influences. As a general rule, cities have more elaborate school systems than do rural districts, partly because there is more wealth and a higher density of population in urban areas.

How well people in various counties of the state have or utilize school advantages is shown in a general way in Table IX. The figures from which these percentages are derived appear in the Eighty-Ninth Annual Report of the State Superintendent of Public Instruction. The percentage of the total number in the school census, all persons 5 to 19 years old, actually enrolled as pupils in school shows considerable variation in different counties. 

Many circumstances account for this and it is impossible to describe them. 

Some of the more important ones are density of school population, value of taxable property, the presence or absence of high school facilities in the various communities in a county, the extent to which school attendance laws are enforced, and the attitude people have toward keeping children in school after they pass the age limit requiring attendance. The high school is especially important in this connection because, if pupils must board away from home or travel an unduly long distance to reach a high school, they are more likely to forego this advantage.

The figures are so varied in the different counties that no well-defined trend seems to be evident when urban and rural counties are compared. The percentages of enrollment in the group 75-100 per cent rural varies from 

60.2 to 92.3. In counties 50-74 per cent rural, the figures range from 61.7 to 89.3 per cent while, in counties less than 50 per cent rural, the minimum and maximum percentages are 50.9 and 87.5. Variations in the percentage of pupils in high school also appear, but again no well-defined trend is evident. Some counties in the group 75-100 per cent rural have as high a percentage of population 5 to 19 years of age enrolled in school as do some counties that are less than 50 per cent rural. 

These data suggest that the utilization of school advantages, especially after they are provided, depends upon social influences prevalent in the community. Some communities are proud of their schools and urge regular attendance in both the elementary and high schools. Others are not so interested. It is known, for instance, that in cities some families keep their children out of school to earn money just as soon as the law will permit. This habit very likely reduces the percentage of high school enrollment considerably in counties that have large cities in them. It is also generally known that a certain number of farm families have their children work on the farms when they could advantageously attend school. Custom rather sanctions this practice

Securing a high percentage of enrollment in school is a common problem in both rural and urban areas. It involves the creation of a favorable attitude on the part of the community towards school attendance and, in this respect, falls in the category of living standards that must be secured by group effort.

One manifestation of such effort is the kind of school building that a community has. One-room buildings are most numerous but they are not necessarily the most desirable. Their presence indicates that either the population and wealth of an area are insufficient to support a consolidated school or that the people have not taken steps to provide it. According to the report of the State Superintendent of Public 

Instruction for 1927, there were 6,506 one-room schoolhouses in Mich-

igan. Nearly all of these were in the country. There were, however, 1,394 buildings with two or more rooms located in rural districts. Four hundred and eighty of these were in counties 50-74 per cent rural and 617 in counties less than 50 per cent rural. If these numbers be divided into the total number of one-room buildings in the respective groups of counties, the resulting figure will show the extent to which one-room schools predominate in comparison with schools having two or more rooms. According to this calculation, counties 75-100 per cent rural have 

5.9 times as many one-room buildings as buildings with two or more rooms; in counties 50-74 per cent rural, the number was 5.7; and, in counties less than 50 per cent rural, 3.1. These figures indicate that consolidation in some form has made more progress in counties that are more nearly urban. 

The statement has been made many times that a consolidated school offers the best means of bringing elementary and high school advantages to farm people. Employment of more highly trained teachers, fewer classes per teacher, improved buildings, and a stimulus for community development are usually provided by consolidation. Two factors are of primary importance in forming a consolidated school district. 

They are sufficient enrollment and a large enough area to support a school without undue expense. The law in Michigan providing for the establishment of rural agricultural school districts which are a modern type of consolidated school embodies a statement regarding at least one of these factors.13 The act specifically states that the proposed district for such a school must have a total assessed valuation of at least $700,000 or a total of not less than 18 government sections of land. No statement is made concerning enrollment, but the average enrollment of 45 schools in 1928-29 organized under this act was 270. 

It is of interest to note also that the average assessed valuation for these schools in 1926-27 was $1,352,984.¹¹

It seems reasonable to believe, therefore, that 270 may be considered an adequate number of pupils to have in a proposed consolidated district unless the density of population in the area is unusually low. Possibilities of future growth, of course, need to be considered. In some parts of the State, where there are areas with a sparse population, consolidated districts may need to be formed with less than 270 pupils. 

In areas where there is sufficient population, the realization of this educational advantage depends on other factors. The chief difficulty is to get people to appreciate the advantages to be gained by consolidation, and then to arrange a proposed district so neighborhood jealousies and town-country antagonisms do not obstruct the plan. Good school work in the elementary grades can be done in a one-room school but it is more difficult. The most effective way to avoid such difficulties is to form a consolidated school whenever possible. With its establishment the way is provided for the realization of other educational advantages in rural districts. 

Towns and Living Advantages 

The relationship of towns to living advantages is not easily observed, but it is very important. In a previous bulletin by this Experiment Station,15 it was pointed out that there is a fairly definite relationship between the size of town and the number and kinds of services it offers. If a farmer lives near a small town, for instance, he will have to go to a larger one to purchase certain kinds of merchandise not available in the small town. Data were presented in the foregoing publication to show that only three types of specialty stores, drug, grocery, and hardware stores, are likely to exist in a town having a population of less than 500. If certain other goods are purchased, the farmer frequently goes to a larger town.16 

There is some evidence at hand as suggested in the discussion in other parts of this study to show that a similar inter-town support is beginning to develop in the case of services offered by hospitals, libraries, schools, and other agencies essential in high living standards, but it is just at this point that the role of towns in determining living advantages becomes important.

Once started a town tries to perpetuate itself by every possible means. 

Most towns want a hospital but few can have this service. Every town wants a library and fails to see the advantages of a co-operative plan such as the county library system offers. Consolidated schools are sought by all towns in a county although neither taxable wealth or school population warrant so many buildings. Each one wants the hard-surfaced road and so on. The result is that competition instead of cooperation exists and rivalries obliterate a recognition of mutual interests. If cooperation should prevail in the future, there will probably be one town at a strategic location having modern social institutions such as the hospital, library, and perhaps a high school, used not by the town alone but by people in adjoining areas and towns who find it advantageous to use these services. The population unit to support such institutions may be placed roughly at 10,000 judging by data presented earlier. The size of the town where they are located is of secondary importance if a good highway system is available. Until small towns and their leaders grasp this fact, efforts of people in rural sections to improve their standards of living by group action will be retarded.

Summary 

In counties which are 75-100 per cent rural, there are 1,072 persons per physician, 3,974 per dentist, and 767 per hospital bed. Counties less than 50 per cent rural have 1,065 persons per physician, 2,250 per dentist, and 246 per hospital bed. Three counties in the State have over 

8,000 people per dentist and 16 counties have less than 2,500. Thirty-two counties have less than 500 inhabitants per hospital bed. About 1,000 people are necessary to support a physician and approximately 2,500 for a dentist.

Counties 75-100 per cent rural have .50 volumes per capita in public libraries. In counties 50-74 per cent rural, the corresponding figure is .75 and in counties less than 50 per cent rural .62. The circulation of public library books in each of these groups of counties is 1.07, 2.22, and 1.63 volumes per capita. A county library facilitates the economical circulation of books in rural districts. 

The percentage of population included in the school census which was enrolled in school during 1927 was 77.6 per cent in counties 75-100 per cent rural; 77.3 per cent in counties 50-74 per cent rural; and 70 per cent in counties less than 50 per cent rural. Eleven and four-tenths per cent of the school population is enrolled in high school in counties 

75-100 per cent rural, 12.6 in counties 50-74 per cent rural, and 12.5 per cent in counties less than 50 per cent rural. 

In 1927, there were 6,506 one-room schoolhouses in Michigan. Nearly all of these were in the country. Also, 1,394 buildings with two or more rooms were reported. Counties 75-100 per cent rural have 5.9 times as many one-room buildings as buildings with two or more rooms. The corresponding figure in counties 50-74 per cent rural is 5.7, and in counties less than 50 per cent rural 3.1. Sufficient population and taxable property in an area are necessary in order to have an efficient consolidated school system.

Towns in Michigan are so numerous that some plan of inter-town cooperation must be evolved to make many important services available to rural people. Improved highways facilitate this type of cooperation.
