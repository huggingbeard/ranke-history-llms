PREFACE 

In writing this book principally for general accounting courses in high schools and colleges, the author attempted to satisfy a long-felt need. As a result of an extended experience with fourth year students in the high schools of New York City, he has concluded that there has always existed a scarcity of drill material available for the student of accounting, who desires a thorough and complete knowledge of accounting theory, principles and practice. Experience has also pointed to the fact that the material and problems and their solutions offered by the current text books on accounting are, if anything, rather discouraging because of their complexity of treatment. The texts are too highly technical, and almost too difficult of comprehension by the average high school student. Let alone the absence of clear and simple content and demonstrations, there is the added difficulty of the presentation of very little or no laboratory material to clinch what in the end may have been obtained from the content of the text. 

An effort, therefore, has been made in this introductory course to present the theory and principles, as well as the practice of higher accounting in such a manner as to invite the student to apply himself untiringly to the point of successful accomplishment. The factors that tend to discourage the student in his pursuit of a more specialized and highly professional training in accounting are eliminated by the simplicity of treatment and discussion, by the simplicity of language, and by the graphic and clear expositions and solutions of the principles and problems presented. Problems that are more or less complex are analyzed into smaller or less involved units. These are treated carefully and graphically before the complete unitiii

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING CHAPTER I BRANCH STORE ACCOUNTING 

Branch Stores. The tendency to-day of all successful business concerns is to extend their activities so as to serve the public in various parts of the city, state and country, and thereby use their increased profits to acquire outside profitable investments, such as stocks, bonds and buildings. The chain tea stores, the chain cigar stores, the chain candy shops, etc., are examples of apparently independent stores, which are in truth controlled by a head office or main store. These chain stores are also a means of creating a market for new goods or commodities, such as sewing machines, pianos, and electric machines of one form or another.

It may be argued that an agency can operate as successfully as a branch store; why, then, the added difficulties and policies incident to the organization of branch stores? The answer is that an agency acts simply as a salesman, securing orders and sending them to the head store. In return for the service rendered, the agent obtains a commission from the head office. A branch store, on the other hand, actually handles the merchandise sent to it by the head store, and has its own independent management for the sale of this merchandise. It decides whom to sell on credit, and it collects from its own set of accounts; and very often it pays its own expenses.

CHAPTER II JOINT ENTERPRISES 

In the days of long ago and before the introduction of speedy conveyances of transportation, speculative enterprises were quite a business practice. Business men were anxious to unload their supplies in distant markets and were willing to run the risk of losing some of their cargoes in the hope of obtaining a sufficiently large financial return in the long run. To-day this method of disposing of an overstocked supply is still practiced, but on a very decidedly smaller scale.

Joint Enterprise. This form of business is similar to a shipment or consignment-out business, except that it is more complex. By applying on a broader scale the principles relative to the accounting in a consignment-out business, such as shipping goods for $1,000 to be sold by an agent on a commission basis, the accounting for this joint enterprise will be found to be a rather simple matter. In organization this form of business is similar to a partnership, because the members of the venture or enterprise contribute their capital, labor and services and agree to share in the gains and losses of this temporary form of business undertaking. As in the case of a shipment to an agent, one person, an agent, in this particular form of organization is also assigned to conduct all the affairs of the undertaking.

Joint Enterprise Accounting. As in the case of shipments to an agent requiring a debit to the shipment account for the cost of the goods shipped and expenses of shipping, and a credit to the account for the net proceeds received for the goods, so in this form of undertaking a "Joint Enterprise" or "Joint

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 

Venture" account is debited with the cost and credited with the net amount received for the commodities disposed of. If the members of the joint venture are all in the same town, one set of accounts is all that is required. If they live in different towns, each member keeps his own set of accounts, obtaining the data for entry from the chief or business agent of the enter prise. Every member will debit the "Joint Enterprise" account with all his contributions, in the form of cash, merchandise, etc., and will credit the accounts representing the items contributed. 

The business agent will debit the accounts representing the contributions made by the other members and will credit each member's personal or "Joint Enterprise Capital" account. Expenses of all kinds are debited to the "Joint Enterprise" account, and each member paying these items, is credited through his individual account, his joint enterprise capital account. All sales are credited to the joint enterprise or joint venture account by the agent. If the agent retains the money, as in the case of shipments to an agent, the other members of the enterprise, debit his, the agent's personal account. At the termination of the venture, the business agent prorates or distributes equally the net profit or net loss and remits by check the proceeds due each member.

CHAPTER III WORKING SHEET 

Accountants prepare trial balances merely as an indication that the books are in balance and that all the transactions have been posted accurately. With the preliminary trial balance as a basis and in the light of inventories and other adjustment items reported to them, they proceed with the preparation of the working balance sheet. This working sheet contains a statement of all the necessary adjustment entries, an adjusted trial balance, a statement of income and profit and loss, and a statement of assets, liabilities and capital.

The working sheet provides a section for the preliminary trial balance; one, for the adjustments; one, for the decreases and increases of capital; one, for the adjusted trial balance; and one, for the balance sheet. By this arrangement it aims to present a picture of a general ledger most readily and most conveniently.

The handling of a working sheet is not difficult if an attempt is made to study it in parts or according to the types of accounts that usually enter into a trial balance. In this chapter three cases will be considered and when the types have been fully explained and treated a complete and business-like problem will be presented and solved.

CHAPTER IV  

Comparative Balance Sheet. A balance sheet gives a business man information, in a general way, about the present condition of his business; that is, it indicates the value of his assets, the amounts he owes on notes and to accounts payable, and the present worth of the concern. This simple balance sheet, however, fails to explain how some of the profit of the preceding period may have been employed during the past year, and how certain assets had decreased while others had increased in value during the past year. Assets are always changing in value either because of depreciation or because an exchange of assets had taken place during the past year. Liabilities increase and decrease from time to time. It is, therefore, desirable to report to an owner, in a more or less detailed manner, the progress which was made by his business by showing him how his profit of the preceding period as well as the profit of the period just elapsed was employed to strengthen and to extend the activities of his business. To do this effectively a comparative balance sheet and a comparative income, profit and loss statement must be prepared.

A comparative balance sheet is a report showing the assets and liabilities and the capitals for two successive periods, and the increases and decreases in the assets, liabilities and capital or net worth during that period. In this way owners and creditors have a basis for judging the progress of a given business. 33

Furthermore, in judging the soundness of the management of a business, the ratio of the current liabilities to the current assets on the one hand, and the ratio of the fixed liabilities to the fixed assets on the other hand, should be compared with the respective ratios of the succeeding year to ascertain whether the same degree of solvency has been maintained, and to see how increases in fixed or capital assets have been capitalized. 

In judging the solvency of a business, it is customary to compare the current assets and the current liabilities of a given period, that is, to determine the ratio of credit or the claim that the current liabilities have on the current or easily realizable assets.

In the illustration on pages 34 and 35, it will be noted that in 1926, the current assets amounted to $41,050 and the current liabilities or short term debts, amounted to $3,300, the ratio therefore being a little more than $12.00 to $1.00, that is, that there were over $12.00 of current assets for each dollar of short term liabilities. In 1927 the current assets were $49,600 and the current liabilities, $4,600. The ratio, in this case, was a little more than $10.00 to $1.00, that is, that there were over $10.00 of current or quickly realizable assets to every dollar of short term liability.

As to solvency then, the above business is not in as favorable a condition in 1927 as it was in 1926. It will be noted that in 

1927 the accounts payable and notes payable were much larger than in 1926, therefore requiring larger payments to creditors. 

But the assets in 1927 which could be readily realizable as working capital from which payments to creditors could be made, were not commensurate with the claim of the short term liabilities in 1927 against the current assets of that year.

The general soundness of a business may be determined from the relationship between the fixed assets and the fixed liabilities and the capital of the proprietor.

COMPARATIVE INCOME, PROFIT & LOSS STATEMENT Purpose. The purpose of the Comparative Income, Profit and Loss Statement is to indicate in one report the operating results of at least two consecutive fiscal periods. In this way a concern can obtain most easily and very readily, a comparison of the business operations for the consecutive periods. Not only are the operations, for the periods shown but the percentages of the respective items of operation are also indicated. The owner then can seek the causes for the increase or decrease, on a percentage basis, of the sales, operating expenses, the cost of the goods sold, and the like.

Explanation. 

Business men consider the net sales for any given period as the basis upon which they figure ratio of their operation expenses, non-operation expenses, operation income, non-operation income, cost of goods sold, gross profit, and final net profit. The net sales then are always considered as 100%. 

In December, 1926, the sales amounted to $28,000 (100%). 

What per cent of the net sales is the cost of the goods sold, $21,100? By dividing the cost of goods sold, $21,000 by the base or net sales, $28,000, you obtain 75%; that is, the cost of the goods sold for the period was 75% of the sales. Dividing the gross profit, $6,900 by the base or net sales, $28,000, you obtain 25%; that is, the gross profit was 25% of the sales. In like manner, you obtain the per cent that all the other important items of profit and loss amount to on the basis of the net sales. 

If the cost of the sales for the succeeding period, December 31, 1927, is a greater or less percentage, the cause of the increase should be investigated. In like manner, the comparison made between the other like sections of the income statement should lead to a careful investigation for the causes of the increase or decrease indicated from period to period.

In the above problem, the cost of sales has increased from 75% to 77%, and the net income has fallen from 30% to 19%. 

The statement for the period ending December, 1927, then, shows that the operations of 1927 were not as successful as those of 1926. The cost of sales increased slightly in 1927, resulting in a corresponding decline in the rate of gross profit on trading.

CHAPTER V STATISTICS AND GRAPHS 

Statistics Defined. Income statements, comparative balance sheets and other accounting reports offer to business managers many items of interest and information in general. The very busy human mind finds it difficult to properly grasp the full meaning of many of the facts therein contained. To present these all-important facts in the best available and most easily comprehensible form, accountants, today, attach to the regular accounting reports other aids, called statistical records. 

The statistical report contains required information in the form of figures or numbers tabulated or diagrammed in such a way as to make a certain definite item of interest stand out very clearly, in the most effective and economic manner. By means of the statistical method, essential facts concerning the operations of a business are classified and summarized, and the most complex facts are thus analyzed. 

Statistics deal with large masses or great numbers; therefore, comparisons can be most easily established and ratios indicated. 

Statistical Methods of Indicating Results. There are gen erally two ways of showing results of management, statistically; namely, (1) in the form of a ratio or per cent, and (2) by graphs, such as rectangles or bars, blocks, circles, and curves. 

The graphic method is the best method of showing results because it accomplishes what statistical tabulations fail to do.

CHAPTER VI  

Consolidated Balance Sheet Defined. When two or more companies are united into one organization or consolidation, it is necessary to combine all the assets and liabilities of these companies. A balance sheet is prepared showing the assets and liabilities of each company, and the result of the gathering together of the several assets and liabilities of the individual companies. The resulting balance sheet is called a consolidated balance sheet of the companies so consolidated. The procedure involved in the preparation of a consolidated balance sheet is simple.

Suppose Companies X, Y and Z, have a capital stock of $250,000 each. Naturally the capital stock of the new organization will be $750,000. Likewise, if Company X, has cash, $10,000; Company Y, cash $15,000; and Company Z, cash, $25,000, the combined cash assets of the three companies will be $50,000.

If Company X owes Company Z $2,500 on a three months' note, the consolidated balance sheet will show that these items (Notes Receivable, $2,500 in Z Company's Balance Sheet, and 

Notes Payable $2,500 in X Company's Balance Sheet), have been eliminated, because the cash of Company X contains the $2,500 which would have been employed to decrease Company X's liability of notes payable, at the same time, increasing the cash and decreasing the Notes Receivable asset of Company Z. 

In this event the final result at consolidation will indicate no change in the general condition of the new organization. The note becomes non-existent, and the total cash remains unchanged as between Companies X and Z.

If the uniting companies have intercompany holdings, or 59

HOLDING COMPANY 

A holding company is one which controls the entire or major part of the stock of its subsidiary companies. These subsidiary companies retain their individual corporate existence, and therefore a complete set of accounts is kept by each one of them. 

The holding company may manufacture its own line of goods and control the major or entire stock of its controlled or subsidiary companies, or it may be engaged only in the executive work called for by the effective management of these subsidiary companies. Steel industrial concerns and large railroad concerns are instances of holding companies.

It is customary to prepare for the stockholders or investors of holding companies, consolidated income statements showing the expenses and revenues of the operating companies in the organization or consolidation.

To prepare such a consolidated income statement, it is necessary simply to add together the items of like character indicated in the income statements of the individual operating companies.

CHAPTER VII INSOLVENCY STATEMENTS OF AFFAIRS, DEFICIENCY AND REALIZATION 

In case a concern becomes insolvent or decides to close out, some special forms of statements are necessary. The statements are known as, (1) Statement of Affairs, (2) Statement of Deficiency, and (3) Statement of Realization and Liquidation. 

Statement of Affairs. The Statement of Affairs, which cor responds to a Balance Sheet and which is but an estimate or an opinion, is drawn up, at the time a concern becomes bankrupt or insolvent, for the following purposes: 

1. To show the amount that the ordinary creditors will probably realize; that is, how many cents on the dollar they can expect. 

2. To show whether creditors should allow the insolvent business to continue. 

3. To show whether the concern should be forced into bankruptcy. 1 4. To show whether a receiver or trustee should be appointed. 

The receiver is an individual appointed by the court to manage the bankrupt concern. It is his duty to realize on the assets and to pay off or liquidate the claims held by creditors; to wind up the affairs of the business, and, if there is any money or other assets left, to turn them over to the owner of the business. The book value of the assets are always greater than the 87

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 

cash that can be obtained for them, hence an estimate is made of the probable amount that can be expected to be realized from the sale of each asset, when such a sale becomes a forced sale. 

Preferred creditors, such as a government claiming taxes and employees claiming wages, must be taken care of first, and liquidated on a 100% basis; that is, the amount due preferred creditors must be subtracted from the estimated value of the realizable assets before determining the amount that is available for ordinary creditors.

The liabilities are usually listed in the Statement of Affairs in this order: 

1. Unsecured claims or ordinary creditors. 

2. Fully secured claims, or creditors, holding securities belonging to the insolvent concern. 

3. Preferred creditors.

At the end of a period and after the preparation of the 

Income, Profit and Loss Statement and Balance Sheet, the above facts as to the realizable values of the assets and the probable liquidation of them, are recorded in statement form, as will be demonstrated in the following problem.

Deficiency Statement. This statement supplements the Statement of Affairs. It is customary to support the Statement of Affairs by this form of Deficiency Statement either in account or report form, to show the causes for the impairment of the capital investment of the owner of a business. 

On the credit side of this statement may be shown the capital of the owner and the deficiency or deficit estimated to exist and already contained in the Statement of Affairs. 

On the debit side are shown the items which have caused the capital shrinkage and the corresponding deficit. REALIZATION AND LIQUIDATION The Statement of Realization and Liquidation is some times known as the Realization and Liquidation account. 

In this form it may be shown as a controlling account on a page in the general ledger, entirely apart from the general accounts contained in the ledger before realization and liquidation. As a matter of fact, the Realization and Liquidation account is never used in actual practice. But because the C.P.A. examinations have always included a question on the topic, as a test of the ability of the examinee to analyze and present in logical form important financial data which are difficult to present in a simple form, it is deemed wise to consider the subject of Realization and Liquidation in this instance. 

One purpose of the Realization and Liquidation account is to show the activities of the receiver or trustee who was appointed to realize on the assets and to liquidate the liabilities, after the bankrupt's ownership had been appraised and shown in the Statement of Affairs. Another purpose of the Realization and Liquidation account is to present to a court a report accounting for the cash received for the assets disposed of in a forced sale, the payments made to creditors, the expenses of the

INSOLVENCY 

111 receiver during the period of realization and liquidation, and all those items of income that may have been obtained. The State ment of Affairs and the Statement of Realization and Liquida tion form a complete unit.

The Statement of Realization and Liquidation must show the following: 

1. The assets and liabilities to be realized. 

2. Additions to assets since the beginning of the receivership. 

3. Income accruing since the beginning of the receivership. 

4. Increases in liabilities since the beginning of the receivership. 

5. Cash realized for assets. 

6. Decreases in the assets. 

7. Losses on realization of assets. 

8. Interest charges paid. 

9. Receiver's expenses and fees.

CHAPTER VIII INVESTMENT ACCOUNTING 

Concerns that have surplus capital, or capital not required for the operation of their business, or excess profits, usually invest this excess amount outside of their business. The managers of these concerns seek investments which will produce an income equivalent at least to the interest or returns that any individual may expect from an investment of his private wealth. 

KINDS OF INVESTMENTS 

There are two kinds of investments; namely, 1. Speculative or temporary, such as stock owned, or stock purchased on margin, to be held until the market conditions improve, when the stock can be sold at a profit. In this case the stock purchased should be recorded on the books at the purchase price plus the fees paid to the broker, and not at the par value of the securities. 

2. Permanent income investments are investments the cash for which when received at maturing will be invested again in some other form of investment. Examples of this form of investment are loans made on bonds and mortgages; investments in stock of other companies; and investments in real estate. 

When a concern makes a loan to another concern and receives a bond or a mortgage, an account is kept for Bonds Receivable or Mortgages Receivable, a mortgage being but a contract of conveyance of property as security for the money loaned. This mortgage contains a clause, called a clause of defeasance, which states that upon repayment of the money loaned, the creditor, 123

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 

or mortgagee must return the property to the mortgager or debtor.

The mortgage or long time note given to the mortgagee must contain the following information: the name of the mortgager or debtor; the date when the mortgage was given and when it matures; the location and description of the property mortgaged; the state and county in which the mortgage was recorded; the interest rate and date of interest payments; the number, the term, the amount, and the date of the expiration of the fire insurance policy; the premium paid; the home of the insurance company; and the person who is to pay the premiums, and the appraised value of the land and of the building. 

BOND INVESTMENTS 

Bond Defined. A bond is a secured obligation, similar to a mortgage, promising to pay a sum of money at a certain time, with interest. It is in reality, a long-term note. Bonds are known by the name of the corporation or government issuing them, such as General Motor 6's, U. S. 3's, New York City 5's, etc. U. S. 4's, 1920-1945, means that the bonds were issued by the U. S. Government in 1920 and that they will mature in 

1945 with interest payable, semi-annually usually, at 4%. A bond differs from a mortgage in that in case of a mortgage the amount is a lump sum while in case of bonds, the amount is split up into smaller amounts, usually $100, $500 and $1,000, and runs for 10 years, 20 years, 25 years, 30 years, etc.

Classes of Bonds. There are two classes of bonds; namely, coupon bonds and registered bonds.

Coupon bonds have interest coupons attached. The coupons are payable to bearer, and are dated at regular intervals (usually semi-annually). They are negotiable notes promising to pay interest on the bond as it becomes due. Registered bonds are bonds that are registered in the name of the owner with the company issuing the bonds.

Price of Bonds. Bond quotations are not subject to sudden fluctuations as are stock investment quotations. The price of bonds depends chiefly upon the following: (1) the security back of the bond; (2) the time the bond has to run; (3) interest rates of money; (4) the earning power of the corporation issuing the bonds. Bonds may be bought at par, above par or below par.

Discount on Bond. Discount on bond is the amount by which its market value is less than its par value. If a $10,000 bond is sold for $9,000, the difference is called discount on bond. 

Premium on Bond. Premium on bond is the amount by which its market value is in excess of its par value. If a $10,000 bond is sold for $10,250, the difference is called premium on bond. Some accountants close these accounts into the loss and gain account, as a decrease or an increase of capital. The better method of procedure, however, is to treat these accounts as valuation accounts on the books of the company issuing the bonds below or above par. The Discount on Bonds account should be considered a debit valuation account, while the Premium on Bonds account should be treated as a credit valuation account. In these cases instead of writing off the entire amount to loss and gain, only a certain portion of the discount or premium would be charged periodically to the loss and gain account. This matter will be explained in greater detail in connection with definite problems later on.

Discount on Bonds. If the rate of interest on the bond, is higher than the market or current rate of interest, the cost of the bond to the investor or the amount the investor will have to pay will be greater than the par value of the bond.

Solution. To obtain the cost of the bond, proceed as follows: 

(1) determine the number of payments and the amounts which the company issuing the bonds will be obliged to make during the life of the bond to maturity; (2) find the present worth of each payment, or the amount which at compound interest, will equal to each payment that the company issuing the bond will have to make; (3) the sum of these present worths will equal the cost of the bond or the amount the issuing company can expect from the purchaser of the bond. To find the present value of a bond, the relation of the bond rate of interest to the rate of interest it is desired to earn, or can earn at the market rate must be considered.

AMORTIZATION OF PREMIUM ON BOND Amortization of Premium Defined. Amortization of bond premium is the application periodically of part of the interest earned on the bond as a deduction from the excess value paid for the bond, down to its par value. 

The excess value paid for a bond by a purchaser of a bond is really an interest cost to the purchaser. When at the first interest period, the purchaser receives a certain amount of interest, this amount is not altogether an income, because the excessive amount paid for the bond contained an interest charge paid in advance by the vendee. Furthermore, if the purchaser pays a greater amount for a bond than is indicated by its par value, at maturity he can expect cash for but the par value of the bond. The difference in this case then is a charge against the interest earnings accruing every bond interest period.

ACCUMULATION OF BOND DISCOUNT Accumulation of Bond Discount Explained. When a pur chaser of a bond pays less than the par value of the bond, he not only earns the periodic bond interest, but in addition, receives, at the time the bond matures, the full bond value. The difference between what he had paid for the bond and the par or redeemable value of the bond, is an added income or interest.

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 

This added accruing interest is apportioned according to the number of bond-interest periodic payments made during the life of the bond. By accumulating these additional periodic interests equal in amount to the amount of the discount on the bond and adding these amounts periodically to the purchase price of the bond results in increasing the purchase price of the bond to its par or redeemable value. In this instance, as in the case of amortization of bond premium, there are two methods of accumulating the bond discount; namely, (1) the Straight-line 

Method, and (2) the Scientific Method.

SINKING FUND 

Sinking Fund Defined. A sinking fund is the amount accumulated by setting aside, each year, a sum of money for a given number of years, so that with compound interest, the sums set aside will amount to a given debt due at a certain definite date. 

By doing this the borrower, usually one who issues bonds, will be prepared to pay his obligation in due time. 

Methods of Establishing a Sinking Fund. There are two methods of creating a sinking fund; namely, (1) the Straightline Method, and (2) the Scientific or Annuity or Compound 

Interest Method. The Straight-line Method, also called the

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 

Periodic Method, divides the entire debt by the number of years of the life of the debt, and the amount obtained is invested every year to the maturity of the debt. The Annuity Method is also called the Scientific or Compound Interest Method, and is a more difficult method of determining the amount to be set aside every year as a sinking fund.

An annuity is a series of payments made at equal intervals during the life of a bond or debt. Each payment is considered as a principal which accumulates at a given rate of interest, compounded, to the end of the life of the bond.

Problem. L. and M. Bond, January 1, 1923, at 4%, maturing in three years, par value of which was $60,000. What fixed amount must be invested every year which with interest at 6%, compounded, will amount to the debt maturing January 1, 1926? 

Explanation. To determine the fixed amount required each year as a sinking fund investment, compound interest tables

153 which will be presented later may be used. For the present, the method of determining the required amount will be illustrated in arithmetical detail.

In all arithmetical calculations, any principal or any unknown amount may be considered to be 100%. Since the present value of any future sum must be less than the future sum which is an embodiment of the principal or present value, 100%, plus the interest on the principal, it follows that if to the principal 100%, the interest, for example, 6%, is added, the total or 

106% must be equal to the future sum. Dividing the future amount by 106%, the result is the principal or present value of that future amount. In the above case then, the future sum for the payment of the bond is $60,000, to be paid in three years. If a fixed amount must be invested each year for three years, which amount with interest compounded must equal to the $60,000, it follows that by dividing the amount or debt, $60,000 by the annuity of $1 for the number of years the debt has to run, the required or fixed sinking fund will be obtained. 

Solution. The fixed amount to be invested at the beginning of each year must be 100%. The Sinking fund investment at the end of the first year will amount to $1.00.

The Sinking Fund investment at the end of the second year will amount to the additional fixed investment of $1.00 plus the first year's investment, $1.00 with 6% of $1.06 or $2.06.

The Sinking Fund investment, at the end of the third year, will amount to the additional fixed investment 1.00 and the second year's sum in the fund, 2.06 plus 6% of the 2.06 which results in 3.1836, the value of $60,000 on a percentage basis. 

Dividing $60,000 by 3.1836 results in the present value or the fixed amount to be invested in a sinking fund, or $18,846.59. 

This sum, then, invested each year will amount, with the interests earned (compounded), to the amount of the debt or $60,000. 

The answer may be a few cents out of the way owing to the dropping of the fraction in the above division. The last interest amount may be increased or decreased by this difference of a few cents.

CHAPTER IX COST ACCOUNTING Cost Accounting Defined. Cost Accounting is the method of determining the actual cost of every job or the cost per pound, barrel, yard, or unit of any kind, manufactured or in the process of manufacture.

Importance of Cost Accounting. When competition in any field of endeavor is strong, as is the case in our own industrial age, it becomes absolutely necessary for a business or factory manager, to manage successfully and profitably, to have a full, complete and extremely detailed knowledge of every operation involved in the process of the manufacture of the products of his particular industry. When the consumer will not buy unless the inducement is powerful enough, and that inducement exists only when the cost of the commodity is as low as it can be, it follows that the cost of production must be kept down and the difference between the cost of production and the selling price must be as small as it can possibly be.

To obtain this knowledge, it has been found necessary to introduce a system of cost records which will give the requisite information concerning every phase in the manufacturing process. That factory will succeed which because of a careful cost system and efficient management, can place upon the market satisfactory commodities at a lower price than can be done by its competitors.

Advantages of a Cost System. There are many advantages of a cost system. Suffice it, however, to mention but briefly the following important ones: 

1. It makes available vital information, such as the absolute 160

COST ACCOUNTING 

161 cost of the raw material, the direct or productive labor, manufacturing overhead (factory expenses and indirect labor), and the administrative and selling deductions. 

2. It offers this information so as to enable the management to make satisfactory cost estimates or bids for work to be done on future contracts or jobs. Manufacturers, publishers, and contractors are usually called upon to submit a price on their articles of production before the prospective buyer or firm will place a contract for the particular article. 

3. The information obtained gives the manager an idea as to the general efficiency of his factory, of each employee, and of his various machines. The manager is placed in a position to decide whether a certain machine shall be discarded or an employee discharged.

Kinds of Business. There are two principal forms of business; namely, (1) Mercantile or Trading, and (2) Manufacturing. 

1. A mercantile or trading business is one which deals in finished goods. It buys finished or consumable goods and sells them as they are. 

2. A manufacturing business is one that buys the raw materials and supplies and general factory supplies, and by the use of machinery and laborers, produces finished or consumable goods. 

Cost Methods. There are three general methods in use for determining the cost of goods manufactured. 

1. The first method is in truth, not cost accounting at all.

This method may be called the Cost Per cent Basis of Manufacturing. In this case, financial statements are prepared as in any other form of business, with the addition of a column in the Income, Profit and Loss Statement to indicate the cost on a per cent basis of raw materials consumed in the production, the prime cost percentage, the factory overhead percentage, the manufacturing cost percentage, the percentage cost of sales, the selling percentage cost, etc. This method will be illustrated later.

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING 2. Production Order or Single Unit method is the second and real cost method. In this case an account is kept in a cost ledger with each job. Each job is charged with the cost of raw materials and supplies, the labor involved, and the factory overhead. The result obtained in each job account will indicate the manufacturing cost. Examples of a production order cost method are: (a) a manufacturing stationer is required by the 

Board of Education to put up a certain kind of attendance note book; (b) a printer agrees to print campaign pamphlets. 

3. Process or Multiple Unit Method is used when the grades of the articles manufactured are such as to require special accounts for each grade of article. Examples are the manufacturing of soaps, leather bags, shoes, dresses, canned goods, etc.

Cost Factors. There are three essential factors or items that enter into the cost of manufacture; namely, raw material, direct or productive labor and factory expenses of every kind. These elements comprise the prime cost of the article of manufacture. 

To this prime cost must be added the selling and administrative cost and a reasonable profit, to enable the factory to continue. 

Raw Materials. All materials which enter into the manufacturing process as basic elements and become a part of the finished article are called raw material. Lumber for furniture, paper for books, cloth for dresses, leather for shoes, are instances of raw material.

Factory Supplies. Supplies such as cotton waste, machine belts, machine oil, cartons and the like, that are used in connection with the factory operations in general are called factory supplies. The cost of these supplies must be apportioned or distributed to each job.

Direct Labor. Direct or productive labor consists of the work of skilled laborers or mechanics who directly apply themselves to the machines producing the commodity. 

Non-Productive Labor. Services which cannot be charged directly to any particular job, but must be distributed on a percentage basis, are called indirect or non-productive labor. 

Services of foremen, superintendents, factory helpers and por-

163 ters, cost bookkeepers, factory engineers, etc., are non-productive labor.

Manufacturing Expense. This form of expense is one of the most important elements of manufacturing cost and is very difficult to determine. Factory expenses, such as factory supplies, depreciation of machinery and tools, factory taxes and insurance, heat, light and power, factory rent, indirect labor royalties paid to inventors of a machine in use, cost of materials in process, etc., are examples of manufacturing expenses. The total sum in this account is closed into the Manufacturing or 

Production Account. 

Books of Record. The books of record are general and special. The general books are the special column journal and cash book, the sales book, the voucher payable or purchase and expense book, the sales and purchase ledgers and the general ledger. The special books are the requisition journal, or record of all requisitions issued for materials and supplies in charge of the stock clerk; the job cost ledger or record of the cost of each job as well as the total cost of all the jobs; time book, or record of labor time consumed in production; and materials or perpetual inventory record showing the units of materials bought and used, and the balance still on hand. 

MANUFACTURING ACCOUNTS Machinery and Tools Account. The object of this account is to show the value of the machinery employed in production. 

The depreciation of machinery is closed by journal entry into the Manufacturing Expense (controlling) account.

Patent Rights and Copyrights Account. These accounts are kept to show the value of the patents and copyrights owned. If part of their values is written off each year, the amount of depreciation is considered as a manufacturing expense and is closed into the Manufacturing Expense account.

Raw Materials Account. This account is kept to show the cost of the raw materials bought to be used in the process of

manufacture. The result found in this account, the cost of raw materials consumed (initial raw materials, inventory plus purchases of raw materials, inward freight, direct labor, less the final raw materials inventory), is closed into the Manufacturing or Production account.

Materials in Process Accounts. This account is also known as the Unfinished Goods or Goods in Process account. The object of this account is to show the cost of the goods or materials in the machines. The difference between the initial and final inventories of goods or materials in the process is closed into the Manufacturing or Production account.

Manufacturing or Production Account. The object of this controlling account is to show the cost of the goods finished or manufactured during the past period. This cost of manufactures is closed into the Finished Goods accounts to ascertain the total cost of finished goods for the period. Deducting the final finished goods inventory from the total cost of the finished goods shown in the Finished Goods account results in the cost of finished goods sold which is closed into the Sales account to find the gross profit on sales.

Spoilage Account. This account is kept to show the cost of the materials spoiled or damaged in the manufacturing process. 

This is a manufacturing expense item. 

Variation of Weights and Measures Account. The purpose of this account is to show the difference between the book or perpetual or going inventory value of raw materials, and the actual or physical inventory value. This difference between these two inventories is closed into the Manufacturing Expense account.

167 Wage Systems. There are several wage systems in use in factories, the principal ones of which are (1) the hourly rate, (2) the piece rate, and (3) the differential piece rate. The hourly rate wage system is the most popular one because it is the simplest one for purposes of calculation. Under this system, employees receive a fixed rate per hour. But to get the maximum amount of work during the time for which employees are taken on, a great deal of supervision is required, and this is an expensive factory item. To overcome this difficulty, the piecework rate plan is frequently operated. In this case every employee receives a fixed amount for each piece of work done. 

The total amount such an employee can earn then, is dependent upon the quantity of work he turns out. The rate allowed on a piece-work basis is determined by what an average good worker can produce. Under the differential piece rate plan of compensation the worker is paid in addition to the piece of work he produces an increased rate for the increased number of commodities he turns out within the average operating time. For example if a workingman receives 36 cents an hour for six pieces of work turned out in 8 hours, his total for the day will be $2.88 or 48 cents a piece. If a more efficient and rapid worker in the same time produces eight pieces of work, he may be allowed 60 cents a piece, or $4.80 for the day of 8 hours.

METHODS OF DISTRIBUTING MANUFACTURING EXPENSES OR MANUFACTURING OVERHEAD 

There are several methods of distributing the exact amount of manufacturing expense that must be charged to any particular job, depending on the nature of the job. Among these methods, there are five very popular ones, namely (1) the Direct 

Labor Cost Method, also called the Labor Percentage Rate; (2) the Direct Labor Hours Method, also called the Man Hour 

Method; (3) the Machine Rate or Machine Hour Method; (4) the Direct Labor and Materials Method, and (5) Machine and supplementary Rate Method.

PRINCIPLES AND EXERCISES IN ACCOUNTING Direct Labor or Labor Percentage Method. In this case the manufacturing expenses or factory overhead is distributed on a percentage basis of the cost of productive labor. It is based on the theory that the cost of the manufactured article increases in proportion to the cost of direct or productive labor, and the greater the amount consumed in direct labor, the greater will be the amount of the factory overhead involved in the supervision of that labor. It is a simple method and is most frequently used.

Illustration. The ledger during the past period shows that the total factory expense was $3,600, and the direct labor $6,000. 

If job A required raw materials amounting to $250 and direct labor amounting to $400, determine the total factory cost of this particular job. 

Solution. To ascertain the per cent of the factory overhead that should be charged to this job, divide the total factory expense for the period by the total cost of direct labor for this period.

The per cent or rate of distribution thus obtained is used in finding the cost of the factory expense that should be charged to the job.

CHAPTER X NON-COMMERCIAL ORGANIZATION ACCOUNTING 

Concerns requiring books of accounts may be divided into two classes; namely, (1) the mercantile or trading or manufacturing concern, engaged in enterprises for the purpose of making profit and increasing their capital, and (2) organizations of a social character, such as clubs, public hospitals, charities, settlement houses, and the like, whose aim is not to increase capital or make profit, but to raise enough money in the interests of the community or for the general public welfare. 

Records of Non-Commercial Organizations. These organi zations require little more than (1) a simple journal, a special column cash book, and a general ledger; (2) a Statement of

Income and Expenditure; (3) a Summary Statement of Receipts and Payments; (4) Statement of Assets and Liabilities.
