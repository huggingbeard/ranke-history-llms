INTRODUCTION 

Not the dogs of war but printing presses of peace have been unleashed if we may judge by the tremendously large number of new dog books crowding the bookseller's shelves. 

The field of canine literature is becoming so extensive that it is necessary for him who wishes to keep abreast of what is being published, to turn himself into a reading field dog and range far and wide.

There cannot be too many good dog books, but who is to determine whether a dog book is good or merely "doggerel, so to speak?" What constitutes a good dog book is rightful matter of controversy, but I am certain that all agree with me that any helpful and scientifically sound book on how to care for the dog is a good book by the very virtue of the aid it may give to the dog owner in the care of his dog, whether newly acuired or already a longtime member of the family. 

It is not possible to write a book which perfectly meets the needs of both the layman and the veterinarian at the same time. 

And I would be convicting myself of small judgment, should I seek to substitute for the honorable and important veterinary profession. I shall try to serve two purposes-first, to tell the reader as a dog owner what to do until the doctor comes; second, what to do so that the doctor may not need to come. 

It may seem that the second is somewhat unfavorable to the veterinary profession but it is not really so. In many instances, a little learning may be dangerous but if a little learning is soundly balanced with logic, it creates a desire to procure more knowledge or to make sure of the knowledge one already possesses. Dog owners who have a reasonably scientific general knowledge of a dog's anatomy, the functioning of the vital organs, and the many niceties of canine nature, can understand their own limitations, and as soon as these are reached, will ask promptly that the veterinarian begin work. 

Not the totally ignorant dog owner but the dog owner who has a scientific attitude, is the more likely prospect to request the veterinarian's skill.

In a work of this kind, written not so much that it can be understood but that it cannot be misunderstood, and above all, [9]

a work that concerns itself directly with well-being and the very life of the dog, the first requisite must be correctness; and this correctness requires a sound, scientific foundation. 

The conclusions must be those in accord with the majority opinion of the veterinary profession, the laboratory, and the scientist.

As long as there are dogs, and particularly doting and overly-kind owners, there will be constant need of books on the care, health and handling of dogs. The dog owner who feeds the dog too much or too often, who feeds the dog too many delicacies, who permits the dog to make a nuisance of itself, who fails to train the dog, will make necessary the publication of books of this sort, prove the most profitable client of the veterinarian, and furnish propaganda material for those who do not like dogs.

The author has written a goodly number of books on dogs, all of which have gone into succeeding editions, some into numerous editions. It is his sincere hope that this present work, thru the basically sound information it presents to dog owners, will bring, restore or insure the health and wellbeing of the one animal out of all the species of the animal kingdom, who became "man's best friend" in the dim distant cave days and remains so in our own streamlined age-the dog.

CHAPTER 2 INITIATION INTO DOG OWNERSHIP 

To say that every home should have a dog is to presume too much. It is vastly true that every dog, of high or low degree, should have a good home.

Those persons who do not care for a dog properly, who do not keep the dog on their premises, who do not develop the mental ability of the dog thru training, who disregard the rights of neighbors, who permit the dog to roam about, who do not properly groom and care for the dog-such persons are not worthy of owning a dog and their homes should not be graced with the presence of one. They are the persons who make necessary the dog pound and the dog catcher. They are the ones who, instead of the dogs, should be thrown into the dog catcher's wagon and taken to the pound.

Today the emphasis is placed not so much upon more dogs but upon better-trained and better-cared-for dogs; and this depends entirely upon owners who realize the necessity of caring for a dog and are willing to put forth effort to this end. 

For months the family has been discussing the desirability of purchasing a puppy. Mother protests that she does not want her rugs ruined. The children insist that they want a dog just like the other boys and girls have. Father seemingly is neutral, but behind the scenes he slyly encourages the children. 

And so, the decision is made, the children shout happily, and father, beginning to realize the extent of the adventure, inquires: "Say, where are we going to get the pup?"

There are 109 officially recognized breeds of dogs in the United States. Which one of these shall the family, perhaps little familiar with the amazing variety, choose as a coming member of the household, one over which they will be much concerned?

Every breed is a good breed. The world around, a dog is a dog, whether purebred or mongrel, large or small. The 2-pound chihuahua of Mexico can shake hands with the 220-pound 

St. Bernard of Switzerland and say truthfully, "Howdy, brother." All dogs possess the same qualities which have endeared them to the human race thru the centuries-loyalty, devotion, unselfishness, patience, usefulness, and courage even unto death.

Unless one lives in a very small apartment, even the largesized breeds can be chosen; they do not run greatly, are mindful of the furniture in the home, do not need as much exercise in proportion as the smallersized dogs, and are especially considerate of children. We have been in homes where great danes were kept and these dogs seldom or never harmed the furnishings in any way.

The medium-sized and small dogs are more popular and naturally so, for they adapt themselves more rapidly to private living quarters, to automobiles, and to easy handling. But

For instance, one can say that the terrier is ideal. He is ideal, yet requires much exercise; he runs much; he barks out of the sheer joy of living; rugs and settees mean little to him in his gay life. And yet we heartily can recommend the terrier as a dog for any home.

Altho we go contrary to most advice on this subject, we believe that there need be little concern over choice of breed. 

The great consideration is that the dog, whatever the breed, once it has been taken into the home, should receive proper care, daily exercise, correct feeding and constant training. 

CHAPTER 4 BUYING A PUPPY OR A GROWN DOG 

Should the prospective dog owner purchase a puppy or a grown dog? He should purchase a puppy by all means. To observe and supervise the development of a puppy is to receive a college course in pedagogy and psychology combined. One notes the same problems as in the growing child. It is worth all the extra trouble to study the ways of the puppy, to surmise his fitful outlook upon life, to mould his habits and personality. 

There is the opportunity to teach him early in obedience, to mould his ways before they have hardened into undesirable habits, to give him the opportunity to build up a strong body for later years, to make him a member of the family, a desirable playmate for the children and an alert guard for the home.

All these lead to the conclusion that a dog should be purchased as a puppy and his education early directed entirely by the few to whom he pledges his lifelong loyalty. 

Perhaps the best age at which to purchase a puppy is that of three to four months, but up to six months is satisfactory. 

He has gotten his milk or first teeth at four to six weeks; by the age of three months he already should have had a worming, learned much from his brothers and sisters; had time and opportunity to associate with other dogs and to learn by imitation the many things which he can learn from other dogs

CHAPTER 5 ADVANTAGES OF FEMALE 

Shall a male or a female be chosen? Prejudice favors the male. Unfortunately there still persists the mental custom of drowning the girls and saving the boys. We are heartily in favor of a campaign in behalf of the female dog.

The author has written a leaflet entitled "In Praise of the Female," which has been reprinted widely, and in a dozen foreign countries. It presents statements with which almost all oldtime dog people agree, namely, that the female is more easily trained, does less damage to the furnishings in the home, is housebroken more readily, does not roam about as much, does her duties more modestly, is more considerate of children, is cleaner in the house, and in general, if there is any advantage in sex, she has the advantage. 

There is only the consideration which is placed upon the female of every species-the coming into season or heat. This occurs twice a year and for a period of about twenty days each time. Remember that at all other times, there is absolutely no mating of dogs.

During these heat periods, the female or bitch should be taken out only on lead. There is the desirable alternative that the female may be bred and raise a litter, thus adding to the family income.

CHAPTER 6 WHERE SHALL THE PUPPY BE PURCHASED? 

Where can one obtain a satisfactory puppy? A few pet shops are reliable and sanitary. But the best source of supply is a kennel, a place large or small, public or private, where dogs are bred, raised and sold.

Buy only from a reliable kennel, one which advertises in dog and other reliable publications.

Page 17-CARE OF THE DOG 

These kennel men or breeders are interested in satisfied customers as much as in making a sale. You can depend upon the accuracy of the pedigree records. You can depend upon dogs being in a healthy condition when purchased. The average breeder is interested in selling good stock which later on will bring more customers to him by reason of the satisfaction the purchaser has found in the puppy.

It can be accepted as a general truth that one gets in this world just about what he pays for. Pay a fair price, get a healthy, truly-represented dog, because you are acquiring ownership of a living investment that will repay daily dividends for a decade.

The dog does not understand words as words; he learns them as sounds. Every dog should have a name, a good name, altho he will respond to any name with which his identity has been labeled by accident or design. Further, the call of a dog's name, loud or low, harsh or gentle, by way of command or attracting his attention, has its supreme importance in training for obedience.

Therefore, choose a name easily spoken, without trills or the necessity of precise pronunciation. Agamemnon is a notable name but not worthy of a dog. A canine's name should be limited to two syllables, contain one or two vowels which can be drawn out into a fish peddler's call, and preferably

CHAPTER 10 THE FIRST DAY AND THE FIRST NIGHT 

Master Puppy or Miss Puppy has arrived. For our discussion, it is immaterial what is the breed for in almost every case, the family falls utterly in love with the breed and its representative.

It now becomes the best dog in the world because it is your dog. He is proud of you for he considers you the most important personage on earth. To him you are a god; your word is his final law. Nothing else matters than your approval down to the smallest item. 

The person who first walks across a field should have a care to walk in the best direction for everybody coming after him, will follow his path. So when the puppy arrives, his habits and reactions are determined greatly by the first forty-eight hours in the new home.

The new dog is as a billboard not yet painted. As in the training of the child, first impressions cut deep and stay a lifetime. Even from the instant of that first bewildered look about him, standing on stiff legs, trying to label in his mind the new surroundings as friendly or unfriendly, the puppy should be broken in with design, so that every routine is initiated, being visualized by you, the owner and master, as the daily involuntary conduct of the grown dog two years. hence. Train him early, train him steadily, and train him with finality, and continue his training to the day of his death. 

The new home is a new world to him-one of terrifying sounds and unfamiliar sights, and above all-of strange smells. 

Determine at once where he shall sleep, where he shall eat, what chairs he shall be permitted to use or not use, what shall

CARE OF THE DOG-Page 22 

be forbidden territory to him; and whatever he does which he should not do, stop him immediately from doing it. 

The very first concept to be forced onto his mind is that he must respect your rights and pleasures as owner of the domain. 

Where he can go and cannot go, where he shall sleep and relax and where he may not, what he is to avoid and what he may appropriate all must be according to prepared schedule. To fail in this matter of pedagogy, is to be faced in the not-distant future with a situation wherein an unruly and spoiled dog is master of your house—and he knows it and struts accordingly. 

The first night may be one of sleeplessness for him and alas, the family too! Have some sympathy because he is a stranger in a strange land, sick of the dreadful malady of loneliness. If he cries, let him sleep on the foot of your bed for he wants companionship; but do not make this a nightly practice. 

The puppy may be too nervous to eat or drink much on the first day, tho some puppies will eat too much. Therefore, allow for two or three days of digestive upsets before normalcy of appetite as well as behavior returns. 

His first day may be a holiday in your home but after it has gone, concessions, special favors and coddling must give way to routine and obedience. A blitzkrieg of discipline is not out of order. Firmness is to join kindness and all the psychology the mother employs for the rearing of her child, well can be repeated in the few short months of puppyhood.

Raise bed from floor Cement floor What shall he sleep upon? A bed is an important matter to a dog. After eating, perhaps the next most important thing to him in all his life is where he sleeps-his bed, his very own property that becomes a guarded part of himself. 

His sleeping quarters should not be at the top of a stairway, not next to a radiator, not on a cement floor (even in summer), not in any spot where there is a draft, and above all, not in a damp spot.

A wooden floor is an ideal floor. However, if this floor is in a large room or a room where there is usually a draft across the floor (as there is in most rooms even in mild weather), have a platform or box a few inches above the floor (about four inches). In wintertime, sidepieces from the floor to several inches above the bed can be attached to keep the draft away. The front side should be open so that the dog can get in and out easily and can look out and see what is going on a natural trait of an alert canine.

Do not use cushions or other stuffed materials unless the stuffing is cedar shavings which can be renewed frequently. 

Use rugs, blankets, sacks and the like which can be sunned and aired (and this should be done at least once a week). 

If you wish to use bedding such as straw or hay, straw has the preference as hay is brittle, retains odors and becomes soggy. Cedar shavings or sawdust is good bedding and also an ideal absorbent and sweeping cleaner; it is cool in summer, warm in winter. Newspapers torn into strips are a warm, absorbent bedding.

-and well so. Consequently your new dog soon finds the softest upholstery and maintains his position by stubborn presence. Be the pedagog then and there; remember that first impressions are deepest; stop the practice on the instant; with a shout and a slap, dispossess him. You can keep him in the basement, not too near the furnace. But either keep him in a place entirely warm or entirely unheated, either inside the house or outside the house.

Section D CONTROL AND TRAINING OF THE DOG CHAPTER 13 HOUSEBREAKING 

We hardly expect a cow or a horse to be clean in their habits if they lived with us in our homes even tho they had lived with us for many years. So when a dog comes into our home as a member of the family, keep in mind that he really is one of the animal kingdom and that he is doing surprisingly well in acting the gentleman even to some degree. 

Every puppy must be housebroken. Some learn readily and others stubbornly. Nature's call would be answered by the dog naturally, that is, openly and promptly at the time and place if the urge were not gotten out of his mind thru man's training.

Consider constantly that the puppy is to be instructed rather than condemned, borne with rather than punished. He does not know the difference in value between a Persian rug and a rag rug. Have great patience, keep a mop always handy, and indulge in an immense amount of hope, faith and charity. [27]

CARE OF THE DOG-Page 28 

Allow from four weeks to six weeks for completing his course, with provision for an occasional lapse and an extension of the period of tuition. The older the dog, the longer must be the course in charm and good manners. 

The keynote of successful training in housebreaking can be expressed as regular regularity. Follow a strict schedule for feeding the puppy day after day, and as the dog usually relieves himself soon after eating, take him outdoors about ten to fifteen minutes thereafter.

Logically, water should not always be available for a puppy. He overdrinks and thereby harms his stomach and wets excessively.

Take him along the same route each time. An ashpile here or a spot of grass there will be allurement to doing his duty. If possible take him to a spot where he has done his duty previously or where another dog has relieved himself. 

Important footnote-Train him at once to do this off the sidewalk for nothing is more disgusting to doglover and doghater alike than to see where a dog has soiled the pavement. "Curb your dog" is an act of good citizenship and good dog training.

If possible, let him loose on his walk. He has a bit of modesty about these matters and will do far more when your head is turned or when he is off lead than when you are standing by and he thinks you may punish him. That is why ofttimes, after you have had him outdoors for a long while, he comes into the house and immediately messes the place. 

If possible, take him out with older dogs, for dogs acquire this and most of their other information thru imitation of other dogs.

The pedagogical idea in all this is to permit nature to be the teacher; therefore, regularity-regular feedings, regular walks at regular times over regular courses are the best aids to your teaching efforts. Dogs really should associate outdoors and walks with a pleasurable doing of their duty. 

If he has committed the crime, perhaps upon your best rug, and he stands there with no remorse in his soul and only a grin on his face, save your temper unless the evidence of the deed is fresh, for a puppy has a memory about thirty seconds long. 

It is always preferable to catch him just the moment before he thinks he is going to relieve himself in the house. That

CHAPTER 14 BASIS OF TRAINING 

Obedience is the basis of all training. At first, the dog, like humans, does what he thinks he can "get away with." In many cases, the certainty of punishment keeps him from such acts as soiling, chewing a curtain, sneaking food from the kitchen table; and yet, most acts of the dog are done willingly, gladly, with pleasure and out of the great overpowering desire which every dog has-to win the approval, the pat of the hand, and the kindly spoken word of the master. 

Therefore, observe your dog diligently. Shape his natural habits and peculiarities into training accomplishments. I was unable to teach one of my dogs to push against a door to open it. A new swinging door was installed and of his own accord, he would push thru it with the side of his head and his shoulder. I began to call to him "Open the door" just an instant before he touched the door. Within ten days, when I commanded "Open the door," he pushed against whatever door was before him. 

The dog must obey commands and obey them promptly and definitely. A disobedient dog causes trouble to all; he dashes across the street, perhaps is killed by an automobile; he tears up the flowers in the neighbor's garden; he leaps up on people and soils their clothes with his front paws; he barks needlessly so that the neighbors are annoyed. 

A disobedient dog is a serious reflection upon the master

for, to a substantial extent, the statement is true "Like dog like master."

Begin your system of training early-on the day after the puppy joins your household. Maintain it day after day, indeed act after act, for one exception is like one fly in a bowl of soup -too many.

Obedience is the vital accompaniment of every act of training; hence we discuss it as the silent partner of every act. 

PUNISH AND REWARD PROMPTLY. When you punish your dog, punish him instantly after the deed so that he connects the deed and the punishment. Puppies have short memories. If the dog does his work well, always reward him; a pat on the head or a kindly spoken word are to the dog as gold to humans-the only pay he wants.

TOO MUCH SHOUTING BEWILDERS. Commands should be short in words and plainly spoken. The same command should be given for the same purpose at all times. Do not shout your commands or lose your temper or give too many commands at one time. Control yourself if you wish to control the dog. 

The dog should never fear his master, should not be made nervous by his master, and should feel that punishment given him is deserved. 

General, our doberman, has never been fooled, deceived or mistreated by us at any time; he places full confidence in us. He does not hesitate and never is fearful to go with us tho we be in strange places and do things utterly confusing to him he knows we will see him safely thru.

DOGS AND AUTOMOBILES. If your dog is inclined to fight other dogs or to chase moving objects, punishment may lessen or remove the desire. Observe the dog or object before your dog does; keep him near you so that he will not start upon the chase. In persistent cases, have a friend in the auto shoot off a tear gas cartridge or throw a bucket of water into the dog's face.

To accustom your dog to riding in the automobile, do not feed or water him within six hours of his first ride. Simply take him with you in the automobile. If necessary, give him a sodium bromide. Dogs learn to behave and quickly take delight in riding in an automobile.

In all general training matters, use common sense; use the same methods which you would use in training your own child. Always remember that you should look at the situation from the dog's viewpoint.

CHAPTER 19 LEGAL RIGHTS OF THE DOG OWNER 

The dog, classed in ancient times by Blackstone and other law commentators, as a wild animal and therefore without rights in itself or for its owner, has progressed to a sizable compendium of legal enactments, has become personal property in most states, and therefore, he who owns a dog, has in him the same rights he has in his other personal property, such as an automobile or household furniture. Here are merely a few general signposts along the legal way. 

To harbor or keep a dog not your own fastens upon you an owner's responsibility.

A dog has a right to be on a public road; the autoist must use every means to avoid hitting or harming the dog.

Dogs when clearly in the act of attacking persons or live stock may be killed by any one present at the time. 

A dog license does not give the dog or his owner any special privileges which the owner of the unlicensed dog does not have.

Dogs are nuisances and their owners can be prosecuted accordingly if they bark incessantly, needlessly, at all hours, have the habit of rushing up to persons in a vicious manner altho they may not bite, or if the owners permit dogs to render a place unsanitary.

Your dog can not be shot or impounded merely because he is on property other than his owner's. 

Unless you have a sign prominently displayed: "Vicious dog at large; do not enter except at your own risk," you are liable if your dog bites the mailman, a salesman, a visitor coming onto your premises (the exceptions are only persons who come without any good reason and perhaps intending to commit an unlawful act). The famed Roman twin words "cave canem" are not sufficient to relieve the dog owner of all liability.

A dog is entitled to the first bite (except in N. J. and 

Calif.). He acquires the reputation of being vicious only with the second bite.

The dog owner, with few exceptions, is fully liable for all damage or injury done by his dog when off the owner's premises. One can obtain liability insurance for his dog but we do not recommend that this be done.

CHAPTER 20 FEEDING THE DOG 

We come now to the most important consideration in the care of the dog. Training the puppy is important; grooming the puppy is important; other matters are important. But the dog's health is connected more vitally with his diet and stomach than with any other part of the dog or any other consideration of care.

Puppies can be received in the best of spirits and within forty-eight hours be ill of digestive trouble. The puppy eats everything, is always seemingly hungry, and consequently his stomach wisely revolts in time.

Much of the dog's pleasure in living consists of eating. 

The puppy indeed is the world's most pleading beggar. Altho his abdomen may be bursting, he looks up at you with wistful eyes as tho he were starving to death. You do him a great disfavor if he conquers and you give him something to eat.

GULPING NOT A VICE-The dog's saliva contains little pitalin. Consequently chewing is of little value to his digestive system and the gulping of food is not a vice in the dog. 

IF YOUR PUPPY VOMITS food just eaten, it is not a serious matter. Let him vomit-and eat it if he wishes, for it is clean and partially digested. 

HOW OFTEN SHOULD A PUPPY BE FED?-From six weeks (the weaning time) to ten weeks old, puppies should be fed five times a day. From ten weeks to four months, four times a day. From four months to eight months, three times a day. From eight months to fifteen months, twice a day.

GOOD" Thereafter, once a day (extra large breeds, twice a day until twenty months old).

CHAPTER 24 THE SKIN AND ITS CARE 

The skin of the dog demands proper care else it presents the most common of all dog ailments after worms and distemper. This is true because the texture of the skin is unlike the human's, having few pores for the release of perspiration with the consequent sweating thru the slobbering tongue, and because the easy and frequent contact of the skin with external objects such as dirty bedding, for instance, makes infection easy. It is to be observed that skin ailments attack the longcoated dog as commonly as the shortcoated. 

Skin diseases may be classed generally as mange, eczema, and rash. The vague term eczema includes almost any skin He may be your dog and cute too-but untrained, he's a propertydamager, a neighbor-irritator and the canine equivalent of a spoiled brat.

Rash is a quick breaking out usually in pimples and later in hot spots or small areas which may turn hard and black as the hair falls out-usually occurring in the hot weather season. However, these "summer spots" often are of fungus origin (external, and contracted from grass and shrubbery). 

It has been the practice of almost all writers to state rather parrotlike that skin diseases mainly are the result of internal conditions. In our opinion the condition is caused externally more often and must be cured externally. Improper diet and the presence of worms are contributing factors at times but minor ones, just as their elimination are minor phases of treatment.

Consequently, a necessary part of treatment is to keep the dog's quarters sanitary, to air, sun and wash the bedding weekly, and to keep grounds free from refuse and droppings.

The reinfection of a dog is easy where objects such as bedding, collar, lead, walls and doors contacted constantly by the dog are not kept clean.

Excessive heat or dryness of air indoors, filthy uncleaned quarters, failure to brush the dog frequently, bruises, bites, strong soaps used in bathing, chemical irritants, fresh paint, and as with humans, certain contacts to which the dog's skin is allergic-all these external factors may contribute to an ailing skin.

The dog, being a member of the larger canine family group Canidae, is a mammal of the vertebra or backbone group. 

Therefore, physically a dog is not greatly different from the human animal except that the dog walks on four legs, is horizontal instead of upright in build, and does not have as many vices.

The dog as a patient is sympathetically responsive to medical and surgical treatment; and because of the similarity in anatomy and vital organs, human and canine health and sickness have almost everything in common save that the dog never inflicts the bromide-"Now let me tell you about my operation."

Anatomy is the science which presents the physical structure of the dog. An understanding of the rudiments helps greatly in diagnosis and treatment. Here we mention briefly a few of the basic facts. 

The blood, bone, muscular, lymphatic, respiratory, glandular and digestive systems comprise the anatomy of the dog and are identical to those of the human. 

The neck, backbone and tail form the vertebrae, the tail having 16 to 21 segments. The backbone is flexible, cannot bear much weight, and enables the dog to move easily. There are thirteen pairs of ribs.

The digestive system is of prime importance. It begins of course at the mouth, continues thru the esofagus or gullet, the stomach, small intestine, bowel or large intestine, which ends with the rectum, a total length of approximately 16 feet. 

The windpipe and esofagus run parallel in the throat, the windpipe on the outside. The esofagus runs past heart and lungs, thru the diaphram near the backbone region, and into the stomach. Dogs have an appendix, the caecum, usually a catch basin for worms, infection and refuse.

The spleen or milt is attached to the stomach. The pancreas or sweetbreads are attached to the small intestine near the pylorus or stomach exit. The liver with its attached gall bladder producing bile, is located near the stomach and is the largest organ.

The three urinary organs are the kidneys (two, kidneybean shaped, attached to the backbone above the loins), the bladder with its handy muscles (sphinxter) enabling the dog to urinate frequently and for which he constantly blesses his creator, and the urethra, the tube leading from bladder to the external opening (in male thru prostrate gland into penis; in female, to vagina).

CHAPTER 30 DIAGNOSING THE ILLNESS 

Diagnosis is the examination of the dog itself along with inquiry concerning the dog's habits, diet, and living conditions in order to ascertain the symptoms of a physical condition and to deduce from the symptoms, the nature and extent of the sick or abnormal physical condition-in brief, to find out what's wrong with the dog.

A correct diagnosis is half the cure. The dog cannot tell in words where and how much it hurts or whether he has been exposing himself to contagion. The veterinarian is forced to rely greatly upon the word of the owner; and too often the owner, perhaps wishing to conceal his own mistakes and negligences, does not tell the whole truth; thereby he is harming both himself and his own dog more than the veterinarian. 

Diagnosis is professional; the dog owner never must be the veterinarian. Yet a few observations which common sense itself makes, are to be done by the owner. Otherwise, as happens not infrequently, the dog well on into the ailment is brot to the veterinary hospital for treatment; then a diagnosis is not necessary for the chief question is how long the dog can hold out before death arrives.

Oncoming disease usually sends its messengers in advance. 

The lively dog becomes listless, lies about, eats little or not at all. Eyes tend toward glassiness; the white of the eyeball acquires a blue rim around the iris. The gums lose their fleshy pinkness; bowel movements become loose or the opposite occurs-constipation; blood appears in the stools. 

Breath may turn offensive in odor; the dog has little disposition to play or run; the body, particularly the base of the ears, feels feverish to the hand; the nose remains hot and dry for a long period of time.

The temperature of the dog is a reliable indication of abnormal physical condition. A hot nose means little unless it is continued over a period of at least five hours. The average normal temperature of the adult dog is approximately 100.53.

Concern is in order if the dog's temperature is above 103; at 

105 a high fever is indicated; over 107, the dog faces almost certain death.

The temperature is registered by a glass tube thermometer inserted usually in the rectum of the dog, kept there for approximately one minute. The temperature should be taken three times daily and allowance made for excitement, recent feeding, and nervousness. 

Vomiting by the dog ordinarily is not an indication of a serious condition, but constant vomiting may be notice of a

The pulse or the recording of the heart beat can best be counted by placing the finger tips on the femoral artery, which is located on the hind leg, on the inner surface of the upper thigh. Also the pulse can be felt plainly on the radial artery just above the elbow (where the front leg joins the body). 

The count varies with age, the size of the dog and temporary conditions. The average adult dog has a pulse beat of approximately 90 to 100. Continued high pulse may indicate fever, heart weakness, internal hemorrhage.

A few or many of these symptoms may be noticeable; and if they continue beyond twenty-four hours, prompt treatment is to be given.

In the dog hospital, the uptodate properly trained veterinarian has complete equipment and uses it intelligently. He does not "flash" a diagnosis after a glance at the dog as it walks into the hospital. 

Clean hands, rubber gloves, proper lights, and a general appearance of cleanness and sanitary precautions are in evidence, as the neatly-gowned veterinarian begins to handle the dog on the table.

The stethoscope checks the heart beat. Eyes are examined with the opthalmoscope. The probe with light bulb attached checks the back of the mouth and the throat. The fluoroscope reveals the presence of foreign objects and broken bones; and the X-ray presents these in detail for study and permanent record.

An urinalysis checks on kidney and bladder. The urethroscope examines into the urether (which leads into the bladder). Vagina and colon are explored with probe.

The microscope is a valuable aid in detecting worm eggs in the feces and also in determining from skin scrapings the nature of a skin disease. An enema is both diagnosis and treatment, particularly if the ailment concerns the digestive tract or the presence of worms.

A careful check of the skin, area by area, is made to detect mange, ringworm or other bad condition, also the presence of lice, fleas, ticks and other external parasites. The coat is checked, particularly if lusterless and dry.

CHAPTER 31 CONSIDERATIONS ON NURSING 

Certain factors are important in addition to medicines and surgery in the care of sick animals as of sick humans. These factors pertain to sickroom technique and the physical comfort and mental contentment of the patient; they are termed nursing considerations.

Water for the dog should be fresh and clean, neither warm nor cold. Quarters should be cleaned daily. Uneaten food and used utensils should be removed promptly.

Examine the body minutely each day for swellings, skin ailments and unusual conditions.

Observe the color and consistency of the feces; whether the dog strains; also the color of the urine. 

The dog's quarters should be warm, preferably 60°, never over 72° (on the floor), free from any draft on or near the dog, yet ventilated to insure fresh air.

DISTEMPER-For countless years distemper, somewhat akin to influenza in humans, has been the scourge of the dog. 

Soon or late all dogs contact it, usually before the age of six months, and at times seemingly without contact with other dogs.

A persistently hot, dry nose, utter lack of appetite, watering eyes and running nose with pus discharge, hot, dry skin, lack of desire to move about or be interested in what is taking place, pustules on the belly, and an increasing fever, are among the symptoms.

There is no cure for distemper but immunity may be had in advance by vaccine injections, usually in a series of three, with ten-day intervals; the third is of the dried virus (actual germ). It is highly recommended that all dogs be inoculated against distemper at about the age of three months. The inoculation is effective in 90 per cent of the cases. 

Treatment of an actual distemper case is to keep the dog in a dry, draftless but ventilated place, force food down the throat, keep bowels open, give tonic or brandy. In two of three cases, the dog survives, dependent upon his reserve of stamina. The use of internal medicines help materially in that they build up the dog's natural resistance.

Distemper in itself leaves few scars; but almost every case permits other diseases to gain headway at the same timepneumonia, chorea, colitis, impaired eyesight, yellow-color distemper teeth, and bronchial trouble. Consequently it can be seen that the maintaining of natural reserves in actual cases is important; and above all, inoculation should be given to avoid these entirely.

An understanding of fits is much in order for countless thousands of dogs have been murdered by police officers and others when the dogs had not rabies, as ignorantly suspected, but an attack of fits, a harmless disease if it may be classed as a disease. 

The dog walks unsteadily, staggers, may even fall on his side; he moves his legs rapidly as tho pedaling. The eye becomes stiff and glassy. The mouth froths and slobbers. The dog may bark incessantly with a sharp voice. He may run recklessly, perhaps pushing into a corner or under an object, continuing to bark until he falls over. The fits may occur several times daily and each attack last for a period of five to thirty minutes.

This dog is not dangerous; he has running fits. The precise causes are not known but a cure has been come upon recently. 

At times it is epidemic in a community; there is some evidence that it may be contagious; whether it is a separate disease, germ-caused, or an attendant of certain conditions is undetermined.

Usually it attacks nervous dogs, younger dogs, improperly fed dogs, dogs suffering from digestive troubles, dogs in need of worming-but there are many exceptions. It is seldom fatal and almost all dogs grow out of it after several months. 

If a dog bites when suffering a fit, the bite is unintended and is exactly the same as an ordinary bite, without danger except from ordinary infection-and this seldom occurs. 

A change of diet, a greatly reduced diet, a fast for twentyfour hours, a quiet, darkened resting place, a complete physicing, a diet principally of lean uncooked meat, a Vitamin B, or protein injection-all help and in time effect a cure. 

But a chief consideration to be remembered is that fits is a curable disease, has no connection with rabies, that the dog is to be sympathized with, not shot to death, and that often the start of the fit condition is to be blamed upon the owner and his poor care of the dog.

GASTRITIS-inflammation of the lining of the stomach. 

Vomiting and excessive thirst, foul breath, distended abdomen, haggard expression are among the symptoms. 

Fast the dog for twenty-four hours. No water to drink;

give buttermilk, barley broth, sour milk. Keep dog warm at all times. Feed mostly lean meat slightly cooked. Give internal antiseptic such as milk of magnesia or kaolin daily. 

Sulfaguonadine, one of the sulfa's, is highly effective in treating colitis (colon bacilli) and also the allied ailment enteritis, and to a certain extent, dysentery and chronic diarrhea.

PNEUMONIA inflames the membranes of the lungs and the bronchial tubes leading into it. The disease is dangerous and often fatal. Give the treatment as described for colds and bronchitis; keep a pneumonia jacket of wool or flannel around as much of the dog's body as possible. Rub camphorated oil or similar preparation on the neck and chest, getting it down to the skin. 

Give the dog constant care; he needs it. He has a worried look, sits on his haunches with front feet wide apart, changes location frequently. One can feel and almost hear a rumbling, boiling movement within the dog's chest. If all these things can be detected early, rush the dog to the veterinarian for a sulfa treatment.

STOPPAGE OF THE BOWELS, when total and contin ued, is not constipation. The intestines are harboring an obstruction of wood, metal, rubber, toy, hair ball or the like, which causes vomiting, loss of weight, intense stomach pain, and finally gangrene of the tract where the obstruction is located and death.

A rectal enema may start the obstruction moving; do not give purgatives. Food should be liquid such as meat broth. 

An operation by the veterinarian is indicated in most cases. 

One cure is to avoid a common cause-do not give your dog rubber balls or toys small enuf to be swallowed.

VOMITING-unless a symptom of dysentery, bowel stoppage, or the like, do not worry greatly. If poison causes it, more vomiting is in order. The mere vomiting of food just eaten indicates hasty eating; let the dog eat its vomit.

WEIL'S DISEASE-akin to leptospirosis or yellow jaundice. Dog's eyes (whites) and membranes of mouth take on a yellowish hue. A serious but very rare ailment, usually contracted by the dog from the urine of rats.

WORMS-Internal parasites or worms present, along with skin diseases, the two most common and continuing canine ailments.

These worms, for the most part, develop from eggs swallowed by the dog, which eggs develop in the blood stream, and then live in the intestinal tract from the stomach to the caecum or appendix.

RINGWORM is not a worm disease but an external para sitic growth. HOT SPOTS or SUMMER SORES are likewise of fungus or external growth. COCCIDIOSIS is not strictly a worm ailment.

Worms in dogs, in order of prevalence, are-round (ascaris mystax), tape, hook, heart and whip.

In general it may be said that all dogs have worms, that puppies suffer most from them because they constantly are sniffing at and picking up objects with the mouth, and that puppies may be born wiht round worms. All animal life has its curse of worm infestation; hygiene and medical care have lessened it almost to the zero point in humans. 

Prompt removal of the bowel droppings and keeping the dog from contact with the droppings of other dogs, plus general cleanliness of the dog and good sanitation in the dog's living quarters help greatly to lessen the likelihood of worm infestation. 

Symptoms of the presence of worms vary. Segments of the flat tape worm, or living or dead round and other worms in the bowel movements, are very definite. The feces may be examined under microscope for worm eggs. It is mostly through the swallowing of worm eggs, principally from bowel droppings, that infestation occurs.

Bad breath, dry skin, "musty" coat, a vomiting, coughing

sound, and a general listlessness or lack of liveliness-these may indicate the presence of worms.

In general, every dog should be wormed at the age of ten weeks, six months and twelve months; thereafter once a year. 

Old dogs continue to harbor worms. 

Worms on an empty stomach and digestive tract. Feed the dog as usual in the afternoon; do not feed next morning but give worm treatments. Follow with a general laxative (milk of magnesia, calomel, castor oil, mineral oil); feed about three hours after the physicking. Repeat this entire treatment two weeks later for the complete de-worming. 

Purchase any of the standard worm preparations from the druggist (or have your veterinarian worm the dog in his hospital). Follow instruction carefully; underdose rather than overdose. 

Worm medicines (anthelmintics) are "strong" drugs. Do not worm-treat a very sick or very-low-vitality dog unless there is no other alternative. The drug preparations for removing roundworms will not remove tapeworms. 

ROUND WORMS are white or with pinkish tinge, shaped like wrapping twine; they may vary from one to ten inches long. The dog may harbor dozens at a time. 

TAPE WORMS may drag a dog down to skin and bones.

In length they may measure as much as thirty inches. They grow in flat, ribbonlike sections, and the one head, embedded in the lining of the intestine must be removed, else the worm continues to grow, tho many segments pass thru the bowels. 

HOOK WORMS are threadlike in size and appearance, about an inch long, embed themselves in the lining of the intestines, are blood suckers, and may bring death to the dog. 

Constant treatment is necessary to remove them. 

HEARTWORMS have spread greatly in recent years. As the name indicates, it is a threadlike worm, of varying lengths; dozens may lodge in the heart itself, until the dog comes to its death.

If the heartworm is detected early, a treatment of the drug feuadin usually is successful; in later stages, death follows. Inability of the dog to run speedily any distance without losing breath and being forced to lie down and rest, is a definite symptom. 

WHIPWORMS have the size and appearance of hookworms and usually lodge in the appendix or caecum, where

One of the noblest professions is that of the veterinarian; he dedicates his life to the relieving of pain in the dumb creation, which cannot speak for itself.

CHAPTER 38 THE OLD DOG 

When the Creator ordained the respective spans of life for man and his dog, tragedy was provided for in that the spans do not run parallel. The puppy which romped with the babies, has grown gray on the muzzle and reached a ripe old age when the same children barely have come into their teens. For each year of his life, seven of yours are represented; the ten-year old dog is on a par with the seventy-year old human. 

Evidence of nearness to the end of his course of life is given plainly. Watery eyes, asthmatic coughing, broken teeth, labored puffing and stiff legs almost unto lameness make him a pathetic figure, but the same devotion and the memory of happier years are shown in the dimming eyes. He still is eager to retrieve the ball you throw but no longer do you expect him to get it on the first bounce. 

There is no cure for the stiffness, rheumatism and light paralysis. Massaging, good food chopped into small pieces, sleeping in the sun, help a little. Keep him out of drafts. Wipe him very dry after his run in wet weather for dampness brings misery. A constant treatment of Fowler's solution of

arsenic lessens the paralysis a bit. Boric acid water or argerol solution daily helps the eyes. 

Treat him kindly, carry him upstairs if necessary, give him an extra pat of reassurance that you love him as much as in days gone by.

Sudden changes arouse the dogs interest to the point of fright. Thunder and lightning have been aggravations to the dog since the first member of his family roamed the woods. 

Gunfire, booming sounds, sudden noises put him on the alert and if the dog be of a nervous nature, may throw him into hysterical fright.

Bombings and blackouts in England presented an interesting study of the mental reactions of dogs and other animals. 

Almost all dogs soon acquired human habits- became alert, realized that emergencies were at hand, and acted selfpossessed in the shelters; in truth, creature of habit, some dogs started promptly to the dugouts at the first sound of bombings.
