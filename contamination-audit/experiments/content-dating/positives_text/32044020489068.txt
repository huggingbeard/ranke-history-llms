SUMMARY 

5-26-58 -111- The two major interruptions in the supply of oil from the Middle East which have occurred in the present decade, in Iran in 1951 and at Suez in 1956, resolved themselves into shipping crises which were met by reorganiza+ tion of the tanker facilities of the western international oil companies. 

This report surveys the effects of these crises on world petroleum production and shipping and examines the impact of a hypothetical future interruption of the flow of Middle Eastern oil to the western world. 

The western boycott of Iranian oil and the virtual shutdown of the Abadan refinery in 1951 after the nationalization of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company eliminated the source for 7 per cent of the non-Soviet world's crude petroleum and more than 25 per cent of all refined products supplied outside the Western Hemisphere. Although most of the decline in Iranian crude production was compensated for by increases in other areas of the Middle East and in the Western Hemisphere, a major share of the burden of meeting the shortage of refined products, particularly east of Suez, fell upon U.S. refineries and U.S. shipping.

The blockage of the Suez Canal and destruction of the Iraq Petroleum Company's pipeline pumping stations in Syria following the Anglo-French- Israeli attack on Egypt in 1956 cut off two-third's of the immediate flow of oil to Europe, and required an organized international effort to supply oil to Western Europe. This entailed substitution of Western Hemisphere 

(U.S. Gulf and Caribbean) oil for Middle East oil and a rerouting of some 

275 tankers (T-2 equivalents) to carry oil from Western Hemisphere sources of supply instead of from Middle East sources of supply. On the destination

RM-2185 5-26-58 -iv- side, about 175 tankers were shifted to European deliveries from East Coast 

North American deliveries.

Transit capacity for oil from the Middle East to the Mediterranean, via existing pipelines and the Suez Canal in its present state of improvement, is 2.5 million bbls/day. Assuming that the old Suez Canal Company's last improvement program is carried out and that one additional trans-Middle East pipeline is built to Iskenderun, Turkey's southernmost Mediterranean port, transit capacity to the Mediterranean would increase to 3.3 million bbls/day. 

The world tanker fleet in 1956 came to the equivalent of about 2,600 T-2 tankers. Petroleum industry estimates of tankers on order forecast a fleet by the end of 1965 of about 4,880 T-2 tankers. At the level of demand for oil expected at that date, this number is a little greater than might be required under normal circumstances. During the Suez crisis there was some talk of a tanker "crash program" to build an additional 900 T-2 equivalents in the form of 300 supertankers of 60,000 dwt each. The significance of this level of emergency capacity may be seen by carrying through a hypothetical blocking of trans-Arab Middle East pipelines and the Suez Canal in 1965. If such a crisis did occur, the additional tanker capacity required to carry Middle East oil to Europe around the Cape of Good Hope would come to an additional 1,000 T-2 equivalents, or somewhat more than the still-born crash program was to build. 

It would therefore appear that a Middle East oil crisis any time in the next few years is likely to bring about an attempt to solve it in the same way as the Abadan and Suez crises were solved by substituting Western Hemisphere for Middle East supplies, since the additional tankers necessary to carry the Middle East oil are not being built. The success of such an 11

RM 5-26-58 -V- attempt would depend on the availability of additional Western Hemisphere supplies.

MIDDLE EAST CRISES AND WORLD PETROLEUM MOVEMENTS 

5-26-58 -1- Twice in the present decade there has been a major disruption of the flow of Middle East oil to the non-Soviet world; and given the rising inten- 

Bity of nationalist and local imperialist sentiment in the Middle East, further interruptions are to be expected. At the time of the last crisis, there was a great deal of U.S. government discussion of means to reduce the vulnerability of the free world to similar disruptions in the future, but with the easing of the crisis the problem has been relegated to the background. It seems useful, therefore, to review the two recent crises and to quantify the physical magnitudes that might be involved if a similar situation occurs in the next few years. 

In appearance, the Iranian oil stoppage of 1951 and the Suez Canal blockage of 1956 were quite different. The Iranian crisis was one which completely eliminated one of the world's major suppliers of crude oil and products from the market. The Suez crisis was one which cut the shortest route between one of the world's major supply areas (the Middle East) and one of the world's major consuming areas (Western Europe). Nevertheless, given the international character of the petroleum market, both of these interruptions resolved themselves into shipping crises, which were met in each case by a temporary mobilization of the commercial tank shipping fleets of the western world, primarily under the auspices of the petroleum industry itself.

The present report therefore focuses on the transportation requirements for moving the world's oil during the crises of 1951 and 1956 and at some date in the not-too-distant future, such as 1965. It is implicitly assumed

RM 5-26-58 -2- that a future crisis would have the same essential nature as those in the past, namely that oil would be available for purchase somewhere in the Middle East but that difficulties would arise in getting the oil to its major consumer, Western Europe. For purposes of projection, the situation postulated is one where all trans-Arab oil arteries are blocked in 1965, and the resulting tanker requirements for maintaining normal supplies in Western Europe are calculated. No attempt is made to evaluate alternative measures to insure the maintenance of normal supplies (such as construction of tankers, construction of storage facilities, exploration outside the Middle East), although the estimates presented here are the necessary starting point for any such evaluation. 

1. The Iranian Oil Stoppage, 1951-1954 

In Iran, negotiations which began in 1948 on revisions of the terms of the Anglo-Iranian Oil Company's concession ended in 1951 with the nationalization of the assets of the Company by the Iranian government under Premier Mossadeq. Refusal by the British to accept nationalization resulted in the withdrawal of all British personnel from the oilfields and from the refinery at Abadan, and in a boycott of Iranian oil by all the major international oil companies. In 1954, a year after Mossadeg was ousted by the Shah and the 

Iranian army, the shutdown was ended by a new arrangement negotiated with an international consortium of oil companies who in effect bought out part of Anglo-Iranian's concession rights and improved the terms of payment made to the Iranian government. a. Iranian crude production did not have a disastrous effect on world oil supplies. Total Middle East production increased steadily, with Middle East crude oil production outside of Iran rising by 43 per cent in 1951 and more than doubling by 1953. A further increment in world supplies was provided by the Caribbean, whose production was increased by 13 per cent in 1951; in absolute amounts, the Caribbean increase came to somewhat less than half of the increase in Middle East output outside of Iran. 

However, the effect on the output of refined products was more serious, with production in the Middle East as a whole declining by 42 per cent from 1950 to 1952, despite an increase of 35 per cent in output of Middle East refineries outside of Iran. The products most critically affected were 1 aviation gasoline, kerosene, and residual fuel oil. By 1952, Caribbean refinery production was stepped up by 19 per cent over the 1950 level, but. the major increases in refinery output were achieved by refineries at the major consuming centers. The immediate problem for the international oil companies arose from the fact that the Iranian refinery, which supplied 5 per cent of the non-Soviet world's petroleum products in 1950, marketed them primarily east of Suez. The Abadan shutdown therefore necessitated an emergency shipping program to route refined petroleum products to Abadan's normal customers from other sources of supply. 1

The Suez Canal Stoppage, November 1956 - March 1957 

The Suez crisis began with the nationalization of the Suez Canal by 

Nasser on July 26, 1956, following the withdrawal of British troops from the

Suez Canal Zone (in 1954) and the breakdown of Egypt's efforts to obtain U.S. and U.K. (or USSR) financing for the Aswan High Dam. The negotiations on the future of the Canal which followed Nasser's action were brought to an end with the Israeli attack on Sinai and the Anglo-French landings at Port Said.

On October 31, 1956, the Egyptians blocked the Suez Canal, and on 

November 3 the Syrian Army blew up several Iraq Petroleum Company (IPC) 

pumping stations to cut off the flow of oil from Iraq to the Mediterranean. 

The Canal remained blocked until the end of March 1957 when its clearance for shallow draft shipping was completed by a U.N. crew following the withdrawal of the British and the French from Port Said. 

In the U.S., shortly after the Nasser action nationalizing the Canal, the Foreign Petroleum Supply Committee was requested by the Secretary of the Interior to prepare a plan of action for supplying Western Europe in the event of "the loss or impairment of the Middle East pipelines, the stoppage 2 of the Suez Canal" or both." Effects on production of the Suez crisis The blockage of the Suez Canal and the IPC pipelines in October and November of 1956 had much more serious, though shorter run, effects on Middle East production than did the Anglo-Iranian crisis. The hardest hit of the oil producers was Iraq, whose output in November was reduced by 75 per cent from the level of the previous months, since most of Iraq's production (73 per cent of total output in 1955)1 is from the Kirkuk fields whence the only outlet is by pipeline to the Mediterranean. Persian Gulf production, affected only by the tanker shortage, was reduced by about half. The cutbacks were smallest for Iran (whose minimum shipments were guaranteed by the consortium under the 1954 agreement and whose markets are still primarily east of Suez) and for Saudi Arabia (since Aramco's TAP-line to the Mediterranean was not damaged by the Syrians).

Stocks and consumption in Western Europe during Suez crisis 

The world's stocks of crude petroleum were sharply depleted immediately following the interruption of the normal flow of Middle East oil. In the oil.¹ last two months of 1956, the withdrawals from stocks of crude were particularly high 0.6 million bbls/day. These withdrawal rates came to 4 per cent of the world's crude oil production (outside of Eastern Europe and the USSR) of some 14 million bbls/day during those months. In November and

5. Conclusion 

The petroleum production and shipping record during the Iranian and Suez stoppages indicates that the oil industry has been capable of meeting piecemeal crises by increasing production rates in the Western Hemisphere and by reassigning tankers from routes originating in the Middle East to routes originating in the Caribbean/U.S. Gulf area. In the time that has passed since the Suez shutdown, no apparent steps have been taken to carry out any sort of program of increasing transport facilities as a means of insurance against possible future interruption by the transit countries of the flow of Middle East oil supplies: the supertanker crash program seems to have been forgotten; the only pipeline constructed has been a small diameter line built out of Eilat by the Israelis to meet part of their own needs; a Kirkuk-Iskenderun line has never been officially proposed to the 

Iraq government while the Iran-Turkey negotiations on a Qum-Iskenderun line have been temporarily suspended. At the present time, the private oil companies are actively discouraged, in the short run, from heavy investment in emergency facilities by the fact that there is actually a tanker surplus since all normal facilities are open and there exists a recession-fostered insufficiency of demand.

MIDDLE EAST CRISES AND WORLD PETROLEUM MOVEMENTS by Harold Lubell; Research 

Memorandum RM-2185, May 26, 1958, Unclassified. 51 pp. incl. illus.

ASTIA Document No. AD 156018 

A discussion of two major interruptions in the supply of oil from the Middle East which have occurred in the present decade, in Iran in 1951 and at Suez in 1956. The resulting oil shortages resolved themselves into shipping crises which were met by reorganization of the tanker facilities of the western international oil companies. This study surveys the effects of these crises on world petroleum production and shiping and examines the impact of a hypothetical future interruption of the flow of Middle Eastern oil to the western world. 51 pp. incl. illus.
