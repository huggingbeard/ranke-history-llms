MORE ABOUT THE FORGED REVELS ACCOUNTS 

EADERS of this pamphlet do not need to be reminded of the essential facts in the dispute centering around the two "books" of Revels Accounts which were "discovered" about 1840 by Peter Cunningham, intimate associate of John Payne Collier, the most notorious literary forger of the nineteenth century. 

Opinion regarding the genuineness or spuriousness of these documents has oscillated back and forth almost with the regularity and inevitability of a pendulum. In 1928 the Columbia University Press published a book of mine, entitled Shakspere Forgeries in the Revels Accounts, in which I gave a detailed history of the impugned documents and the results of an investigation which was made in accordance with the scientific methods employed by the modern professional "examiner of questioned documents" ("handwriting expert," "bibliotist"). As a result of that investigation, which had covered several years, I reached the conclusion that the "Cunningham papers" (as they are usually named) are forgeries from beginning to end, and that the forgery was essentially the work of Collier, at any rate, that he was the brains behind the whole nefarious business. Since the publication of that book several reviewers have expressed dissent from my views and have attempted to rehabilitate these documents in the esteem of students of the Elizabethan and Jacobean drama. 

Generally speaking, it is undoubtedly the part of wisdom for the writer of a book to pay no attention to the aspersions and banalities of his reviewers. This is especially true when one has discussed technical matters of which his reviewers know nothing, and, by virtue of their training (or, rather, lack of training), can know nothing. My discussion of the questioned Revels Accounts was conducted along the lines of the scientific and professional handwriting expert (whom I prefer to call a bibliotist). From the reviews I have read it appears that not one of my reviewers is even fairly acquainted with scientific method, or knows how to use the microscope, and not one knows anything about the theory or technique of the science and art of bibliotics. 

Why, then, it may be asked, do I pay any attention to such criticisms? There are at least three reasons: (a) Failure to answer my critics is sure to be interpreted as meaning that their objections cannot be met, that the suspected "books" are genuine, or that their spuriousness cannot be proved. Thus, for example, Professor Charles J. Sisson, reviewing Mr. A. E. Stamp's book, The Disputed Revels Accounts, London, 1931, says (in The Review of English Studies, January, 1932, vol. 8, pages 103-105): "the question [of the genuineness of these accounts] may now be considered to

The Forged Revels Accounts be finally settled, and the documents in their entirety be treated as authentic beyond all cavil." A reviewer like Professor Sisson, who does not investigate what he reads, may be pardoned for reaching a conclusion so contrary to the truth. Dr. 

Wolfgang Keller, the genial editor of the Shakespeare Jahrbuch, in his review of 

Mr. Stamp's book, says (vol. 67, page 89), regarding Mr. Stamp's verdict: "sein

Richterspruch 'Sie [die verdächtigen Dokumente] sind echt' dürfte endgültig sein.” 

He, of course, being far from the papers, had no choice but to accept Mr. Stamp's statements at their face value. Professor F. S. Boas, speaking of the late Mrs. 

Charlotte C. Stopes before the Royal Society of Literature (on May 21, 1930), refers to this controversy in the following words: "There appears to be no one now of repute who upholds the forgery theory except the gladiatorial Dr. Tannen baum of New York." (Transactions of the Royal Society of Literature, Vol. X, page 88.) (b) Many who are interested in literary problems will welcome information regarding some of the fundamental principles of bibliotics. (c) Since the publication of my book I have, I think, found the solution for some problems connected with the forged documents which needed clearing up. 

In what follows I shall consider the relevant arguments of my critics seriatim.

Professor Tucker Brooke did me the honor to review my book in the Saturday 

Review of Literature (New York), in the issue of May 11, 1929, page 1,000. 

1. The first point made by Dr. Brooke is this: the principles laid down by 

Mr. Albert S. Osborn in his excellent work on Questioned Documents, "by which forgeries in present-day writings may be detected," are not "applicable to writings over three hundred years old."

Even a little thought will show that there is not the slightest particle of validity in this purely theoretical objection. The principles underlying the science of bibliotics are based upon natural phenomena in the realms of physics, chemistry, and psychology. The underlying phenomena are as fixed and immutable as nature herself. Forgeries perpetrated hundreds of years ago show exactly the same characteristics as those of to-day. Forgers of three hundred and even more years ago employed the same technique, were guided by the same principles, and made the same mistakes which we find in the work of more modern forgers. The ancient [2]

Reply to T. Brooke forger's mind worked exactly as the mind of the modern forger. For proof that this is so, the reader is referred to one of the very first books written on the subject of bibliotics: M. Francois de Melle's Advis Pour Iuger des Inscriptions en 

Faux et Comparaison des escritures & signatures, pour en faire & dresser les moyens, voir & descouurie toutes falcifications & faussetez, published at Paris in 

1609 (3d edition).

Besides, Dr. Brooke gives not a single reason in support of his assertion that modern methods are not applicable to the investigation of documents three hundred years old. It has evidently not occurred to my distinguished reviewer that if what he says were true we should have no way of knowing whether any old document is genuine-which is clearly absurd. 

Dr. Brooke commits a serious blunder in logic when he speaks of "writings over three hundred years old" in connection with the suspected Revels Accounts. If they are forgeries as I maintain they are,-they are only some ninety years old, and therefore, by Dr. Brooke's own admission, not too ancient for investigation by modern methods. Whether they are ninety years old or three hundred years old is the very question at issue. 

2. Some persons, says Dr. Brooke, will not agree that "the study of facsimiles, unsupported by direct examination of the originals, is sufficient to determine the spuriousness of these particular papers."

My study of these documents was based upon photographs and photographic enlargements, not on "facsimiles." Collotype facsimiles, line cuts, and half-tone facsimiles, in the size of the originals, are, as a rule, of little or no value in studying a questioned document. My book makes it clear that I used photographs; it is illustrated with collotype facsimiles of parts of the documents merely as an aid to the student. "Direct examination of the originals" is not always possible. For examinations not involving ink and paper, properly-made photographs and photographic enlargements are not only as good as the originals but sometimes even better. For proof of this the reader is referred to Mr. Osborn's book. He will learn there that, for one thing, enlarged photographs show details which are not apparent to the naked eye.

Even if the original were destroyed, it would be possible to reach a definite conclusion as to the genuineness or spuriousness of a document if good photographs of it were in existence. 

Dr. Brooke gives no reason for saying that "these particular papers" are not [3]

The Forged Revels Accounts subject to the same methods of investigation which apply to other papers. 

3. Other "documents faked by Collier are now riddled with anachronism, as new sources of information have wrecked his assumed chronology; whereas the dates and facts in the Revels Accounts of 1604-5 and 1611-12 are practically as unassailable today as they were in 1842. They cannot yet all be corroborated by independent authority, but there is not a single one that has been really invalidated." 

All this, even if it were absolutely true, which it is not, is illogical as well as irrelevant. A document may be a forgery even though every statement in it be correct. In the present instance we have no way of knowing that even a single fact mentioned in the play-lists contained in the documents is correct. Dr. Brooke says that the data in these documents cannot "all be corroborated"; the fact is that not a single one of its data relating to Shakspere and his contemporaries has been corroborated by discoveries made since 1842. Every supposedly verifiable item in these papers is based on information previously known; their new data have not been verified.

I maintained in my book-and still maintain-that the forger or forgers worked from genuine originals when he or they concocted the forgery. The purpose of the forgery was to supply the literary world with certain interesting data which are still awaiting verification. 

Professor Brooke insinuates that all of Collier's other forgeries have been exposed and that the exposure was due to the discovery of chronological errors. 

This is, however, incorrect. We have no way of knowing whether all of Collier's forgeries have been recognized as such. It was as recently as January, 1929, that 

I was able to show that a Stationers' Registers entry regarding a non-extant "ballad of Macdobeth" and of "The Taming of a Shrew" is a Collier forgery.

And even more recently Dr. Greg has called attention to the fact that he has discovered another and hitherto unsuspected Collier forgery. Furthermore, chronology had nothing to do with the exposure of some of Collier's forgeries. That is why a considerable number of his forgeries, e.g., Dr. Forman's "Notes" on 

Shakspere's plays, are still generally regarded as genuine. See also E.K. Chambers's 

William Shakespeare, II, 386-393.

That not a single one of the new data has "really been invalidated" is not quite true. My book shows that several of the items mentioned in the Cunningham papers cannot be reconciled with known facts. 

4. Professor Brooke is "not convinced either by the alleged examples of 'patched letters' and criminal hesitation microscopically revealed." [4]

Reply to T. Brooke 

When he has familiarized himself with the subject, and has had the opportunity to study forged documents, he will, I have not the slightest doubt, be convinced by such indicia. He will learn that in genuine writing one never finds such patching and mendings and alterations as occur throughout the Revels books, whereas in forged documents one always finds them. 

5. Dr. Brooke attempts to explain away my evidence for forgery by saying that "The documents are not the fluent and continuous composition of a ready writer but rather lists of separate items copied into the official books by a clerk only languidly interested in what he was doing." 

Lists of separate items are written with the same speed and naturalness with which anything else is written. The writer of mere lists of items does not draw his letters piecemeal, in sections, and stop to mend them and patch them and correct the shading. Such things are done by forgers, by no one else. Indolent clerks would be the last ones in the world to do anything so laborious and so wearying. 

6. "If it was one of King James' countless 'brither Scots' who happened to be doing this work in the new king's Revels Office in 1605, one would need no other explanation of the spelling Shaxberd and the other little variations from English clerical habit." 

But this is only assumption, baseless and reasonless assumption. Dr. Brooke advances not a single fact or reason for thinking that an indolent brither Scot would have written Shaxberd for Shakspere or Shakespeare. There is not the slightest probability that he would have done so. It is not characteristic of the 

Scotch to add a d-sound after final r. 

Dr. Brooke speaks of "little variations from English clerical habit." This misrepresents my case against the questioned books. I did not charge that they contain “little variations from English clerical habit." I showed that the writing in those books was not done currente calamo, that the letters were drawn, mended, patched, and overwritten, that the scribe did such things as a clerk-not merely an 

English clerk-would not have done. Would any Jacobean clerk have altered "Tragidye" to "Tragide," "one" to "on," "Spanishe" to "Spanish," "Caled" to "Called," "whitfriers" to "whitfriars," "Nobelmen" to "Nobilmen," "kandle" to "Candle," "doble" to "dobell," "Cordes" to "Cords," and so forth? Can Professor Brooke produce a single authentic example of such changes in an official document of the period? I more than doubt it.

The alterations to which I have just called attention-they are to be found in [5]

my book—are significant because they are exactly the things that forgers of ancient documents are habitually doing so as to give their fabrications an antique air. They are the things that Collier was in the habit of doing. In his little book, The 

Shakspeare Fabrications (1859), Mr. C. M. Ingleby points out (p. 68) that in the infamous "Perkins" Folio (page 181, column 2) Collier wrote the word "body" in pencil in a clear modern hand and that the "corrector" wrote "bodie" over this with ink and in the antique character. 

7. "In any case," concludes Dr. Brooke, "since the paper and the ink appear to be of unquestionable antiquity, and the facts recorded hold water quite beyond the wont of nineteenth century forgeries, it seems likely that these much scrutinized papers will survive the brunt of Dr. Tannenbaum's microscope and camera, though both have been long and well laid on." 

I have no quarrel with Professor Brooke's assumption of the rôle of prophet. 

But I strenuously object to his leading his readers to think that I have conceded the ink to be of unquestionable antiquity. I devoted a whole chapter to the subject of the ink and showed that the early investigators (Halliwell, Dyce, Madden, and others) condemned the documents as spurious because of the character of the ink, that the recent examination of the ink made by Professor Dobbie was worthless, that an official of the British Museum (Mr. D. T. Wood) reported recently that the ink behaved peculiarly and peeled off like paint, and that the tenor of Professor Dobbie's report on the ink was misrepresented by an over-enthusiastic champion of the Revels books. Without examining the original documents, without having any new evidence, Professor Brooke is not justified in saying that the ink appears to be of unquestionable antiquity. 

Dr. Brooke is in error when he says that "the facts recorded hold water quite beyond the wont of nineteenth century forgeries." The documents do not hold water. My book shows that a number of the new data in the Cunningham papers conflict hopelessly with known facts. I shall mention only the items about Love's 

Labor's Lost, about a performance of Hymen's Holiday before the King, and about The Maid's Tragedy. As to these matters I must refer my readers to my book.

Nor is one warranted in speaking of recorded facts holding water when these facts have never been verified. All he can honestly say is that they have not been proved to be incorrect, and that's quite a different matter. Shaksperian facts mentioned in the questioned books which await verification are these: that 

The Comedy of Errors, The Merchant of Venice, The Merry Wives, and Henry V 

were revived in the winter season of 1604-5, that Measure for Measure and [6]

II Professor Thomas Baldwin's review appeared in The Library (London), 

December, 1929, volume 10, pages 327-338. 

1. Taking advantage of the fact that I had pointed out that some eight or nine lines scattered through the Cunningham papers are genuine, Professor Baldwin presents a long and elaborate argument to prove that if these few lines are genuine all the rest of the papers must be genuine. But Professor Baldwin is mistaken. 

If Cunningham, or Collier, discovered genuine Revels Accounts for the years in question and was disappointed in their contents (because they made no mention of Shakspere), it would have been an easy matter for the forger to wash off as much of these papers as he wished and to leave behind such portions of the originals as would serve to give an impression of genuineness. Whole pages of written matter can be washed away, either with plain water or with any of the following chemicals, depending on the composition of the ink: "solutions of chlorine, chloride of lime with weak acid, antimony chloride, dilute aqua regia, and oxalic acid." (See C. A. Mitchell's Inks, Their Composition and Manufacture, London, 1916, page 133.) Dr. Baldwin is therefore wrong when he says (page 331) that I assume that the whole outer sheet of four pages was blank except for the few genuine notations. On the contrary, I assume that the forger had at his disposal genuine Revels Accounts for the years in question and that he washed out everything but these notations. The signatures at the end of the second "book" may have been damaged in the deleting process and this necessitated the imitation of the original signatures. "Jo: Sotherton" is, I am fairly convinced, a forgery; some of the other signatures may not impossibly be genuine. 

I shall surely be asked, as I asked myself, if the forger had access to genuine documents, and was interested only in the play-lists, why did he go to the trouble of rewriting so many pages of inconsequential matter? Why did he not content himself with making the three pages which list the plays and leave the remaining pages as they were? The answer to this question is not difficult. To devote two whole pages to the play-lists in book A (1604-5) and a whole page in book B (1611-12) it became necessary to crowd all the rest of the matter into the remaining pages. This made it necessary to copy out all the items about expenses incurred by various officials and then to rewrite it all in a smaller hand. It may be recalled here that in my book (page 22) I pointed out that the writing was unnaturally and unnecessarily crowded, as if the scribe were following a copy. [7]

Another reason for rewriting the whole account lay in the necessity of having all the writing the same. Obviously, it would be suspicious if the play-lists were in one hand and the other items in another. To Collier this would have been an easy task, for he would not have had to imitate any particular clerk's handwriting. 

Here we probably have the explanation for the fact, noted in my book, that the script of the Cunningham papers bears only a remote resemblance to that of the previous Revels Accounts. 

2. Professor Baldwin is not free from saying more than is warranted by the evidence. He says (page 336): "Malone refers to the accounts of Sir George 

Buc as authority for saying that The Silver Age was performed in January, 1612. 

He thus saw the accounts of 1611-12. This entry shows too that in the inner pages of the original accounts there was a play-list." 

In the above paragraph Dr. Baldwin makes his readers think that Malone must have seen these very accounts, the Cunningham papers. I showed in my book (page 26) that Malone could not have seen these papers inasmuch as his account differs from them in at least one important respect: he says that The Silver 

Age was given "before the King and Queen," whereas the questioned document says "before the Queen and the Prince."

Even if Malone and the Cunningham papers agreed exactly, it would not prove the latter genuine. Collier was thoroughly acquainted with Malone's writings and, like Ireland, based some of his forgeries on data gleaned from Malone.

That the Cunningham papers cannot be right regarding a performance of 

The Silver Age on January 12, 1612, is proved in my book (page 26). Dr.

Baldwin, it should be noted, makes no attempt to meet my arguments as to this. 

3. "He [Malone] also found an entry there [in Buck's accounts] which caused him to say that The Tempest was in existence in the autumn of 1611, though he does not specifically refer to the accounts in this connexion." 

That Malone did not base his statement regarding The Tempest on the Cunningham papers is proved by the fact that he says that this play was "performed before the middle of 1611." The Cunningham papers record a performance at 

Court on November 1, 1611. November the first is not before the middle of the year. Malone therefore must have had another source for his information, exactly as in the case of The Silver Age. [8]

4. "Present evidence makes it certain that The Winter's Tale was put on in the first half of 1611," says Dr. Baldwin.

But present evidence does nothing of the sort. Professor Baldwin implies that evidence discovered since the publication of the Cunningham papers confirms their dating of The Winter's Tale. He probably refers to Simon Forman's diary. 

But the Forman entry regarding The Winter's Tale was "discovered"-at any rate, published-in 1836, six years before the publication of Cunningham's book. 

The entry in the Cunningham papers was probably added by the forger to verify the Forman entry and to divert suspicion from it. (That the Forman "Notes" on 

Shakspere's plays are also Collier forgeries I argue in my forthcoming book.)

Professor Baldwin informs his readers (on page 334) that "It is admitted that page one [of the 1611-12 book] is wholly genuine, the only writing on it being the date." But this is not admitted. All that I "admitted" (to use Professor 

Baldwin's word) was that one short line on this page (reading "A° xmo Rs Jacobi") is genuine. The title at the top of the page (three lines, "The Booke, of the Reuells :/ Endinge the last Day of Octobar,/ Anº Domi: 1612") is spurious.

In connection with the fact that the scribe of the 1604-5 accounts calls the second year of the reign of King James the third, I pointed out that in the second of the Cunningham books the regnal year (the tenth) was originally omitted and that subsequently the word "nono" was crowded into the vacant space with a different pen and different ink. I expressed the opinion that for William Honyng, the alleged scribe, this was "almost impossible." Professor Baldwin, noting this, instructs his readers that this "inconceivable impossibility" (his words, not mine) occurs in genuine accounts and refers them to Professor Feuillerat's 

Documents Relating to the Office of the Revels, 1908, "pages 349, 468, 365, &c."

When we consult Professor Feuillerat's book we discover, to our amazement, that Professor Baldwin's references are, as our lawyers say, incompetent, irrelevant, and immaterial. On page 349 we find that the clerk was evidently copying a form in which there were blanks for the dates. He wrote "Betwene the daie of 

- 158/Anno XX - Regni Regine Elizabethe And the xiiijth of ffebruary 1582. 

Annoq' R Eliz xxvto." Clearly, he did not know from what date the accounts ran. The scribe of the 1611-12 accounts was in a wholly different position; he saw the regnal year on the recto of the leaf on which he was writing and he had the date right before him. On page 468 Professor Feuillerat merely informs the reader that the Auditor supplied the information omitted by the clerk. On page 

365 (the accounts for 1584) we find that the clerk did not omit the regnal years, though Professor Baldwin wishes his readers to think he did. (The clerk omitted only the day of the month to which the account ran.) Professor Baldwin's "&c," [9]

which may make his readers think that I had ignored or been ignorant of other instances of the omission of the regnal year in the Revels Accounts, is, in view of the fact that there is not in those books a single instance of such omission (at any rate, I have been unable to find one), surely an error.

A few other points raised by Professor Baldwin I shall not discuss here, inasmuch as they do not concern the subject under consideration. 

III 

Dr. R. B. McKerrow's review (in Modern Language Notes, February, 1931, vol. 46, pages 120-124) is fortunately short and therefore easily disposed of. 

1. "The paper is admittedly of the right date, and so, as far as can be determined, is the ink."

Dr. McKerrow misleads his readers into thinking that an analysis had been made for the purpose of determining the age of the ink. No such analysis has ever been made. In fact, no one has yet attempted an analysis for the purpose of determining even the composition of the ink. My reviewers do not know that the age of old ink cannot be determined. 

2. "They [the documents] contain nothing that could not have been known by the person or persons by whom they may be supposed to have been written." 

This is as misleading as it is incorrect. There was not in 1842, as there is not now, a particle of evidence in existence to show that The Comedy of Errors, The Merry Wives, The Merchant of Venice, or Henry V were revived for King James or members of his family. There is nothing to show that Othello was in existence in 1604, or The Winter's Tale in 1611, or Measure for Measure in 1604. 

How, I wonder, can anyone be said to have "known" facts for which there is no extant evidence? 

3. "Nor, in spite of more than one attempt, has anything of real importance in them [the documents] been shown to conflict with facts to us known from other sources."

This would be, as Mark Twain is reported to have said, "important, if true." 

But it is not true. Dr. Greg, as will shortly appear, acknowledges that the documents "do offer some real puzzles to the historian." See also my reply to Professor Brooke's point number 7. [ 10 ]

Reply to R. B. McKerrow 

4. "But is not this theory of a document of such length written first in outline and then gone over again an extraordinary one?"

It is. But Collier did extraordinary things. Witness the frequently cited "Perkins" Folio. All forgers do extraordinary things.

In connection with Dr. McKerrow's question, it may perhaps be well to say just how I think this forgery was perpetrated. Cunningham discovered genuine accounts for 1604-5 and 1611-12. Collier examined them and was disappointed to find nothing of interest in them. He prevailed on the young and ardent 

Shaksperian to consent to a hoax: to introduce the play-lists. Collier took the papers and made an exact copy of them, even imitating the handwriting. Then he washed out the genuine contents of the papers with a chemical solution, retaining a few genuine lines, let the papers dry, and resized them (with a hot iron, perhaps with the help of a little wax, or glue, or some resinous solution).

Thereupon he ruled the pages off into parallelograms and wrote out his "copy," adding the play-lists, with a pale ink or dye, imitating the handwriting of the original. Then he went over it all with his darker ink, correcting what did not meet with his approval in what he had previously written. After that there were more corrections in the shading, ornamentation, shapes of letters, etc. The bulk of the visible original outlines was deleted and the job was finished. 

Professor McKerrow's other points, characterized by rhetoric rather than logic, are too irrelevant for serious discussion.

Dr. Walter W. Greg's review, prepared in collaboration with Mr. A. E. 

Stamp and Sir Edmund K. Chambers, was published in The Review of English 

Studies in July, 1929 (volume 5, pages 344-358).* 

1. "His [i.e., my] transcripts are not always correct: in play-list A, Henry the fifth should be Henry the fift."

I quote this to show the smallness and the unfairness underlying Dr. Greg's review. On the basis of a single error, due to the printer's failure to correct a misprint, the reader is made to believe that all my transcripts of the documents are untrustworthy. * Lest the reader should think that I am unduly severe with Dr. Greg, I beg him to read 

Dr. Greg's review. Let him then judge whether one who writes so insolent and so insulting a review deserves to be treated other than I have treated him. [11]

2. "His [i.e., my] Latin contains various errors (e.g., for the ordinary rum symbol)."

Dr. Greg's hostility has here played him a strange and well-deserved trick. 

Had he studied my book in a scientific spirit he would have realized that my Latin was not at fault, that my knowledge of Latin has nothing to do with the occurrence of a single instance (in the word "Revellorum" on page 70) of another variety of printer's error: the employment of a tiny italic s instead of the vertical s-shaped tilde employed elsewhere in this book by me as a sign of abbreviation. 

3. "In play-list B (line 17) he [Dr. Tannenbaum] prints S[']"roue and notes 

St was clumsily altered to Sh. This is an error. The scribe accidentally omitted the h, interlined it above, and joined it to the S with his customary curl, as seen in the next line. It is true that the head of the h has been touched up, but at no time was there a t." (See Mr. Stamp's Plate XIII, or Facsimile 6 of my book.)

The error is Dr. Greg's, not mine. The scribe made a t, exactly as he did in the word S' in lines 7 and 8; realizing his mistake he omitted to cross the t, added a fake head to the stem of the t and a tail (resembling an undotted i) to the basal tick, thus converting the t into an h. A photographic enlargement shows this clearly and unequivocally. But Dr. Greg professes not to believe in photographs, and he evidently does not know how to use even a pocket magnifying glass. 

Besides, if the scribe made an h, why did he have to touch it up? 

4. "In the margin opposite lines 13-14 he [Dr. T.] supposes that in the word prize the ze has been written over ce. (He is not the first to make this mistake.) 

As a matter of fact the original letters were es." 

The last two letters have been "doctored" so much that it is impossible to say positively what the word was originally. Besides, it is of no consequence whether the scribe altered price or pries to prize. If it was pries that he altered, it speaks so much more against him. The significant thing is that he made an alteration which an Elizabethan clerk would not have made. Collier, on the other hand, made just such corrections in his forgeries. Elizabethans, it must be recalled, constantly used s for z (the "unnecessary letter"). 

5. "He [Dr. Tannenbaum] twice quotes 24ty as a proof of forgery (pages 

19, 37); it is common seventeenth century form, and perfectly correct."

I must admit that I was guilty of an error as to this. Errare humanum est. 

What I had in mind was not the use of ty for th but that the scribe made only the head of the y and then stopped, leaving the letter incomplete. The forger may have [12]

done this because he was momentarily in doubt whether the ty in his copy was correct. An Elizabethan scribe would, of course, have finished the y. 

6. "He [Dr. T.] is grossly careless in his references; those to his own facsimiles are repeatedly wrong." 

Even a single error is, I suppose, a "gross" error. Three or four erroneous references warrant the use of the word "repeatedly," I grant. In self-defence, not in justification of the error, I shall say that these errors, again, were not mine. At the last moment the printer found that he had to shave and rearrange some of the facsimiles, and it did not occur to us that this would involve corresponding adjustments in the text. Not that the errors are of any consequence. 

In fact, it is fortunate that they occurred: Dr. Greg's comments on them give the reader an excellent opportunity to realize the bias and the spleen with which Dr. 

Greg has approached this subject. (Note the words "grossly" and "repeatedly.")¹ 

7. "Most misleading of all," says Dr. Greg, "is the passage on page 38, where it is argued that the writing was inked in over a faint outline previously drawn by the forger: 'in [line 32 of page 7B] the outline for wch was mistaken for wth (where the text clearly requires which).' . . . On reference to the original we find that the scribe accidentally wrote with for wch (not an uncommon slip) and then altered the t to c currente calamo. There is no trace of any outline whatever." 

But for Dr. Greg's addiction to superlatives he would probably not have said, as there is no reason for saying, that this is "most" misleading. The Disputed Revels Accounts, by Mr. A. E. Stamp, Deputy Keeper of the 

Public Records, which has impressed some Elizabethan scholars, has the merit of being brief in addition to presenting us with rather good, but not unexceptionable, collotype facsimiles of all the documents in question. His case for the genuineness of the questioned "books" is, as I shall show, built on sand and is not a whit more logical or of greater validity than the reviews I have already analyzed. 

1. Mr. Stamp's most telling point is said to be the fact that at the Public 

Record Office there is preserved a duly authenticated and unquestionably genuine copy of the Revels Accounts for the years 1604-5 and 1611-12 which tally financially and in many other details with the questioned books. To Mr. Stamp this proves the genuineness of the Cunningham papers. 

A more glaring non-sequitur is hardly conceivable. In the first place, I stated emphatically (p. 26 of my book) that the forger must have had genuine originals to work from.

In the second place, there is not a syllable in the genuine Declared Accounts which tends even in the remotest degree to confirm even a single item in the playlists, the only important parts in the suspected papers. 

How, then, can the existence of these Declared Accounts be said to disprove the spurious character of Cunningham's great "find"? And how, in the name of reason, can they be said to "confirm" it? 

2. It is extremely unfortunate and regrettable that there has been so much wilful misrepresentation in the discussion of these documents on the part of English critics. The point, an unpleasant one, must be stressed because it serves to caution unsuspecting readers against accepting unquestioningly important statements made by prejudiced partisans. 

Mr. Stamp, reciting some of the facts in the history of the investigation, says (page 9): "Mr. [Ernest] Law asked us to have the document [for 1604-5] examined chemically and microscopically to see if the ink with which these pages [i.e., the play-list, pages 3 and 4] were written was of the seventeenth century and was the same as that used throughout the body of the document. The first question could not be answered with certainty." (My italics.) 

The truth is that Professor Dobbie was not asked to see if the ink was of the seventeenth century, and no examinations were made to determine that fact. 

Professor Dobbie, in his official report (reprinted in my book, pages 63-64) says [ 30 ]

Reply to A. E. Stamp clearly and distinctly that in 1910 "Mr. Stamp stated that the special object of the enquiry was to ascertain whether there is evidence that the ink on pages 3 and 4 is of different character or of different age from the ink in other portions of the document." "The first question," says Mr. Stamp now, "could not be answered with certainty." Professor Dobbie says not a syllable on the subject. Had he been asked to determine the age of the ink he would have had to say something about it. In this connection I may surely remind the reader that in my book I proved that Professor Dobbie's examination of the ink had no bearing on the questions at issue. Had he wished to obscure the truth, he could not have done a poorer piece of work-even though Mr. Stamp stood by and watched him. 

3. Scholars and paleographers of the nineteenth century who examined the 

Cunningham papers-and who approached their task open-mindedly-rejected these documents at sight because of the appearance of the ink and the patchy character of the writing. The late Mr. Wood said that the writing looked woolly, like writing on a blotting paper, and that the ink cracked like paint. (See my book, page 7.) Mr. Stamp thinks he can explain away these phenomena by the theory that "the scribe's ink had been unusually thick owing to an excess of the gum used to give it body. In consequence it had not penetrated the paper but lay on the surface." Thereupon he explains the suspicious alterations and doubled outlines as being due to a failure of the thick ink to flow easily and evenly from the pen, which made it necessary for the scribe "to go back and touch up." 

Common sense is all one needs to realize the absurdity of this explanation. 

A person employed in a clerical position in a government office knows enough to thin his ink, if it is so thick as to make writing troublesome, by the addition of a little water, vinegar, or fresh ink.

Furthermore, what person will believe that the scribe who was compelled to write the 1604-5 accounts with such thick ink was destined to write the 1611-12 accounts with the identical thick ink? And in the course of seven long years no one thought of thinning that ink or getting a fresh supply. If we were not familiar with the ways of political office holders, we would probably make a point of the fact that in these very accounts Sir George Buck presents bills for "Incke" he had purchased. But he undoubtedly bought some ink.

And, finally, why do not the defenders of the Cunningham papers produce, in corroboration of their theories, even a single genuine document of the years in question which also was written with such thick ink? Some of Dr. McKerrow's rhetorical questions would be quite appropriate here. [ 31 ]

Though I have dealt with the subject at considerable length in my book, I shall offer the reader proof, indisputable proof, that my opponents in this controversy are indulging in theories and generalizations in support of which they offer not facts but offensive personalities and sarcasms. If the reader will turn to Mr. Stamp's Plate V, he will note in line 17 the word "Ossidewe" (i.e., arsedine, "Dutch gold"); let him examine this with a magnifying glass, and he will see that the pen was not running dry and that there was no need for retouching. The nineteenth-century scribe did not know how to make a secretary double-s naturally. 

Another example on the same page will probably clinch the matter. In the line below the one we have just been examining occurs the word "Cords" (with a "final s" flourish). Note, however, that this word was originally written "Cordes," just as plainly as could be; that the pen did not run dry, and that there was no apparent need for changing the form of the word. Did Sir George Buck do that? Or was it an indolent brither Scot? Or was it a stupid forger who, having lapsed into "Cordes," thought it would be more Elizabethan if he wrote "Cords"?

It must have been Sir George Buck, who had nothing else to do but to change "to" to "To" at the beginning of line 30 of the same page, and who corrected the abundantly inked "Rehersales" to "Rehersalls" in the sixth line from the bottom of Mr. Stamp's Plate XV. And, of course, it was he who altered "aganest❞ (Plate XIV, line 19) to "againest," "doble" to "dobell" (in line 30). Presumably, too, it was he who, in a merry sport, doubled the dots over the i's on page 8 (especially the last paragraph) of book A. (See Stamp's Plate VIII, or my facsimile 7a.) Now we know why he became insane.

6. The woolly appearance of the writing, resembling, to some extent, writing done on a blotter or on improperly sized paper, is one of the suspicious features of the Cunningham papers. Mr. Stamp has, he thinks, an explanation for this phenomenon, for which, he says, he is indebted to Mr. George Stubbs, formerly of the Government Chemist's department. His explanation is this: the scribe used ink which was too thick. "When the ink first dried," he says (page 11), "being heavily charged with gum, it drew up the surface of the paper slightly as it shrank. In course of years the paper settled down again and the ink was obliged to crack. Paper, whether written on or not, takes many years to settle down, and indeed the surface never ceases working as it is subjected to differences of temperature and moisture. It slowly disintegrates and becomes covered with minute cracks and faults. The fact that in the document under discussion [as shown under the microscope] the cracks in the ink are not absolutely irregular and are prolonged into the paper, shows that their direction was dictated by the cracking of the paper itself, and this is positive proof [says Mr. Stubbs] that the ink must have been applied when the paper was new. Had it been say two hundred years old, the surface would have broken away under the strain and the cracks in the [ 35 ]

ink would have borne no relation to those in the paper. Moreover, the ink would have penetrated a certain distance beneath the surface and the cracks that appeared would be black."

Inasmuch as the above argument is accompanied with some "microphotographs" (as Mr. Stamp erroneously designates photo-micrographs), which give Mr. Stamp's book the appearance of a scientific document, his argument may impress some who cannot know whether what he is saying is valid or invalid, true or false, scientific or merely fantastic. That it is wholly unscientific, merely speculative, and invalid will be evident from what follows. (a) Cracks in dried ink have never been studied scientifically and it is not known just what causes them. Mr. Stubbs' notion-it is only a notion-that they are caused by the surface movements of the paper is not based on observation or logical deduction. It is probably much nearer the truth to say that the ink cracks mainly because of the contraction and expansion of the dry gum which is a constituent of all inks (to hold the Sulphate of Iron in suspension). Handling of the document undoubtedly also contributes to this. A coating of gum would crack in time even if it were laid on such a comparatively inelastic surface as glass or marble. (b) Not a particle of validity attaches to Mr. Stamp's confident assertion (page 11) that the "ragged appearance" of the writing is due to the cracking of the ink, which caused fragments of ink to break away wherever a crack reached the margin. Anyone who has had any considerable experience in microscopy knows that such fine microscopic details as can be seen only under a minimum magnification of 35 diameters do not affect the macroscopic appearance of an object. Such fine cracks as are to be seen in Mr. Stubbs' photographs (Stamp's 

Plate XIX) would not crack off enough marginal ink to cause the writing to look "woolly." It may be noted, by the way, that only very few cracks in the ink reach the margins of the letters. Also that the D which Mr. Stamp selected for photographing does not look woolly.

The woolly appearance of the writing is due, in all probability, to the fact that the sizing of the paper was defective and uneven, as the result of the chemical process to which the document had been previously subjected by the forger. (c) Mr. Stubbs' speculation about the relation of the cracks in the ink and the cracking of the paper is nonsense. If this ink contained so much gum, as Dr. 

Greg and Mr. Stamp say it did, that the scribe had difficulty in writing with it, it would have remained on the surface and not penetrated the paper. The cracking and flaking of such poorly-made ink would expose the paper underneath, and [36]

Reply to A. E. Stamp from this no inference whatsoever can be drawn as to the relative age of the paper and the ink. Needless to say, too, the cracking of the paper since 1840 or 1841, when these documents were cooked up, would have contributed materially to the cracking of the gummy ink. Another, and perhaps "most" important point, which would have to be determined experimentally, is whether such ink would not have cracked exactly as this ink is said to have cracked if it had been laid on cracked paper more than two hundred years old. Painters say that paint cracks above and parallel to cracks in a canvas. If this happens also in the case of ink laid on old paper we may have the explanation for some of the phenomena mentioned by Mr. 

Stamp. 

On this point I take pleasure in quoting the opinion of Dr. Mitchell, who, it may be recalled, was honored by Oxford University with the degree of Doctor of Science in recognition of his researches on ink. On November 19, 1929, after he had studied Mr. Stamp's photomicrographs, he wrote me as follows: "The microscopic appearance is woolly and blurred as in old iron-gall inks, but this is not conclusive of the age of the ink, since in my experience it would be quite possible for a poor iron-gall ink eighty years of age to have this appearance." 

In conclusion I have no hesitation in saying, "with entire confidence," that my case against the Cunningham papers has not been weakened one iota by the criticism of those who have reviewed my book. The history of the documents, their association with two notorious forgers (Cunningham was involved in other forgeries besides these papers), the chronological difficulties, and the presence in them of practically all the earmarks of spuriousness, leave no room for doubt that they are forgeries. | 37 ]
