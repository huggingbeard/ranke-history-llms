FOREWORD 

This report was prepared by the staff of the Subcommittee on Patents, Trademarks, and Copyrights, as part of the subcommittee's responsibility under Senate Resolution 65 to "conduct a full and complete examination and review of the administration of the Patent Office," and is concerned with a survey of the efforts which have been made to introduce automation into the search procedures of the Patent 

Office. 

The Congress, over a period of years, has received many complaints about the examination procedures of the Patent Office. This subcommittee has on several occasions expressed its concern with the delays involved and the extent to which relevant prior art apparently has not been considered by the examiner. The Congress in the past decade has substantially increased the funds appropriated for the operation of the Patent Office. Unfortunately, this has not effected significant reductions in the backlog of the Office or improvement in the quality of patents issued, judging from the continued high percentage of litigated patents found to be invalid. The rapid rise in technology and the growing complexity of disclosures can only aggravate present defects of the examination system. 

The subcommittee attaches great importance to the research and development program in the Patent Office, and hopes that it will be eminently successful. Because of my strong interest and belief in the necessity for the advancement of science and technology, I have introduced a bill (S. 816) for the establishment of a Commission on Science and Technology. As I indicated to the Senate on February 18, 1963, one of the tasks of the Commission would be "to conduct a study of Federal scientific and technical activities, such as the deficiencies in scientific, engineering, and technical information programs, including acquisition, processing, documentation, storage, retrieval, and distribution of scientific information; the urgency for accelerating scientific, engineering, and technical progress in a number of Federal agencies which perform some functions in these areas; and to recommend necessary reorganizations of scientific and technological activities of the Federal Government to improve their operations and to better coordinate their activities."

It is too early to tell whether the research and development program of the Patent Office will be successful in permitting mechanized searching in most classes of the arts. If, after further research, it should appear that such efforts do not afford reasonable prospects of success, it would then become necessary to consider substantial changes in our present examination system, before the entire system runs the danger of collapse.

A full examination system does not exist in a number of industrial nations and the proposed Common Market Patent Convention provides for a deferred examination. It may well be that a study of the

U.S. PATENT OFFICE RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT PROGRAM I. INTRODUCTION 

Outstanding among the serious problems confronting the Patent Office is the necessity for a quicker and more thorough method of examining the prior art. A number of committees and individuals have been vigorously seeking to improve the examination procedure by developing a system of mechanical searching. Commissioner of 

Patents David L. Ladd has declared that breakthroughs in information retrieval are essential to the survival of present examination procedures. This report provides a survey of the efforts which have been made to introduce automation into the search procedures of the Patent 

Office. 

The principal objective of the research and development program in the Patent Office is to provide as much mechanical assistance as possible to the examiner. The decision whether a patent application contains a patentable invention involves a combination of legal knowledge, scientific skill, and sound judgment in the use of this knowledge and skill. No attempt is being made to have a machine replace an examiner in making the judgments. 

Dr. Vannevar Bush in the first of this subcommittee's research studies on the patent system wrote of the need for a more complete Patent Office examination of the prior art. He stated in 1956 that: 

Appropriate mechanization is greatly facilitating the conduct of various forms of business. It appears that it could be of especial benefit in the Patent 

Office. There are some 7 million documents in the Office to which reference is made in the course of examination of an application. Even so, this by no means cover the publications which may be pertinent. In spite of a system of classification to aid search, and in spite of the fact that about half the time of 600 examiners is spent in searching this complex maze of material, it is inevitable that important references will be overlooked. Many times, when this occurs, it places a severe burden on industry, the courts, and the inventor himself. An invalid patent can do a lot of damage. There are those who feel that this particular problem, because of its diverse nature, cannot be readily solved by mechanical means. Certainly the experience of examiners, who have become familiar with special fields through long experience should not be sacrificed. 

But modern data-handling equipment is exceedingly flexible and versatile. It is often thought of, by those who do not understand it, as a mere card-shuffling affair. But it is far more than this. It can have a memory, so that the experience of one search will become available to other searchers. It can be fast and comprehensive. It can even be arranged to facilitate its own evolution, as its subject matter grows and the art changes. The arrangements for proceeding toward an adequate and well-thought-out mechanization are excellent. 

There is a committee in the Office studying the applicability. The Bureau of 

Standards has undertaken to investigate the best applicable mechanism. An advisory committee of highly competent individuals serving voluntarily is engaged in guiding the development. I trust that adequate funds will be available for the support of this important move.¹

U.S. PATENT OFFICE RESEARCH PROGRAM II. HISTORY OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT PROGRAM 

The rise in the number of patent applications filed, the backlog of pending applications, the increasingly outmoded classification system, the high percentage of litigated patents found invalid and the more complex nature of new inventions encouraged the Patent Office after World War II to study methods of improving the efficiency of the examining operation. First consideration was given to plans to apply punched card techniques to the searching procedure. After some experimentation with this method, it was judged to be quite inadequate for satisfying the complex searching requirements of the patent examiners.

In 1949, the task of further developing a mechanization system was attached to the Chemical Classification Group. Although some progress was made by this group, the project was terminated in January 1951 because of the desire to conserve manpower and to reduce nondefense expenditures. During the following 312 years no research in the mechanized literature searching field took place in the 

Patent Office. 

In June of 1954, the then Commissioner of Patents Robert C. Wat son, established a Committee on Mechanized Searching. The group was given the responsibility to "review the work which already has been accomplished on this project and to plan and direct future mechanized searching activities within the Patent Office." This Committee, in November 1954, proposed a three-point program. It sought to (1) mechanize the searching operation related to chemical composition applications, using the system tested by the Chemical Classification Group as the basis for an operating system, (2) accelerate the reclassification of the nonchemical arts, apparently looking to the use of the reclassified materials as a foundation for a mechanized searching system useful in these arts, and (3) set up an experimental project to investigate the feasibility of mechanizing the nonchemical arts.

Prior to the formulation of the above recommendations by the 

Committee on Mechanized Searching, the Secretary of Commerce created the Advisory Committee on Applications of Machines to Patent Office Operations. This committee was under the chairmanship of 

Dr. Bush.

It was an outgrowth of the directive issued by the Senate Appropriations Committee in 1955 instructing the Department of Commerce tomake an aggressive and thorough investigation as to the possibility of mechanizing the searching operations and installation of up-to-date types of equipment in order to modernize, insofar as practicable, the Patent Office operations. 

Although the language of the Appropriations Committee embraced an examination of the application of machines to all aspects of Patent Office activities, the role of the committee was limited to the search process. 

The committee made five major recommendations: 2 

1. The Patent Office should put machine searching of compositions of matter on an operational basis. 

2. The reclassification of patents should be accelerated.

The committee concluded that "mechanization of a substantial portion of the search process is certainly coming. It will result in marked improvement in the operations of the Patent Office that will be of great and continuing benefit to industry in this country.” 3 

As a direct outgrowth of this report there was created within the Patent Office an Office of Research and Development with the task of bringing to an operational phase various methods of mechanized search and retrieval, especially for literature dealing with compositions of matter. In accordance with the committee recommendation, a cooperative program was established with the National Bureau of Standards. An advisory committee was established in the Office of the Secretary of Commerce to coordinate the program, but this committee became inactive following the death of one member and the resignation of the chairman.

In 1958 the Secretary of Commerce asked the National Academy of Sciences to appoint a committee of experts to examine the role of the Department of Commerce in the area of science and technology and "to undertake an up-to-date evaluation of the functions and operations of the Department of Commerce in relation to present national needs." In March 1960 Dr. Detlev W. Bronk, President of the National Academy of Sciences, transmitted his report to the Sec- =retary. The committee consisted of a number of scientists and administrators with Dr. Mervin J. Kelly, of the International Business

Machines Corp. and Bausch & Lomb Optical Co. serving as chairman. 

One chapter of that report is devoted to the Patent Office. 

In the course of preparation of this report a number of studies were undertaken. Included were studies of (1) the research and development organization, its budgets and its staff; (2) cooperation with other organizations of Government, industry, and the universities; (3) present research and development programs; (4) future plans; and (5) financial, space, and facilities requirements. A group of scientists, under the chairmanship of Dr. B. D. Holbrook, of Bell 

Telephone Laboratories, was convened to evaluate the research and development program. Their report entitled "Mechanization of Patent Office Search Procedures" was submitted on May 25, 1959. It was obvious that the Patent Office represented an extreme form of the search and retrieval problem since the examiner is supposed to exercise his judgment concerning the novelty of an invention only after a complete search of the available literature in all languages which might contain the same or an equivalent disclosure.

For the first time, mention was made of the barrier to success in information retrieval being traceable to the absence or inadequacy of theory rather than the failure to use well-established theoretical principles. The report declared:

Consistent with this appraisal the King Committee recommended that the National Bureau of Standards should establish its own program of research in information retrieval. The Committee also made general division of labor between the Patent Office (whose projects were then envisioned as small and with limited objectives) and the Bureau of Standards (which had more ambitious and longer range research programs).

The U.S. Patent Office, meanwhile, is operating under much the same systems and procedures that it did 50 years ago. The patent applications have grown in size and complexity and the material to be searched has doubled and redoubled, so that the time required for each search grows commensurately longer. 

In an age that is characterized on every side by a high degree of mechanization and automation, the individual examiner is for the most part obliged to conduct his searches by what is essentially a primitive manual method. It is reasonable to expect that the advances of science and technology can be applied to this part of the process so that the bulk of the examiner's time can be devoted to the intellectual aspects of his tasks. A mechanized search procedure, properly conceived and executed, would materially reduce the time required for each search, would eliminate the factor of human fatigue and could be expected to 'discover remote and previously unobserved connections,' thus contributing to the issuance of better patents. We congratulate the Patent Office on the excellent work that it is doing in this area, but I believe it is fair to say that a greatly expanded research and development activity is needed if the work of mechanizing the search procedure is not to be outstripped by the rapid accretions of the material to be search. expanded research program should, of course, be closely related to other current work being done in the broad field of information retrieval. This The Patent Office has been among the pioneers in research on the use of mechanized search techniques, but like others active in this field, has not yet been able to devise ways of using to the fullest the capabilities of the remarkable electronic devices produced by our advanced technology. Office were to be given the funds and the staff to tackle this problem on the broad scale warranted by its importance, it would not only enhance the efficienecy of the patent system, but it would undoubtedly contribute in a major way to the solution of scientific information problems which are of pressing importance to industrial research, and in fact to the scientific community as a whole.

SUMMARY 

The Committee believes the U.S. patent system to be a positive force in our expanding economy. If it is to continue to make its contribution, mechanization of the search process is essential. Fortunately electronic computers have reached a stage of development which makes feasible their application to this complex problem. When achieved, mechanization will markedly aid the patent system and will be generally helpful to our society. 

Since there are several types of computers whose full potential is yet unexplored, it would be unwise to designate one search system and device at this time. Instead, the Committee recommends that the Patent Office immediately proceed in one designated field and work with industry and other agencies in developing the new machines and techniques which will permit full application at an appropriate time. 1. The Patent Office should put machine searching of compositions of matter on an operational basis. 

2. The reclassification of patents should be accelerated. 

3. A Research and Development Unit should be established in the Patent 

Office. 

4. The National Bureau of Standards and the Patent Office should undertake a joint program to stimulate and develop machines and techniques specifically adapted to the Patent Office operations.

APPLICATION OF MACHINES TO PATENT OFFICE PROBLEMS I. STATEMENT OF THE PROBLEM 

The industrial growth and prosperity of the United States has been an outstanding achievement of a free, competitive society. However, the special impetus provided by our patent system has not always been recognized. The patent system was established by our Founding Fathers with the hope that it would turn our almost exclusively agricultural economy toward industrial development in competition with European manufacturers. Throughout the history of our country, it has indeed facilitated the growth of industry and it continues to do so. 

In particular, it facilitates the advent of new industrial units, and it has done so notably in the period since the last war. 

Our patent system creates jobs, offers industry the opportunity for growth and improves the consumer's living standards. The system is based on a simple but wise philosophy. The grant of a U.S. patent gives the inventor a temporary exclusive right in return for a full disclosure of his idea. Creative thinkers are thus provided with a solid incentive for the development of the new and improved. Simultaneously, the disclosure of the invention stimulates further and constant advancement. In effect, each novel idea becomes a "seed" from which a flourishing tree of practical knowledge can grow. 

Thus the files of the Patent Office comprise a veritable treasure house of information upon which much of our industrial progress has been based, including ideas whose significance may not be realized for years after the disclosures. 

They give today a clear indication of the present thinking of the most ingenious minds in the country as to where we will be 5 years or more in the future. 

The patent system plays a major part in the creation of new products and processes. These appear every year and stimulate the establishment of new companies to produce them. However, the individual inventor or small businessman is severely handicapped if he must wait several years for his patent to issue or if he must operate for a period of years in uncertainty as to the possible appearance of a conflicting patent. If valid patents cannot be obtained with reasonable dispatch, inventors are discouraged and small businesses are without the modest protection which enables them to enter the field in competition with the larger, well established firms Yet it is the efforts of inventors and the continuous advent of small firms that keeps our economy flexible and free of stagnation.

Much of the delay is caused by the time required to search the pertinent patents among the 2,700,000 which today make up the record. Even if this is well done, there remains the problem of searching foreign patents and scientific literature. To be entitled to a patent in this country, one must be the first inventor-anywhere. If the foreign patents and scientific literature are not readily searched, the validity of patents is always in jeopardy. 

It is estimated that 60 percent of the time of patent examiners is now spent in the search operation. This involves a thorough comparison of specific detailed claims with all prior disclosures, to determine whether the wording of the claim as expressed fails to represent invention over what has gone before. In addition to the searching by examiners, the public search room of the Patent Office is also filled every day, and often late into the evening with hundreds of searchers and attorneys poring through the files on behalf of industry and the inventor.

The U.S. patent system is at the crossroads today. While the examiners are as dedicated and competent as their predecessors, they face a task infinitely more complex than that of an earlier day. The unprecedented pace of science and industry at home and abroad is producing new facts, scientific reports, and patent information at a rate beyond the capacity of procedures which served our fathers well.

These facts are obvious to the patent expert but are not appreciated by the general public, for whose welfare the system was established. Equally, the serious situation now facing the U.S. Patent Office today is not being generally

I recognized although some of the difficulties had been pointed out to the Appropriations Subcommittees of both House and Senate during the hearings held in connection with the 1955 Patent Office appropriation hearings. The Office has a force of approximately 700 examiners struggling to keep abreast of these mountains of knowledge. Despite their efforts, there are more than 200,000 applications awaiting action, and the present staff is unable to keep the size of this backlog from increasing. 

A. Background II. HISTORY OF COMMITTEE 

The Congress of the United States has a direct responsibility for the maintenance of a sound and vigorous patent system and has expressed concern with the Patent Office difficulties. In considering the U.S. Department of Commerce budget request for the fiscal year 1955, the Senate Appropriations Committee specifically directed the Department to "make an aggressive and thorough investigation as to the possibility of mechanizing the searching operations and installation of up-to-date types of equipment in order to modernize, insofar as practicable, the Patent Office operations."

In view of this mandate from the Congress, Secretary Sinclair Weeks and 

Assistant Secretary James C. Worthy of the Department of Commerce asked Dr.

Vannevar Bush for advice and guidance. Dr. Bush was persuaded to form and accept the chairmanship of a small committee of recognized experts to examine the problem and make recommendations for action by the Commerce Department.

The members of the committee, who were appointed by Secretary Weeks to serve with Dr. Bush, include Dr. Samuel N. Alexander, National Bureau of 

Standards; Dr. A. V. Astin, Director, National Bureau of Standards; Rear Adm. 

Luis de Florez (retired), president, de Florez Engineering Co.; John C. Green,

Director, Office of Technical Services, Department of Commerce; Dr. Karl F. 

Heumann, Director, Chemical-Biological Coordination Center, National Research 

Council; Albert V. Morris, Department of Commerce; L. Quincy Mumford, Librarian of Congress; C. F. Van Aken, Bureau of the Census; Dr. Alan T. 

Waterman, Director, National Science Foundation; Robert C. Watson, Commissioner of Patents; and Dr. Warren Weaver, the Rockefeller Foundation.

It was noted that the language of the Senate Appropriations Committee embraced an examination of the application of machines to all aspects of the Patent Office activities, although Secretary Weeks' request to Dr. Bush was restricted to the search process. This was discussed with the Assistant Secretary of Commerce for Administration who indicated that his Office was investigating those additional activities which might benefit from increased use of modern machines. 

Although the committee studies were directed specifically to the search process for patent purposes, it is believed that two additional, vital areas of our society may directly benefit. These are industrial research and business management. 

A primary step in a research project is the collection and study of the pertinent scientific literature. Existing patents form a significant segment of this literature. This is rarely recognized at present, because most scientists are unable to use the U.S. Patent Office collections effectively. Better access to the Patent 

Office storehouse of scientific and engineering facts will be a spur to scientific progress. The provision of new and relevant information to the scientist, at the time he embarks on his project, will avoid unnecessary duplication, will provide helpful background information for the resolution of the problem, and will suggest additional creative ideas for his analysis and inspiration. In consideration of this point, the attention of the committee was invited to the special responsibilities for supply of scientific and technical information to business assigned the 

Secretary of Commerce under Public Law 776 (September 1950). The successful application of machine searching in the U.S. Patent Office will lead the way for the Secretary to develop mechanization of search for other scientific information. 

We have considered the significance of patent protection to those who are embarking on new business ventures. There is another fruitful field, that of management decisions. Those who chart the courses of business enterprises must perceive opportunities and estimate trends if their decisions are to be sound and their firms are to flourish. There lies at hand as a valuable aid to management the records of the U.S. patent system. In the issued patents will be found the published thinking of the most creative minds in the world. This reflects the new production fields for industry which will be in operation 5 to 10 years in the future. 97076--63-3

An improvement of the search process will enhance the usefulness and status of the patent system in general. If the search process is made more thorough, there will be fewer patents issued which are later found by courts to be invalid. 

Thus the cost to society of expensive litigation will be reduced by the load thus taken off the courts. 

B. Committee operations

The committee undertook first to examine the nature and magnitude of the problem, with the close cooperation of the Patent Office; secondly, to solicit from industry and others constructive suggestions for solution of the interrelated classification and equipment development problems, and, thirdly, to determine ways and means by which far-reaching improvements may be realized. 

The committee had a number of meetings, formally and in small groups. Contact was maintained with many experts in Government and in industry. The 

Patent Office Committee on Mechanized Searching was of great assistance. Several members of the committee visited the Patent Office at some length to study various aspects of the operations at firsthand. 

The Commissioner of Patents presented charts outlining the major problems and demonstrating the effect of volume and complexity of patents, and other technical documents, on the rate of disposal of applications. The number of U.S. patents, foreign patents, and technical articles in the examiners' files has grown from 1,200,000 in 1900, to about 7 million in 1955. Over the same period the number of applications finally disposed of (allowed or abandoned) by the average examiner fell from 235 in 1900, to 100 in 1955. (It was pointed out that "final disposal" may require several actions by the examiner, and that each of these may involve additional search.)

The Commissioner pointed out the increasing complexity of inventions which is reflected, for example, by an increase of 133 percent in the size of the average application file record. Another chart exhibited by the Commissioner showed that the examiner must spend at least 100 percent more time in disposing of the average application than in 1910, while the size of the average patent, measured by pages of specification and sheets of drawing, grew 100 percent. Through examples, the Commissioner demonstrated the extreme variation in sizes in several chemical, electrical, and mechanical patents.

The Commissioner presented other evidence showing the relationship of reclassification activity to the classification backlog. The increase in the number of patents needing reclassification was shown to be at least keeping pace with the present reclassification activity in the Office. Thus, no inroads are being made in the large reclassification backlog. 

A plan was presented for overcoming the backlog which had been previously submitted to the Department, Budget Bureau, and to Congress. The plan contemplated expansion of the classification group to 141 men by 1956, maintaining that level until 1962, when it could drop to 30 men. This would consume the backlog at the rate of 300,000 patents per year, at a yearly cost of $1 million. 

The committee quickly came to appreciate the continuing diligent efforts of the Patent Office to deal with ever more complex problems within its appropriation and manpower limitations. The Patent Office, established to stimulate industrial progress, is now heavily overburdened by the products of the very inventive genius it has fostered and encouraged. There is a growing backlog of over 

200,000 patent applications. The ability of the individual examiner to dispose of applications has been slowly decreasing in direct proportion both to the increasing complexities of technological development, and to the ever-growing number of previously issued patents and other technical publications to be searched. On the average, 3½ years now elapse before a patent application is processed to final action.

The patent collections, as they grow, must be reorganized from time to time to reduce the number of patents which must be studied in the search in actively growing fields. Individual groups of patents which must be studied in connection with incoming claims must be kept to a minimum size, or the time spent in study and comparison may run into long hours or days. A more detailed study of this operation was made by the firm of Wallace Clark in 1948. Extracts from their findings are quoted in appendix II. 

The need for continuing regrouping is illustrated in a very simple example by the patent application on a golf ball capable of floating. Before the reclassification process, it would have been necessary to study 320 separate patents. 

After dividing these into smaller specific groups, it was necessary to look at only five patents.

It is obvious that inventions in such complicated fields as aircraft and television are growing so fast that the related patents need to be subdivided every few years.

In the more complicated fields, it is often necessary to give a lengthy and careful study to each patent as it is compared with the group of claims being examined, to exercise effective judgment as to whether a court would find enough in common to decide that it may form a part of an anticipation or invalidation. The determination always involves also the judgment as to whether a person "skilled in the art" of the subject being studied would think of making the changes or substitutions involved. Claims are often drawn to "means" foi performing a function-for example, the function of determining the relative position of a moving target with respect to a gunner. If expressed in broad terms, such claims may be completely met by a wide variety of alternative substitutes ranging from electronic circuits to mechanical arms and trains of gears. 

It is for this reason that the searcher must succeed in knowing the broadest possible meanings of the words of a claim and must let his imagination take him to all possible alternative variations on the subject matter he is investigating. 

In practice, the classification system of the Patent Office includes thousands of collected "search notes," based on hundreds of thousands of searches which have been made, and which provide associative trails to obscure or distantly related groups of patents which may include relevant elements or appropriate combining features.

The search problem is very critical. It is so severe that conventional means offer little hope for adequate improvement. Fortunately, new developments in automatic information processing machines encourage a new approach. These machines offer a promise of rapid selection of the most appropriate patents, and also the possibility of a vast and never-failing memory of trails once pursued in a difficult search.

The committee directed a preliminary canvassing of industry to determine the present availability of equipment and ideas applicable to the patent search problem. A considerable number of constructive suggestions and proposals have been received, and a number of them are summarized in appendix I. However, it quickly became evident that the committee could not merely select from the varied proposals any one offering an ideal solution. The problem involves a pioneering application of new and rapidly evolving techniques. 

Great advances are being made in the art of automatic data handling. We now have electronic computers fully capable of logical selections of data having specified salient characteristics. Matching of the desired selection criteria against the recorded characteristics of material on file can be carried out at speeds of 10,000 or more operations each second. New, compact, large-capacity storage media use magnetic or optical recording techniques that make rapid processing of very large files of information possible. Vigorous research and development, aggressively undertaken, is now being directed toward still higher speed operation, greater storage capacity, faster access rates, and lower cost. 

The committee has therefore reached the conclusion that although experimental mechanization of selected search operations should go ahead at once, it would be most advantageous to avoid the "freezing" of plans for the overall system of search.

It appears to the committee that the problem of search in the Patent Office is not one problem but several, for there is great diversity between fields and hence between methods of search which are applicable and which can be mechanized. Searching of chemical patents differs substantially from searching of patents on mechanical devices, where constant reference to drawings is essential. 

The creation of a classification and indexing system so that machinery can be effectively used for searching is an intellectual problem of formidable proportions because it involves a diversity of complex subject-matter fields. Decisions as to the type and extent of index entries or selection criteria, including their relation to different possible levels of data storage and retrieval, must be made before it is practical to specify the desired characteristics of a machine or combination of machines. An additional reason for not making firm choices now is that the potential capabilities of machines are undergoing rapid development and necessarily affect the desirability of organizing, indexing, and storing the patent information in one way rather than in another. 

We have given much thought to the nature of the machinery which would be fully applicable. It should be highly flexible, a dynamic rather than a static affair, capable of embodying the experience of examiners as it proceeds, so arranged that it can assist in its own coding, of such nature that it can readily be extended to encompass the great field of scientific literature when that becomes

mechanized as it must, and so constructed that interchange with industry is facilitated. A summary of the state of the art in this field is included as appendix III, which we trust will be valuable in itself, and will emphasize features which should be kept prominent as the work proceeds. 

Although at the present time there is no machine system for data handling commercially available which exactly meets the needs of the overall problem, there are three main lines of approach to the technological problem, all now being vigorously pursued. These are: the use of punched cards combined with electronic, analytical, and computing mechanisms; the systems based on magnetic recording techniques; and the systems based on photographic methods with photoelectric analysis of codes. 

While each of these has its important advantages, they have been developed in general for other less exacting purposes than patent search. In some cases, for example, where very high speed in analytical procedures is necessary, magnetic methods are showing great promise. The combination of microfilm facsimile records with punched cards and with electronic data processing is mak ing great strides. The photographic method with its enormous compression of storage space, and its facility in bringing the actual data in facsimile form immediately to the user, is capable of extraordinary versatility. But all of these are being developed primarily for office work and their application to more general data handling is in an exploratory stage. This It appears, therefore, that a group thoroughly conversant with this entire art and its present progress should influence the development in such a direction that machines explicitly adapted to patent search problems will emerge. should be done in collaboration with other departments of government which are active on similar problems, in collaboration with industry, and at the appropriate time if necessary by direct contracts for development. It appears to the committee that this approach promises to produce within a reasonable time machines which have far greater power than those at present immediately available.

The Patent Office conducted a successful experiment during 1950 mechanizing the search in connection with certain chemical compositions. A commercial punched card machine was modified for the purpose. Searches made on actual pending applications in the class of medicines turned out to be equally effective to those made by the Examiner by conventional means. It was demonstrated that the machine approximates "a multidimensional approach" to patent collections on the basis of many systems of identification. It was found that the class of carbon chemistry (class 260), which had just been reclassified, required very few changes to provide a framework for the chemical compound ingredients. 

It was found that the coding process necessary for mechanization required most of the same analytical operations and involved the same principles as are being carried on in the regular reclassification process. 

Tests made with the experimental project indicate— (a) Machine searching of 300,000 composition patents is immediately feasible; (b) Machine searching of compositions is faster and more accurate than the conventional method; (c) Machine searching uses a number of independent classification systems simultaneously; and (d) The cost per document for analysis will probably be approximately the same as for conventioned reclassification. 

The committee has been particularly impressed, through its deliberations, with the unanimity with which the idea of the closest possible collaboration between patent experts and data processing specialists has been advanced and enthusiastically received.

The background of experience in the design and application of data processing systems available at the National Bureau of Standards provides a basis for extensive cooperation with the Patent Office. In turn, this cooperative effort will be of benefit to other agencies of the Department of Commerce, and will provide a sound basis for further effective teamwork between Government and industry. The committee has especially appreciated the interest of industry both in the specific proposals received and in the many suggestions that industry might collaborate in such activities as analyses of patents for improved search procedures.

There are many places in Government where machines of this general nature apply. In fact it is hard to see how the business of Government can be carried out effectively in the future without greatly increased use of machines. There are other programs in the Government at the present time which have many

21 1 factors in common with the Patent Office problem. For example, the Air Force has an extensive program of research and development being carried out by the Eastman Kodak Co. on the storage and retrieval of intelligence information. 

The Bureau of the Census and other agencies have a number of data processing machines, which may be made available on a cooperative basis for experimental work. The Office of Technical Information has developed an experimental model of the "Rapid Selector" originally designed by Dr. Bush at MIT. 

This machine has been made available to the Patent Office. It would appear that collaboration with such other agencies having such similar programs will be of mutual benefit and may reduce the overall costs. 

A. Conclusions III. FINDINGS OF THE COMMITTEE 

To recapitulate, the committee has concluded that mechanization of a substantial portion of the search process is certainly coming. It will result in marked improvement in the operations of the Patent Office that will be of great and continuing benefit to industry in this country. 

The committee is convinced that it would be unwise to enter into an attempt to mechanize the entire search operation of the Patent Office in all of its phases at one time because of the rapid rate at which the application of machines to similar problems is advancing. It appears wise to permit the development to proceed step by step, taking full advantage of the latest advances available as each step is taken.

Machines now available commercially are not intended or adapted for immediate application to the specific search problems of the Patent Office. For this reason, the Patent Office must stimulate and guide the development of machines and techniques adapted to its particular requirements. The machine to be initially employed may have to be selected with the foreknowledge that it has limitations which could ultimately prevent its general application. The justification for its acquisition must rest mainly on the vital role it would play in the perfection of the system of organizing the material to be searched and in the design of improved machines. It would appear very desirable that the expert assistance of the National Bureau of Standards should be made available to the Patent Office in this program. 

There must be an intensive program of research and development within the Patent Office working toward the adaptation of machines to its specific and unique problems, which are not exactly the same as those found elsewhere. 

There are several different search problems in the Patent Office corresponding to the different types of patents, and a single machine development may not solve them all. The problems posed by complex machinery requiring constant reference to drawings may be different from the problem of searching chemical compositions. Electronic circuits present still other specific problems, and further study will bring out additional unique problems in specific fields. 

At the present time the Patent Office is far in arrears on the reclassification of patents, and its work is thus greatly hampered. The committee gave serious attention to this matter, in its relation to mechanization. It might be thought, offhand, that mechanizing would decrease the need for reclassification. On the other hand the introduction of machinery involves coding, and a basis for this is found in the system of classification. The committee hence conclude that the reclassification program should be accelerated. As it proceeds it should be accomplished with a continuing emphasis on those aspects which lend themselves readily to mechanization. We feel sure that the office can accomplish this well, and it certainly appears that reclassification is necessary for proper functioning of the office, whether mechanization is introduced or not. There need be no lost motion as reclassification and a program aimed at mechanization go forward simultaneously, if they are carefully coordinated. 

In order to be sure that this is done, the Patent Office will require a staff group free of daily operational responsibility, charged with the duty of continuing the intensive study of the problem and with the further duty of maintaining continuing close working relations with the National Bureau of Standards and with other agencies of Government and industrial organizations actively pursuing related objectives. This group should provide expert knowledge of the patent search problem, as a comparable group in Census has done in planning the effective use of computers by that agency. 

The Nation cannot afford a moratorium on Patent Office operations while the decisions are made, the system established, the information encoded, and the machines built and installed. Therefore, the regular work of the Office, includ-

ing the serious problem of reclassification, must go forward simultaneously with aggresive experimentation and development. 

Much of the cost of development and installation of equipment might be recaptured in time by providing for the use of the more efficient machine results by industry and the individual inventor on payment of reasonable fees. Industry will undoubtedly want portions of the mechanized record for its own use, and will be willing to pay for such duplicated machine records as apply to specific fields, such for example as petroleum refining, medicines, communication electronics, servo-mechanisms, and many others which come readily to mind. 

The committee firmly believes in the need for mechanization of a substantial portion of the Patent Office search operation. Further, the committee considers that such mechanization within the reasonably near future is both feasible and desirable. If these findings are to be translated into results the Department of 

Commerce must now take positive action. In formulating the specific recommendations which follow, the committee recognizes that their accomplishment will require funds. However, the committee considers that budget needs can be best determined by the experts in the Patent Office and the National Bureau of Standards. 

B. Recommendations 

1. The Patent Office should put machine searching of compositions of matter on an operational basis.—The classification group of the Office developed a system for mechanically searching compositions of matter on multidimensional basis. 

This activity culminated in a successful test on a limited number of patents in 1950, using a modified commercial punched card machine. This pilot operation by the Patent Office definitely establishes the feasibility of immediate mechanization of a substantial subject matter field. 

This has been successful in demonstrating that machinery can be of genuine value in such an application, and it has yielded important information on the characteristics necessary in machinery to handle the enormous bulk of the reference material to be consulted when a comprehensive undertaking is launched. This pilot plant operation should be continued, and the Patent Office should be strongly supported in the effort. It can be carried on in such manner that it will not involve a commitment on final plans, for example by using rented machines, or by cooperative use of machines now in possession of other Government agencies, such as the Bureau of Standards or the Bureau of the Census. It will promptly facilitate the work of the part of the search problem to which it is applied. It will yield information which will be helpful in planning the machinery for more general use. This pilot operation should probably be in the chemical field, based on recently reclassified groups of patents. 

2. The reclassification of patents should be accelerated. This will go hand in hand with the long-term mechanization program. The patents must be carefully analyzed and systematized for search whether by present manual methods or in any machine operation which may develop. The experience of the Patent 

Office has demonstrated that additional material can be placed in a classification system once organized at a rate many times faster than the process of establishing the original system.

As pointed out in the Wallace Clark report, "Classification is the most important single tool which the patent examiner uses in his work." The necessity of searching hundreds of patents in a given subclass which was made many years ago obviously takes very much longer than is required for the study of a well-defined reclassified group of the order of 20 to 30 patents. 

The experimental project with machines has proven that a class which has recently been reclassified provides the basis for very effective coding for machine searches. The use of new methods of classification may be stimulated by the utilization of machines working with the classification systems. However, the successful experiment using machines for search in the Patent Office proves that machines operate successfully when the material put on the machines has been recently reclassified according to the present system. 

It has been stated during the Committee's study that a man who has recently reclassified a class knows more about that art at that moment than anyone else in the world. With this in mind, industrial firms concentrating in specific fields might well contribute some manpower to the reclassification process. 

There have been a few offers from industry to cooperate in this way and these should be encouraged. In addition to the assistance provided by these men, such cooperation from industry would be a clear indication that the activity was useful to industry.

3. A Research and Development Unit should be established in the Patent 

Office. This unit should be free from daily production responsibility. It should be charged with the duty of continuing intensive study of problems of patent = classification and its relationship to automation and electronic computers. 

This group should be constituted as a general planning staff, reporting directly to the Commissioner of Patents. Although its first assignment would be the machine search problem, it should be useful over the long term on a much 

5 broader basis.

This group should develop working relations with the computer laboratory at the National Bureau of Standards and with other Government agencies having =related objectives. For example, the Air Force now has a large-scale development project with Eastman Kodak for the development of MINICARD which may provide techniques and equipment useful to the Patent Office. The National Science Foundation is supporting basic research into the theory of ma- Ichine translation, which may bring to light useful information on the storage of elements of information. The Rockefeller Foundation has supported studies in basic problems of classification. Other foundations and professional organizations have held conferences and symposia in the fields of computer application and information theory. Cooperation with these agencies should be established wherever possible in order to benefit from appropriate research and development activities being done elsewhere which may be useful to the Patent Office program. Close contact with industrial and nonprofit research organizations engaged in research and development in these fields should be maintained. 

Industry has come to recognize clearly that effective management involves planning ahead as well as turning out quantities of the current product. Companies with excellent production records have gone out of business from a failure to plan advances with the times. In order to save money in the long run, time and money must be dedicated to research. It has been found by industry that this is often returned a thousandfold. 

4. The National Bureau of Standards and the Patent Office should undertake a joint program to stimulate and develop machines and techniques specifically adapted to the Patent Office operations.-The statutory functions assigned to the Secretary of Commerce and delegated to the National Bureau of Standards include the provision of advisory service to Government agencies and the invention and development of devices to serve special needs of the Government. In accordance with this mandate, the National Bureau of Standards has been conducting studies in the field of data processing for other agencies, and has an able group of experts on the subject.

It appears highly desirable that this trend toward centralizing at the Bureau the more adanced parts of general programs for application of these new techniques should continue. If possible, an organizational unit should be established at the Bureau charged with the specific responsibility of advising and working with other Government agencies, such as the Patent Office, on the application of data processing machines to their problems. 

It is hard to see how the business of Government can be carried out effectively in the future without greatly increased use of machines. The Committee considers that direct, continuing support to the Bureau for this purpose will pay for itself in beneficial results throughout Government. In particular, it appears highly desirable that the Bureau should be asked to bring pertinent technical advances into focus in terms of applicability to the special requirements of the 

Patent Office. 

In so doing, the Bureau should keep fully abreast of current industrial de velopment in the field, it should conduct its own experimentation, and, at the proper time, it should supervise the construction by industry of a machine or machines explicitly adapted to the Patent Office problem. Careful judgment will be necessary as to the time when construction should be undertaken. 

There should be very close relationship as the work proceeds between those who have intimate knowledge, in the Patent Office, of the intricate problem of effective searching, and those who understand fully the complex art of the design of datahandling devices. The National Bureau of Standards has done excellent work in this field, and should receive strong support in its activity as an adviser and assistant to other Government agencies in these fields. 

5. An advisory committee should be attached to the office of the Secretary of 

Commerce to stimulate and coordinate the joint program.—The Committee noted the many related responsibilities of the Department of Commerce for providing organized information to industry and to the public. In addition to the Patent 

Office and the Bureau of Standards, there are such other bureaus as the Weather

Bureau, the Bureau of the Census. The Office of Technical Services is struggling with a heavy responsibility under the law for the "collection and dissemination of scientific, technical, and engineering information * * *.”

In order to insure that a program of mutual benefit is kept in operation at a maximum of effectiveness, the Committee urges that a continuing advisory group on the mechanization of information organization and search be attached directly to the Office of the Secretary of Commerce. 

This advisory group should make sure that appropriate experts in the problems of patent search engage in such a joint study with those expert in computer techniques. It should actively stimulate and supervise a joint program. In addition, it should see that agencies with related problems, such as the Census Bureau, are kept in touch with developments. It is very probable that existing equipment, such as that now in use at the Bureau of the Census, may be utilized during the development program. 

This group should also continue and expand the contacts with industry and research organizations which have been made during this preliminary study. 

Finally, the group should keep the Secretary of Commerce informed as to progress.

II. HISTORY OF PATENT OFFICE ACTIVITIES IN MECHANIZATION OF SEARCH 

In considering the Patent Office budget for fiscal year 1955, the Senate Appropriations Committee specifically directed that "an aggressive and thorough investigation be made as to the possibility of mechanizing the search operation and the installation of up-to-date types of equipment in order to modernize insofar as practicable the Patent Office operations.”

As a guide for implementing the Senate Committee recommendation relating to the patent examiners' search operations, Secretary Weeks prevailed on Dr. 

Vannevar Bush to organize and chair a committee of experts to examine the problem and recommend actions to be taken. A distinguished and able committee was assembled and after a most thorough study submitted their report, 

"Application of Machines to Patent Office Operations." This report presents an excellent appraisal of the values to be realized from mechanization, the character of the problem, and the possibilities of solution and recommends an organization for carrying out the work. It makes five specific recommendations. 

I These recommendations are: 1. The Patent Office should put machine searching of compositions of matter on an operational basis. 

2. The reclassification of patents should be accelerated. 

3. A research and development unit should be established in the Patent Office. 

4. The National Bureau of Standards and the Patent Office should undertake a joint program to stimulate and develop machines and techniques specifically adapted to the Patent Office operations. 

5. An advisory committee should be attached to the Office of the Secretary of 

Commerce to stimulate and coordinate the program and related efforts within the Commerce Department.

The report was submitted on December 22, 1954-almost 5 years ago. In the interval, there has been major advance in electronic computer technology, in the programing of computers; and the dimensions of the information retrieval problems are better known. Even so, today the report presents a currently good overall appraisal. In retrospect, the recommendations were excellent. 

It might well be asked: Why mechanization of the search process and what are the values in so doing? There are positive and unequivocal answers to these queries.

The patent examiners' search of the world's patent literature, scientific and technical publications, and all other likely publication media to determine whether or not the applied for invention had been previously disclosed, in whole or in part, required about 60 percent of the time he gave in 1948 to his consideration of a patent application. With the ever-increasing amount of invention and of scientific and technical publication, the search time continues to grow, so the productivity of the patent examiner, as measured by the number of patent applications he can act on per year, steadily decreases. This brings ever-increasing cost to issuance of patents. But perhaps of even greater importance are the delays in processing applications. There is probably not an adequate supply of people for the examiner work as now performed. The inadequacy in size of staff has resulted in a continuing large backlog of applications. The present backlog is 200,000 applications. This is 10 percent less than 5 years ago. backlog of 100,000 is considered desirable for most efficient operation. The resultant long time from filing an application until the issuance of a patent brings large, even if not measurable, penalties to the national economy. The Patent Office may also render an important service to the Nation's industries when an adequately effective mechanized search system is available. A large part of the time of the patent staff of an industrial company is devoted to search to determine- 1. before filing for a patent if an invention has in fact been made; 

2. freedom or right to use a new facility.

If the facility infringes an issued patent, rights must be obtained under the patent or it must be "designed around." A really effective mechanized system with its ready access file could provide a search service to industry on a fee basis that would speed up and lower the cost of its search efforts. 97076-63- -4

The Bush Committee believed that the mechanization of search would bring direct economies in Patent Office operation through the increase in productivity of the examiner that it would provide. Also, it would make possible a speeding up of patent action with resultant national economic gains. It should also result in a more complete search, thereby raising the level of integrity of issued patents, which should decrease the volume of patent litigation. The Panel concurs in this evaluation of values from mechanization of search. 

There did not appear to the Bush Committee, nor do there now appear, other technical areas of Patent Office procedures where significant scientific research and development are required.

The Patent Office had long been alert to the possible values of mechanization of search. Work at a low level was initiated in 1947 and continued through 1950, when it was terminated as a part of the general Federal cutback of nondefense expenditures. While an experimental search system of limited value resulted from this small pioneering venture, it was not considered far enough advanced for placing in productive service. However, the study of the problem, the development experience gained, and the interest aroused among the examiners were of value when the Office again turned its attention to mechanization of search in 1954.

In June 1954, Commissioner Watson established an internal Committee on Mechanized Searching with Don D. Andrews as Chairman. Its mission was "to review the work that had already been accomplished on this project and to plan and direct future mechanized searching activities within the Patent Office." 

Shortly after publication of the Bush Committee report, Commissioner Watson initiated actions for implementing all five of the recommendations. Throughout the period since the report, he has steadfastly pressed for their full and effective implementation. An appraisal of this implementation with recommendations for future action has been the task of this National Academy of 

Sciences Committee. 

An advisory committee attached to the Office of the Secretary of Commerce was organized in 1955 (recommendation 5 of the Bush Committee). It held its first formal meeting in November 1955, when the plans and programs of the Office and of the National Bureau of Standards were presented. This, alas, was the last meeting and the Committee has since been inactive. This has been of great concern to the Commissioner. He has awaited the findings of this Committee before attempting the organization of a new advisory committee. 

In the report of the group of experts appointed by the Panel, this comment is made: "In many respects, the very brief active life of the Scientific Advisory Committee established under recommendation 5 of the 1954 report must be regarded as highly unfortunate, and it seems essential to fill this gap as soon as possible. 

We believe that if such a committee had continued to provide broad general guidance for the last 3 years, their influence on choices of specific projects, on maintaining a proper balance between long-term and short-term items in the program, on effective use of consultants, and on administrative matters affecting the morale of personnel, would have been very helpful." 

The Panel fully concurs in this view. 

A mechanized searching section of "Classification Division I" was established in February 1955. In April 1956, this was converted into the Office of Research and Development with Don D. Andrews as Director. This organization has been the instrument for carrying forward the actions under Bush Committee recommendations 1, 3, and 4. IV. PRESENT ACTIVITIES-SUMMARY 

The Patent Office research and development staff, organized in accordance with recommendation 3, has engaged in a number of research and development efforts, partly on its own, in one case with the assistance of the Census Bureau, and in part in cooperation with the National Bureau of Standards under recommendation 4. Most of this work has been primarily directed to investigation and development of suitable search strategies for mechanized operation in the field of compositions of matter (recommendation 1).

The first project undertaken was one involving the use of punched-card equipment in fairly standard form to search the steroid file. This was based on the exploratory development, during the period 1946-50, referred to in the 1954 report of the Bush Committee. This process is now in routine production operation for searches in this area; the methods and the card decks developed are also being used by a number of chemical companies concerned with this specific art. Work was then begun on a method using punched-card equipment extensively modified for the Patent Office by the Census Bureau. This, the 

ILAS (interrelated logic accumulating scanner) project, was carried through to pilot plant operation. The success of the methods used resulted in programing the operation, with some extensions, for the Bendix general-purpose computer. This adaptation, though operationally successful, was abandoned because of the high costs incurred in using an electronic computer that was not at all well equipped for the specific job. The ILAS experience led, however, to the DRAM project, now underway, to modify the previous search techniques for application to the resin and polymer arts by use of an "inverted-file" technique which can be applied on the RAMAC random-access memory device. 

This technique, inapplicable on punched-card equipment, should markedly reduce

search time by directing the search only to pertinent areas of the file, rather than searching every "document" in the file. 

A second area of effort is in the field of language studies; that is, the investigation of methods of encoding the content of documents that will be applicable outside the field of compositions of matter. Research in this field is necessary if progress is to be made in areas lacking the generally well-organized structure which nature has provided in the case of the chemical arts, which have proven so useful as a basis for development of usable search strategies. 

Another major effort is Project HAYSTAQ, which is a joint project of the Patent Office and the National Bureau of Standards. Though still concerned with searching in the chemical arts, this is definitely a longer range project. It employs a general-purpose computer as a tool for studying search strategies more generally than can be done on types of mechanisms more or less specifically designed for a limited range of logical search techniques. 

In addition, the National Bureau of Standards, mainly through outside contractors, has carried out a number of projects for the Patent Office, or in areas directly related to its needs. Some of these have yielded disappointing results; a few of the more promising ones are still active. A brief résumé of this work will be given below.

Personnel of both the Patent Office and Bureau of Standards merit the admiring attention of others working in related fields for achieving an encouraging degree of success in mechanized searching despite limitations of time, personnel, facilities, and other resources; one handicap has definitely been lack of guidance from a continuing Scientific Advisory Committee. The work on the short-term projects has perhaps been too specifically oriented to available equipment. All of the work has also concentrated on the problem of search strategies; other important aspects of even the immediate problem, such as methods of file construction and maintenance, have not as yet received anything like the attention necessary to develop a system with reasonable cost balances. 

It is noted that this work has been done mainly by a research and development staff comprised of skilled patent examiners, without specific training or experience in engineering, mathematics, or other applicable skills. 

In many respects, they have progressed much further than could have been expected. 

Specifically, they seem to have done rather better, in terms of actual accomplishment, than most other workers in the field of information retrieval. 

In following sections, somewhat more detail will be given on the more important specific projects, with references to available descriptive material.

Interrelated logic accumulating scanner (ILAS)

This is described in P.O.R. & D. Report No. 6 "Interrelated Logic Accumulat ing Scanner (ILAS)," June 25, 1957, by Don D. Language studies

The Patent Office has done much work in the field of pseudo languages; that is, studies of means of expressing the content of a filed document and of a search question, involving not only specific items of content but also a large class of possible interrelations between specific items, in suitable form for machine use. 

Reference is made to several P.O.R. & D. reports covering this work, specifically: 

No. 1. Storage and Retrieval of Contents of Technical Literature-Nonchemi cal Information-Preliminary Report; May 15, 1956, by Andrews and Simon Newman.

No. 4. Do-First Supplementary Report; June 1957, by Newman. 

No. 12. Do-Second Supplementary Report; November 17, 1958, by Newman. 

No. 9. Linguistic Problems in Mechanization of Patent Searching; December 

17, 1957, by Newman.

This work has been done mainly by Newman and C. G. Smith. The importance of work in this field to progress in extending mechanized searching to areas outside the field of compositions of matter can hardly be overemphasized. The result obtained to date in the chemical arts seem to be essentially connected with the availability in this area of a natural structure and its symbolic representation in providing a useful means of encoding and its successful exploitation. Even so, one of the first major obstacles was encountered at the first level of generalization, when descriptors such as resins, solvents, or alcohols, involving properties rather than specific structure, had to be encoded. This obstacle was successfully overcome, but extension of meachanized techniques into other arts will require much more powerful tools than have yet been provided. 

This is a field in which a great deal of research will be needed; the men in the Patent Office group working in this field have attacked it with enthusiasm and energy, and have shown themselves bright, inventive, and systematic. 

They have not however, in our judgment, come close to providing what is required; at best they have explored some promising approaches for a short distance. The problem seems to us a much more difficult one than they consider it, and we believe that it requires the use of a wide variety of mathematical and logical tools that they are not equipped to use. It is impossible as yet to be sure just which tools are appropriate to the task, though we observe that in one particular case the application of Boolean algebra (whose utility is certainly not appreciated) would probably have been of great value. It seems likely that set theory, lattice theory, and abstract algebra, to pick a few likely candidates, might be of great help here.

To summarize, this seems to us a field of importance in which technical mathematical skills not available as routine working tools to the present staff must be provided if much progress is to be made. If personnel with adequate technical qualifications could be made available to the project (and these must be sought as consultants or through contract), the interest and the long experience in Patent Office problems of the present personnel could be put to much better use in providing indoctrination and liaison for the needed experts than in continuing on the present basis. 

Project HAYSTAQ 

This project is a cooperative effort with the National Bureau of Standards; the staff includes H. R. Koller and H. Pfeffer of the Patent Office and R. A. 

Kirsch and Ethel C. Marden of the National Bureau of Standards. The work is described in Patent Office R. & D. Report No. 5, “Organization of Chemical 

Disclosures for Mechanized Retrieval," June 14, 1957, by Lanham, Leibowitz,

Koller, and Pfeffer, Report No. 10, "A First Approach to Patent Searching Pro cedures on Standards Electronic Automatic Computer (SEAC)," January 28, 1958, by Pfeffer, Koller, and Marden, and R. & D. technical notes No. 2, "The 

HAYSTAQ System: Some Experimental Results," April 1, 1959, by Pfeffer, 

Koller, and Marden.

HAYSTAQ is a long-range experimental study of the requirements of largescale searching systems, using an available high-speed electronic computer as a tool. The machine used is the National Bureau of Standards SEAC, because of its ready availability, though it is recognized that in a number of respects it is far from being an ideal machine for actual production operation. In essence this project is simulating a mechanized searching system on a large-scale generalpurpose computer.

There are four main areas of effort. These are: (1) Study of types of questions presented by users of a file of chemical disclosures; (2) Design of a large file suitable for use on an electronic computer in this art-this includes methods of preparation and maintenance of the file; (3) Development of efficient search strategies; and (4) Study of possible extensions to other arts. 

Thus far most of the effort has been devoted to the third area listed. This has developed through several stages, and testing of programs for handling both specific and generic questions is now in progress. In designing search strategies, the project has concentrated on quick determination of whether a given entry in the file is relevant to any of the questions asked. The method uses a serial search, and speed is obtained by early rejection where possible; this is accomplished by testing general features of the document before starting detailed examination. Here they have obtained valuable results. 

The problem of organizing large files for such an operation presents problems. 

This is being handled by dividing files into subfiles, with the arrangement within these by documents in serial order; the subfiles are scanned first according to the questions which specify the search. There are a number of reasons for supposing that such a file organization may prove inefficient, and that other techniques (perhaps the inverted file used in DRAM) would be preferable. It is possible that limitations of the particular computer (SEAC) used for the project influenced the decisions on this point. On the other hand there is a possibility that here research has proceeded in the wrong order, in that components of the program have been designed before sufficient thought was given to the operation of the overall system. Thus there may be here a warning that when research is bound too closely to production there may be a pressure to show that something specific is being worked on; since initially the only specific things are components, there may result a misdirection of effort. 

It now seems that both the DRAM and HAYSTAQ programs will be checked out within a few months of each other. To become operational each will require a large file of chemical disclosures. It is possible that some of the file preparation work may be shared between the projects, but the panel is generally of the opinion that insufficient thought has been given to this problem, and that substantial duplication of effort may be necessary. 

There are as yet no cost figures on file preparation or operational costs for HAYSTAQ. Since this project is a simulation estimates of direct operational costs on the SEAC would not be immediately useful, but attempts should be made to estimate times of detailed operations in terms permitting translation into cost figures for actual operation on other machines better designed for production use. 

National Bureau of Standards projects In addition to its important contributions to Project HAYSTAQ, the National Bureau of Standards, directly or through outside contractors, has carried out a number of projects, and has others still in the mill, either for the Patent Office or in areas related to its needs. In direct connection with Project HAYSTAQ, they have examined problems of editing raw data input for rearrangement into a variety of data formats, a prototype method for a dictionary, thesaurus, or “see also" type of lookup that may prove useful in handling search questions, and a search method for element-by-element inspection of chemical formulas. 

Closed projects include: (1) A study of mechanized search procedures that essentially attempted to duplicate manual methods-this was discontinued because the HAYSTAQ approach seemed more profitable; (2) an experiment in indexing a sample of the patent art dealing with earth-boring tools by conventional library techniques, carried out by consultants experienced in the library field-the National Bureau of Standards concludes that at least in this field the Patent Officer classifier does better with his present manual methods; it would, however, consider another proposal after more thought has been given to the difficulties found on the first trial; and (3) exploration (by a consultant) of

frontier techniques for information retrieval systems, such as the use of lattice theory in the definition of systems. 

Work in progress includes (1) a contract with Dr. Yngve at MIT on the application of machine translation techniques to development of pseudo-language structures of possible use in mechanized information retrieval; (2) experimentation with reducution of digital storage requirements for computer identification of basic shapes and patterns found in printed text and schematic drawings; and (3) development of improved Bush microfilm selectors for experiments on automatic selection from Patent Office copy files. 

The Patent Office has made large (in proportion to total effort) use of other agencies of Government-specifically the Bureau of Standards and the Census Bureau-industry, and consultants from universities and professional firms. (In their complete report the experts group makes some critical comments of the "consultant" experience.)

Many of the projects described are primarily aimed at the broad extension of the mechanized search (so profitably implemented for steroid compounds) to the entire area of composition of matter in the chemical area. A few of them may well contribute to solutions in the mechanical or electrical areas. Giving emphasis to the chemical area in the pioneering stage of this new art was good strategy. 

For here the generally well-organized structure which nature has provided in the chemical arts has proven most useful as a basis for development of usable search strategies, working within the area of established methods of programing so that the problems can be fed into the machines. The extension of mechanized search beyond the composition of matter area, however, is a completely different story. Unique designations so that information can be programed for machine solution of drawings of mechanical systems, of electrical circuit diagrams and of descriptive material present challenges of the greatest magnitude. There are simply not now available knowledge and art for accomplishing this. It is here that research must make its contribution--must be first. The problems of getting the information into unique and usable form or language and the retrieval of the information are really the big unknowns. The development or selection of machines for solving the problems, while presenting difficulties, are now manageable with existing computer know-how.

The Patent Office, aided by the Bureau of Standards, is taking some limited steps into this area through its "language," indexing, information retrieval, and search strategy researches. Much well directed and inspired research will be required before any significant extension of mechanized search beyond the composition of matter area will be possible. Our Experts Group report makes some apt comments about this: "The program to date has yielded valuable results in mechanized searching of certain arts in the field of compositions of matter. Projects now underway may be expected to provide additional information in this area; operational data on 

DRAM and HAYSTAQ should be available in the next few months. Thus far the retrieval theory used is basically that of descriptors-each document is described in terms of a prescribed list of features, and the questions must be formulated from the same list. The second limitation is the method of indexing, which depends essentially on use of the chemical structural formula. This is fairly straightforward in this particular case, but may be much more subtle in the case of describing technology in general. "The difficulty may be indicated by considering extension of the present techniques to the electronic arts. From a circuit diagram one may compute functional characteristics of the circuit. From a structural formula it is not yet generally possible to derive useful properties of a chemical compound; such properties must be recorded explicitly, whereas electronic properties could be computed from values of the circuit parameters. Thus the abstract of a chemical document can be "read" by the system only in terms of concepts prevailing at the time the abstract was prepared, while in principle an electrical document can be "read" in terms of currently used concepts. Accordingly a circuit filed 

20 years ago as a push-pull amplified might today, with some changes of parameters, be interpreted as the modern flip-flop. Further difficulties with electrical circuits are the problems of similarity and equivalence. The corresponding problems have been essentially solved for chemistry. As for circuits, we need only cite the radically different appearance of two schematic diagrams, one of which is a transistorized version of the vacuum tube circuit disclosed in the other. "The language studies being carried out in the Patent Office and, through the National Bureau of Standards, by contractors, represent attempts to increase the power of mechanized methods in overcoming these difficulties. Other tech-

niques should also be examined, such as the possible use of document-association techniques in file construction as an alternative to descriptor techniques." 

The programs in progress divide themselves roughly into two distinct areas: 

1. Near at hand projects, which largely are directed at broadening to the maximum possible the area of composition of matter searches that can be mechanized. 

2. Research to obtain new knowledge and techniques that will permit progress in the other areas of technology. 

V. EVALUATION AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

The projects in the near at hand area appear to be well conducted and give promise of broadening the area of coverage of mechanization of search in the composition of matter area. They will, no doubt, also provide some background of value to the more challenging areas ahead. Within the time available, neither the Panel nor its Experts Group could form judgments: 

1. As to whether or not there was a more general attack on the composition of matter area that would be more promising, or 2. Whether or not the number of projects was too large--somewhat of a "shotgun" approach. It was primarily for these reasons that the Experts

Group in their report recommend that a "Systems Engineering Study" be obtained through contract.

The Panel, while in full agreement with the Experts Group as to the need for an examination and evaluation of the "near at hand" projects of the general type that system engineering studies make, does not believe that such a study obtained through contract provides the best means for the desired examination and evaluation. The Panel's view on action to be taken in the "near at hand" area will be included in its analysis of the "long-range" or research area. 

The work of the Patent Office to date makes it clear that a large body of research must be done before specific development projects in areas other than composition of matter can profitably be undertaken. Much of the research to be done here is also of general interest in the extension of the areas of use of electronic computers. Language translation is a typical example of the broadening areas of their use. 

It is now important to arrive at policies on the scope and rate of such broad research effort to be done or sponsored by the Patent Office. On one extreme, should the research program be planned for a coverage so broad iu scope and at such a speed that most of the burden of providing the required new knowledge and techniques is assumed by the Patent Office? On the other extreme, should the policy be one of no basic broad or general research, limiting the effort to an effective awareness of progress in research by others accompanied by a continuing, in-balance effort to apply the developing knowledge to the mechanization of search?

The Panel believes that the time is ripe for a study that will provide a basis for forming this research policy. This study must be made in close collaboration with those now in the program in the Patent Office and the Bureau of Standards. It should be staffed and led by most competent scientists and engineers who are experienced in this area. 

For this study the Panel therefore recommends the following procedure: 

The Commissioner obtain the service as consultants to him of three mentwo from industry, one from the academic community-to aid him and the Director of the Bureau of Standards in planning, directing and evaluating the study. The choice of these three men is most important. The two from industry should be chosen from among directors of industrial research in the electronic computer area. The blending of scientific knowledge and mature research management judgments that this will provide is most important. The academic member should be chosen from among the distinguished professors of mathematics who are working in this general area. These consultants will not conduct the study. They will advise and aid in procuring the men from the 

Nation's industrial and academic communities to associate in the study with selected members of the Patent Office and the Bureau of Standards. They will broadly direct and follow the course of the study in much the same pattern as an industry research director follows, monitors and consults on similar studies conducted in his own organization. 

Such a study, evaluation, and decision on research policy can be done in 6 months. Until it is completed, the research programs should continue as at present.

These consultants and the study group are also admirably suited to the study of the "near at hand" program recommended by the group of specialists. It

is recommended that this study also be a part of their assignment. The questions and critical comments in the report of the specialist group (filed in reference material) can well form a basis for this study. 

Until the magnitude and character of the mechanization program are decided upon after the completion of this study, firm recommendations on the conduct of the program are inappropriate. However, our views that are not dependent on the character and size of future effort will be given. 

The research and development personnel of the Patent Office are all professional examiners. While they have worked with great dedication and made substantial progress, the personel must be expanded by the addition of specialists with deep training in appropriate areas of physics, mathematics and linguistics. 

There are such almost insuperable difficulties in attracting and holding scientists of the required level of competence for advanced research in automata in the Patent Office that we do not believe that it should be attempted at this time. 

An experienced and competent leadership in scientific research is essential. An environment that is favorable to scientific endeavor and an employment that gives promise of permanence and a career in science are basic necessities for obtaining and holding the scientists of the high quality required. These conditions do not now exist in the Patent Office to the degree necessary for scientific research of the level and diversity of that required for a dynamic Patent Office program. 

Such leadership, environment, and opportunities for permanence do exist in the National Bureau of Standards. Members of its staff are now in the program. 

Further, the Bureau has been, and is now, the established and natural fulcrum for a balanced Federal effort in research relating to information systems. It is, therefore, our judgment that the future programs on automata, whatever their nature or size, should be cooperatively conducted by the Patent Office and the Bureau of Standards. The continued participation of many of the examiners now in the program is essential. They provide professional knowledge of highest quality in manual search and in classification. They should be integrated into the teams of the projects, but with primary residence for the projects at the Bureau. The Bureau will be responsible for the mathematical, physical, and language research and the development of machines. It will provide the scientists and development engineers in the programs. This, within the Government teaming, coupled with well considered contracted support from the academic community and industry, will provide an effective research and development effort on information systems.

This pattern of organization of effort has the appearance of modifying the Bush committee recommendations 3 and 4 (p. 4). In fact, it is not a modification. In the body of the Bush report in elaborating on its recommendation for a research and development unit in the Patent Office it states: This unit "should be charged with the duty of continuing intensive studies of patent classification and its relation to automation and electronic computers." Also, it recommends "that the 

National Bureau of Standards and the Patent Office*** undertake a joint pro gram to stimulate and develop machines and techniques specifically adapted to the Patent Office operations."

A very large expenditure, whether or not a large and dynamic research effort is made, will be required for the research and development effort before mechanization of the search operation is realized. The $1.4 million already expended is perhaps less than one-tenth of the minimum expenditure that will be required. 

A continuing Advisory Committee for the entire mechanization of search is vitally important. We recommend that the three consultants for the ad hoc study be continued as the nucleus of such a committee and that after program policy has been determined two additional members be selected. The Commissioner and the Director of the Bureau of Standards, with advice from the consultants, should establish terms of service that will permit of a continuing background of experience within the committee. 

The Bush committee recommended that reclassification of patents be accelerated. (Recommendation 2.) Within the available manpower, it has not been possible to make progress. The reclassification backlog is now 1,200,000 original patents. It was 1,100,000 in 1955, the time of the Bush committee recom. mendation.

Reclassification must go hand-in-hand with the mechanization program. This backlog should be eliminated to the full extent that such reclassification will be usable when the ultimate general mechanization of search is realized. It is recommended that additional staff be made available for this work, limited only to the extent to which such effort provides classification that will be suited to mechanized search.

VI. SUMMARY AND RECOMMENDATIONS 

The mechanization of search is of great value. The knowledge already obtained in the modest program of research and development provides basis for optimism as to its ultimate realization. However, there is now need of a determination of the scope and rate of future work. The organization of the operations of research and development also should be changed. It is therefore recommended— 1. that a study, planned and organized as recommended, be promptly made to establish size, content and rate of the future research and development program in mechanization of search. Present programs shall be continued as at present until the study is completed and future plans established; 

2. that the operations of the program be shared by the Patent Office and the Bureau of Standards as detailed earlier. The patent examiners now in the program shall continue. They will be supplemented by the scientists and engineers required for scientific and language research and for the development of machines. The primary location for the work will be at the Bureau. This plan is recommended independent of the size and scope of the future program; 

3. that an advisory committee, formed as recommended, for the research and development program be established immediately after the plan of the future program is determined. This committee should advise with the 

Commissioner of Patents and the Director of the Bureau of Standards; and 

4. that the reclassification activity be accelerated, limited only by the requirement that the reclassification done be fully applicable when mechanization is established. 

Respectfully submitted.

REPORT ON THE PROSPECTS OF AUTOMATION TO THE SEARCH PROCEDURES OF THE PATENT OFFICE TO THE COMMISSIONER OF PATENTS (Submitted by the Commissioner's Panel, Gilbert W. King, Chairman, Andrew 

Gleason, and Thornton Fry, March 1961)

THE PROBLEM 

In the course of each year the Patent Office is required to examine between 50 and 100,000 applications for patents and to determine which, if any, of the many claims made under each application represent ideas of sufficient novelty to be patentable. This work is carried out by patent examiners who must have expert qnowledge of the state of the technology and of the literature describing the technology. Since the criterion of patentability is novelty in an absolute sense, any individual claim must be compared, in theory at least, with each of the 3 million already granted U.S. patents, with each of the many patents granted by foreign governments, and with all the technological literature published here or anywhere throughout the world. A device useful in radio receivers was once quoted against a claim for a clutch. Even the Bible has been quoted against the novelty of a claim. In practice, however, most claims need only be searched through a very small portion of the vast store of available documents. The real problem lies in the continuing increase in the rate at which knowledge is accumulating. As narrowly defined as the categories of patent classification may be, the background material has grown so rapidly in each as to put it beyond the grasp of any single man. 

The problem is, of course, a general one throughout science and technloogy and particularly worthy of attention for that reason. In the 17th century there were men who kept abreast of all of science, but today even the fragments into which all subjects have been split are vast, and a scientist is hard put to keep up with the reserach published on a small segment of a single field. In spite of the growing number of information services, information gaps become more prevalent and more difficult to fill. Mathematicians have been known to work out a theory from scratch rather than attempt to find it in the literature; a chemist will devise a new synthesis rather than search the journals; a physicist may perform an expensive experiment rather than look for previous work on the subject. It could very well be true that more than half the effort of the scientific and technological personnel of the world is now being spent in

a duplication of previous effort. If this estimate is correct, then an efficient method of making knowledge available would double the effectiveness of the scientific community.

It is interesting to note, in returning to the Patent Office problem, that its relationship to the general problem of access to scientific and technical information has a historical basis. One of the primary purposes of the patent system, as originally devised, was to serve as an information clearinghouse. Un- fortunately this latter function has all but disappeared. In their present arrangement, the patent files are of little value to anyone looking for a device to accomplish a specific technical objective. This underlines the existence of "strategic" aspects of searching which must be distinguished from the "methodical" aspects, especially where the use of mechanical or electronic aid is contemplated. In human systems, "strategic" shortcomings in the system organization can in some measure be overcome by the ingenuity of whoever is conducting the search. In machine systems, there are no "free" sources of strategy. 

That steps be taken toward mechanization is dictated, of course, by the sheer quantity of the material to be searched. There is little question, however, that the effectiveness of very high-speed, mechanized searching procedures will depend on the formulation of adequate searching and retrieval theory. The Patent 

Office has quite properly set up an Office of Research and Development to study the possibilities of obtaining mechanical or electronic assistance. This Office has made a number of excellent research contributions to the general problem of retrieval and has started a number of small-scale projects to help the searchers in particular areas. One of these projects, the steroid chemistry file, has already proved valuable not only within the Patent Office but also to the drug industry as a whole. It is well worth noting that one of the reasons for the success of this project is its contribution to the forgotten clearinghouse function of the Patent Office alluded to above. 

With these facts in mind the Kelly Committee, in its analysis of the role of the Department of Commerce in science and technology, proposed that a committee should be formed to advise the Secretary of Commerce concerning the information retrieval problem. This committee was appointed by the Secretary in 

August 1960.

RECOMMENDATIONS 

We have the honor to submit the following major recommendations: 

1. The mission of the NBS should be amended to establish a substantial program of research in information retrieval under the regular NBS budget. In addition to its primary research factor, the group responsible for this program should continue to serve as a clearinghouse for ideas of importance in the field. 

To strengthen the position of the NBS as a coordinating center, the budget for information retrieval should provide the additional overhead necessary to accommodate qualified long-term visitors from industry, universities, and other 

Government departments. 

2. The Patent Office should transfer its efforts in pure research to the NBS but continue its work in setting up small information retrieval systems within suitable sections of the Patent Office. The Patent Office should continue its close liaison with the NBS through the provision for visiting suggested above. 

3. The Patent Office should conduct an adequate program of comparison tests to determine the relative efficiency of various methods of patent searching. 

Such a program must begin with an objective appraisal of the present hand methods of search. This primary appraisal must be done by an outside organization.

COMMENTS 

At present the barriers to success in the information retrieval problem do not lie in technology but rather in theory. If there was a sufficiently well focused picture of just what is to be accomplished by an information retrieval system, it would be reasonable to attempt a crash program to implement it through the design of equipment. But beyond the vague idea that an information retrieval system should find all documents in its file relevant to a given subject, we have no such picture; therefore, a great deal of fundamental research is necessary before any substantial progress will be made. We feel that it is very unlikely that information retrieval systems competent to handle files comparable with those of the Patent Office will be available for many years. 

On the other hand, the importance of information retrieval for the economy as a whole leads us to recommend that the Commerce Department should do more work in the problem. Most of the work now being done in this country is

being financed by the Government either through the NSF or some branch of the DOD. With financing from the NSF, the NBS is now maintaining a clearinghouse for information on information retrieval work and is itself making a small effort in the problem. We feel that this effort should be considerably expanded and the very large efforts supported by DOD be made generally available in an unclassified form.

The NBS is a very appropriate place to center an activity for fundamental research. Work on information retrieval requires the combined services of logicians, mathematicians, linguists, engineers, and probably experts in many other technical fields. The well-established scientific reputation of the NBS makes it possible for them to hire trained personnel in these fields. Moreover, the NBS has in the past cooperated effectively with other research centers through a system of visiting research appointments. Under such a plan, organizations with specific information retrieval problems could send one or two of their people to work in cooperation with the NBS staff on the NBS premises. The salaries of these persons would be paid by their home organizations, while NBS would pick up the bill for overhead.

Within the Patent Office we find several projects in information retrieval underway. Of these the most notable is the sterioid chemistry file, which, as we have noted above, has already proved invaluable both within and without the 

Patent Office. 

The very considerable success of this project is unfortunately no indication that a corresponding success can be achieved for the Patent Office problem as a whole. 

We must emphasize that it handles a rather modest number of patents (about 1,200) and that the nature of the compounds is such that there will be very few references from this class to other classes; the steroid file operates within a small and effectively closed system. Moreover the structure of chemical compounds is imposed by nature in a methodical way which makes it possible to find effective classification schemes. When we leave the chemical field, we find problems which are superficially similar but complicated by the fact that components can be attached to one another in a far more arbitrary way than is possible with chemical atoms.

These differences are well understood by the men in the Office of Research and Development in the Patent Office, a group for whom we have no little praise. 

This group is devotedly exploring the possibilities of various techniques for crossreference filing. It can be expected that they will produce effective, workable techniques for handling other fairly small blocks of patents after the fashion of the steroid chemicals. The results they achieve will undoubtedly be helpful to the patent examiners in certain fields, but it is extremely unlikely that a really major improvement in the overall efficiency of searching can be accomplished in this way. We feel that these efforts should continue and that the group should regard itself as primarily concerned with development; the fundamental research aspects of their work should be transferred to the proposed group at NBS. Of course, we expect that close liaison will continue between the Patent Office group and the Information Retrieval Division at NBS. 

Cooperation between an enlarged theoretical group at NBS and the experienced group of patent examiners at the Patent Office will be of great benefit to both. 

A theoretical group cannot work effectively if too far separated from practical problems, and the Patent Office can apparently find restricted areas within its files of various sizes and posing various particular problems. On the other hand, the Patent Office stands to profit greatly from the existence of a group at NBS able to hire experts in a variety of fields, which the Patent Office probably cannot. 

THE OUTLOOK FOR DATA RETRIEVAL AT THE PATENT OFFICE 

It is extremely unlikely that an information retrieval system of such a scope as to radically alter the manpower requirements of the Patent Office will be available for many years. All operational planning should be based on the assumption that only small-scale assistance can be expected from data retrieval systems.

The Office of Research and Development in the Patent Office should continue its efforts, however, to introduce retrieval systems within those sections of the Patent Office where it seems advantageous, but those aspects of its work which are of a purely research character, not immediately applicable to this program, should be transferred to the National Bureau of Standards. 

From the success of the steroid chemistry project one naturally tends to conclude that within the next few years a full-fledged information retrieval system can be built which will substantially reduce the amount of time required to pro-

cess patent applications. This optimism is not justified by the facts. We must emphasize that the steroid chemical file handles only a relatively small number of patents and that, because it is concerned with a distinctive class of chemicals, few references need be considered outside of this small group of patents; hence it is not representative of the great bulk of Patent Office work. These facts are clearly understood within the Office of Research and Development, but the tendency to overoptimism is strong among those who are not continually reminded of the difficulties by day-to-day wrestling with the problems. 

We have remarked that the problem of large-scale information retrieval has not yet reached the theoretical level necessary to be regarded as a technological (as opposed to a research) problem. Yet even if all the theoretical background were now available and whatever technological problems concomitant with actually installing a system were solved, the capital cost of the installation might very well be prohibitive. We refer to the tremendous effort necessary to convert the data now existing only in printed form to whatever form the retrieval machines could accept. This capital cost might very easily be more than could be justified in terms of the annual savings to be anticipated, unless the system is also useful for the broader function of the Patent Office. Of course, the conversion may eventually be possible automatically, but this will delay even more the advent of an effective large-scale system. 

Although a retrieval system geared to the Patent Office as a whole is not yet in sight, we know that some assistance can be obtained through small systems in those areas where special circumstances prevail. The Office of Research and 

Development has found a number of promising areas, and there are several projects underway to develop appropriate systems for them. The men working on these projects are enthusiastic and able, and their work is certainly as good as any now being done in the whole field of information retrieval. We recommend that this group continue its developmental work. 

Pure research should be carried on in a research environment. As we have pointed out above, the NBS probably can hire specialists in all the lines of thought which may be necessary to the development of data retrieval theories and systems, but the Patent Office probably cannot. Therefore, any general research project in the data-retrieval field is certain to be benefited by transfer to the proposed NBS information retrieval center, where its personnel will be continuously in contact with all the associated disciplines. 

In recommending that "pure" research projects should be transferred, we recognize that it is difficult to distinguish the "pure" from the "applied." An appropriate criterion in this case might be: If a patent examiner's knowledge of Patent Office operations is essential to carry on the project, it should stay in the Office of Research and Development; otherwise it should be moved to the NBS.

Of course, we envisage that the Office of Research and Development in the Patent Office will maintain close liaison with the NBS center. Coordination at the working level should serve both parties well. It will give Patent Office personnel the opportunity to discuss their problems with a wide variety of experts and at the same time provide the NBS people with practical problems of many sizes and varying degrees of complexity to keep their research in contact with reality.

STUDY OF PATENT OFFICE OPERATIONS 

The Patent Office should engage the services of an outside organization to appraise the efficiency of the hand search methods now being used in the Patent Office.

We were indeed surprised to discover that the Patent Office has not conducted any methodical investigation of the accuracy and efficiency of the search methods now being used in the processing of patent applications. The frequent appeals within the Patent Office and the considerable volume of patent litigation carried on in the courts indicate that there is considerable imperfection in the present operating procedures; it is important to study the sources and measure the frequency of Patent Office errors. It is obvious that no effective appraisal can be made of the new information retrieval systems unless the standards of present performance are known, but quite aside from its relation to the development of data-handling techniques, an objective investigation of present efficiency would appear to be a matter of sound managerial practice. 

The whole philosophy of the patent system is dependent on the accuracy of the patent examiner's work. Some errors are inevitable; this is no reflection on the Patent Office or its examiners, nor is our recommendation that the error

rate be measured. As time goes on, it is quite likely that the rate will go up because the search problem is becoming intrinsically more difficult, although the introduction of information retrieval systems may reverse the trend. If the error rate increases sufficiently and it proves to be impossible to reduce it, then our present patent system will have to be abandoned or seriously modified. The error rate may thus be regarded as important an indicator of the health of the potent system as the size of the backlog and the mean time of application processing.

The problem of measuring the efficiency of any operation as large as that carried on in the Patent Office is very difficult. Since it will at least require the services of expert statisticians and operations analysts, we recommend that the services of a competent outside organization be retained. Moreover, we feel that, especially at the beginning of such a self-study progam, it would be impossible for a team from within the Patent Office to maintain objectivity. We are aware that the confidential character of the information contained in patent applications complicates the problem of retaining an outside organization, but we note that outside organizations have made surveys in the Department of Defense in the face of even more serious security problems. 

When the idea of repeated efficiency measurements becomes established, and techniques for making such measurements are sufficiently developed, a group should be set up within the Patent Office to continue the work, perhaps in the Office of R. & D., perhaps in a new division. Such a group would also check the effectiveness of new retrieval systems as they are worked out and introduced by the Office of R. & D. We predict that the knowledge gained in designing measures of search efficiency will prove to be of great value in the study of information retrieval systems.

THE INTRODUCTION OF INFORMATION RETRIEVAL SYSTEMS WITHIN THE PATENT OFFICE 

To permit the effective introduction information of retrieval methods of matters now feasible or on the point of becoming feasible, greater administrative allowance should be made for the disruption of the standing routine which necessarily accompanies any such progam. 

Whenever a data retrieval system is to be introduced into an operating section of the Patent Office, it will be necessary to expend a considerable effort in converting data into the form appropriate to the system. Although this has been understood and budgetary support has been given to such effort, it does not appear that the administration has tolerated disruption of the routine to the extent necessary to accomplish such a conversion in a reasonable time. 

Consider the present effort to put the transistor circuit file on "peekaboo" cards. Although the administration is prepared to pay for this transfer of data, at the present rate of progress the work will not be completed in the foreseeable future since new patents are accumulating as fast as the old ones are transferred. 

Only overtime work on the part of the examiners in the affected section, with the full-time support of three people from R. & D., is authorized for this project. 

In spite of the obvious inadequacy of this arrangement, no regular-time work on the transferral within the transistor section has been authorized because it would slow down the rate of processing transistor patents. We believe that this is an administrative error.

REPORT ON CURRENT AND PROJECTED PATENT OFFICE PROJECTS DIRECTED TO MA- CHINE STORAGE AND RETRIEVAL OF INFORMATION 

The review of the existing efforts required interviews with staff personnel associated with each project, plus extensive reading of a wealth of information supplied us by the chief of the unit. Mr. Don Andrews and his associates were most helpful in arranging interviews, providing needed facts and responding to questions. In addition, we studied that section of the Report of March 1, 1960, of the National Academy of Sciences to the Secretary of Commerce, which commented on this program and the supplementary study report made by Dr. Gilbert 

King of IBM and his task force of specialists. We find ourselves in complete accord with the general recommendations set forth in both of these documents. 

Further, they have set out the justification and need for a virgorous Patent Office program so effectively that additional comments from us would be unnecessary repetition. Hence our report is much shorter than it otherwise would have been. 

It should be used in conjunction with the details of the prior reports, particularly of the King report.

DISCUSSION OF PROJECTS 

Major new emphasis should be directed toward exploratory and applied research on the theory of information storage and retrieval rather than toward increased efforts on the immediate mechanization, based upon present technology of fragmented sections of the patent art. 

The current projects should be continued at approximately present levels pending the introduction of certain organizational changes to be discussed later. 

It should be recognized, however, that the main value of the present projects will be in learning about machine methods of handling information in patents and in introducing larger segments of the examing force to mechanize storage and retrieval. Savings in examiners' time based upon these projects will probably not be great although there may be some small improvements in accuracies of search.

The observation that these projects merit continuation does not mean that their selection, progress, or supervision are entirely satisfactory. We believe that the choice of projects to be undertaken has been too casual with little consideration of their impact upon the Patent Office's total problem. We will offer recommendations to correct this situation hereafter; for the present we find that there are important examples of enthusiasm, interest, and ability among the personnel concerned; thus continuation can be expected to produce useful information. However, said personnel need better guidance and close association with research specialists in the field.

The Assistant Commissioner for Research and Planning, which post we are recommending hereafter, should be free to make his own choice of leader for the present development effort in the Patent Office and for new activities that we are recommending. New leadership, when chosen, should provide closer guidance and supervision. One measure which should be begun as soon as feasible is the development of specialized training courses for both senior and junior personnel on storage and retrieval and related matters. Those who do not wish to

acquire the necessary knowledge to go further in this field through additional training should be permitted to return to the examining divisions. 

Our status quo recommendation on current projects is based primarily on our conclusion that probably none can be expected to provide more than an interim solution to the search problem in an extremely limited segment of the total active patent art. Consequently plans to introduce mechanized techniques for "encapsulated" areas into the work of the examining divisions of the Patent Office should be based on sound estimates of expected savings or increased accuracy. Operational analysts, trained in the techniques of making such estimates, are not now in the Patent Office. Furthermore, with presently available techniques the major cost of mechanizing selected segments of the patent art is in the preparation of the mechanical file, that is, reducing pertinent information in existing patents to a form that can be manipulated by machines. 

Hence significant investments in file preparation, which in general will be only interim files, should be based upon a reasonable expectation of a demonstrable net gain. The staff presently engaged in file preparation appears of adequate size to support the experimental nature of current projects. Augmentation of the staff would be warranted to prepare working files in areas where expert operational studies predict advantages for a particular area of mechanization. Our proposal provides for such additional effort in file preparation. 

Part of the difficulty in predicting gains of particular approaches to limited mechanization is the absence of criteria for appraising the efficiency of present hand searching procedures. We agree strongly with the recommendation of the 

King committee for a critical analysis of the accuracy and efficiency of the search methods now being used in the Patent Office. Such studies are essential to the judicious introduction of new mechanized search techniques; they would be critical in the selection and conduct of research and development projects in the mechanization program, and in addition they could be quite helpful in improving Patent Office operations in advance of substantial mechanization. 

The Assistant Commissioner should give early attention to the organization of an operational analysis unit to remedy this situation. In addition to an overall look at the search process, this group would make a critical evaluation of existing and proposed projects to insure that the future efforts make a maximum contribution to the needs of the Patent Office. Such a unit in fact be the 

Assistant Commissioner's principal means of dealing with the long-range planning part of his assignment. While it would be preferable for the major activity to be carried out by personnel recruited by the Patent Office, we recognize the difficulty of acquiring the necessary competence at the present time. Therefore, if efforts to recruit qualified personnel prove unsuccessful, an arrangement should be entered into with an operations research organization. In either case, the personnel carrying out the assignments should be physically located in the Patent Office to maintain an intimate awareness of the special needs and problems.

There is an area of effort which is crucial to the success or failure of the overall program which has had insufficient attention. This is research directed to mechanization of the preparation of the file. Experience to date shows that the cost of file preparation rises in direct relationship to the care and detail employed in analysis and coding of the patents. Obviously the entire program will be economically unfeasible if the cost of file preparation is not controlled. 

Therefore, we believe that a vigorous research program should be undertaken aimed at new techniques to permit mechanizing the preparation of the patent files. In addition, it would seem useful to develop cooperative relationships with private industry in order that special patent files set up by companies for their own needs can serve a wider purpose.

PROPOSED ORGANIZATION 

We believe that the present organizational structure and administrative relationships could be improved. In particular, we urge that the Commissioner create the position of Assistant Commissioner for Research and Planning to give general guidance and support to this and related efforts. This man should be experienced in the application of scientific methods to production problems. His authority should encompass all Patent Office activities directed to improvement of the examining process and related operations. While we are reluctant to "spell out" all the activities for which he would be directly responsible, the present

Office of Research and Development (for which a new name might prove more suitable), plus the proposed Operations Analysis Unit would be under his direct control. In addition, the Classification Group, the Machine Tabulation Branch, the Organization and Methods Division, and the Mechanical Examining Divi sion should be considered since their work is pertinent to that area of effort for which he will be responsible.

One important function which an Assistant Commissioner for Research and Planning would provide, and which has not been possible in the present organizational arrangement, is the assignment of selected members of the examining force to experimentation of the application of machine methods to arts in which they have specialized experience. The employment of qualified examiners in carrying out projects will accelerate the work. Further, the transfer from experiment to acceptance and use by the examining force will be smoothed. Frequently it may be necessary to slow up or even to suspend operations for extended periods in a particular division in order to effect the installation of mechanized procedures, using the skill of the examiners in the file preparation process. The recommended Assistant Commissioner would facilitate such decisions and actions. 

ALLOCATION OF EFFORT BETWEEN THE PATENT OFFICE AND THE NATIONAL BUREAU OF STANDARDS 

We believe that the Patent Office's search needs are sufficiently serious as to justify substantial expenditures covering a wide spectrum of activity. However, we feel that for the reasonably near future those projects which relate to exploratory or applied research can best be performed at or guided by the National Bureau of Standards. Equally, work directed to demonstrations of systems or pilot runs belong in the Patent Office where it can be carried out in close relationships with the examining force. This general philosophy may be clarified by the following explanation.

Exploratory research has the purpose of investigating intuitively chosen areas that have a plausible connection to the Patent Office information-handling problem. This category cannot be specific as to where in the program the benefits, if any, may accrue. Also it cannot be specific regarding a time scale for the completion of such exploration. Responsibility for continued initiative in this category is an appropriate one for NBS and has a natural relationship to similar efforts in behalf of other information-handling problems of the Government. 

The effort should be augmented through contracts with universities and foundations and occasionally with carefully selected private contractors. While the 

Patent Office will probably make only limited contributions to this category, it is important that they have a continuing and informed interest in the areas being explored. They can contribute by supplying constructive comments on the intuitive aspects of selecting areas for exploration. This in turn also contributes to continuity of communications between NBS and the Patent Office. 

Applied research has the purpose of estimating the adequacy of possible approaches identified through exploratory research. This category can be expected to identify where in the program the potential benefits will accrue and roughly in what form. However, there is still an indeterminate time scale for this type of effort. The time scale is dependent upon evolving a meaningful "measure" of adequacy of the approach from the user's point of view. (In systems engineering parlance this is often called the single-thread analysis, in that the content of every included element of the system must be provided for.) 

For some while much of the initiative for this effort will come from NBS but with close collaboration with the Patent Office. It is suggested that at the outset the collaboration take the form of assignment of mutually acceptable guest workers from the Patent Office to work under NBS technical supervision. The purpose is to develop a pool of skilled manpower from which the Patent Office can draw technical leadership for its own projects that will begin to grow out of this effort. It may also provide the foundation from which which aviable program in applied research can evolve within the Patent Office. In addition to the joint NBS-Patent Office effort, contract assistance should be sought from interested universities and from a number of qualified private contractors.

Prove-in runs and demonstrations have the purpose of providing a rational basis by which to estimate the effort and the time scale needed to prepare for productive operation. The demonstration part is essential for estimating the extent of "customer satisfaction." The experience gathered from the runs and demonstrations should be extensive enough to provide a rough measure of the trade-off between total time and effort required and the observed degree of "customer satisfaction." (In systems engineering parlance, this is often called the volume and priority analysis, in that the relationship of each of the included elements to the overall objective of the system has been considered.) Responsibility for continued initiative in this category is an appropriate one for the Patent Office. NBS is to collaborate to the extent necessary for a smooth transition of the applied research results to this purpose. 

Preproduction pilot runs have the purpose of generating detailed data for the preparation of budget and staffing plans needed prior to initiating productive operations. This category is characterized in that the major issues have already been resolved as the result of prior effort and the trade-off position has been selected. Now it remains to supply the myriad of details associated with successful initial operations, such as transition time and costs, staffing level, staff training requirements, and unit operating costs. (In systems engineering parlance this is often known as the detailed cost analysis.) The responsibility for this category of effort is clearly one for Patent Office personnel. 

Operational analysis is concerned with the application of logical analytical procedures to operating problems. Skills in the design of experiments and statistical analysis are basic requirements for such endeavors. An operational analysis unit should be concerned with total Patent Office operations as well as the various phases of research and development on information storage and retrieval problems. In the latter area the unit should be an important link in the sequence of transition between the activities listed above. Since the unit will be an important adviser to the Assistant Commissioner it should be responsible directly to him. COSTS

File preparation involves the translation of the information now searched manually by the examiners into a form where it can be searched by machines using the techniques involved in the exploratory and applied research effort and in the prove-in and pilot runs. Frequently mechanical files will have to be provided for the research and development (or prove-in) effort that will be satisfactory for final mechanized production searching. More often, however, substantial additional file preparation will be an essential part of the transition from a satisfactory preproduction pilot run to the routine use of mechanized searching techniques by an examining division. Such file preparation activity should not be confused with the research on file preparation techniques previously recommended. File preparation research would be conducted under the research activities discussed above. As was mentioned earlier file preparation with existing techniques is costly. Hence it should be deferred, except to the extent that files may be essential for research or proving in purposes, until there is reasonable assurance that a proposed mechanization technique can produce specific advantages.

In most of the present projects in O.R. & D. the types of activity from limited applied research to routine file preparation have been telescoped together. 

Preproduction file preparation should be done in the Patent Office except to the extent that the cooperation of industry can be secured in preparing files for joint use in special areas of interest. It is important that such industry 

1 cooperation should be encouraged since it is probable that sufficient advantages can ultimately be demonstrated to warrant the investment of private funds for this purpose. File preparation essential to research or prove-in activity should be controlled by the agency requiring the files. 

The fiscal support required for these four areas plus the equally important operational analysis and file preparation efforts cannot be closely calculated at this time. However, we feel that a substantial enlargement of the present appropriation is in order. We have endeavored to set out some general estimates

for each area but these should be the subject of review and readjustment by the Assistant Commissioner for Research and Planning. 

A total budget of approximately $3 million per year is recommended to start the proposed program. Although over $1 million of this is for research work at 

NBS and in outside research organizations to be supervised by NBS, the entire amount should be a part of the Patent Office budget.

APPENDIX 6 CURRENT STATUS OF PATENT OFFICE RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT PROGRAM STATEMENT PREPARED BY PATENT OFFICE IN SUPPORT OF BUDGET FOR 1964 FISCAL YEAR 

The Office of Research and Development conducts a research program concerned with developing a mechanized system for searching recorded knowledge and retrieving information for determining the patentable novelty of claimed inventions; conducts pilot examining operations to facilitate research and technical development; represents the Patent Office in arranging for the assistance and cooperation of public and private agencies and foreign patent offices in furtherance of program objectives; and plans and coordinates the joint efforts of contributing agencies and the Patent Office. 

The areas of research are: 

Acceleration and appraisal of present programs of developing machine assisted search. The current program of developing and putting to use various systems and techniques of encoding specialized subject matter files must be continued and expanded. At the end of fiscal 1963 the only systems that will be operational will be the steroids, the organo phosphorus and the organo metallics. The first of these include U.S. patents and the open literature, the latter two include only U.S. patents. We propose to reactivate the projects that have been stopped and to develop files for other selected arts. Further we plan to include the open literature and foreign patents in several of these files. The These diverse files serve two primary purposes. First, they provide direct assistance to examiners in these arts in conducting patentability searches. 

Second, and of greater significance, they provide a vehicle for testing and evaluating the benefits to be derived from specialized mechanized searching. experimental procedures involved in such evaluations are most demanding and additional competence in the necessary mathematical, statistical, and psychological disciplines must be obtained either through additions to the staff or through external resources.

Preliminary attempts at designing and conducting tests on the efficacy of mechanized searching systems have clearly indicated that the only way to obtain generalizable results is by conducting a number of experiments on different bodies of art. Accepted statistical procedures preclude the use of only limited areas of art and a few expert examiners if generally applicable results are to be obtained. Thus we must continue the effort directed toward encoding various groups of patents and other documents in accordance with known and demonstrably effective (from the standpoint of retrieval) technology. These efforts must continue at least until an adequate general evaluation of specialized encoding schemes has been made.

Development of more advanced techniques of document search.-A number of projects are directed toward the exploitation of more advanced techniques of abstracting, encoding, indexing, searching, and displaying the contents of files and technological documents. Also included in such projects is the development of theories and methods of file organization and adaptive systems. The longer term needs of the Office dictate that automatic techniques be developed for the initial input of data to a mechanized file and the internal reorganization of such data in accordance with inquiries and inputs of new data. Such needs necessitate the continuance and increased emphasis on more basic areas of research such as linguistics, self-adaptive systems, mathematical theories of retrieval, and the like.

Currently in this area, we have the HAYSTAQ project, which is a joint project with the Bureau of Standards. Other projects include investigations in file organization, linquistics, adaptive systems and heuristics. Considerable efforts in these areas are underway in various universities and other research organizations, and the Patent Office can benefit considerably by tapping such outside sources and, if necessary, contracting for partial or full support of specific projects which are particularly important to our information storage and retrieval problems.

It is proposed to significantly increase our efforts in this area by combining resources as economically as possible: within the Patent Office itself; with contractors monitored by Patent Office staff; at the Bureau of Standards; through

contractors working in fields related to the National Bureau of Standards program and therefore readily monitored by National Bureau of Standards staff; and by cooperative projects with other Government agencies. One project of the latter type planned for 1964 consists of a joint effort by the Office of Technical Services, the Patent Office, and the Bureau of Standards in the development of remote viewing stations.

Operations research program.--During fiscal 1963 efforts will be initiated to systematically investigate the examiners' process of searching the files and conducting the patentability inquiry. The efficacy of machine-assisted search techniques and the criteria for system design are dependent upon knowledge of the mental processes involved in the examiners' search techniques. In addition, various tests will be conducted on mechanized files to evaluate the effectiveness of machine-assisted search in increasing productivity and in improving quality. 

The problems in this area are enormous but they must be faced, as each of the advisory committees of scientists recommended to the Commissioner, and solutions obtained if we are to achieve any adequate measurement of the performance of machine-assisted search systems. 

During fiscal 1964 these efforts will be continued and considerably intensified. 

These projects, like so many of the others, are necessarily long range in nature. 

It may take 5 years before the ideal level of testing and evaluation is achieved, because of the many technical and staffing problems that exist. 

The proper conduct of this type of research requires highly skilled experimental phychologists, statisticians, and human factor experts, as well as patent experts of various kinds.

Participation in international technical projects.-Some assistance in the measurement of the completeness and effectiveness of search for scientific materials will become possible as the newly formed international organization of patent officials develops. Plans are forthcoming for the search of the same patents at various offices under this sponsorship. Moreover, five foreign patent experts have been assigned by their respective countries to the United States for 2 or more years, with the assistance of a National Science Foundation grant, to work on the research program of the U.S. Patent Office, including preparations for the comparison of searches conducted in different countries. 

It is anticipated that one more such expert will be assigned during fiscal 1964.

APPENDIX 8 REMARKS OF ALAN T. WATERMAN, DIRECTOR, NATIONAL SCIENCE FOUNDATION, DURING CELEBRATION OF THE AMERICAN PATENT SYSTEM, OCTOBER 19, 1961 Dr. ALAN T. WATERMAN. Mr. Commissioner, Dr. Bush, distinguished guests, ladies, and gentlemen, it is a privilege to be with you this evening on one of the several occasions that have been arranged in celebration of the 125th anniversary of the Patent Act of 1836. Having only just observed the 10th anniversary of the agency with which I am associated I am awed by such venerability. I hope that when the National Science Foundation attains such ripe old age, the occasion will be marked by as distinguished a company as is gathere here tonight.

When I asked myself what point of view I, as a relative outsider, should present this evening, I reflected that probably there is greater knowledge of the patent system concentrated under this roof than has ever been assembled before in one place. There would be nothing I could add therefore to what you already know about this important aspect of our economic system. Furthermore the 

National Science Foundation is primarily interested in basic research which, in its own domain, is antithetical to the purposes for which the patent system was established. It is the common belief of scientists generally that new knowledge and discoveries of the working of nature's laws should be freely available to all with no restrictions of any kind. 

Several years ago, in describing the patent policies of the National Science Foundation to one of the Senate subcommittees, I observed: "The nature of basic research is such that the possibility of patentable inventions resulting during the conduct of such research is very limited. It has been stated from time to time in judicial decisions relating to patent matters that a conception of the mind, not represented in some physical form, is not an invention within the meaning of the patent laws and that patents are not granted on discoveries of abstract laws or principles that can be awarded only with respect to the embodiment of the concrete idea, whether such embodiment is a device, a product, or a process. Since basic research is concerned primarily with the study of natural phenomena and the ascertainment of natural laws and principles, the prospects of patentable material being contained in such research are not very

substantial, particularly patents having commercial value. I hope you won't hold it against us that we are not too much involved in patents. We do appreciate their importance nevertheless. 

Despite its emphasis on basic research, however, the Foundation has subsidiary responsibilities which I believe are relevant to a discussion of the patent system. One of these is the collection and analysis of data relating to the nature and growth of the national research and development effort; and another is a continuing concern with problems relating to the more effective storage and dissemination of scientific information. It seemed to me, therefore, that we might properly ask whether the patent system, in its present form, is keeping pace with the progress of research and development, and whether we might profitably apply to the operations of the Patent Office itself some of the new tools that technology makes available.

It is fashionable nowadays to relate everything to the technological revolution; hence, I have chosen as my topic, "The Patent System and the Technological Revolution."

We Americans are fond of subjecting to endless analysis and discussion even our most highly respected institutions. Obviously, the world in which we live today is much further removed from the world of 1790 than the mere lapse of time would indicate. For example, we are told that more scientists and engineers are living today than in all other ages combined. It is all the more remarkable then that in the still largely agrarian economy the Founding Fathers should have had the foresight to provide for the establishment of a patent system that would stimulate and encourage growth of an industrial society. We salute, also, the orderly instincts that prompted the revision of the patent system as early as 1836, and the passage of the act that charted the course of its operations for the next century and a quarter. In the last two decades, however, there have been many increments of growth in the economy as a whole; and research and development, to which the patent system is so closely linked, have multiplied at a rate much faster than at any previous time.

The National Science Foundation, in a program of comprehensive studies made on a periodic basis, attempts to measure various aspects of the Nation's scientific effort. Beginning with the year 1953, these studies have yielded trend data on the total number of scientists and engineers engaged in research and development and on expenditures for the performance of research and development by industry, the Federal Government, colleges and universities and other nonprofit institutions. Data on science and engineering in the United States for the years prior to 1953 are fragmentary, but can, nevertheless, be used for the purpose of drawing broad comparisons between present and past levels of research and development activity in our economy. 

The growth and importance of the research and development activities of scientists and engineers during the last 30 years is demonstrated by the nearly eightfold increase in the numbers of scientists and engineers engaged in such work-from 50,000 in 1930, to 395,000 in 1960. The most spectacular measures of the growth of research and development during this period, however, are those relating to expenditures. The best available estimates indicate that less than $200 million was spent for research and development in the entire country in 1930, compared with 1960 outlays totaling approximately $14 billion. 

In contrast to the tremendous increase in the volume of R. & D. activities characterizing the post-World War II period, the numbers of patent applications and of patents granted for inventions have not varied appreciably during the period. The number of patent applications has increased from the post-World 

War II low of 65,000 in 1951, to a high of 84,000 in 1960. It is interesting to note, however, that the number of patent applications reached a peak during the 1920's. During the 10-year period ended in 1930, patent applications averaged 89,000 per year, compared with annual averages of 70,000 during 1931—40, 69,000 during 1941-50, and 79,000 during 1951-61. The number of patents issued for inventions was highest in 1932. In this connection, one might wonder whether or not there is any relationship between depressed economic conditions and inventive ingenuity. During the 10-year period ended in 1940, patents issued for inventions averaged 44,000 per year, compared with 31,000 per year during 1941-50, and 43,000 per year during 1951-60.

One must approach with caution, however, the seemingly anomalous situation in which tremendous increases in scientific research and development are accompanied by relatively small increases in the number of patents granted for inventions. During recent decades, patented inventions have increased enormously in scope and complexity. There are more comprehensive applications from large firms and fewer from individuals.

It is a pity, incidentally, that funds have not been provided for a patent museum in which the models for patents that were once required to accompany applications could be publicly displayed, and an even greater pity that many of these models were completely destroyed in a disastrous fire. Otherwise, one might be able to see, with his own eyes, how American inventive genius has advanced from the simple to the complex. 

In the "attic" of the Patent Office, a few of these models survive. There is a model of a "corpse cooler," for example, consisting of a wicker table of somewhat macabre shape; there is a walking cane embellished with a coiled serpent, the 

American Flag and Holy Bible and more elaborate models of steam engines and early reapers. When one compares these objects with the electronic data system for processing bank checks at a speed of 750 per minute, for which the 3 millionth patent was issued in September, one gains some notion of the progress of technology. There has been a shift, furthermore, from the individual inventor working on his own, to the corporate inventor working as a member of a team of specialists with all the resources of a modern industrial laboratory at their command. I am told that today about 70 percent of all patents are issued to corporations.

One must also take into account the role of the Federal Government. More than half of the estimated $14 billion expenditure for research and development in the United States is financed by the Federal Government. Much of the federally financed research and development work is allocated in the fields of aircraft and missiles, nuclear energy, and other defense-related fields, and security classification becomes a factor in the number of patentable inventions. 

The preponderance of Federal funds in the national research and development effort also raises the point, which is periodically debated, of whether or not the Government should take title to patents that are issued under its research and development contracts. I do not wish to take sides in a controversy in which a vast amount of information and talent have been marshalled to support each point of view. I do wish, however, to emphasize the need for a wise policy with respect to the administration of such patents as may arise from Governmentsponsored research and development. Such a policy should take into account the fact the patent system was designed to promote the use of new inventions, and unless inventions are used, the public interest is not served. Obviously, the performance of Government-sponsored research on problems relating to defense, space, and atomic energy, produces, incidentally, new products and proceses of great commercial value. A policy governing their use should preserve industrial incentives to participate in Government enterprises and insure that scientific information is suitably disseminated. It might be well to consider the establishment, perhaps as a unit in an existing agency, of a central Federal inventions organization which would have the responsibility of devising suitable licensing procedures, and seeing that Government patents are put into use. 

To return to the relationship of research and development to patentable inventions, I should like to discuss one or two additional factors that help to explain the apparent lack of correspondence between the growth of research and develop- I ment and the issuance of patents. In recent years, about two-thirds of the 

Nation's research and development expenditures have been allocated to development. Development includes technical activities of a nonroutine nature concerned with translating research finding or other scientific knowledge into products or processes. Development frequently involves the utilization of large numbers of scientific, technical, and other supporting personnel and extensive materials in the building and testing of models or prototypes. A considerable portion of Federal R. & D. expenditures is for development in such fields as missiles, atomic energy devices, space exploration vehicles and equipment, and electronic guidance and control systems. It is likely, therefore, that relatively fewer inventions per dollar expended result form the development phase of R. & D. 

E projects than form applied research. 

As I mentioned previously, that portion of the Nation's R. & D. expenditures allocated to basic research performance results in comparatively few patentable inventions. In 1960, funds for basic research performance totaled slightly less than $1.5 billion including capital facilities), or approximately one-tenth of the 

Nation's total research and development effort measured in terms of dollars. 

Although the rate of patent applications and patents issued cannot be regarded as a true index of the growth of research and development, nevertheless, I believe it is pertinent to explore the question whether or not the patent system in its present form is going to be equal to the anticipated growth of science and technology.

The National Science Foundation has recently completed a 10-year projection that predicts a steady rise in the number of scientists and engineers and in dollar expenditures for research over the next decade. The number of doctorates in science and engineering, for example, is expected to be approximately double the present number by 1970. [The U.S. Patent Office, meanwhile, is operating under much the same systems and procedures that it did 50 years ago. The patent applications have grown in size and complexity, and the material to be searched has doubled and redoubled, so that the time required for each search grows commensurately longer. In an age that is characterized on every side by a high degree of mechanization and automation, the individual examiner is, for the most part, obliged to conduct his searches by what is essentially a primitive manual method. It is reasonable to expect that the advances of science and technology can be applied to this part of the process so that the bulk of the examiner's time can be devoted to the intellectual aspects of his tasks. A mechanized search procedure, properly conceived and executed, would materially reduce the time required for each search, would eliminate the factor of human fatigue and could be expected to "discover remote and previously unobserved connections." thus contributing to the issuance of better patents.] 

Such a proposal is, of course, by no means new. The problem has been examined and reexamined a number of times, and the Patent Office has a research program directed toward the design, construction, and experimental use of mechanized search systems, although it has had neither the funds nor the numbers of personnel for an effort commensurate with the magnitude of the problem. 

In 1954, I had the pleasure of serving on an advisory committee, of which Dr. Bush was chairman, which examined the whole question of the application of machines to Patent Office operations. The problem has two major aspects: the physical capacity of existing automatic data processing equipment for storing the vast amount of information involved, and the intellectual task of reducing the information to a form that can be handled by machines. 

On the basis of certain arbitrary assumptions regarding the size of each patent, the Bush committee decided that for the 1954 file of 5 million foreign and American patents, a mechanical storage capacity would be required for at least 300 billion bits. A "bit," as you know, is a technical term in information theory used to designate a unit of information. Allowance would have to be made for annual accretions, again excluding drawings, at the annual rate of 3,200,000 bits. Storage capacity required for coding indexed entries and other selections of criteria was estimated at around 50 billion bits for the patent files in 1954. Accretions since 1954 have raised the total number of U.S. and foreign patents to 8.5 million and the related scientific and technical literature has increased by a much higher factor. 

Since commercial available reels of magnetic tape can store up to 100 million bits of information, the 300 billion bits estimated by the committee could be stored on 3,000 of these reels. There are digital computer installations that have libraries of several thousand reels of tape; hence, this amount of storage is now available. New developments give promise of achieving the same amount of storage at less cost in smaller size and with more rapid access. 

Having determined that the storage capacity which would be required of an automatic indexing and retrieval system for the Patent Office was realizable, the Bush committee went on to note: "The creation of a classification and indexing system so that machinery can be effectively used for searching is an intellectual problem of formidable proportions because it involves a diversity of complex subject matter fields." The committee recommended an intensive program of research and development within the Patent Office working toward the adaptation of machines to its specific and unique problems. 

In the 6 years since the Bush report was submitted, the Patent Office has established a mechanized system for the searching of steroid patents and similar work is underway in the field of polymers, chemical processes, organic phosphates, and electronic arts. It has logically begun with the arts that are simplest to adapt to machine processing, on the theory that as the work progressed, rapid advances in the development of automatic data processing equipment would provide increasingly sophisticated equipment for the more complex subject matter. 

We congratulate the Patent Office on the excellent work that it is doing in this area, but I believe it is fair to say that a greatly expanded research and development activity is needed if the work of mechanizing the search procedure is not to be outstripped by the rapid accretions of the material to be searched. 

This expanded research program should, of course, be closely related to other current work being done in the broad field of information retrieval.

The National Science Foundation has a special interest in the Patent Office problem because it represents in microcosm the whole scientific information problem for which the National Science Foundation has direct and continuing concern. The volume of scientific information is increasing at an exponential rate which makes it impossible for the individual scientist to keep abreast of the published material in his own field and in related fields in which he may be interested. There are great problems of storage, retrieval, dissemination, and translation from one language to another.

An increasing amount of research is being devoted to these problems, which is regularly reported by the Foundation in a semiannual report entitled "Current Research and Development in Scientific Documentation." This research is primarily concerned with the handling of information in language and other nonquantitative forms such as photographs and circuit diagrams as distinguished from quantitative forms or numerical data. The latter can be quite readily coded, stored, and manipulated by widely understood electronic data processing techniques. The mechanized handling of ideas, concepts, and techniques embodied in texts and drawings present much more difficult problems and these are yet to be resolved.

To illustrate the scope as well as the interest of some of the work that is being done in documentation research, I cite a few examples. The Ramo-Wooldridge 

Division of Thompson-Ramo-Wooldridge, Inc., and the International Business 

Machines Corp. are attempting to devise automatic indexing and abstracting techniques. Research groups at the University of Pennsylvania and the Radio 

Corp. of America are concentrating on the mechanization of certain techniques of linguistic analysis of English, with the thought that such analysis will prove to be an essential step in automatic indexing, abstracting, and retrieval. The 

National Bureau of Standards is engaged in research on the mechanized processing of both language-texts and pictorial and other forms of graphic information. 

Ideally, an information processing system should be able to handle both types of information, for they are usually encountered together in scientific papers and in patents.

Western Reserve University has developed partially mechanized information retrieval procedures which are being tested in the field of metallurgy. A group at the Itek Corp. is doing research on a special documentation language based on logical principles and a systematized technique for indexing the concepts and relationships in scientific papers. The British Association of Special Libraries and Information Bureaux is in the third year of a controlled comparative study of the searching effectiveness of several indexing and classification systems. The 

Chemical Abstract Service, using existing equipment and techniques, is experimenting with ways of coding data on the structure and properties of chemical compounds for mechanized storage and retrieval and for automatic preparation of data compilation.

In the field of mechanical translation, about a dozen research groups in the United States are preparing automatic dictionary machine programs for a number of scientific fields, devising machine programs for the grammatical analysis of certain foreign languages, working on techniques of semantic analysis and on the synthesis of English sentences-and, in general, are gaining deeper insights into the nature of language.

Although the foregoing gives only a hint of the present scope and objectives of research on information retrieval, a great deal more remains to be done before we know how best to use machines to aid the handling of information and patent searching.

Nevertheless, the expected consequences of such research are of the greatest importance for science and industry. Improvements in the means for consulting the records of accumulated scientific knowledge will certainly benefit researching of the patent records. 

In the meanwhile, the backlog of unsearched patents remains constant, the elapsed interval of time for the granting of a patent has increased and the overall number of items in the patent file has almost doubled. A little over a year ago, IBM estimated that industry was spending about $2 million a year on systems for information retrieval and that by 1965 the expenditure will jump to over $100 million a year and will double every 3 years thereafter. 

The Patent Office has been among the pioneers in research on the use of mechanized search techniques, but like others active in this field, has not yet been able to devise ways of using to the fullest the capabilities of the remarkable electronic devices produced by our advanced technology. If the Patent Office were to be given the funds and the staff to tackle this problem on the broad scale warranted by its importance, it would not only enhance the efficiency of the

patent system but it would undoubtedly contribute in a major way to the solution of scientific information problems which are of pressing importance to industrial research, and in fact to the scientific community as a whole. 

Before closing, I should like to say a few words about even broader needs for research and data relating to the patent system. Although we are here to pay tribute to the patent system and to contributions it has made to progress in the last century and a half, we cannot close our eyes to the fact that the system is frequently subject to attack and and the suggestion has been advanced from time to time than it might be eliminated entirely. 

In June of this year, the Division of Engineering and Industrial Research of the National Academy of Sciences-National Research Council sponsored a sym posium on the effect of patents on research. Members of the symposium were drawn from big business, small business, the universities, economics, and patent law. During 2 days of intensive deliberations, these people who are well informed on the subject of patents, contributed their experience and point of view to discusion of the problem. The report of this committee has not yet been published and I do not wish to anticipate its findings. I think I may say, however, that the group agreed that although the merits of the present system have been debaed pro and con, careful study is required before changes other than administrative or procedural should be considered. The group further agreed that our Nation's technical progress could be seriously affected by ill-considered action that might disrupt present incentives and methods of information dissemination.

This conclusion emphasizes, in my opinion, the need for much more research and study on the patent system as a whole and its effects upon the economy. 

To date, relatively little has been done in this field. The rapid advance of computed technology has made possible, among other things, the construction of models for the study of complex, socioeconomic situations. Through its 

Social Sciences Division, the National Science Foundation is supporting several studies on the economic aspects of inventions and on the economic implications of research and development. In addition, we have a specific contract for the study of factors affecting the diffusion of innovations in industry. May I say that the Foundation would welcome additional sound proposals in these fields where clearly much study remains to be done. 

It is my personal opinion that by and large the patent system has served us well and it has been a considerable factor in the growth of the inventiveness and ingenuity for which as a Nation we are famous. We are, however, in the midst of a technological revolution in which the character of invention seems to be changing rapidly. Whether the patent system as we now know it can continue to serve adequately the needs of an expanding research and de velopment effort and enable us to meet foreign competition successfully is a question that cannot be answered because we lack sufficient data. 

In any event, it is timely to apply to an assessment of the patent system the orderly processes of research, and by all means to place on the most modern basis possible the techniques of patent processing so that we may enjoy the maximum benefits of a system that has long been a bulwark of free enterprise.

DAVID L. LADD, COMMISSIONER OF PATENTS, BEFORE THE ROTARY CLUB, SEATTLE, WASH., AUGUST 15, 1962 

It is a great pleasure for me to come to the great Northwest. It is the first time that I have been here. My enjoyment has not been confined to an enjoyment of the magnificent scenery hereabouts. There have been broader satisfactions. For one sees here in the Pacific Northwest the kind of growth, pace, and energy that we must have if the country as a whole is to achieve our national goals of economic growth.

These goals, of course, do not represent aspirations for merely a yet higher standard of living for this country. They represent, in addition, the need for

sustained economic strength so that the United States may continue to serve as the mainstay of the West. For those reasons, today in the Patent Office and our parent Department of Commerce, the ever-present question is: Will it promote economic growth?

Today I want to talk, of course, about the patent system; about the patent system in relation to the productivity and effectiveness of the American scienific community; and about its relationship to the national strength. 

Let me try to lay out these relationships, as I see them, as explicitly as possible. 

All planning for economic growth involves reliance on technological innovation as a major factor. And here I distinguish between invention and innovation. 

For, our objectives are both to produce new inventions and to assure that they are introduced into commercial application. The patent system serves both objectives. It would be superfluous before this audience to give an elementary exposition of how the patent system operates to encourage research, how the I research produces inventions, and how the necessary investment is attracted to translate scientific achievements into actual production. 

Now the United States must compete as it has never competed before. We 

I must first compete with our friends-including our revived allies of Europe and the heretofore underdeveloped countries everywhere for whose interest the United States is concerned, both for our sake and for theirs. Moreover, there is the relentless and-as far as we can see now-endless competition between the world's two major contending economic systems. This competition, we devoutly hope, will not ultimately be resolved by military conflict. Indeed, it is a dominant objective of American policy to achieve a reasonable and secure disarmament which will make such a resolution impossible. Instead, the outcome will probably depend largely on the competition in technology. Even if the resolution were to depend on conflict, the advantage would go to those with a superior technology. This is the fundamental contest in which we cannot afford to languish. The Russians have more people than the United States; they are increasingly turning out more scientists than we; they will apparently make more sacrifices than we will in the distribution of resources between consumer goods and services and military and capital goods. It requires no elaborate argument to conclude that our welfare, and indeed our safety, depend upon the most efficient and economic marshaling of our resources. 1 

The Russians understand this only too well, as you yourselves know. And as a part of this, they understand quite well that the efficiency and effectiveness of their scientists depend upon how well those scientists use the published record of earlier work. This use of previous and continuing work to support present scientific efforts and to avoid duplication I am going to refer to here as documentation. That is a simple word which covers a complex field and a complex problem. And while they have been somewhat slow, the Russians now understand the importance of patents, first as economic incentives, second, as a source of revenue and third as a source of information by which to support their research.

For some time the Russians have been well aware of the need for systematically organized files of technical documents. Far more than we have, they have allocated experienced scientific personnel to the indexing of scientific literature in all of the various technical specialties. While it should not be concluded that great success has been reached by this effort, it would be dangerous to presume that they are not achieving important advantages.

Let me explain to you why you hear this from the Commissioner of Patents. 

I could have omitted everything I have said thus far and begun at this point, introducing my remarks by saying:

The survival of a full-examination patent system in the United States depends upon the successful development of a comprehensive information retrieval system. For those of you who are not experienced in patent matters, this is what we mean by an examination system: All American applications are examined for novelty. This means, at least theoretically, that every patent application is compared against all relevant patents and technical literature, in any language, anywhere in the world, which were recorded at any time. To make this comparison, we must search all of the "prior art." "Prior art" is a technical term used by patent lawyers. It means simply everything that has been done or published before on this subject. This prior art must be searched; and the search is one of the principal jobs of the Patent Office and the patent examiners. 

This job has always been a big one and today it is overwhelming. And it is growing.

In our files we have over 3 million American patents, over 7 million foreign patents, and several hundred thousand pieces of technical literature. And we have organized these materials by classes and subclasses according to technology into what many people consider the most sophisticated system of technical classification available. A very few statistics will give you some idea of the magnitude of these files and the problem of maintaining them. There are over 300 classes and over 55,000 subclasses in the classification system. Every year in the 

Patent Office we need over 3,000 square feet of floorspace to accommodate the additional paper which must be integrated into these files; and we maintain a staff of 75 experienced patent examiners whose sole responsibility is to carry on a continuing reorganization of these files as required by the proliferation of the materials and new technological developments. (Incidentally, even with this, we are far behind in our reclassification effort. Everybody knows it. 

Everybody laments it. Nobody knows what to do about it.) We get 1,500 patent applications a week, all of which must be classified, and some of them go into many classes. We issue 1,000 patents a week. All of these, too, must be classified. The chemical literature which goes into our files has doubled in the last 8 years.

Now the validity of any given patent and ultimately the soundness of the examination system depends upon how thoroughly these files are searched for each application. The question is now squarely before us: Can we continue to search this literature to determine novelty in patent applications with such thoroughness as to justify the legal presumption of validity which arises because of this examination and issuance of the patent? We think the answer is "Yes," but we know that the answer would have to be "No" if we assume that we will be continuing to search in the same manner as we always have.

That is why the Patent Office is deeply concerned with documentation. That is why the Patent Office has established a research and development program whose objective is to assist the examiner in his search of the technical literature by using computer technology, high-speed filing and display devices, and related equipment and methods for assuring rapid and complete search. 

This, then, identifies the particular and parochial interest of the Patent Office. 

We are aware that the results of our research and development program can, and should, bring benefits to the scientific and business community even beyond those which will come to the Patent Office alone. The same files which we use to determine novelty on patent applications can also be used by scientists as an affirmative tool for research, to avoid duplication, or, as some people pic turesquely put it, to reinventing the wheel. 

To a very limited extent, our classified files have already served this purpose. 

Patent lawyers routinely conduct what are called preliminary searches when their clients inquire about the possibility of patent protection. More relevantly here, clients, companies, or inventors sometime obtain state-of-the-art reports. 

That simply means a general survey of a defined area of technology. My main argument here is that this state-of-the-art investigation should become a general-indeed a standard-step before the definition and adoption of any research program, whether or not patents will be involved. 

In the very near future, this will be a lot easier for you. For a program has been developed to provide libraries of documents, previously available only in Washington, at 10 document centers throughout the country. 

The National Science Foundation is now committed to this program, which will begin with complete collections of the documents distributed by the Office of Technical Services. The OTS is the Government agency to which businessmen and scientists make requests for technical documents arising in all of the Federal agencies. In addition to the usual agency reports, there are two important kinds of documents in this collection. First, there is a substantial volume of scientific literature translated from other languages. Second, all research documents of Federal contractors (with the exception of those bearing security classifications) including unpublished specialized staff papers, are fully indexed and are contained in the OTS collection. 

In each case the technical document center will be associated with a large scientific library with adequate physical facilities and an outstanding library staff. The combination of this extensive and up-to-date collection and the necessary supporting facilities and staff should provide a type of regional technical document center previously unknown in this country. 

We are now considering the possibility of adding the patent literature to this collection. The properly classified and indexed patent documents, in addition to the original technical libraries with their treatises and scientific journals, already augmented by the OTS collection, would provide an unparal leled resource of scientific documents at each of the 10 regional centers. 

The usefulness of this program depends upon the quality and the completeness of the indexing systems that will lead an investigator to the appropriate documents. The development of principles and methods of document indexing represents the first area of research in the Patent Office's own program and it constitutes a necessary step in the provision of the Nation's systematic documentation program. 

I could have used all the time of this address in recounting the problems inherent in this research effort and in relating some of the projects already completed or currently active in the Patent Office and elsewhere. Suffice it to say that it is extremely difficult to classify a technical document in such a way that it will come to the attention of future investigators no matter what their specialty or immediate interest if the article has any relevance in applied subject matter, research methodology, conceptualization of the problem, exposition of the previous work, or references to other literature. Much of the present research effort of the Office of Research and Development of the Patent Office is directed at this problem. 

After the appropriate documents have been identified, it becomes necessary to place them into the hands of the researcher. At this stage he has learned what and where the documents are. Now he wants copies of them. All of the technology of microfilm, ultramicro reductions, photo-reproduction of all sorts, and related techniques are available to produce the documents, page by page, once the investigator has succeeded in identifying them. 

Returning to the 10 document centers, the present indexing of the OTS collection will be available. In addition, it is hoped that the most advanced techniques available will be used to index the patent literature. 1 11

With reference to the actual display of the documents, the OTS collection might be stored on microfilm with provision for selective reproduction of paper (conventional) photocopies. We are investigating the ability of ultramicro reductions to carry the burden of displaying and reproducing the patent documents, American and foreign, and related scientific literature. 

It is our hope that eventually-even though this is many years off-the investigator will be able to make his technical inquiries from any of the regional document centers, with the search conducted centrally using advanced computer techniques. The results of the search would be in the form of signals back to a single document center which would automatically allow the investigator to display for his own use each of the documents specified by the mechanized indexing system. He would read these documents, page by page, making paper copies to meet his needs as he goes along. 

By this time you understand I regard our work to make such resources available important, critically important. Therein lies the danger. For when the need is so important and hopes are so strong, hopes often become confounded with expectations. I suppose that one can say that this research and development program in the Patent Office has about the same likelihood of success as putting an astronaut on the moon. The commitment and the work certainly require the same kind of faith. Yet we will quite likely have a man on the moon before we have the kind of system in the Patent Office that we have been talking about. This does not say that there have not already been signal successes. There have. This does not say that the work is not moving along well. 

It is. But I do say that our expectations must be commensurate with the difficulty of the problem. If we are to have a truly comprehensive and general file readily and remotely accessible within 20 years, we shall be very fortunate indeed. I expect that we shall be fortunate.

Obviously, by this speech I intend to do more than merely expose the problem. 

Quite frankly, I am urging you to action. Those of you with responsibilities for companies and organizations having any substantial research program have the right to expect me to be as specific as possible in what I think your action should be.

First, when you leave here today, if you have not done so already, get a full briefing from your own research people in your own company of what their documentation problem is. Specifically, find out how thoroughly, and when, in their research procedures a search of the relevant literature is made both in patents and in technical publications. If such a search-and a thorough searchis not made, why isn't it? You can justifiably ask whether your research people follow a general policy of search before research. Before your company lays out substantial sums of money for any research project, you owe it to yourself and your stockholders, from a dollars-and-cents point of view, to be sure you are not building a road which someone else has already paved. 

If you do not get satisfactory answers from your research people, you should not automatically condemn them. For the deficiencies you find may be due more to the difficulty of the problem than to any professional deficiency. In that case you will at least see at first hand in your own organization a manifestation of this general problem. You can be sure that your competitors will find it in their companies as well. The failure to routinely search for relevant scientific materials might reflect the lack of a well-organized facility for making such a search. It is, in fact, necessary in each case to gather up the necessary documents without benefit of adequate indexing devices generally available on a national or international scale.

Second, if you find there is a need in your own organization—and again I would ask nothing more than a dollars-and-cents long-term profit evaluation on your part-then support within your own organization and your own staff a professional approach to the documentation problem. This, I hasten to add, does not mean merely maintaining an adequate technical library in your own company or your own community, or even-as important as this is-the staffing I of the library with competent personnel experienced in technical materials. 

It means, rather, the selection and support of staff primarily concerned with the broader problems of indexing, coding, recovering, and reproducing the technical W literature which your own research people will need. 

In addition, your professional staffs should be supported when they wish to attend meetings of professional societies for the purpose of promoting the orderly collection and indexing of documents and, indeed, becoming active in those pro- Ifessional societies concerned with the improved and mechanized methods of search and display of technical documents. This will not require action unprecedented in American business. Already several major American companies

have formed such staffs and they have made valuable contributions to the solution of the general problem. Specifically, Du Pont, Shell Oil Co., and Monsanto, among others, have organized ambitious programs in this field. 

Third, quite likely, your company can participate in more broadly supported efforts. In fact, if your business is small, this may be the only way in which you can move. Aside from individual companies, various trade associations and technical societies have, in some cases, combined their efforts. Specifically, the 

American Society for Metals has actively supported the work at Western Reserve University in Cleveland. The American Chemical Society regularly issues a series of chemical abstracts which many scientists find indispensable to their work. It may be that your industry, likewise, can develop a program which, while directed to your particular needs and interests, can at the same time valuably contribute to the broader objectives. 

Fourth, rather than working through a trade association or a technical society, your business may prefer to support directly the work now in progress to say nothing of that which should be begun-in various universities and professional societies throughout the country. You here on the west coast are in an especially fortunate position. Much of the computer technology is concentrated on the west coast and much of the work on the theory and practice of using computers for the indexing, abstracting, and classification of scientific documents is also concentrated on the west coast. To give you one example, the work of the 

Stanford Research Institute in this field is universally respected. 

Finally, you, your research people, and your documentation staff-which I hope you have by this time resolved to establish―should watch continuously and critically, and I hope sympathetically, the work of various Government agencies in Washington, including the Patent Office. As you would expect, this problem does not belong to the Patent Office alone. It just happens that ours is more severe than others. The Library of Congress, the Smithsonian Institution, the 

National Science Foundation, the Office of Technical Services, as well as those agencies with more specialized problems, such as the Central Intelligence Agency, and all of the military organizations are involved. 

Just as this problem is not confined to the Patent Office within our own Government, so also it is not confined to the United States. All examining patent offices of the world are concerned with the problem of documentation and information retrieval. The United States has by far the largest research program mounted in this field. Last fall, in connection with the celebration of the 125th anniversary of the Patent Act of 1836, the U.S. Patent Office sponsored the first international workshop on information retrieval as it relates to patent examining. Through the generosity of a grant from the National Science Foundation, we were able to pay transportation and expenses of numerous representatives from foreign patent offices so that they might attend. The The success of the conference far exceeded our fondest expectations. From it, upon the motion of Dr. deHaan, the president of the Dutch Patent Office, a new international committee was formed. This committee has a title which is not very euphonious but is most descriptive, the Ad Hoc Committee for International 

Cooperation in Information Retrieval Among Examining Patent Offices. 

steering committee comprises representatives from the United States, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and the Federal Republic of Germany. Next month in Munich the organization will hold its first formal meeting. Representatives from many countries will be in attendance. More importantly, six of the offices which will be represented have agreed to send to the United States, as guest workers for 2 years in our research and development program, six highly qualified professionals from their own staffs. England, Germany, Japan, Sweden, Holland and the International Institute at The Hague are all cooperating. 

Again, the National Science Foundation will help by contributing funds in support of this important work.

Here, again, let me emphasize that this ambitious and expensive, although still inadequate, work by your Government has not been undertaken because anyone feels that it is a good thing to spend a lot of money, that it is nice to have libraries, or that it is a good thing to put even heavier demands on the short supply of available scientists and engineers. It is not a work of choice, but of compelling necessity; nor is it an enterprise of academic interest, but rather of utmost urgency for the national welfare and security. 

But I do not put this plea to you on such a general basis. Rather, I put it to you on your own self-interest as businessmen, investors, scientists, and lawyers.

You and your business, of course, are welcome to the benefits of the research by Government agencies which you are paying for through your taxes. As a matter of fact, we will go out of our way to see to it that they are made available to you. In the first place your staff should regularly receive the publications of Government agencies working in this field. The Patent Office is an example. However, let me be less agency-proud for a moment and tell you that perhaps you should first receive a master list of publications in the informationprocessing field which is issued by the National Science Foundation. And, once more to be specific, Senator Humphrey has told me that before the end of this year the Senate Committee on Reorganization and International Organizations, of which he is chairman, will issue a committee print which will describe the Patent Office problem, its history, its research effort and results, and its prospects. Although the print will deal with the Patent Office alone, the problems are general enough to be of great interest to you. 

The benefits of this work within the Government will soon come to you in a dramatic way. I hope that you will watch with interest the creation and functioning of the new regional document centers which I have already discussed. 

You will not have to look far, because such centers will be established in Denver, San Francisco, Los Angeles, and Seattle. More than watch, I hope that you will use them. I hope that you will find the classified patent files a new and important resource for your technical and scientific personnel. 

Let me summarize. The country has necessarily set ambitious goals of economic growth for American business and for the country as a whole. The patent system will continue to serve American business by providing its traditional incentives for research, investment, and growth. Beyond that, the 

Patent Office and the patent system now aims at an equally dynamic function of supplying a flow of information to American business and its research efforts.
