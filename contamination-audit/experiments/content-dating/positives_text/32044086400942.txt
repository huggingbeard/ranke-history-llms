THE REVEREND THOMAS BRAY By Edgar Legare Pennington 

THE CHURCH OF ENGLAND AMONG THE EARLY SETTLERS IN AMERICA-THE CRYING NEED 

The Sixteenth Century explorers carried chaplains on their vessels. "Maister Wolfall" was appointed by the 

Queen's Council as minister and preacher of Martin Frobisher's expedition, in 1578. (). When Sir Francis Drake landed in his "faire and good Bay," on the western coast of 

America, in 38 degrees North latitude, he "went to prayer, and to reading of the Scriptures," at which the Indians were "attentive and seemed greatly to be affected by it."(2) To "discover and to plant Christian inhabitants in places convenient," was an avowed object of Sir Humphrey Gilbert's expedition. In his charter (granted by Queen Elizabeth in 

1578) the first charter for the founding of an English colony -there was recognition of the Church of England. (3) 

Raleigh's expedition included efforts to convert the Indians; (*) and in 1588, Sir Walter gave £100 for the propagation of Christianity in Virginia. Other instances could be cited, to show that religion was an important consideration during the period of the first English explorers.

With the planting of the Virginia colony, there were religious services. Soon churches were built; and in a short time, the Church of England secured such a hold that it was legally established in the first permanent British colony. 

Elsewhere, as in Maryland and New York, the Anglican system developed strength as the years went by.

More and more it was realised in England that the Church had lagged behind in the great foreign possessions of the Crown. Colonisation had been carried on at first by adventurers and through private grants from the King, and no organised work had been undertaken by the Church of England. The field was too vast to be made permanently successful through the isolated efforts of the heads of the Church. 

The crying need was the co-operative efforts of religious societies. (10)

DOCTOR BRAY'S LIBRARIES. 

Thomas Bray was mentioned as the second commissary appointed by the Bishop of London. A brief sketch of his life is now in order.

He was born at Marton in Shropshire in 1656, of respectable parents. He soon gave promise of fine intellectual development. We know that he was sent to the grammar school at Oswestry, and next to Hart-Hall, Oxford. There he studied. divinity, although he was compelled to leave the University soon after he began his Arts course, because of straitened circumstances.

Having entered holy orders soon afterwards, he was first employed as a curate in a parish near Bridgenorth in Shropshire. Shortly, he removed to Warwickshire, where he served as chaplain in Sir Thomas Price's family, of Park-Hill. Being called on to preach the Assize Sermon at Warwick, he made such a favourable impression on Lord Digby, one of his hearers, that the latter recommended him to his brother, the fifth Lord Digby, who gave him the vicarage of Over-Whitacre in the same county-a very advantageous position. In 

1690, his Lordship presented him to the vacant rectory of 

Sheldon, in Warwickshire. He took his Master of Arts in 

Hart-Hall, Oxford, the 12th of December, 1693.(¹)

A man of indefatigable zeal and great industry, he became active in various societies for the reformation of manners, for the revival of Church discipline among the clergy, and for the 

(1) Sprague: Annals of the Amer. Episcopal Pulpit, 17-18.

reform of prison conditions. We shall have more to say regarding his efforts. 

While at Sheldon, he composed his Catechetical Lectures, which had a wide circulation and were highly approved. They were designed for the religious instruction of the poorer class of children. It was probably due in great measure to the impression which they made on Bishop Compton, that the author was selected as a proper person to model the infant Church of England in the province of Maryland and establish it on a solid foundation.

There had been a petition from that province for the assistance of a "superintendent, commissary, or suffragan;" so, in April, 1696, Thomas Bray was appointed commissary. 

The income was represented as £400 per annum; none the less, sacrifices were involved. (2)

Bray expressed his willingness to accept the office, though he was unable to go promptly. He felt that some difficulty stood in the way which had discouraged good ministers from attempting the colonial field; this difficulty he wished to understand. When he sought to find clergymen for the colony, he discovered that he could secure none but poor men who were not in a position to buy books. Shut off from educational opportunities, such clergymen must needs deteriorate. So 

Bray, always alert to improve his opportunities, laid before the bishops the results of his investigation. He reported that only the poorer clergy could be persuaded to leave their friends and their native country and go so far; that without a competent provision of reading matter, the ministers could not prove useful to the design of their mission; that the poorer clergy were not able to purchase books for themselves; and, therefore, a library would be the best encouragement to studious and sober men to enter the service. If, he concluded, the bishops would encourage and assist him in providing (2) Ibid., 18.

libraries for the missionaries, he would accept the office of commissary.

Bray was soon hard at work establishing his library system. When the seat of government of Maryland was moved, it was decided to name the new capital after Princess Anne of 

Denmark; and Sir Thomas Lawrence, secretary of the province, waited on the heiress to ask her acceptance of the name "Annapolis." Bray accompanied Sir Thomas; and the

Princess not only graciously accepted the honour but made a generous donation to the commissary for his libraries. In gratitude, Bray was careful that the first library established in that region, which was fixed by him at Annapolis, should be dedicated to her and called the Annapolitan Library. (3) 

This collection of books, numbering 1095 volumes, was the first lending library in the British colonies and the largest collection of books at the time in the American plantations. (*) 

In 1876, 188 folios and 210 quartos of the original collection were still to be found; and the remnants are now at St. John's 

College, Annapolis, where they are stamped: Sub auspiciis

Wilhelmus III, E bibliotheca Annopolitana.(5) 

In further pursuance of his plan, Bray issued printed 

Proposals for the Incouragement and Promoting of Religion and Learning in the Foreign Plantations. Therein he set forth a scheme for a parochial library in every parish in America. It was his idea that each parochial library should be strictly for reference; that it should be kept in the parsonage, for the use of the minister and his successors; that the ministers should be held accountable for books embezzled or lost through their fault; that they should account for the books annually to the wardens and triennially to the commissary. 

To secure current treatises, requests should be made to learned authors to donate copies of their books and merchants should be asked to contribute money. The two archbishops

In 1697, Doctor Bray published An Essay Towards Promoting all Necessary and Useful Knowledge, both Divine and Human. In all the Parts of His Majesty's Dominions, Both at Home and Abroad. This work contained proposals to the gentry and the clergy for purchasing lending libraries in all the deaneries of England, where the author had also found many clergymen too poor to own books; there were likewise proposals to provide parochial libraries for Maryland, Virginia, and other foreign plantations. It was stated that onethird of the parochial clergy were unable to purchase a fourth of the books absolutely necessary for every pastor to peruse, notwithstanding the fact that great knowledge was expected of them. Bray went into details, concerning the cost of libraries, the method of purchasing the same, the preservation of lending libraries, the marking and care of books. The author anticipated some of the perplexities of the modern librarian. Notes, for instance, were to be made whenever books were lent; lists of books were to be kept, and checked by the archdeacon or three of the clergy once a year. THE SOCIETY FOR PROMOTING CHRISTIAN 

KNOWLEDGE. 

The difficulties which Doctor Bray experienced in obtaining assistance to carry out his library project caused him to originate the plan of a society, to be incorporated by charter, for the spread of Christian knowledge at home and in the plantations. His design was laid before the Lord Bishop of 

London in 1697; but it was impossible to put it into effect at that time. Out of this beginning, however, the Society for 

Promoting Christian Knowledge-known to this day as the S. P. C. K.- -arose. 

Doctor Bray prepared the first sketch of the Society. 

This included the libraries at home and abroad, charityschools, and missions both to colonists and heathens. Not only 

British settlers, but Indians and negroes were comprehended in his scheme. Bray was himself one of the first five members; he was the only clergyman among them at their first meeting. 

His faith in his vision is evident from the fact that all this while he was entirely without any provision to support him. (¹) 

It was designed that the S. P. C. K. should consist of the clergy of the chiefest note and of such lay gentlemen as were eminent for their worth and their affection towards religion. 

It should be their care to provide and support such missionaries as the Bishop of London should think necessary to send to the plantations abroad, where no establishment or provision existed for the clergy's support. They should fix parochial libraries throughout the plantations; they should allot gratuities or pensions as rewards to those ministers, who by their (1) Dictionary of National Biography, II., 1147.

learning, labour, and success in their ministry and mission might merit more than the ordinary encouragement; they should also allot what pension they thought fit to those who hazarded their persons in attempting the conversion of negroes or native Indians; indeed, they should make provision for the widows and children of missionaries. To accomplish such ends, the Society should receive gifts, grants, and legacies. (2) 

The S. P. C. K. undertook to incorporate in one association all the underlying principles of the religious society. movements which had preceded. "It had for its aim the counteracting of schisms, the opposing of profaneness and immorality by coercive force, the spread of religious education there and at home, among the poorer classes especially, and the effecting of the above by means of missionaries, schoolmasters, the disseminating of literature, and the establishing of libraries." (3) 

The first meeting of the S. P. C. K. was held March 8th, 1699. The members present were Lord Guildford, Sir 

Humphrey Mackworth, Mr. Justice Hook, Colonel Maynard

Colchester, and Doctor Bray. Three of these men were members of the English bar. Colchester had already started a charity-school on his own account in Gloucestershire. 

The foreign branch of the S. P. C. K. declared at the outset that their work was "the fixing of Parochial Libraries throughout the Plantations (especially in the Continent of 

North America)." The avowed objects of the Society were defined as (1) the education of the poor, (2) the care of the colonies, and (3) circulating books of sound Christian doctrine.

At the first meeting, the members took steps to ascertain what had been done towards bringing the Quakers, "that mis- (2) 200 Years, S. P. C. K., 22-23.

The Archbishop of Canterbury should be asked to see that a clause be inserted in the bill for employing the poor, in order to provide that the children be taught to read and be instructed in the Church catechism. Doctor Bray was desired to lay before the Society his scheme for promoting religion in the plantations and his accounts of benefactions and disbursements towards the same. (4)

Other aims grew out of the original design of the members. Cases of profaneness were prosecuted; the infractions of the stage were observed and brought to public notice. The 

Society not only took up the task of circulating books and founding libraries at home and in the colonies, but later began translating books into foreign languages so as to increase their dissemination. In order to improve the moral and spiritual condition of the mariners of England, the Society started distributing books and tracts to them. The problem of reforms at Newgate and other prisons was taken up in due course; and through the efforts of the Society, the conscience of the nation was awakened out of its indifference to such distressing matters. The Society was destined to prove the friend of the reformed communities on the continent; it helped the Salzburger emigrants to find homes in Georgia and collected nearly five thousand pounds for them. Its work in the East Indies began as early as 1710. But all the while, its most important field was educational. It was "the first society in Great 

Britain which came forward to advocate and support the general education of the people. (5) Through its appeals, the

The reaction which had set in as a contrast to the low moral ebb of the Seventeenth Century and to the religious discord which had existed served to favour the new organisation. From the outset, the S. P. C. K. proved helpful in affording that organised support of which the colonists stood in dire need. Though far from ostentatious at the beginning, it grew into a great society; and its influence has broadened ever since. Its care for emigrants on the high seas, the help it gives to medical missions, its efforts to train up a native clergy, are all worthy of liberal support. (6)

On the 20th of June, 1632, King Charles the First gave the charter of Maryland to Caecilius Calvert, Lord Baltimore, a member of the Roman Catholic Church. The charter granted him the patronages and advowsons of all churches to be built, together with the license and faculty of erecting and founding churches, chapels, places of worship, and "causing the same to be dedicated according to the ecclesiastical laws of our kingdom of England, with all and singular such, and as ample rights, and temporal franchises whatsoever, as well by sea as by land within the region, islands, and limits aforesaid, to be had, exercised, used, and enjoyed, as any Bishop of Durham, within the Bishoprick or County Palatine of Durham, in our kingdom of England, ever heretofore hath had, held, used, or enjoyed, or of right could, or ought to have, hold, use, or enjoy." This clause was evidently inserted to prevent the ultimate supremacy of the Roman Church, as well as in effect to establish the Anglican as the favoured religious body in colony. (6) It implied that every church must be consecrated according to the laws of England, and, therefore, according to the Church of England, since the very existence of the Roman Catholic faith was at that time contrary to law. DR. BRAY IN MARYLAND 

On the 16th of December, 1699, Doctor Bray set sail for Maryland, the territory to which he had been assigned as representative of the Lord Bishop of London. The promised stipend was not forthcoming; and he was compelled to sell his effects and borrow money for his voyage. 

His usual industry was unflagging in all his maneuvers. 

With characteristic zeal, he founded libraries in three ports where his vessel touched in England-Gravesend, Deal, and 

Plymouth. He knew that boats were often detained in those places, that the clergymen were delayed en route without reading matter, and that sea-coast towns were loose places for young men; so he declared that he would like to have a library in each seaport, so that travellers, sailors, and so forth could "study in during their stay on shore, and this not only that they might lose no time towards their better improvement, but to prevent also the expenses and scandalls, to which they would be too much exposed, by saunting away whole hours together in Coffee Houses, or may be less sober places. THE SOCIETY FOR THE PROPAGATION OF THE GOSPEL IN FOREIGN PARTS 

When Doctor Bray returned from Maryland, he found that the work of the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge was largely increased. The organization which he had conceived to render possible his great vision of an educated and enlightened clergy and people had branched out into several fields; and it was deemed necessary to make one of its departments the field of another society, in which all efforts would be concentrated on the missionary enterprise of the English Church. There was a good deal of interest in Doctor 

Bray's reports concerning the opportunities for Church expansion in America. Archbishop Tenison was deeply impressed, and so was Bishop Compton. Unless the missionary work were projected on a vast scale, such was the consensus of opinion, it must prove futile; the colonies would be lost to the Church. To undertake such a program, however, funds must be collected and the public sentiment of Great Britain aroused. 

The new society which sprang into being on this account was the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts-the S. P. G. It owes its inception to Doctor Bray. On the 15th of March, 1701, the Commissary petitioned the King for letters patent for the Society's charter. The charter was drawn up by Bray. In his petition, Bray outlined his reasons for desiring the incorporation of the Society. The number of inhabitants in America had increased; the colonists were in much want of instruction in the Christian religion; in some places, they were utterly destitute of the same; they were

unable by themselves to maintain an orthodox resident clergy. 

It was also stated that many would contribute to providing for the spiritual needs of the American settlers, if there were a body corporate, of perpetual succession, for the lodging of legacies and grants. 

On the 5th of May, 1701, at a meeting of the S. P. C. K., the draught of a charter was read. It was discussed at subsequent meetings, at which it met the full approval of the parent Society, the members of which recognized the necessity of a new organisation.

On the 16th of June, the same year, the charter of the 

S. P. G. was granted by King William the Third. By its terms, it incorporated the Archbishop of Canterbury and ninety-three others-more than half of whom represented the leading dignitaries of the Church of England. 

The new Society lost no time in facing its work. Immediately the Society proceeded to enquire into the religious affairs of the colonies. Letters were written to governors, congregations, and people of distinction in the plantations. (¹) 

The answers were very gloomy; it was learned that the colcnies were exposed to atheism and to the inroads of the Roman Church, and that in some of the colonies the Church of England was having a hard struggle holding its own members. It was evident that during the decades of more or less spontaneous patronage on the part of the establishment, without the steady oversight of a strong missionary organisation, Anglicanism in America had been allowed to weaken. 

So, at the meeting of February 15th, 1702, the Society agreed that all bishops, who were members of the S. P. G., should cause notice to be given out, offering clergymen an opportunity to apply for missionary posts in America. They were required to meet certain qualifications, however, as the Society was desirous of securing only men fit for the task. Funds

were solicited; and some generous donations were received. 

This was the beginning; the Society was destined to prove the greatest missionary force in the American colonies. 

For over two hundred years, the S. P. G. has been engaged in its work of evangelisation; it has a record unexcelled in history. It has been active in every province where the 

British flag has gone, and it has lent its assistance to nearly every country in Europe where its help has been sought. Its chief enterprises have been supplying and supporting missionaries under Anglican orders, establishing Church organisations, distributing Christian literature, supporting schools and schoolmasters, distributing libraries to the missionaries, and assisting and founding colleges so as to ensure a trained clergy. Its work has been directed to meet the needs of colonial settlers and heathen natives; at the outset there was considerable emphasis on the evangelisation of the Indians and the negroes.

For more than eighty years, from 1702 to 1782, the majority of Church of England missionaries in the American colonies were chosen, sent over, and in great measure supported by the S. P. G. 309 men were employed during that period in the Society's service in America. The colonies of New 

England received 84; New York, 58; Pennsylvania and Delaware, 47; New Jersey, 44; South Carolina, 54; North Carolina, 33; and Georgia, 13.(2) To these missionaries a stipend of forty or fifty pounds was usually given; they were granted £ 10 worth of books and £ 5 in tracts at the start; other books were sent over, in response to the frequent requests. 

The contributions of the S. P. G. to establishing schools and maintaining teachers in the colonies were tremendous.

THE REVEREND THOMAS BRAY By Edgar Legare Pennington VII THE DOCTOR'S LATER ACTIVITIES 

In 1706, Doctor Bray accepted the living of St. Botolph 

Without, Aldgate, where he remained till his death. The seclusion of parish life was not permitted to interfere with his interest in the colonies, however; he continued his activities for their welfare. He wrote several works on behalf of his favourite scheme-the libraries; and as late as 1726, he published a plan for enlarging parochial libraries-the 

Primordia Bibliothecaria. (¹) 

In order to seek the King's patronage, he visited Holland. 

While there, he met Monsieur Abel Tassin, secretary to the King, who was commonly known as the Sieur d'Allone. That official was impressed with Bray's endeavours, and during his life he gave £ 900 towards the instruction of the negroes. 

Bray undertook the expenditure of this sum. The needs of the negro had been in the clergyman's mind for years; in fact, he had outlined a plan for a society which would carry on work "amongst ye Poorer sort of people, as also amongst ye 

Blacks & Native Indians.''(²) When D'Allone made his will, in 1721, he bequeathed one-tenth of his English estate and the arrears of the pension due him from the Crown at the time of his death, as a fund, the income of which would be used by Doctor Bray and his Associates for erecting a school or schools for instructing the young children of negro slaves in the Christian religion "& such of their Parents as shown themselves inclineable.
