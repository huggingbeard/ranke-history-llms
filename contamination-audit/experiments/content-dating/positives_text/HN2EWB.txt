FOREWORD 

HIS book was written at a time when America was girding to defend her liberty. 

Thousands of young Americans were becoming soldiers-young men who were accustomed to the less rigorous life of quiet homes, tolerant schools and an economic world in which they were free to follow their own tastes. Army life is different. It means discipline, obedience and unquestioning loyalty. 

Did you ever stop to think how well a welltrained dog exemplifies the real soldier? Not in blind obedience alone, but in alertness and courage and intelligent adjustment to whatever situation may face him. And in the satisfaction that he gets from doing his duty. 

This is the story of a young American who saw nothing good in the old Army virtues, who thought that soldiers were dumb, and who got his only satisfaction in pampering himselfand very little in that. And it is the story of 

Sergeant Squiffy, the dog who showed this lad 3

4 Foreword what life can hold for those who learn to appreciate the virtues which military life-and dogs-have always taught. 

It is the story, too, of the adventures which these two have together, on and off the army post-adventures which lead to the unmasking of one of the deadliest of America's enemy, an ace saboteur. That is an important triumph. 

But more important, as far as Sergeant Squiffy is concerned, is the transformation of his master from a downright spoiled brat to the sort of self-disciplined young man who is a credit to any dog-or to any family, or any army. 

It has been my privilege, during these busy days of national defense, to visit many army camps, and to talk, as a journalist, with many of the men in our army, from privates to generals. Indeed, so much of my time has been devoted to this sort of activity that I have several times despaired of this book ever being finished and I wish to take this opportunity to acknowledge that it would not have been finished without the tremendous help of my good friend and fellow-journalist Ben James of Washington, D. C. But now that it is finished, I sincerely hope that it reflects, at least to some extent, the great spirit of our modern army, as 

I have seen it. And I hope that it will not only

Foreword be entertaining reading, but that it will help to give young Americans an understanding of the satisfactions to be found in the kind of life that 

Sergeant Squiffy liked so well. 

Essex, Connecticut. 

November, 1941.

SOMERSET SMITH, JR., descended from a taxicab, curtly acknowledged the doorman's polite "Good evening," stepped into the elevator, ignored the operator's "Glad to see you, Sir," rode to the sixth floor, and strode with sulky anger into his mother's apartment. "Why, Somerset !" said the dark haired, young looking woman who was sitting at the tea table. "What in the world are you doing here? And Somerset-darling! What is the matter with your eye?"

The matter with Somerset's eye was quite obvious. It was black. As a matter of fact, it had been blackened, just a few hours before, by one Peewee McAllister in one of the briefest

The fight had consisted of one two-handed push by Somerset, who, at sixteen, tipped the scales at a hundred and sixty, and one one-handed blow by Peewee, who, at fifteen, was still unable to balance the hundred-pound weight. It must be admitted, however, that Somerset's hundred and sixty pounds were very flabby, soft ones, whereas Peewee's slight frame was all litheness and muscularity. In any event, after that one blow to the eye, Somerset had picked himself up and retired from the field.

To understand this brief encounter, it is necessary to know that J. Somerset Smith, Jr., was the only son of the famous Senator J. Somerset

Unfortunately, the great Senator had not lived to inculcate into his son the virtues which had made him one of the most popular figures of his day; and his young widow, Somerset's mother, had lavished so much affection upon him, and had tried so hard to make him happy, that, to put it bluntly, Somerset was rather a spoiled brat. VERYTHING worked out just as Somerset had foreseen it for a while. His mother took the full care of Squiffy, mixing his food, grooming him, training him patiently, cleaning up after him when it was necessary, taking him on his airings. Somerset found time to look on occasionally, during these operations; and even deigned to give the pup a few offhand pats now and then, much to his mother's satisfaction. It was all she required of him.

When Colonel Upham was calling-and to Somerset's annoyance his calls seemed to be getting more and more frequent-Mrs. Smith always spoke enthusiastically of how fond the pup was of Somerset, and what a wonderful thing it was for Somerset to have him. But the

Colonel wasn't fooled. With that half-smile of his, he would keep asking Somerset questions, pretending to believe that it was really Somerset who was bringing up the pup. "What are you feeding him now, Jack?" Or: "How is his training coming along, Jack?" Or: "He seems 33
