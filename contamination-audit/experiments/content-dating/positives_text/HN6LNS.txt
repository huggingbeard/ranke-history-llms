FOREWORD 

Adventures in Thinking is a workbook to be used in conjunction with the regular textbook in biology. Some of the problems and exercises are intended primarily for laboratory work, others can advantageously be done at home. The supplementary activities are suggestions for the pupil, showing him how he may pursue the subject beyond the regular work of the class and make some investigations of his own. This material is starred to mark it off from that which is intended for general class use, and the number of supplementary activities covered will depend entirely upon local conditions. These supplementary activities include outdoor studies of living things, microscope work, topics for discussion and investigation, and special reports. At the end of the workbook, preceding the tests, will be found a note on the preparation of special topics together with several blank pages for the recording of special reports. 

This workbook contains the material for the laboratory work in a high-school course in biology. A number of simple experiments requiring but little in the way of equipment will be found. These may be done as individual problems or as class demonstrations, but in either case the results are to be recorded in the workbook.

A considerable proportion of the laboratory work in biology consists of observations of living or preserved plant and animal material, together with microscope slides. The procedure to be followed and the observations to be made is explained so fully that but little additional help will be necessary. Space for the recording of observations and results is given in the workbook. 

There is a sharp division of opinion among teachers of biology on the matter of drawings. Some hold that drawing has little or no biological value and that the making of a careful drawing entails a needless waste of time. These teachers would provide a drawing and merely require that it be correctly labeled. On the other hand, there are teachers who claim that the labeling of an already completed drawing is dull and uninteresting work which contributes but little to the pupil's grasp of the subject. They feel that the time spent in drawing is amply justified because it necessitates close observation of the material and later a careful representation of what has been seen. The author recognizes the merits and the flaws of both of these opinions and has endeavored to find a middle course which will save the time that pupils often waste in setting down a preliminary outline, without sacrificing the value of making a real biological drawing which involves care in observation and representation. To that end this workbook provides skeleton drawings of a number of objects. Details and significant structures must be added. Close observation and accurate drawing are not eliminated. The drawings are to be completed with pencil, but they can later be inked if desired. 

The workbook is a test of the mastery of the essential ideas and material of the biology course, and necessitates the pupil's utilizing the information already gained in doing the problems set for him in the workbook. Nothing is given that iii

the pupil can find out for himself. It will be noticed that there is a good deal of tabular material and a distinct emphasis on comparisons. Thus each new topic is linked with preceding lessons. 

A set of tests of various types has been prepared and will be found at the end of the book. These include the kinds of tests now in vogue, such as the truefalse tests, the multiple-choice tests, fill-in or completion tests, and matching tests. 

Since they can be torn out of the workbook, they can be used in any way that the teacher desires.

While the arrangement of topics in this workbook more nearly follows the arrangement of the author's textbook in biology, The Living World, it can be used with any of the textbooks for high schools. Therefore, at the end of each topic, page references to a majority of the recent high-school texts in biology will be found.

This workbook has been made with the hope and the expectation that with its use the biology student may experience that rare but delightful adventure, the adventure of thinking.

H. G. M.

Note on the Preparation of Slides 

If prepared microscope slides are not available, many objects can be satisfactorily mounted by the following methods. 

1. GLYCERIN JELLY 

Purchase glycerin jelly from a dealer in biological supplies. When ready to make the slides leave the bottle of glycerin jelly in warm water until it is a liquid. Do not heat beyond this point. Place a drop of the jelly on a slide, put the object in the jelly, add another drop of jelly, and cover with a cover glass before the jelly has hardened. Leave for several hours until it has fully hardened. 

These glycerin jelly mounts will last a long time. 

2. CANADA BALSAM 

The object to be mounted is first dehydrated by running it through alcohols of increasing strength and finally up to 95% alcohol where it can be left for some hours. Pass it through absolute alcohol and then clear it by passing it through xylol or cedar oil. Place a drop of balsam on a slide, put the object in the balsam, put on another drop of balsam, and cover with a cover glass. 

If the shell of an insect is hard and opaque, it can be soaked in a 10% solution of potassium hydroxide until it is nearly transparent.

Mendel's Experiments 

Mendel studied many characteristics in garden peas and some of his results are summarized below: 

1. Form of seed. When plants with round seeds were crossed with those having wrinkled seeds the resulting plants all had round seeds. In the next generation, however, there were 5474 round and 1850 wrinkled seeds. 

2. Color of seed coat. When plants with yellow seeds were crossed with those having green seeds all the resulting plants had yellow seeds. In the next generation there were 6022 yellow and 2001 green seeds. 

3. Form of pods. When plants with inflated pods were crossed with those having constricted ones all the resulting plants had inflated ones but in the next generation there were 882 with inflated pods and 299 with constricted ones. 

4. Color. When plants with red flowers were crossed with white ones all the resulting plants had red flowers but in the next generation there were 705 red and 224 white ones. [231]

5. Position of flowers. When plants with axillary flowers were crossed with those having terminal ones all the resulting plants had axillary ones, but in the next generation 651 plants had axillary flowers and 207 were terminal. 

6. When tall plants were crossed with dwarf ones all the resulting plants were tall but in the next generation there were 787 tall and 277 dwarf ones.

*Suggested Activities 

Establish and maintain terraria to represent different habitat groups. A desert, a swamp, and a forest are suitable ecological groups. Containers: The glass aquarium tanks with a cover of glass or of glass and wire netting are excellent. Terraria can be made by ingenious pupils or by pupils in woodworking and metal shops. Make a wooden frame of the desired size. Use cypress for durability. The sides have glass panels. The top of the desert is wire. The other tops are all glass or glass with a small inset of wire. Line the bottom of the terrarium with zinc so that the soil will not touch the wood. [ 252 ]

Make joints tight with aquarium cement. Paint all metal surfaces with black, waterproof paint. "Ponds" of any desired size and shape can be made of zinc in the metal shop and painted with waterproof paint. Dishes can be used for the "ponds." All terraria are lined to a depth of one or two inches with gravel, for drainage. From then on the treatment depends upon the habitat represented. 

1. Desert. Fill the terrarium with sand. Appropriate plants are cacti and succulent plants. Use small plants. Sink the plant pots into the sand so that the pot does not show. A small container for drinking water must be supplied for the animals. Lizards, "horned toad," and small snakes will live in such an environment provided it does not get too cold. A desert scene in color makes an effective background for this terrarium. 

2. Forest scene. Cover the sand with a layer of soil from the woods. Use small rocks around the edge of the "pond" to conceal the rim. Mosses of many kinds, small ferns, lichens, and other "woodsy" plants will thrive here. Tiny evergreen trees can be used in the background. Lichen-covered rocks and mossgrown wood can be used. The level can be varied by making hummocks at one end of the terrarium. Small snakes, little wood turtles, salamanders, toads, snails, and slugs are suitable animals. Bring in ground beetles and bark-loving insects. 

3. A swamp. The swamp differs from the woodland in that the soil should be acid and the whole scene is more moist. Sphagnum moss can be used as the layer next to the sand and the soil should come from a bog or swamp. The acid soil is needed if pitcher plants and sundews are to thrive. Mosses and similar plants can be used here. The animals are about the same as for the forest but frogs will live here. If the "pond" is large enough tadpoles, water insects, and possibly fish will live in it. For a background for these terraria green cloth, or blue, to represent the sky, can be used. 

The arrangement of the materials for the terraria will give scope for the ingenious pupil to use his originality. Much of the material can be obtained locally but some forms can be purchased from dealers in biological supplies. 

Detailed directions for establishing and maintaining terraria are given by some of the commercial dealers in biological supplies. Write to such firms for information.

THE PREPARATION OF SPECIAL TOPICS Collection of Material 

1. Consult books or pamphlets you already know that are likely to contain material bearing on your subject. 

2. Look up your topic in the card catalogue of the school library or the public library. 

3. In the Readers' Guide to Periodical Literature look for recent periodical literature on your topic. 

4. Sometimes valuable material can be secured by writing to government officials or commercial firms. Consult your teacher about this. Continue to hunt for information until you feel sure that you have exhausted all available sources. 

Seek help and advice from teacher or library attendant after you have found out all you can by yourself.

Preparation of Material 

1. Read material all through once to see whether it contains the information you are seeking. If it does, proceed to take notes as follows: 

List title of book, or name of periodical if it is a magazine article, name of author, page or chapter, and date. Why is this necessary?.

In case you find conflicting statements, how can you decide which is probably correct?

2. Reread slowly and decide what facts have a bearing on your topic. 

3. Make an outline of the important facts. Do not copy entire sentences, but jot down all important facts with dates and definite figures if they are of interest and of significance. 

4. Decide on a general plan of discussion and make an outline of your topic based on all the information you have been able to get. Include what dates and figures are necessary, but do not write out your topic in composition form. Have pictures or illustrative material in shape to be shown to the class. 

Presentation of Your Topic 

Remember that you enjoy a topic given orally much better than you do one read from a paper. Remember that a pupil's own words make a more interesting account than fine phrases from a book. Face your audience and try [263]

to hold their attention by speaking distinctly and loud enough to be heard easily. Don't slouch. Be familiar enough with your subject so that you can answer questions when you have finished with your topic. Use pictures or other illustrative material to make your subject more interesting.
