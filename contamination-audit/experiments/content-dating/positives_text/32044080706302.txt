THE FOLDING-TRIGGER PATERSON COLT FOREWORD 

SECTION 1. The Colt Revolver may well be acclaimed as the most important American firearms. In all probability, it has had a greater influence on the destiny of this our country than any other single weapon. 

An untried stripling, it spoke its rapidly repeated message in the everglades of Florida and upon the plains of Texas in a language which both Seminole and Mexican could not fail to understand. Grown to manhood it was the first United States regulation revolver. It crossed the Great American Desert and rounded the 

Horn in '49. It rode with both Sheridan and "Jeb" Stuart. For years as "Judge Colt" it was the arbiter of human disputes from the Mississippi to the Pacific. Impartially it handed down decisions for sheriff and bad man; for pale face and redskin; for cowboy, rustler, roving prospector and permanent settler. 

A seasoned soldier, it followed the flag to Cuba, to the Philippines, to China, and finally to the battlefields of Europe. What other weapon of ours has had so long continued and so glorious a career? What wonder that this great firearm has always possessed a special appeal for the American collector by virtue of its inherent mechanical, historical, and romantic interest! Especially fascinating, too, is the beginning of a thing. And so I have selected the first Colt Revolver as the subject of this monograph. 

SECTION 2. In order more clearly to designate my target (a pre-requisite of any properly conducted shooting contest), let me explain the proposed scope of this article and define a few terms. What has always interested me most in the study of the firearm is the tracing of the various evolutionary steps by which it developed from an inefficient noise maker to the world's most deadly weapon. These steps may roughly be divided into two classes: (1) The normal step which is a direct link between one type and another in any given progression, and, (2) The variant step which goes off at a tangent from the main line and has little or no permanent influence on the evolution of the art. To this latter class belong experimental and "freak" pieces which for one reason or another were never successful or popular. 

Each step in a given evolutionary progression is evidenced by certain distinctive characteristics or "points" in construction or design. These "points" may be classified into three main kinds: (1) Efficiency points, which tend to make the piece a better weapon; (2) Economy points, which reduce the cost of labor or construction without materially detracting from the lethal efficiency of the piece, and (3) Style points, which are changes in design or construction in no material way effecting either efficiency or economy. These latter are valuable chiefly in fixing the date of a given specimen. 

Note also that it is quite possible for a single characteristic to be at once an efficiency, economy and style point. In fact this is apt to be the rule rather than the exception. Obviously, therefore, it is by the recognition and proper evaluation of these points that we may hope to place a given specimen in its correct position in an evolutionary sequence. 

To apply the above generalities to our present subject, let us inquire: How, then, did the Colt 

Revolver come to be, and thru what stages did it pass from infancy to lusty youth? Omitting variant and experimental specimens—a subject in itself—let us seek to determine the distinctive points in a representative series of commercial folding trigger Paterson Colts and attempt to trace by step the normal evolutionary progress of this, the first successful revolver during its initial period of existence. 1

ARTICLE I PRELIMINARY SURVEY AND CLASSIFICATION 

SECTION 1. It is an unquestioned fact that Samuel Colt developed the world's first practical revolver firearm, namely a gun or pistol with a multi-chambered cylinder revolving behind a single fixed barrel. 

Yet, strange as it may seem, the revolver principle is very ancient.¹ Matchlock revolver muskets of indeterminate age may be seen in the Wallace collection, London, the Germanic Museum, Nurnberg, Germany, the Tower of London and elsewhere. One of the most ancient revolver long guns (of whose age I can be reasonably positive) is a three-chambered wheel lock Arquebuse at present in the Musee de L'Armee, Paris, which dates from the last quarter of the sixteenth century. Furthermore, in my own collection there is a wheel-lock, self-spanning, three-shot revolver pistol of about 1580, the mate to which (probably the other one of a pair) is in the Army Museum, Munich, Germany. These I believe are the earliest-known one-hand revolvers.

At periodic intervals during the 17th, 18th and early 19th centuries, revolver arms were produced with match lock, wheel lock, composite battery (a form of snaphaunce) miquelet, and flint lock discharge mechanisms. 

The last and most highly perfected of the said pre-percussion revolvers is the Collier, which is sometimes included among American weapons because the inventor, Elisha Collier, was a Boston man, altho he actually developed his product in England during the second and third decades of the nineteenth century. 

This excellent firearm might have won a high place for itself, as compared with any previously developed multi-shot gun had it not been so soon outmoded by the much more efficient percussion revolver of Samuel 

Colt and his imitators.

The great difficulty, however, with all these primitive revolvers was that neither the match nor any of the stone against steel discharge mechanisms were suited for use in connection with revolving chambers. 

The problem of priming the pan was a difficult one, and most important of all, the danger of communicating flashes leaping from pan to pan or vent to vent causing a premature discharge of all the chambers at once was never satisfactorily eliminated. In short, it was not until the invention of the nipple cap that the revolver could be made really safe or efficient.

SECTION 2. As I have said, it was the American, Samuel Colt, who first successfully combined this novel discharge mechanism with the revolving-cylinder multi-shot mechanism and produced a masterpiece. 

How young Colt, a lad scarcely out of his teens, developed his original idea and persuaded capital to back this apparently fantastic venture; how it failed in a few brief years; and how Colt requickened it with the breath of his own genius till it grew to international proportions, is one of the first and most romantic chapters in American business. But our present concern is with the product of the initial organization called the Patent Arms Manufacturing Co. of Paterson, New Jersey, which was formed to manufacture and sell under Colt's patents. This company of which, by the way, the young inventor was not actually a member but merely a profit-sharing employee, started business in 1836 but unfortunately was obliged to suspend operations in 1841 due to the conservative hesitancy of both government and public in adopting this revolutionary form of firearm. 

During this period, the factory produced both guns and pistols. The guns or long arms-revolving rifles, carbines and shotguns-never achieved great popularity because of the actual or supposed danger of premature discharge of the loaded chambers which might well seriously damage the aiming hand of the shooter which normally grasps the forestock in front of the chamber muzzles. This is a most interesting subject in itself which I hope to take up in a future article. 

The revolving pistol, however, or to use its popular name, the revolver, was not normally discharged with any part of the shooter's anatomy in front of the cylinder muzzles, and in consequence rapidly forged ahead of its big brother in popularity. It was, and still is, by far the most successful type of one-hand, multi-shot weapon ever produced.

Two main kinds of Colt Revolver were manufactured during the Paterson period: 

1. The folding-trigger revolver, frequently known as the "Texas pistol" by reason of its popularity among the Texas borderers during their struggle for independence against Mexico. SECTION 3. Before proceeding to a discussion of the various models of folding-trigger Colts, I am going to diverge for a moment from the established lines of classification in order to call your attention to two distinct varieties which may be recognized by their distinctive grips. The first variety has a sharp reverse curve on the backstrap near the butt and a corresponding curve opposite on the front strap. (Plate 

I, Fig. 6, W. & V.) The second variety, on the other hand, has no sharp curves either front or back. Since, as the Chinese say: "One look is worth a thousand words," a glance at Plate II, Fig. 9, and Plate III, Fig. 

16 will tell you just what I mean by reverse curves and the lack of them. 

While these grip curves appear at first glance to be merely "style points," there is a very good practical reason for both of the shapes which raise them to the dignity of efficiency points as well. The flared curves of the first variety grip enable the hand to get a better purchase on the weapon, particularly when the same is equipped with a long, heavy barrel, and make it less likely to slip from the hand on the occasion of a "fast draw." Moreover, these curves tend to act as a guide to the fingers, causing the pistol to be held instinctively in exactly the same way and at exactly the same angle every time. This is of vital importance to accurate "snap shooting." But with a pocket pistol of limited range and efficiency, these considerations are secondary to those of concealment and surprise. Hence the second variety grip was made to fit as inconspicuously as possible in the pocket or under the clothes. 

Furthermore, I am stressing this hitherto unemphasized distinction for another very definite reason, since I am beginning to believe that the Colt factory regarded them as distinct products and assigned them each a separate series of factory numbers. By recognizing this fact, we may gain a clue to the mystery of 

Colt serial numbers which has puzzled many a collector, myself included, and has caused much difference of opinion as to whether a given model or type with a higher factory number was or was not actually manufactured before another specimen with apparently later characteristics but with a lower number. But more of this anon (see p. 7). This hypothesis is strengthened by the fact that the mechanical design and construction of both these varieties progresses more or less concurrently and fall almost exactly into the same model classification.

Both varieties were made in a number of sizes. It is quite possible that the style or grip was optional with the purchaser. Nevertheless, in the larger calibers the reverse curve grip seems to have predominated, while the smaller caliber and pocket pistols were apparently more frequently made with the smaller grip. 

I have never seen a stock folding-trigger pistol of the holster or belt size with other than the reverse curve grip. Neither have I seen a stock specimen of the second variety larger than pocket size or over .34 caliber. 

Have you?

SECTION 4. Classification by models and types. And now a word with respect to the nomenclature of models. It has been customary to refer to the folding-trigger type without loading lever as the "Colt 

Model 1836." Now, it is unquestionably true that the first factory products were put out during that year. It is also obvious that these products underwent certain definite alterations both in design and construction during the next five or six years.

Furthermore, after reading this little article, I feel sure that you will agree with me that it is quite possible to trace the period sequence of these alterations and "points" because most of them are obvious improvements, many of which were retained in the later Hartford products. Thus, we may determine with reasonable certainty which of the two different specimens is the earlier product. But I, for one, am by no means sure of the exact date when these changes took place. Much original research will have to be done, both at the U. S. Patent Office and elsewhere, before these questions can be definitely settled. (See Art.

At this point, the question naturally arises, what changes in design and construction are sufficiently important or noticeable to warrant the creation of a new model designation? Under normal conditions, the factory itself would settle the question by a definite pronouncement, but alas, in our case, as far as I know, there remain no authentic records on the subject. In fact we are already making some progress in that direction. On general principles, however, I don't think that a given specimen should be dignified by the name of "model" unless it so differs from its predecessor or successor as to necessitate a new set of tools or dies to manufacture one or more of its major component parts. In the following pages, therefore, I will more or less adhere to the above rule in classifying models while speciments with minor variations will be subclassified into types. I assure you that this is far from the case. I will try to refrain from emphasizing a lot of unimportant differences and creating types to fit them. For example, changes in style or position of inscriptions, novel bits of decoration, etc., may well merit notice as dating points, but, in my opinion, do not rate a separate type. Furthermore, I do not consider mere caliber or length of barrel of vital evolutionary. importance unless definite mechanical changes were necessitated thereby. ARTICLE II ILLUSTRATION AND DESCRIPTION OF SPECIMENS SECTION 1. The inventor's model. Our first exhibit (Fig. I) is a copy of the original revolver draw ing submitted to the U. S. Patent Office by Samuel Colt. This is a good place to begin our study of normal evolutionary points since it shows that from the very first there were present certain basic principles which made Colt revolvers the first really successful repeating weapons, namely: 

1. A cylinder rotating mechanism connected with the hammer and operated by the simple act of cocking the piece. 

(See Pl., Figs. 1, 2 and 3.) The cylinder actuating arm (a, Fig. 1) operated by the toe of the hammer (z, Fig. 1) engages a ratchet (s, Fig. 1) on the head of a sleeve (e, Figs. 1 and 3) which rotates about the base of the cylinder pivot pin (h, Figs. 1 and 3). The forward portion of this sleeve emerging from the face of the frame head (r, Fig. 3) slips into the pivot hole of the cylinder (c, Fig. 2) and engages the same by means of a slot (cc, Figs. 1 and 3). The act of cocking raises the toe of the hammer which pushes up on the actuating arm which, by means of the ratchet rotates the sleeve which in turn rotates the cylinder to which it is attached. 

2. An automatic cylinder locking and unlocking device, also operated by the act of cocking, which holds the chamber firmly in line behind the barrel when the piece is ready to fire, and is released when the hammer begins to operate the cylinder-turning mechanism and re-engages when the piece is brought to full cock. This device consists of a cylinder-stop dog or catch (q-qq, Figs. 4 and 5) which engages cylinder-stop slots (p, Fig. 6) in the circumference of the cylinder. As the hammer is cocked, the toe (z, Fig. 1) trips down the catch bar and lowers the nose of the catch (qq. Fig. 5) disengaging same from a stop slot. The cylinder-turning mechanism then brings another chamber with its stop slot into position and the toe of the hammer trips past the catch bar, permitting the same to rise again by means of its spring (q, Fig. 4) and engage the new slot, locking the cylinder firmly in firing position. 

Principles 1 and 2 have been retained not only in all subsequent Colt models, but in practically all modern revolvers as well. Even the mechanical details whereby these principles were applied have remained substantially unaltered, a fact which clearly demonstrates the surprising soundness of Colt's practical engineering ideas. 

3. Partitions between cap nipples on the cylinder (Plate I, Fig. 2, d) to prevent the flash of discharge from being communicated to the other loaded chambers. 

The value of this third principle is also demonstrated by its lasting qualities. In fact, both the original position of the nipples and the construction of the partitions were continued with scarcely any modification at all in Colt revolvers until the "cap and ball" system was superseded by the metallic cartridge. 

At the time, much was made of this side flash danger from unprotected adjacent nipples; a danger which 

I personally think was greatly exaggerated, particularly with the perfected percussion cap. Possibly this deep-rooted fear arose from the fact that in the early days before the correct composition of detonating fulminates was fully understood, certain accidents of this nature may have occurred with too strong a detonator. Be this as it may, however, the practice of employing nipple partitions was almost universal in all percussion revolvers down to the end of the period. 

4. Cap nipples at the end of the cylinder (see Plate I, Fig. 2, 1). This is the obvious place for them, and competitors found this patent hard to get around. This is doubtless the reason why certain other makes of revolver had the nipples inserted in the circumference of the cylinder at angles ranging from 90 to 30 degrees. The original position of the nipples was never changed in the Colt commercial models.¹ 

5. The principle of uniting the barrel with the cylinder by means of a pivot pin (Plate I, Figs. 1 and 

3) one end of which is fixed in the head of the frame (b, Fig. 1) and the other is inserted in the barrel frame (n, Figs. 3 and 7) and locked there by means of a transverse wedge (k-j, Figs. 1, 3 and 7).

In addition to the above basic principles, Colt designed this first revolver of his with a folding trigger (u), and patented the trigger-release bar between it and the hammer. 

A glance at Plate I, Fig. 1 will show you that said trigger (u, Fig. 1) is held in its folded or concealed position by means of a spring (m, Fig. 1). When the hammer is drawn back to the full cock position its toe (z, Fig. 1) pushes the trigger-release bar which in turn snaps down the trigger into firing position (u, Fig. 6). This class of pistol which forms the basis of our present study, was manufactured in successive models and constitutes by far the greater part of the New Jersey factory output.2 

Now the folding-trigger idea was by no means original with Colt, the same having been applied for some years previously to single-shot pocket pistols. The basic object of this somewhat delicate and complicated device is to "stream line" the pistol in order to facilitate a quick and easy draw without a projecting trigger or guard to get tangled in sash, belt or pocket. Had the Colt factory confined its output to little self-defense revolvers, the folding trigger might well have been retained indefinitely. But a large caliber, long-barreled revolver is by its very nature intended for heavy duty, and destined for constant use in battle, or on the frontier, under field conditions and far from gunsmiths. Hence, its construction should above all things be rugged and fool proof. Furthermore, these pistols are essentially holster weapons, and it is a fact that with trigger guard and holster both well designed, the danger of a disastrous catch is negligible. 

For the above reason, therefore, it follows that the disadvantages of the folding trigger more than overbalance its advantages when applied to this class of revolver, and when this fact was fully realized (thanks, it is said, to the exhortations of Captain Walker), the offending mechanism was discontinued. It is quite possible that this happened with respect to the larger caliber pistols even before the failure of the New Jersey factory, and that during its last days the only revolvers manufactured were the improved Walker sixshooter with fixed trigger and guard and the small caliber pocket pistol, still with folding trigger, but otherwise brought up to date with the latest type attached loading lever, etc. (See Figs. 21 and 23, Plate 

III.) This hypothesis is borne out by the fact that I have never seen a folding-trigger, large caliber pistol with a barrel factory-designed for the fully improved attached loading lever, nor have I seen this combination in a folding-trigger pistol except in pocket size. Have you? Be this as it may, however, the folding trigger was permanently abandoned in all models both great and small, after the close of the Paterson plant in 1842.

No matter how great the inventor's genius, no human invention ever leaped into being full grown and perfect like Minerva from the head of Jove. The Colt revolver is no exception and the following features of the inventor's model-surprisingly few and unimportant—did not last long in practice. In fact, I am inclined to believe that they were never incorporated into any but experimental pieces. 

1. The almost rectangular drop of the grip. This is the principal distinguishing characteristic of the inventor's model. (See Sawyer, "The Revolver," Pl. 2, Fig. 2, Page 4, for an experimental model with this kind of drop.) The angle of grip to barrel as an efficiency point will be discussed later. See Section 3, Page 3.) 

2. The shield over the nipples, intended as security against external moisture, and the internal action of powder gasses upon the lock and rotating mechanism. (See Fig. 8, a.) 

3. The cover over the front of the cylinder, i. e., the muzzle protector. (See Fig. 8, b.) 

4. The wooden forestock. (See Fig. 8, c.)¹

The nipple shield was a complete failure. Apparently it confined whatever flash there was, thereby increasing the danger of communicating it to an adjoining nipple. It also acted as a conduit whereby the powder fouling was directed into the very portions of the mechanism which this device was designed to protect. Then again, it had to be removed in order to load the piece which made another operation necessary and another loose part to look out for in an already far too complicated system. In short, this is at good example of a theoretical efficiency point which proved "anything else but" in practice.

accidental discharge of the loaded chambers. This was the great bugaboo of early muzzle-loading revolvers. At this late date it is somewhat difficult for us to determine just how much of this fear was well founded and how much imaginary. The consensus of opinion of certain modern marksmen who still continue to practice with cap and ball pieces seems to be: First, that with good quality percussion caps well seated on all loaded chamber nipples, and with safety partitions as above described, it is practically impossible for a flash to leap from nipple to nipple; and, second, that if the chambers are loaded with any degree of care, communicating flashes from muzzle to muzzle very seldom occur. Nevertheless, in all muzzleloading revolvers of standard design,* there is an unavoidable lateral flash thru the space between the chamber muzzle and the rear of the barrel. Consequently if there is the slightest leak around the bullet and wad in chamber adjacent to the one being discharged, the powder below is quite apt to catch fire and explode. The fact remains, therefore, that premature discharges of this kind did actually occur now and then, and it cannot be denied that once a finger is shot off, it is gone forever, even tho the accident happen but once in a lifetime. Hence, the original distrust of these new-fangled weapons was not altogether groundless.

For this reason early revolver designers devised all sorts of would-be safety gadgets which in most cases were merely talking points to help overcome what we would now call sales resistance. Frequently they were not only superflous but a positive menace. For example, this first Colt muzzle protector actually aggravated rather than diminished the danger it was intended to prevent, since it carefully conveyed the flash about the circle of chamber muzzles, which would have to be very carefully loaded indeed not to pop off. Furthermore, under these conditions, if the protector was made heavy enough to stop the bullet, a tremendous kicking and spitting, both backwards and sideways, would naturally result as the imprisoned powder gasses sought to escape; while on the other hand if the protector was sufficiently light to be pierced or shattered by the runaway charges, it was not only useless but worse than useless since chipped lead and flying steel splinters would of necessity be added to the general debacle. Finally it was realized that in a one-hand revolver the said premature discharge of loaded cylinders, while annoying and somewhat disconcerting, was not really dangerous. In any case, the extra bullets would naturally be discharged in the direction of fire, and, more important still, there is normally no part of the shooter's body in the path of these projectiles since in a pistol the left hand does not, as a rule, grasp the forestock in front of the chamber muzzle, as in the case of a long arm. To digress, this is the principal reason why the twohand revolver carbine failed to achieve popularity. 

The chief reason for a wooden forestock on firearms is to keep the shooter's hand from getting burned by contact with the barrel, which, as you all know, gets decidedly hot after rapidly repeated discharges. Such a protection is of value in a long hand gun which is constantly being grasped by the barrel near the point of balance, both to carry and to aim. (This point is almost invariably just in front of the breech where a forestock, if any, is located.) But a pistol, normally a one-hand weapon, is naturally held by the grip when carried or aimed. Hence the wooden forestock was discontinued presumably for reasons of economy.

The principal distinguishing characteristics of Model A are the following points in combination: 

1. An improved angle of drop. I use the word "improved" advisedly since this is the most fundamental difference between this model and the original patent drawing. The correct angle of drop, i. e., the angle of the grip to the line of the barrel, has always been, and still is, a most important efficiency point in pistol design, particularly with respect to fast or "snap" shooting where one aims instinctively rather than by deliberate sighting along the barrel. Just exactly how acute or how flattened (i. e., more nearly approximating a straight line) that angle should be, is a disputed question. It is a well-known fact that one instinctively points one's index finger directly at a designated object. Theoretically, therefore, the correct angle is the one which will cause the barrel to follow the line of the forefinger when the grip is grasped naturally in the hand. Alas, hands, and wrists, and theories of how best to hold a pistol are most varied and conflicting. Some marksmen shoot with a straight wrist, others permit the wrist to bend, etc.

Without entering upon a discussion of the inherent merits of these various schools of marksmanship, suffice it to say that, almost at once, Colt revised his original idea on the subject and slightly "flattened" the angle of drop in what I believe to be his first factory model, which angle has undoubtedly satisfied the great majority of American shooters since it has remained practically unchanged in most American revolvers. Needless to say, "such popularity must be deserved.”* 

2. Nipple shield, chamber muzzle protector, and wooden forestock are dispensed with. The first two efficiency, or rather inefficiency points, for reasons hereinafter described, disappeared from Colt revolvers forever. So did the wooden forestock, to be replaced by a metal forestock (hereinafter referred to as the barrel frame) integral with the barrel, a definite economy point. 

3. The original cylinder-turning mechanism is retained. 

4. As in the inventor's model, the massive frame head swelling out behind the cylinder, constitutes a flash-guard (Plate 1, r) designed to protect the shooter from damage in case of that ever present bogey, a premature discharge. Such a safety device is indeed most necessary, especially when the nipples point directly backwards. Not only is there danger from a powder flashback through the nipple vents, but in case one or more capped chambers are ignited from the front, the resulting explosions would blow the unused solid metal caps backward off the nipples with considerable force and, without some sort of shield, would cause the shooter's face to be bombarded with a barrage of perilous little projectiles. The value of this device is demonstrated by the fact that in one form or another it is to be found on practically every subsequent percussion Colt, and on other makes as well whose nipples point directly to the rear. particularly, however, that in this early model (and also in Model B) (see Plates II and III, Figs. 9, 11, 12 and 16) there is no cut-out in said flash guard to permit the nipples to be capped by hand without dismounting the barrel and removing the cylinder. This is an important defect from the standpoint of rapid reloading, but as will be seen later, this omission is doubtless due to excess caution on the part of our pioneer experimenter who very wisely hesitated to sacrifice safety to speed.

First of all let me say a word respecting calibers. You will note that I have taken the dimensions of the bore in three different places, the barrel muzzle, the barrel breech and the chamber muzzle. You will note that there are certain variations between the diameter in these three places and between the measurements of certain specimens undoubtedly designed to shoot the same caliber projectile. Some of this variation is undoubtedly due to rust and wear, unavoidable in pieces which have seen service. Some of it, on the other hand, is undoubtedly due to different theories as to how tight a bullet should fit in a rifled barrel. Despite these slight variations, therefore (after all one caliber, i. e., one-hundredth of an inch, is a very small unit of measure), I think we may properly consider Nos. 138, 284 and 323 as .28-caliber and 

Nos. 377, 979 and 530 as .40-caliber pieces.

Very closely related to the depth of groove and fit of bullet, is the pitch or rate of twist of the rifling. 

Once again, time and space do not permit me to take up in detail the various minute but fascinating angles of this problem. Nevertheless the measurements noted (altho made without special precision instruments and therefore more or less approximate) are sufficiently accurate to show us: (1) That the rate of twist seems to increase with the caliber, (2) that in the smaller calibers the rate of twist is so slow as to be almost straight, and (3) that the rate of twist in the earliest and latest .40-caliber model is almost identical. Apparently, also, the rate of twist is uniform and not "augmented," i. e., rate of twist increasing towards the muzzle, as in some of the later Hartford models. 

While I do not consider chamber muzzle chamfering a point of sufficient importance to become a "type characteristic," nevertheless, it illustrates one of a number of minor efficiency points worthy of being noted. The object of this slight reaming of the sharp edges about the chamber muzzle is to facilitate the insertion of the bullet into the said chamber and to prevent the soft lead from being cut during the ramming-home process. Curiously enough, the earliest and two latest specimens are not chamfered, but in the very latest of all (323), while the chamber muzzles are not reamed out about the edge, the chamber is slightly funnel shaped, narrowing from .750 cm. at the very rim, to .735 cm., about 5 to 

10 mm. down the chamber, and to .70 cm. at the bottom just above the cup-shaped base. 

Note that the flash guards of both Nos. 530 and 323 are deeper in proportion than the corresponding caliber specimens without capping cut-out. This shows clearly that the whole frame head was redesigned in order better to accommodate the cut-out, i. e., that models D and E were not merely adaptations of previous models but were new models requiring new patterns and therefore are properly classified as such in accordance with the general principle laid down in the introduction. 

The fact that Model A, Type 1 has two shoulders on its nipples and all the rest have four, is of no great importance except give another indication that Model A-1 was indeed a separate output. 

Apparently all Paterson Colt cylinders are truly cylindrical, i. e., have the same diameter throughout (rounded shoulder nipples excepted). 22

ARTICLE III QUESTIONS AND CONCLUSION 

SECTION 1. How should these successive models be dated? You will remember that I hesitated to give exact dates to the various models hereinbefore noted and described. Nevertheless, for the sake of a beginning, I am going to make a few tentative suggestions and leave the matter open for discussion. 

I think we may be quite sure that Model A was made during 1836 when the factory began operacions and possibly as late as 1837. It is true that the contrary might be argued from the fact that Model A, Type 2, was put out in a box with a complete set of accessories which were not patented until August 29, 1939.* Nevertheless, it is a well-known fact that accessories of this kind were frequently manufactured and sold several years before patented. Consequently the date of patent does not positively determine the date of manufacture and sale.

Judging roughly from the number of specimens extant, I should give it as my opinion that Model B began to be made in 1837 or 1838; Model C in the latter part of 1838; Model D in 1839, and Model E in 1840.

I repeat that this is not much more than a guess, and I would greatly appreciate any positive information which would either prove or disprove the above tentative chronology. 

SECTION 2. In accordance with what basic scheme, or system, were Colt revolvers numbered at the 

Paterson factory? This, as has been previously indicated, is indeed a perplexing question. Normally, one would merely have to consult factory records in order to get a positive answer, but unfortunately, in the present instance, Colt records were destroyed by fire or otherwise lost. As a result, the only way in which we can settle this question with any degree of accuracy is to get together, either actually or by photograph, all known Paterson Colt revolvers and let the numbers speak for themselves. Incidentally, who knows what other valuable and hitherto unguessed information such a comparison would bring to light? 

Nevertheless, the data which we have already compiled should warn us against jumping at conclusions. As a general proposition, we may reasonably assume that the absence of a given successful efficiency point in one group of specimens and its presence in another, raises a strong presumption that the former group antedates the latter. This presumption is greatly strengthened if the preponderance of specimens in the second group are positively established as subsequent by other evidence. Finally, if two or more points concur in meeting the above requirements, presumption is raised to practical certainty. To apply this general principle to our immediate problem, there are three outstanding Colt points which clearly fall into the final catagory above mentioned, viz., the rounded nipple partitions, the capping cut-out, and the improved cylinder-turning mechanism. All three, from the time of their adoption, are found (in most cases together) in an unbroken series of Colt models, including Paterson, Whitneyville, and Hartford products, down to the end of the percussion period. Obviously, therefore, if numbers seem to contradict the evidence of characteristics such as these, numbers must take second place. 

That this is frequently the case is evidenced by comparing Model A-1, numbered 377, with Model B-1, numbered 284, which, according to the above, is obviously a later specimen. Even more significant are the differences between Model B-2, numbered 979, and Models D-2, numbered 530, and E-2, numbered 323. I will not elaborate further at this time, but these and numerous other apparent contradictions force me to the conclusion that we must rely on characteristics rather than numbers to establish which of two specimens or groups is the earlier. As to the rest, until more specimens are available for comparison, or more other data unearthed, I cannot even hazard an hypothesis. It is possible that not only each model, according to my tentative scheme of classification, but each of the two varieties of that model, started with a new set of numbers. It would even seem that type A-2 rated a separate series, in which case I should have classified A-1 as a model rather than a mere type. Something like the above must have occurred, but whether my classification and the Colt scheme of numbers exactly coincide is a question. Or again, a new set of numbers may have been commenced at the beginning of each year (actual or fiscal) or of each semester or But all this is mere guess work. The fact remains that these numbers will be of little use to us, except as an incentive to mental exercise, until in some way we can definitely discover upon what theory the Paterson factory issued its numerical series.

SECTION 4. Conclusion. I realize all too well that my tentative classification is incomplete; and furthermore that it can not be completed until there is compiled a comprehensive photographic and descriptive record of every known Paterson Colt revolver. Only by a comparative study of these original "documents" covering the whole field can we be sure that some hidden but important point has not escaped our observation. A beginning has been made, but a considerable number of most illuminating specimens must still exist which are either unknown or imperfectly described. Now, possibly, even probably, these pieces are in your hands, and if we all collaborate such an ideal work is quite within the realm of possibility. It rests with you, therefore, whether or not this ideal will ever be attained. 24
