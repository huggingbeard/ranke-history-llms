PREFACE 

HE function of the mind has been very beautifully expressed by Samuel Butler in "The Way of All TH Flesh." “All our lives long, every day and every hour, we are engaged in the process of accommodating our changed and unchanged selves to changed and unchanged surroundings; living, in fact, is nothing else than this process of accommodation; when we fail in it a little we are stupid, when we fail flagrantly we are mad, when we suspend it temporarily we sleep, when we give up the attempt altogether we die."

History has been kind in furnishing us the men whose minds have functioned properly, and have left to us some of their greatest thoughts.

I have attempted in the following pages to select not all, but some of the men, who in their own country and time, have been outstanding leaders.

These leaders have been selected without regard to Race, Creed, or Religious beliefs.

INTRODUCTION 

HE "wisdom of the ages" is a phrase occasionally tossed about by those who are anxious amidst their perplexities to bring the world to a philosophy which will govern what they call the "Anarchy" of our time. There is a venerable air about these words, which, to the unthinking, has quite a crushing effect. The wisdom of our ancestors, it is somehow assumed, is an entity, and to go against this ancient sagacity is to show one's self crossgrained and obstinate.

It is a pity to disturb this fine old antiquity, but when we ask ourselves precisely what is meant by the "wisdom of the ages," we are very much puzzled to find out. The wisdom of the ages has at one time or another included a good many contradictory bits of wisdom; and it is difficult to find any effective common denominator for a modern man among thinkers as opposed as Plato and Lucretius, Bacon and Plotinus, the author of Ecclesiastes, and the author of the essay on "Self-Reliance." One who gives his mental assent to the proposition that vanity of vanities, all is vanity can hardly, without committing mental suicide, give his assent at the same time and with the same degree of heartiness to the proposition that he should trust himself for the sufficient reason that every heart vibrates to that iron string, inasmuch as under the first head, any such direction as the second is a bit of illusory nonsense. 

He who holds literally to the direction to sell all he has and give to the poor can not at the same time hold literally to the injunction: "Seest thou a man diligent in busi- [ 3 ]

ness. He shall stand before kings," for the simple and effective reason that if he sells all he has, he is no longer in business. Moreover, the distinction that is sometimes drawn between an entity called "the wisdom of the ages" and another entity called "the wisdom of the laboratory" does not upon analysis prove very fruitful. 

This somewhat specious opposition springs into being from the minds of those who are in our time dissatisfied with the progress of scientific research on the ground that it lacks effective control towards wise ends. Such persons believe, or seem to believe, that, opposed to the scientist in his white coat, there is an immensely wiser person, the sage in his long coat. Unfortunately for the distinction, a good deal of the wisdom of the ages has been brought into the laboratory and found wanting; Bishop Berkeley and the Rev. John Wesley, both very wise persons in their day, having advocated, for example, various nostrums and panaceas for human ills which, brought to the test of the medical laboratory, are found not merely to be useless but positively harmful. On the other hand, it is equally clear that a good deal of the wisdom of the ages is the wisdom of the laboratory; the classic instance of Archimedes weighing Hiero's crown by use of his discovery of the principle of specific gravity being but one of a number of bits of laboratory research carried on many centuries before Bacon. Some philosophers have invented theories which, brought to the test of laboratory research and exact measurement, have proved to be rather remarkable previsions of the truth; while others, using the inadequate data of their own time, have invented equally plausible hypotheses which our modern methods of exact measurement

5 and analysis have broken down and cast aside. Nevertheless, like many other striking phrases, "the wisdom of the ages" contains a kind of truth. We can not, because we operate the most marvellous machines ever known on earth, quite afford to ignore the experience of the past. 

There is a sense in which, however greater our knowledge may be than that of Socrates, our wisdom is perhaps not so fine. The words of the wise are still excellent goads; and provided we avoid the fallacy of believing that there is any single and unified body of doctrine which we can get up as we do a church catechism or an examination, provided we go to the sages of the past in the right way and ask them the right questions, the wisdom of the ages is an excellent thing to consult. Honesty is still, on the whole, the best policy; it is still a wise remark that, if you would civilize a man, you had best begin with his grandmother; necessity is still an excellent maternal parent for both courage and invention.

Accordingly it is a fruitful thing from time to time to remember the words of the wise. A sententious saying goes sometimes farther than a sermon; and most of us are likely to remember best the Demosthenes who defined the three parts of an orator as action, action, and again action, as over against the orator who pronounced the lengthy and not always very interesting oration on the crown. It is always interesting, if rightly done, to study those pithy statements of a creed, those sudden flashes of wisdom, with which thoughtful observers have expressed their intuition of life; and it is hoped that the present collection, if it is not novel, is at any rate in a number of its portions new.
