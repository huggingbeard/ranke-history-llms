INTRODUCTION 

This Commentary presents an annual rating of state traffic laws to show how they compare with the rules of the road part of the Uniform Vehicle Code as of January 1, 1978. Prior ratings were based on state traffic laws, as they existed in 1968, 1971, 1972, 1973, 1974, 1975 and 1976.1/

This rating covers 1977 state traffic laws but does not include changes adopted by state legislatures in 1978 or 1979. This rating also does not include changes in the Uniform Vehicle Code which were adopted by the 

National Committee on Uniform Traffic Laws and Ordinances when it met in August of 1979.

Traffic Laws Annotated2 shows how state traffic laws compare with provisions in the "Rules of the Road" Chapter of the 

Uniform Vehicle Code³/ in over 150 "Statutory Annotations."

Reading these Annotations would provide a general picture of the traffic laws in any one state. Any law that differs from a section in the Uniform Vehicle Code could also be detected by reading all the Annotations. This Commentary attempts to provide a more precise picture of the status of state traffic laws as of January 1, 1978 that will facilitate rapid identification of particular areas of a state's laws that may not be in substantial conformity with the Code, indicate areas of the statutory law where uniformity may be markedly poor on a nationwide basis, and identify states whose traffic laws may need substantial attention on a priority basis as part of contemporary efforts to improve highway safety. This document, when used with Traffic Laws Annotated, is particularly useful in determining which laws in any one state differ from provisions in the Uniform Vehicle Code (and from laws in other states). 

Uniform rules of the highest quality are important for highway safety. Substantial improvement in such uniformity, and the termination of laws that are archaic or that cannot be reasonably expected to improve the safe and efficient use of our highways, are imperative and should occur as soon as possible.4/

CAVEAT VIATORS/ 

The above rating system and its application is not perfect and could be misleading for many reasons.

Reducing the law to numbers is a process of questionable value. 

- 4 

---

The rating system also fails to make distinctions based on relative importance among rules of the road. That is, a law duplicating the Code's rule against riding in house trailers and a law duplicating the Code provision on the duty of a driver making a left turn to yield to oncoming vehicles each receive equal treatment plus five points. Thus, if a state duplicates 17 one rule but not the other, its total score would be five. 

Though all rules of the road are deemed necessary for highway safety by the National Committee, some contend that certain rules contribute more than others. Certainly, some rules do cover situations encountered more frequently by many people and, for that reason alone, may make a more significant contribution to highway safety particularly when they are reasonably uniform. 11

SUMMARY OF 1977 CHANGES IN STATE TRAFFIC LAWS 

The traffic laws of Idaho and South Carolina were extensively revised in 1977 and there was significant progress toward uniformity in Massachusetts and Oklahoma as well. Indeed, the adoption by 

Massachusetts and South Carolina of many of the UVC's right of way rules may well be the highlights of 1977's rules of the road legislation. The states also eliminated 10 differences in laws. relating to traffic-control signals. 

The number of substantial differences decreased in seven states: Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, Oklahoma, South

Carolina, Vermont, and Wyoming. South Carolina ended five such differences, Massachusetts three, and Oklahoma two.

Two states (Idaho and Missouri) increased the total number of differences by one each. Actually, Idaho ended one difference (by adopting absolute speed limits) but adopted two more (allowing drivers to complete a passing maneuver in a no passing zone and requiring drivers to yield to emerging vehicles using a siren or a light). The Missouri blooper would allow a driver to go around or under a gate at a grade crossing if it is safe to do so. Indiana eliminated one difference related to passing on the right only to adopt another permitting use of flashing red lights on school buses though still in motion.

OBSERVATIONS 

One of the principal reasons for publication of the previous ratings was to stimulate consideration and elimination of major differences between state laws and the Code. Though many states - 9 - 

---

made significant advances toward uniformity during 1969-1977, 200 substantial differences still remain. Particularly in view of the fact that almost 45% of these differences involve traffic control devices and right of way, this number is too high. All substantial differences should be eliminated on a priority basis by each state as soon as possible unless the difference can be clearly justified as improving safe and efficient movement of traffic.

Another disappointment is the failure of 14 states with total scores of less than 426 to make greater progress toward uniformity.1/ 

Not only are the total scores in these 14 states low, but these states account for a large percentage of all substantial differences. 

Seven encouraging developments in this regard were significant changes in the laws of Maryland, Nebraska, Ohio, Oregon,

Pennsylvania, South Dakota and Vermont resulting in their moving up from the last 14 states in the rankings during 1969-1977. 

Most states with low scores are located in the eastern half of the United States.

Legislators in states with scores of less than 426 should be informed that their laws appear to be nonuniform, obsolete and in need of modernization based upon the most recent edition of the Uniform Vehicle Code. Legislators in these states probably do not know about the condition of their traffic laws and cannot be expected to take action without knowledge of the problem. 1/
