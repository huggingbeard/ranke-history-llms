To the Pupil 

1. Try to do each exercise neatly and accurately, and as quickly as possible, but remember that accuracy is more important than speed. 

2. Do not be discouraged if you find that you do not work as fast as others, for the main purpose to keep in mind is mastery of as many ideas and topics as possible. It is better to understand a reasonable number of fundamental ideas than to go superficially over a larger number. 

3. Individual work is honest work; do your work by yourself in order that you may obtain real practice in doing the important things that are expected of the welleducated citizen. 

4. Do as much work as possible mentally. Where there is sufficient space, put any necessary written work on the page with the test so that you can locate the sources of any errors. In certain exercises, where the computations are long, you may find it necessary to do some work on separate sheets; these sheets should be kept in this book until you have checked your work. In other exercises you will put all of your work in a separate loose-leaf mathematics notebook. 

5. After you have checked your work and have verified the number of "rights according to the plan used by the teacher, enter your score in the proper place in the record-blank on the inside of the back cover.

Because the French writers at that time were trying to get away from the more difficult type of demonstrative geometry which Euclid had put in his textbook, they introduced what we now include as Informal Geometry in the seventh, eighth, and ninth grades, as follows:- 

1. Intuitive geometry, in which the pupil looks at a figure and says that something is so because it could not be otherwise, as, for example, "If two straight lines intersect, the vertical angles are equal." 

2. Experimental geometry of a simple type, such as cutting out a paper triangle, tearing off the angles, and placing them adjacent so as to show that their sum is 180°. 

3. Observational geometry, in which the pupil recognizes objects in the world about him as typifying certain geometric forms, and sees certain relationships that exist between them. 

4. Fundamental geometric skills, such as constructing a perpendicular from a given point outside a line to that line. 

5. A simple approach to demonstration through informal proofs of theorems, such as, for example, "The sum of the angles of a quadrilateral is 360°" after the pupil has learned that "The sum of the angles of a triangle is 180°." 

By means of this survey the French writers enabled students just as has the author of this series of General Mathematics workbooks-to find answers to the questions they might ask about an object, namely: "What is its shape?" (Geometry of Form), "What is its size?" (Geometry of Size), "Where is the object located in space?" (Geometry of Position).

The three pictures on this page, which appeared in Le Clerc's Treatise on Geometry, show how the author tried to make his geometry fanciful and also to give reality to the facts which were stated by the theorems he used. The three pictures on the preceding page, which appeared in Mallet's Practical Geometry, indicate how another author also tried to do this.

Following this attempt in the eighteenth century to enliven and give point to the study of mathematics, most teachers soon returned to the formal method of presenting the subject. However, some of the textbooks published in this country many years ago indicate that progressive teachers continued to recognize the value of an informal geometry course for beginning pupils. In very recent years, moreover, teachers generally have accepted the belief that it is desirable to make the study of geometry more interesting and more valuable by tying it up with life situations. 

Over half of this workbook deals with some of the more interesting and important facts which appear in geometry. In these materials the pupil will find many applications of the theories of geometry to objects around him and to everyday experiences. 

It is believed that he will find this study not only interesting, but also useful in any more advanced courses that he may take and in his life after leaving school.

Calendar Measure 

A common year has 365 da., and a leap year has 366 da. In a leap year, February has 29 da. instead of the usual 28 da. April, June, September, and November always have 30 da. each, and the other months always have 31 da. each. In computing interest, a year is generally taken as 12 mo. of 30 da. each, or 360 da. in all. [155]

To the Teacher 

HE MATERIAL in this workbook may be used either as teaching or as testing material. If the pupils have not yet studied the content with which the exercises are concerned, the teacher may regard the various pages as teaching devices. If, however, the pupils have been studying similar material, then the exercises may be used as diagnostic (or achievement) tests to reveal the strength or weakness of each pupil with reference to the items tested. 

When the exercises are used for purposes of diagnosis, their main value is that the results obtained point with a fair degree of certainty to the causes of individual difficulties, and thus give the teacher valuable assistance in improving his instruction a much more valid aim of modern testing practice than the standardization of pupils' accomplishments for purposes of comparison with those of other school systems.

In the first part of the book there is a comprehensive review of the essential features of Informal Geometry covered in the previous year. Together with this review there is an extension of the topics to include some of the more difficult geometric features. More involved formulas and new algebraic ideas are also presented here. 

In the second part of the book the Language of Algebra, begun in the previous year, is reviewed and considerably extended. This section includes a study of the formula, the equation, the graph, and directed numbers. 

In the last part of the book there is a review and more extended treatment of the basic Arithmetic Skills, followed by their application to more advanced topics of business arithmetic.

Informal geometry, algebra, and arithmetic are presented in psychological and logical order instead of being interwoven with one another; as a result, the teacher can locate more easily the material he wishes the pupils to study. 

The space given to geometry, measured in number of pages, is as great as that given to arithmetic and algebra. The time required, however, for the completion of the exercises in arithmetic and algebra will, in general, be considerably greater, page for page, than that required for the geometry.
