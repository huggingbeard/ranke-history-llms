Foreword 

The egregious fallacies of the Townsend Plan have been so often exposed in the public prints during the past few months that, in writing this pamphlet, I did not consider it necessary to pile up all the valid objections to it in great detail. Instead, I have merely summarized its most characteristic weaknesses and have presented an alternative plan which will redistribute income more effectively than the Townsend Plan, and will accomplish that purpose without wrecking the national economy.

THE TOWNSEND PLAN 

Puts ninety per cent of the burden on people of small incomes. 

Increases government expenses and government bureaucrats. 

Transfers buying power from the young to the old. 

Increases idleness.

Through inflation it will destroy the realizable values of life insurance and savings.

Will destroy the value of government, state and municipal bonds by reducing the purchasing power of the interest paid on these securities. 

The transaction tax will increase the cost of living to the point where wageearners will be reduced to a coolie system of living. 

Will not augment buying power. The tax on "all transactions" will fall chiefly on the living expenses of the poor. The money collected in this manner will go to old people, who will spend it. The amount of money will be the same. The poor will be reduced to the point of beggary; old people will do the spending — but in any case, the total amount of spending will be approximately as it is now. 2

THE PLAN PROPOSED IN THIS PAMPHLET 

Puts the burden of taxes on those who are best able to pay. 

Exempts incomes under three thousand dollars. 

Does not include a sales tax, or a transaction tax, and therefore does not raise the cost of living. 

Will increase consumption and put idle plants to work.

Will put an end to unemployment because increased demand will lead to increased production. 

Will check extravagant speculation because there will be not enough idle funds for this purpose.

Will stabilize the economic life of the nation. 

Will simplify our present income tax system. 

Will get rid of the ridiculous tax on capital gains and losses.

Will reduce income taxes by half, at least. 

Will aid the farmer because of increased consumption of his products. 

Will force the wealthy to support the government. 

Eliminates the necessity of the government supporting the people. 

Gives industry its opportunity to utilize its physical assets and makes possible its future growth by increasing its ability to give more and more value for less and less money. 3

AN ANSWER TO THE TOWNSEND PLAN 

by 

Robert G. Elbert

Part I 

1 The Townsend Plan is an emotional adventure in the field of economics. 

In fairy stories children are told that a pot of gold may be found at the end of the rainbow which shines so beautifully in the clear-washed sky. The Townsendites are looking for the rainbow's gold. But no one, in the long centuries of crusades, has ever found this fabled treasure. Children of all ages, and of various degrees of mentality, have sought it and have been sadly disappointed. 

The Townsend adventure is the capitalization of a wish; it has nothing at all to do with reality. Yet, like most wishes, its seed and core is a definite human need which cannot be charmed away by laughter or derision. It is both absurd and mean to say that Dr. Townsend is a fakir and racketeer. 

All the available evidence shows that he is honest and sincere, and is moved by a desire to benefit his fellow man, even though less scrupulous people may be using him and his dreams for their own selfish purposes. The only defect in his scheme of things is that he does not know what he is talking about. 

Here is the Townsend Plan expressed in its simplest form. Dr. Townsend proposes that the Federal government give lifelong pensions of two hundred dollars a month for every person sixty or more years of age who will voluntarily retire from constructive labor and agree to spend all of the two hundred dollars in the month it is received. Persons with private incomes of two hundred dollars or more monthly would not receive pensions. 

It is estimated that eight million people might benefit from the plan, which would be financed by a two per cent tax on all transactions. 

By the tax on "all transactions" Dr. Townsend figures that the Treasury will collect enough funds to meet the eight million monthly pensions of two . hundred dollars each. This may or may not be so; there are no statistics bearing on this point. It is clear enough, however, that no less than $19,200,- 

000,000 would have to come from somewhere to pay the annual bill.

The tax on "transactions" is not a sales tax in the usually accepted sense; it is a tax on every step of the processing, transportation and sale of commodities, as well as a tax on all business transactions of every kind. 

On many articles two per cent would be collected ten or twelve times. 

Consider a loaf of bread. The farmer who grows the wheat would pay two per cent in selling it to the grain elevator; the railroad that carries it to the elevator pays another tax. When a flour mill buys the wheat a tax is paid again.. The miller is assessed another two per cent when he sells the flour to a baker; and the railroad that carries it to the baker pays; and the trucking is another transaction and so on, and so on. The final buyer, who proposes to eat the bread, will find that its price has gone way up. That is how the $19,200,000,000 would be raised. It is estimated that 50 to 60% of all transactions on our exchanges are by speculators. What would a transaction tax do to them and the marketability of securities and commodities? 

Could the banks survive a 2% transaction tax which would have to be paid either by the bank, or the depositor, on every check? 

In 1934 the entire national income was approximately $46,000,000,000. 

The Townsend Plan would take almost exactly forty per cent of it. A man on a salary of forty dollars a week would contribute sixteen dollars of his pay every week-not directly, of course, but indirectly through the transactions tax.

On the basis of current income it simply cannot be done; it is hopelessly impossible. The rainbow in the sky is too far beyond the horizon. But the Townsendites believe and say that the paying of two hundred dollars a month to each of eight million old people who would be obliged to spend the money within thirty days would increase consumption to such an extent that unemployment would come to an end. The whole productive capacity of the nation would be put to work, and the national income would be increased enormously. 

That's the Townsend Plan.

It is essentially a crude device for putting money in circulation. The old people who are to receive the money and spend it are used simply as a convenient transmitting mechanism.

The economic fallacies of the Townsend Plan are numerous, though its objective is praiseworthy. It fatal defect is that it will not do what Dr. Townsend thinks it will do. It will destroy just as much buying power as it creates. 

Through the medium of indirect and unseen taxes the forty-dollar a week

man will contribute sixteen dollars a week which will be given to an aged person to spend. The buying power of the forty-dollar man will be reduced accordingly. 

The cost of the scheme will fall almost entirely on those who are least able to afford it · on the people who live on wages and small salaries. The income of this class is consumed almost wholly in living expenses and it is they who will have to bear the heavy load of the tax on all transactions. 

In popular language the whole thing is cockeyed from beginning to end. 

The only feature of it that touches reality is the assertion that the spending of money on a large scale for consumable goods and services would prevent depressions.

It would do just that, in all probability, provided our Federal Reserve Banks co-operated. Why tax the poor whose spending power will be accordingly reduced? Money can be forced into circulation and buying power developed in a much better way. 

The tax on "transactions" would be intolerable, when added to income taxes, real estate taxes, school taxes, gasoline taxes, inheritance taxes, and thirty or forty more taxes direct and indirect. 

Costs of desirable things would go up so high if Dr. Townsend's plan were tried that the actual purchasing power of his proposed $200.00 monthly would probably not be much more than $50.00 at present prevailing prices. 

What a wreckage that would make of the worker's pay envelope. 

Why stop at $200.00 per month? Anyone gullible enough to believe it would work at $200.00 would believe it still better at $300.00. So, in order to get Townsend's following away from him, a political demagogue would only have to raise his ante.

Dr. Townsend threatens a third party. This is just bluff. All that opposing honest candidates have to do is to have a gentleman's agreement that neither one will support his plan and it will soon flop. 

It should be remembered that, at the insistence of President Roosevelt, Congress has already made provision for aged persons. If, however, our legislators still feel that because of political intimidation from the Townsendites, they must do still more, the following might be apropos. 

Based on need of the applicant for old age pension the Government agrees to pay two-thirds and the State one-third up to $45.00 per month.

Of the state's $15.00 the local communities to pay two-thirds and the state treasury one-third. A local community board should be made to assess the taxes to raise its portion of the contribution by and with the consent of the State Board. The State Board should consist of three Federal appointees and three State appointees. This would put on the local community the responsibility of deciding the need of each applicant and the amount necessary for each case. It would also put the major cost of administration on the state; prevent too much over-centralization in Washington and possibly eliminate the necessity of homes for the aged. It would give an aged couple the present average wage in America; and cost only about 25% of what is now proposed by Dr. Townsend. If, after given a fair trial, this experiment works and we then decide we want to increase the amount we can do it in the light of intelligent experience. The money necessary to pay these pensions could be raised by a variable general retail sales tax. 

If, however, our legislators conclude that Dr. Townsend's proposal has merit and that, by giving all over sixty two hundred dollars monthly, it will increase purchasing power and consumption to such an extent that we will have no unemployment, why not try it out on a 25% basis? That is, give the 

8,000,000 old folks $50.00 per month? That would cost only $4,800,000,000 per annum. This could be raised by a 10% general retail sales tax. This in all probability would not completely wreck our economic structure, but would come so near it that many people would not know the difference. But, in any event, give the aged real, instead of phantom, dollars. 2 There is a much better way to distribute income, put money in circulation and end unemployment, with a consequent increase in national buying power than the method proposed by Dr. Townsend. 

This alternative plan is presented here. 

Its fundamental principle is to 

TAX SURPLUS INCOME. 

That is, to

TAX INCOME NOT SPENT. 

Suppose income taxes were reduced to a minimum, and the taxpayer were compelled by law to expend the major portion of his income in the

business of living. More people would be employed, more goods would be bought, money would circulate more freely.

It would be much more conducive to economic stability in the long run to bring out the spending power of the nation by taxing income not spent rather than taxing income earned or received. The basic conception of this idea is quite simple, though it is contrary to accepted and conventional notions. 3 The plan for taxing income not spent which is presented here requires the division of all personal income into two parts—one part to be used for investment or saving; and the other part in living, which means expenditures for consumable goods or services. The essential idea is to put a tax so large on the spendable portion of the income in the event that it is not expended in living — that the tax will force that portion of the income into circulation. 

In case of all incomes of $25,000 or less it is suggested that there be an exemption of $3,000. That is, the recipient of a $3,000 income, coming from whatever source, should not be required to pay any income tax at all. If he has an income of $15,000 he would have to pay on $12,000, and if his income is $25,000, his tax would be on $22,000 only. Above $25,000 there would be no exemptions at all. The purpose of the $3,000 deduction is that it should take the place of the prevailing exemption for dependents. It should not be necessary to make any exemption for dependents when a taxpayer's income is in excess of $25,000. 

This plan does not recognize any distinction between earned or unearned income. That has always been an unnecessary complication, and an annoyance. 

Under the existing law income from tax-exempt securities is not subject to income tax, but a tax on "money not spent" might be levied on income derived from such securities. Possibly it might be in the nature of an excise tax. That would not be an income tax but something entirely different. The isolated immunity of tax exempt securities should be repealed as soon as it can be done constitutionally. 

There should be no "normal" tax. There is no reason for it at all. The income tax here proposed is a straight tax, all the way through. There is an urgent need for simplicity in the whole field of income taxation.

The essential part of this plan is to force the spending of income in living, so as to provide employment and to keep industry going. So, after the tax is paid, then the taxpayer may set aside twenty-five percent of the remainder which he may put into investments, or savings or gifts or do anything else with it that appeals to his fancy. 

The other seventy-five per cent, remaining after the regular income tax is paid, must be expended by him in living, or in gifts, within the next twelve months or he will have to pay a ninety per cent tax on the amount not so expended.* This is to force spending. It will do vastly more than the 

Townsend plan could do in putting real money in circulation, money that has real purchasing power, which will not be the case if the Townsend plan is adopted. By increasing consumption it will relieve unemployment and will make it possible for the relatives of aged people, in most cases, to take care of them.

It would not be anywhere nearly as complicated as the making out of an income tax return under the existing law. 

The twenty-five percent of the income — after paying the tax- that may be utilized for investment or saving need not be so utilized if the taxpayer desires to spend it in living. He is simply allowed to invest that much and no more, but there should be no obligation to invest or save it. 

In the category of investments or savings should be included capital investments; bank deposits. purchases of lands and buildings (not to be used as a home for himself), life insurance, and any other interest acquired by purchase in an enterprise intended for profit-making.*

The entire sum and substance of this plan of income taxation is the distribution of the major portion of all income above $3,000 in expenditures which include the purchase of goods and services. And the underlying dynamic purpose is to stimulate industry and employment by buying. 

No one over a period of years should be allowed to invest or put into his savings account each year more than twenty-five percent of his annual income. This plan, it may be noted, has a double-barreled objective It will provide funds for supporting the government

While, at the same time, it will relieve the government of most all of the cumbrous and unsatisfactory machinery of keeping people on home relief, work relief and the whole category of useless public works. (Not all public works are useless, by any means, but there can be no doubt that the greater part of them are, and that they have been contrived only to furnish employment.) *It has been suggested by some of those who have read this plan that life insurance be taken out of the investment and savings class and be included in the "remainder that must be spent," on the ground that life insurance is chiefly a form of protection. That suggestion is worth considering, but if life insurance be changed to the other category a definite limit, in percentages, should be put on it. 12

Stuart Chase, an economist and writer of distinction, says in a recent issue of the New Republic: 

"If everyone spends his income as fast as he receives it, the dollar system can never break down. Depressions will be unheard of. Any reasonable number of new dollars can be added to the system, and there will be no trouble as long as they feed through outgo and are promptly spent." 

He says, further: "Dollars that go out as costs (outgo) must come back as sales and fees (income), or the mechanism fails. If the system does not work this way, if it can continue to function when income dollars are hoarded, mislaid or diverted, then there is magic in it somewhere; business men had better give up keeping books and take to crystal gazing.' 

Under the existing law the government takes the greater part of all very large incomes; the surtax on a $500,000 income is $286,000; and, in addition, the normal tax amounts to about $20,000; and there is also the state tax. The government uses an enormous percentage of the vast fund so collected to support unemployed people in idleness. This plan would eliminate the necessity for that.

This is simply a plan for doing in a more wholesome and direct fashion what the government is now doing in a roundabout, clumsy and harmful manner — harmful because its tendency is to create a permanently idle class, and because it gives sanction to an economic fallacy. 

We have here the wealthiest country in the world, not only as measured in money but in natural resources. With efficient economic management the 

United States could support two hundred and fifty million people without any difficulty, yet we have here eleven millions, at least, out of work. 

Whatever criticisms there may have been to the accumulation of large fortunes would be obviated because everyone would, after payment of his taxes, have to spend seventy-five per cent of his income. 

Whereas under this plan everyone with a substantial income uses seventy-five per cent of it to increase consumption, under the Townsend plan the "transaction tax" is disproportionately wrung out of those who are least able to pay it.

We have paid far too much attention to the accumulation of fortunes, which turn to dust and ashes in time of depression. A personal fortune must rest on the general prosperity of the people, and if we spend the national income, or most of it, in consumable goods or in services we shall lift, with a single effort, the whole burden of depression. 13

The fallacy in the Dr. Townsend theory is that he proposes to distribute income in equal amounts to a certain specified class of persons without regard to their needs or their social worth, and the income which he intends to distribute so lavishly will necessarily come from those who are least able to pay. 5 Under the existing law capital gains and losses are taken into account in calculating taxable income, but the provisions of the law are nothing less than fantastic.

The income tax law provides that all gains made by an individual from the sale of capital assets, such as stocks, bonds, buildings, etc., shall be taxed as income. But 

Losses from the sale of capital assets can only be offset to the extent of gains from the sale of similar capital assets plus two thousand dollars. 

Here is an illustration:

Suppose a man loses $50,000 in the sale of an office building; in other words, he has to sell it for $50,000 less than it cost when he bought it. But, during the same year, he made $50,000 from the sale of stocks. To any person of ordinary common sense it would appear that these two transactions in capital assets balance one another; and that, if he has no other source of income, then his income for the year is zero. 

It is not so in the eyes of the law. He will have to pay an income tax on $48,000, because he is not allowed to deduct more than $2,000 of his loss, while his gain is assessed at its face value. The office building and the stocks are not "similar assets." 

Mr. Fred I. Kent, of the National Industrial Conference Board, to whom this paper was submitted for comment before publication, had something to say on this subject that is very much to the point. "Capital gain taxes," says Mr. Kent, "are received by a government during periods of prosperity when taxes from other sources are at their height. 

At such times they are not required but when received are spent usually through some form of legislation that carries continuity and develops a need for the money for the taxes when times are difficult and capital gain taxes fall off. The collection of capital gain taxes is, therefore, harmful to govern- 14

ment as they are received during periods of prosperity when they are not required, inducing extravagance; and on the other side when the government needs funds during a depression, they are not available. "Capital gain taxes prevent men from wisely adjusting their portfolios as markets and industries develop. This is harmful to all the people as it results in great numbers of individuals entering depressions with their portfolios in an unsound condition. They are, therefore, not able to meet obligations that they may have assumed that fall upon them because of the depression. "Again, reluctance to sell and turn profits over to the government prevents switching from stocks to bonds when the former get too high, keeps off of the market millions of shares of stocks that if turned over would hold market price within bounds, and results in widespread speculation and running from bonds into stocks, in large part, by a public not competent enough to determine what they are justified in buying, but influenced by rising values. "When stocks are held on a rising market and they are sold after an important rise, the proceeds being put into other stocks which have been rising also, there is no real profit. Many investment trusts have distributed such phantom profits and as a result have found themselves with greatly reduced capital structures after market reactions. "It is impossible for anyone to tell during a rising market whether there is a profit in a sale or purchase or not. To use an example to which I have called attention many times, suppose a man purchased a home for $50,000 in a certain town where he was employed and that after living in it for five years, during which time its market price had doubled, he was obliged to change his address and move to another town. He sells his house for $100,- 

000 and finds when he reaches the new town that prices have gone up equally with the price in the town that he left, and that in order to obtain a house with the same conveniences and the same character of layout, he is obliged to pay $100,000 for it whereas five years previously he could have obtained it for $50,000. This exchange of property has not increased the value of his assets one dollar, but he must pay an income tax upon $50,000.” 

The British handle such matters in a more sensible fashion. Under their law no income tax is paid on capital gains — that is, on profits from the sale of capital assets nor is deduction allowed because of loss from the sale of capital assets. The increase or diminution of capital is wholly outside the income tax field. Our rule works only one way; we make a taxpayer pay on any 15

account of his gains but do not permit him to deduct his losses on capital assets transactions, unless the assets on both sides of the equation belong to the same class.

We have a graduated income tax scale for capital gains and losses which diminishes according to the term of ownership. A taxpayer who sells at a profit capital assets which he has owned ten years or more pays on only thirty percent of the gain. Nevertheless, if he sells at a loss - no matter how long he has owned the assets he can deduct only $2,000 of the loss against profit or income from other and similar sources. 

If we want business to thrive and unemployment to decrease we should eliminate this capital gain and loss tax as the harm it does to industry more than a thousand times offsets the benefits to the government. In the plan here presented capital gains and losses are ignored. A citizen-under its provisions may dispose of his capital assets, bit by bit, and live on them for years, but he is merely living on his fat. He has no income; his savings are keeping him alive. On the other hand, a man may turn one capital asset into another one with an immense ledger profit, but the increment is not income. 

By trading this for that, he may pile up capital assets that look as if they are worth ten million dollars and yet he may not get a cent of income from them.

An income tax should deal only with actual income, which means dividends, salaries, wages, rents, interest, royalties, bonuses, etc. With this plan in effect the savings and buying power of those in the lower income brackets would be increased. 

There would be plenty of money for investment in necessary enterprises, but not enough to encourage extensive stock market speculation and overexpansion of industries.

The purchase of consumable goods would be greatly increased, and production would be stimulated. 

In all industries there would be a large increase in the number of small stockholders. 16

Part II 

1 It is an important economic fact, which may be readily demonstrated, that depressions and unemployment go hand in hand with high taxes. As taxes go up the economic foundation grows more and more insecure; and, indeed, the burden of taxation may become so heavy that the system of business and industry is strangled to the point of suffocation. In that case, very few people are daring enough to undertake the hazards of business enterprise. 

If they win if their enterprises are successful—they face a certainty that the government will take the greater part of their profits; if they lose, then in that case there is no redress, their investments disappear. 

It is the universal experience, without a single exception, that in every land where income taxes have risen higher and higher the number of the permanently unemployed has increased in a somewhat similar proportion. 

Why? It is because the government absorbs the profits of industry and initiative. It destroys enterprise by taxation and it must, therefore, as a logical consequence, take care of the industrial wreckage. 

That is exactly what is happening in the United States today. Millions of people are out of work, and they must be supported in some way or other. 

The best way out of this economic trap would be for industry to give jobs to these millions of the unemployed, but that cannot be done until the public has sufficient spending power to buy the goods that would be produced by putting the unemployed to work. Factories and stores are not charitable institutions. Naturally not. They must sell their goods or go out of business, and they cannot employ people until there is a consumer demand for their products.

The government has always attempted to meet the situation by getting on the horse backwards. In time of prosperity taxes are lowered for the reason that governmental expenses are lower; yet the taxpayer at that time is better able to pay without undue strain on his resources. 

In a period of depression the government increases taxes because it needs more money for home relief, work projects and so on. The result is that the enormous increase in taxes deepens and prolongs the depression. President 

Roosevelt knows that very well. He said in his speech at Pittsburg, October 

19, 1932:

Taxes are paid in the sweat of every man who labors because they are a burden on production and can be paid only by production. If 17

excessive, they are reflected in idle factories, tax-sold farms, and, hence, in hordes of the hungry tramping the streets and seeking jobs in vain. 

Our workers may never see a tax bill, but they pay in deductions from wages, in increased cost of what they buy, or (as now) in broad cessation of employment.

There is not an unemployed man- there is not a struggling farmer - whose interest in this subject is not direct and vital. 

All that is quite true; yet Congress has raised income taxes enormously in the higher brackets, and the consequences are just what Mr. Roosevelt said they would be. Yet there can be no doubt that the underlying purpose is honest and well-meaning. Something had to be done to relieve distress, and to keep the millions of the unemployed alive. Apparently it did not occur to anyone who had to do with the matter that the entire theory of income taxation might be wrong in principle.

As far back as 1919 Carter Glass - then Secretary of the Treasury said, in his annual report for that year:

The upmost brackets of the surtax have already passed the point of productivity and the only consequence of any further increase would be to drive possessors of these great incomes more and more to place their wealth in the billions of dollars of wholly exempt securities heretofore issued and still being issued by states and municipalities, as well as those heretofore issued by the United States. This process not only destroys a source of revenue to the Federal Government, but tends to withdraw the capital of very rich men from the development of new enterprises and place it at the disposal of state and municipal governments upon terms so easy to them (the cost of exemptions from taxation falling more heavily upon the Federal Government) as to stimulate wasteful and non-productive expenditure by state and municipal governments.

When Secretary Glass made that statement the country had just got out of the World War and taxes were considered unbearably high. The surtax on an income of $100,000 was $23,510. Today, under the 1935 Act, the surtax on a $100,000 income is $28,000. A $50,000 income then paid a surtax of $5,510; under the 1935 tax law it pays $7,700. 

During Secretary Mellon's administration of the Treasury a study of the subject of income taxation was made by the government tax experts and the conclusion was that from surtaxes at a rate of not exceeding twenty-five percent "the Government when the full effect of the reduction is feltwill receive more revenue from owners of large income at the lower rate of taxation than it would have received at the higher rates; and that experience 

18

has shown that the present high rates of surtaxes are bringing, in each state, conspicuously less revenue to the Government." Mr. Mellon said: "My own recommendations on this subject are in line with similar ones made by Secretaries Houston and Glass, both of whom served under a Democratic President." "High taxation," he said, "means a high price level and a high cost of living. It is largely borne by the ultimate consumer." 

Let us illustrate this idea by taking an example: 

Here is a man, let us say, who has a million dollars invested in city apartment houses. He wants to get eight percent net, and this is certainly not an exorbitant aim, considering the hazards of the real estate business. 

In short, he wants $80,000 a year left over after he has paid for service, repairs, heating, realty taxes, and so on. Well, he finds that he must put up his rents to the point where they will still yield about $130,000 after service, heating, depreciation and local taxes are paid. Under the 1935 law the surtax on a $130,000 is about $47,000. 

Deduct that from $130,000 and you have $83,000. In addition, however, there is the state income tax. The tenants, of course, pay their landlords' income tax, both Federal and State.

But he may not be able to raise the rents. His tenants cannot pay any more. If he boosts the rent they will move, and he will have vacant apart. ments on his hands. In that case his income goes down, and so does his purchasing power and his ability to consume. He loses heart about the real estate business. The government begins to take it over and goes into the business of building and managing apartment houses. 

Now I, for one, do not think the government should be in the housing business; nor do I believe in State socialism. I am fully conscious of the fact that there is an urgent need of comfortable, low-cost houses; and that the slums in the large cities ought to be abolished, but I know that can be done by private enterprise, if properly encouraged by the government. It cannot be done while the government imposes such a high income tax that real estate owners are forced to raise their rents to protect themselves from loss and extinction. The best way to reduce rents and to encourage building is to reduce taxes.

The government may, by legislative enactment, take wealth, in large quantities out of the productive-consumption cycle. This may be done by drastic tax levies of one kind or another. Of course taxation for the support 19

of the government is necessary, but when it passes the point where it threatens the economic movement, then the government has to assume burdens which the natural free play of production and consumption could normally carry without straining a muscle.

One of the most formidable obstacles in the way of the solution of our national difficulties is the almost total lack of realistic thinking on all economic subjects. In the average American mind the conceptions of credit, debt and value are amazingly jumbled and misunderstood. Money is not real wealth; nevertheless there is a widely prevalent belief that money and real wealth are synonymous.

Wealth is the product of goods and services in action. 

The wealth of a people is in their ability to consume as much as in their ability to produce. Many people talk about the re-distribution of wealth; what they are really talking about is the re-distribution of savings. The savings of this country have been built up since its beginning on the basis of about three percent a year. Obviously this plan allows for a greater amount than has ever been used. If we destroy these savings we destroy the nation's working capital. Let us keep a clear distinction in our minds between savings and income. Anyone living off his capital will obviously soon be bankrupt, but a man should be made to spend the major part of his income. That will not bankrupt him.

Wealth must be active; it must move. It is a result of the creative function of the great cycle of production and consumption. 

The fundamental basis of prosperity may be expressed in five economic principles: 

1. Purchasing power increases in direct proportion to the money and credit in active circulation. 

2. Money expended for consumable goods increases employment; and it follows that there is a definite ratio between the amount of money expended in living—that is, on consumable goods and personal and professional services and the number of persons employed. 

3. Investment of the national income in productive enterprises, in new plants, and in the expansion of established enterprises, increases employment as long as production does not exceed consumption, or the capacity to buy. Beyond that point expansion in business and industry leads to unemployment. 

4. For every dollar's worth of goods produced there must be a pur- 20

chaser with a dollar that he is willing to spend. It is useless and foolish to produce commodities when purchasers are lacking. 

5. Money saved is money invested. If too much money is saved, investments and production grow out of proportion to purchasing power. The results are a piling up of unsalable commodities, a general decline in value, an increase in unemployment, and a loss of accumulated savings.

There is a widespread economic fallacy that production leads consumption, when in fact the reverse is true. The wealth of a nation is measured by its consumption. Production follows buying power. In short, production needs no incentive beyond the pressure of money being expended in living. 

The Townsend Plan, if put in operation, would not increase buying power to any extent worth mentioning. It merely takes money from one section of the public - chiefly from wage-earners and salaried people — and gives it to 8,000,000 old men and women to spend. It is like taking water from one bucket and pouring it into another one. As you fill the empty bucket you will find that there is less and less water in the other bucket. 

There is just the same amount of water at the end as there was in the beginning.

The young would inevitably pay a heart-breaking penalty for being young. Their income, in purchasing power, would be reduced to a bare pittance, while the old folks spent the money. Among young people there would be a nation-wide movement to quit their jobs and go back to live in idleness with Pa and Ma.

But under the alternative plan given here the wealthy would be forced to spend their money, and the money so expended would percolate through all the arteries of business and industry. The young folks would have better jobs; there would be more of them employed; they could afford to support their aged relatives. 21 232

APPENDIX A 

Will the low rates proposed in your plan purchase sufficient revenue for the government?

This question, in some form or other, has been asked by many of those who have read this plan in manuscript. It is a query that naturally arises. 

I shall try to answer it to the best of my ability. 

A point that should not be overlooked is that the distribution of income by spending which is the essence of this plan would increase greatly the number of income taxpayers in the lower brackets; that is to say, those whose incomes run from $3,000 to $25,000 a year. They have goods and services to sell. Another effect would be to put a large number of the unemployed to work, taking them off the government payroll. In this way the expenditures of the government would be reduced. 

Obviously it is impossible to calculate with precision the financial effect of these two tendencies. All that we can be sure of is that we would be moving in the right direction. 

Although the tax rates proposed in this plan are considerably lower than those in force in 1928, they would produce almost as much revenue for the government, notwithstanding the fact that incomes under $3,000 would not be taxed at all. There are several reasons for this. One is the abolition of all exemptions and deductions in incomes of over $25,000. 

Let us take the case of a man, under the present income tax law, who has an income of $52,000 a year, of which $20,000 is earned. He has, in effect, a $20,000 salary and the remaining $32,000 comes from investments.

Before publication this plan of income taxation was sent in manuscript form to the forty-two members of the Business Advisory Council of the Department of Commerce, and to about sixty other gentlemen in various walks of life, for their comments and criticisms. Among those who received it were Walter S. Gifford, Gerard Swope, Alanson B. Houghton, Henry I. 

Harriman, Thomas J. Watson, Pierre S. du Pont, James H. Rand, Jr., General 

Robert E. Wood, Winthrop W. Aldrich, Walter C. Teagle, Lincoln Filene, Myron C. Taylor and George H. Mead.

So many replies were received that it is not possible to include more than a few of them in this pamphlet without making it unwieldy in size. Accordingly, I have selected extracts - chiefly critical - from a few of the letters. 

The purpose in reproducing criticisms rather than eulogies is that the critical comments give an opportunity for an explanation of the points brought up in criticism.

The distinguished head of a large industrial corporation writes: "To supplement the present system by a law which makes all incomes above a stated minimum subject to an arbitrary division between what shall be saved and what shall be spent, seems to me to take from us that necessary freedom of operation upon which our productive efforts now depend. Our incomes, in principle, would cease to be our own. They would in effect belong to the state. And the immediate effect of such a law would be, of course, to place in the hands of Congress definite control of the creation of capital. Congress would then decide whether at any one time, more or less capital should be created out of the national income, and more or less expenditures of wealth be devoted to pure consumption." 

Yes; under this plan Congress would have the authority to decide how much of the national income should be spent in living, and what percentage should be devoted to the creation of new capital. It would be better, of course, to have the decision made by a National Economic Council in which all sections of industry, finance, labor and agriculture would be represented. 

If that authority had existed in the 1920's it is wholly probable that the insane speculation and over-expansion of industry in that era would not have occurred. Millions of people went mad in the pursuit of more wealth, instead of spending their incomes and increasing consumption. 

Note that he says, "Our incomes, in principle, would cease to be our own." I call his attention to the fact that they are not our own today, as it is. 25

The figures show that personal exemptions and deductions on account of dependents amounted to $11,859,000,000, or $2,913, on an average, for every person who filed a return. Under the plan proposed here personal exemptions and credits for dependents would have been reduced to $6,426,- 804,000, a difference in taxable income of more than five billion dollars. 

How about the division of the total income for 1928 into spending and investment classes? We have taken into account only the incomes reported to be above three thousand dollars. A calculation shows that $3,503,687,000 would have been available for investment, while the portion that had to be spent in living amounted to $10,511,061,000. This does not include $6,426,- 

804,000 of exemptions—that is, the total sum of the $3,000 exemptions which are deductible by all who report incomes of $25,000 or less. This huge sum might be invested, or spent in living, at the option of the taxpayer. 

In 1934 and 1935-years of depression the direct revenue from the income tax herein proposed would have been considerably less than the revenue actually collected. The Internal Revenue bureau reports the total income tax collected in 1934 as $506,481,000. Under the proposed plan the total sum would have been approximately $250,000,000. But here the "imponderables" come powerfully into action. More than $9,000,000,000 would have been forced into circulation as expenditures in living. There is every probability that this large expenditure would have lifted at once the burden of home relief, work relief, and all other forms of charity off the shoulders of the government.

Under the plan herein proposed there would be no deduction for bad debts. If a man lends money that he cannot collect, or sells goods to a concern which fails, that is his personal problem; there is no sense in dragging the government in to take a corresponding loss in income tax. 

This from one of the wealthiest men in America: "The principle of your suggested plan is that of success brought about by spending; that is, by keeping capital employed and money passing quickly from one to the other. The soundness of such a plan cannot be questioned, but I do question the method of doing it. It would seem to insist upon spending, regardless of whether or not there was any sound reason for the expenditure." 

My dear sir, do you believe for a moment that if the expenditure of income were left to private individuals and they were forced to spend the whole volume of spending would be more unwise or more unsound than the colossal expenditures of the Government? 26

Without knowing anything of your personal affairs I should say that, in all probability, you pay a whale of an income tax. Under this plan your income tax would be greatly reduced, and you could put twenty-five percent of the remainder in new enterprises, or in any profit-making venture. 

As to the rest, you would hardly want to spend it all in giving dinners, or buying clothes, or in keeping up country houses. But you could clear up slums and replace them with decent houses; you could endow hospitals; you could make gifts wholesale to talented and deserving people; you could send young men and women to college; you could buy up mortgages on little houses and hand them over to men and their wives who have been struggling for years to pay them off. All that would mean the employment of many people; it would send money circulating through the channels of consumption. And it would be lots better, wouldn't it, than turning over a fortune every year to the Internal Revenue Department?

And here is a comment from the chairman of the board of a great lumber company: "The principles on which fortunes are built up seem to be entirely ignored I cannot agree with these suggestions. People save money and build up their assets with the thought of protecting them in the future, and any suggestions made which mean spending the money as fast as it is made will change the entire atmosphere surrounding business sentiment." 

No; the principles on which fortunes are built up have not been ignored in the preparation of this plan. On the contrary, they have been strictly observed. Industrial enterprises can exist and grow only if their products are sold in quantities to people who want and have the money to pay for them. The history of sound industrial expansion shows that demand always precedes production, and keeps step with it, and not the other way around. 

At the present time American industry is overbuilt; or, to express it another way, there are more sellers than buyers. The banks are full of money, and they are willing to lend it, but manufacturers do not want to borrow money until they are sure their goods will be sold. 

Business is getting better; the lines in the charts are rising. There is a reasonable probability that in the not distant future we shall be back to about the 1926 level of production. We shall find when we get there that there will still be 7,000,000 or 8,000,000 people out of work. They can be put to work by the spending of surplus income; of, if we prefer it, they can be carried along on the dole which means higher and higher taxes. Exorbitantly high taxes mean simply that your profits turn to ashes in your hands. 27

A steel manufacturer makes this comment: "It would seem to me that any plan that calls for such Federal enforcement of personal expenditure and control of the manner of life of individuals. would be dangerous and hopeless from the standpoint of governmental regulation and administration."

On the contrary, it would be quite simple from the standpoint of governmental regulation. The check-up of the disposition of income would be made almost entirely on the list of investments made during the year to see that they do not exceed twenty-five percent of the income. The report on living expenses, gifts and so on, would be more or less perfunctory. Within certain broad limits the government would not care what you did with your income, provided you did not save or invest more than twenty-five percent. 

The administration would not control the taxpayer's manner of life. He could spend seventy-five percent of the remainder — after paying the regular income tax in any way he pleases, as long as he actually spent it or gave it away.

The vice-president and comptroller of a capital goods industry says: "If the large incomes are to be disbursed under compulsion, how will worthy and constructive enterprises, with the possibilities of employment they offer, be financed? Heretofore the originators of such enterprises would interest one or two men with large capital in financing, with returns on investment deferred for the period while the business was being built up. "With no such accumulation of capital in individual hands it might be necessary to interest fifty men to amass enough capital, a practical impossibility."

Why is it an impossibility? All that would be required on the part of the projectors would be a little more work. To get as many men as possible behind every worthy new enterprise would be very helpful in preserving the economic stability of the country. A hundred, or a thousand, would be better than fifty.

Let us not delude ourselves in the mistaken belief that capitalism is in no danger in the United States. It is in very serious danger, and one way to avert the danger is to increase largely the number of small stockholders in all corporations.

On the other hand, there would be a great deal of pleasure in it. John D. Rockefeller, Sr. gave away six hundred million dollars over a term of years without noticeably interfering with his constructive work or his wholesome recreation.
