FOREWORD 

Lack of knowledge of distribution costs frequently results in a wholesaler making profit from handling some commodities and dealing with some customers, but losing enough in transactions in other commodities or with other customers to offset that profit almost entirely. The fact that the merchandising activities of a concern are profitable as a whole for a certain period does not indicate that a profit has been made in all of the associated activities. The absence of substantiating data obscures the real picture; tangible evidence is required for intelligent understanding.

Costs of production have been analyzed scientifically and are usually thoroughly known, and thus profits in manufacturing can be predetermined to some extent, but costs of distribution are not so exactly known and are apparently increasing. One of the reasons for the increase is the lack of precise information concerning the expense of handling different commodities. Knowing the operating expense for each commodity, pricing may be directed to yield more equitable margins, and costs may be pared down on items or processes on which they are above average. Increasing pressure of competition makes such knowledge of distribution costs imperative. 

The purpose of surveys of this sort is to adapt principles of sales and cost control to the line studied and to show, by applying those principles to the specific problems of a representative establishment, that the method of analysis used will produce definite practical results.

The survey reported in this publication was made at the request of the National Electrical Wholesalers' Association. The Bureau of 

Foreign and Domestic Commerce was fortunate in having placed at its disposal in this instance the most complete and flexible data so far obtained in any of its cost studies. Results are presented in a variety of ways for the purpose of illustrating alternative methods of using such data. Refinements were made in the calculations, for the sake of thoroughness of treatment, which would not, perhaps, be essential to the everyday use of such analysis in a business establishment. 

The explanation of the method used should enable any merchant to build a similar system adaptable to his own business. 

Acknowledgment is gratefully made of the assistance given by the officers of the concern studied. Much of the analytical work on this study was performed by William H. Meserole and John R. 

Bromell of the merchandising research division of the Bureau of 

Foreign and Domestic Commerce.

PROBLEMS OF WHOLESALE ELECTRICAL GOODS DISTRIBUTION SURVEY FINDINGS 

In the fall of 1928 the Department of Commerce, acting at the instance of the National Electrical Wholesalers' Association, undertook a distribution survey of a representative electrical wholesaling establishment. The house studied is located in a southern State and has an annual sales volume less than $500,000. It is really a branch of an establishment with headquarters in one of the larger northern cities, which, in turn, is affiliated with one of the national electrical concerns as a distributor. The branch under survey attempts to serve nearly all of the electrical needs of a growing industrial community. Its customers range from contractors and dealers to industrial concerns and municipalities. The commodities handled cover a wide range of construction goods, industrial goods, radio, and appliances. Its trade territory extends to a slight extent into three other States, but its principal volume is obtained within the home 

State. 

The analysis presented is based on sales and cost figures for 1927, 1928, and 1929, with the earliest year analyzed in greatest detail. 

Some difficulty was experienced because the house studied was a branch plant. Buying and other functions performed by the parent organization for the branch have been studied so as to adjust the figures reported for the branch as nearly as possible to what they would have been if the establishment surveyed had been operating independently. The chief features of the survey results have to do with commodities, customers, and territories. 

ANALYSIS OF FIGURES FOR THREE-YEAR PERIOD 

The 3-year period from 1927 to 1929 has marked an emergency of increasing seriousness for the house studied. Net losses have been greater each year as a result of a combination of factors. There has been a net decline in total sales volume over the period, with a falling level of wholesale prices of electrical goods and with increasing operating costs.

Interest on investment is included as a cost in the present analysis for the purpose of presenting an accurate basis of comparison between functions of the business and costs of commodities carried. These operating-cost ratios are different from those carried in the present accounts of the business, in which interest on investment must be excluded from operating cost for purposes of taxation. The third ratio presented is for operating cost exclusive of bad-debt losses. 

Bad-debt losses would in all cases be included under cost of operation, but are separated here to indicate that the increase in operating cost ratio was due entirely to bad-debt losses. When that item is left out of account it appears that other operating expenses declined slowly but steadily over the period. The increase in bad-debt losses is undoubtedly largely due to a real increase in the seriousness of the credit situation. another for those of 1928 and 1929. There is some evidence that the second firm made a definite effort to place the business on a more conservative basis.

NET PROFIT 

When interest on investment is not considered a cost the operations of the house resulted in a net profit of 3.45 per cent in 1927 and net losses of 0.08 per cent in 1928 and 12.62 per cent in 1929. When interest was included as an expense there was a net loss each year- 

0.15 per cent in 1927, 3.783 per cent in 1928, and 15.488 per cent in 1929. 

The principal weakness of this house is in its relation to its customers. As a newcomer in its market, the concern had tried by the usual concessions and liberal terms to build up its sales volume in the territory. During the three years studied the profit and loss statement has continually grown less favorable with a constant decline in percentage of gross profits, a decrease in sales volume, and the changing of small net earnings for serious losses. The reasons for this changed profit position are found in the character of retail outlets with which the house did business. 

REASONS FOR SALES ANALYSIS BY CUSTOMER TYPES 

The concern moved into its present territory during a boom period and accepted the accounts of many men who had quite recently grown from small contractors to big operators. A local slump, accentuated later by the business recession of 1929, caused many of these customers to become insolvent. 

Examination of the business obtained from the customers carried on the books in 1927 indicates that the characteristics of customers which make them assets or liabilities to the wholesaler can be readily determined from sales analysis. All of the customers of the house were classified into eight types, namely contractor-dealer, contractor, dealer, industrial, public-service, municipality, jobber, and unclassified. The business factors on which these several customer types were compared with each other included average annual sales volume, average size of order, reliability in credit, gross margin on purchases, and such special service requirements as special order from the factory. 

Striking dissimilarities between customer types were revealed in connection with each of these factors. The municipalities, for example, were not responsible for any credit losses, turned in orders of more than average size, and paid regular prices on most merchandise, so as to return better than average gross margin. Total sales to municipalities, however, were relatively small and covered only a small commodity range. The wholesaler with a full line of electrical merchandise to sell would not in any case derive more than a small proportion of his business from such customers.

The three principal types in point of sales volume were the contractor-dealer, contractor, and dealer. Among these three types the dealer presented the most favorable showing of credit reliability and gross margin on purchases. In every other respect the contractordealer was outstanding. That type bought a greater average volume of business during the year than any other customer class. The average size of order from the contractor-dealer was over $32, as against approximately $25 for the contractor and $24 for the dealer, which

indicated that the business of that customer class entailed considerably lower selling and handling costs. Sales by commodity departments to the contractor-dealer show a percentage breakup closely parelleling that for the business as a whole. It is apparent that the contractor-dealer presents a well-rounded outlet for the various types of electrical merchandise handled by the wholesaler. 

In building its business the house would have done well to take account of these fundamental customer characteristics. The first requirement, of course, is to select customers of stability and integrity in order that striking bad-debt losses may be avoided. Customers with that preliminary qualification will, no doubt, furnish a sure basis for growth if they are interested in all of the principal lines of electrical development. Customers depending only on construction contracts are likely to remain inactive for a considerable period because of a building slump. The straight dealer type, on the other hand, is in some instances too strongly dependent on sales of radio or some other one line. Other advantages suggest themselves in favor of the generalized electrical retailer. Electrical merchandise can probably best be sold and serviced by merchants with a certain technical knowledge of electrical uses. This fact has been recognized in a recent movement on the part of some of the electrical associations to popularize the so-called "outlet survey." This plan has already been tried out in some sections, and the contractor-dealer has found it a valuable way of building business. Buildings are studied for the purpose of making suggestions as to further wiring requirements or the installations of new outlets. 

In some places it is impossible to use appliances already on the market because of the lack of proper electrical outlets. Provision of such outlets is said to be one of the most direct means for stimulating sales of appliances. Desire for appliances thus stimulates, in turn, new wiring and outlet installations.

It has been thought by some merchants that the States of the 

Southeast are not yet favorable as an appliance market. In that section of the country, it is held, low-cost domestic labor is still available to a greater extent than in the Northern States. Thus, it is believed that the homes in which labor-saving electrical devices might be used prefer to continue with domestic service. The personal interests and abilities of the sales force have a great deal to do with the type of goods sold, however. In the house studied, radio sales grew rapidly under a sales manager who was interested in that product, but slumped under another man whose experience and interest was in the field of construction goods. But more important than direct promotional efforts on the part of the selling staff of the wholesaler is any aid which he may be able to lend to the building up of stable and well-rounded electrical retail houses prepared to service all types of electrical needs in their own communities. 

FINDINGS 

The principal findings of the analysis may be summarized as follows: 

1. The operations of the house in the year studied resulted in a slight loss from the standpoint of strict profit showing but, from the standpoint of operating income, netted nearly 6 per cent interest on investment.

20 2. Cost of handling varied greatly among commodity groups. 

3. Gross profit (margin) obtained on any group bore little relation to the operating cost allocated to that group. 

4. Out of 42 commodity groups, 22 showed a net profit and 20 showed a net loss. 

5. The principal factors tending to throw a commodity group into the loss column were, in the order of their importance, high investment and storage charges resulting from excessive inventory, high cost of routine selling and clerical effort resulting from small average unit sales, and, in a few instances, extra costs of physical handling. 

6. Three commodity groups were responsible for the greater part of losses sustained, namely, conduits and fittings, wiring accessories, and fuses and accessories. Construction materials and heatinga ppliances were next in the loss column. 

7. The commodity groups registering the greatest net gains were radio and accessories, safety switches, B. X. cable and fittings, and the standard type of electric lamps. 

8. In a few instances commodity groups appeared to fall into the unprofitable class because of inadequate gross margin. Net profit was possible, however, on a rather narrow margin, since 10 out of 22 profitable commodity groups were handled on a gross profit less than the average for the business of 16.62 per cent. 

9. Losses on a considerable number of items in stock reflected changing trends in demand and in distribution channels. For example, a few industrial motors remained in stock, although at the time of the survey most of the mills in the territory were buying motors direct from the manufacturer. 

10. Drop shipments from the factory and back orders were used to cut down the range of merchandise actually carried in stock. 

11. Some items were stocked primarily for the benefit of a small and, in some instances, unimportant class of customers. Control of inventory could not, therefore, be put into effect without some definite policy controlling the selection of customers. 

12. The classes of customers served varied more widely than with most types of wholesale houses. As already noted, this variation in customer type was reflected in the range of merchandise carried and also in character of sales effort and other phases of operation. Some of the other disparities pointing to the necessity for rigid selection of customers were in credit showing, average size of order, annual sales volume, and service requirements. 

13. The most favorable type of customer on the basis of these considerations was the contractor-dealer (an operator maintaining a regular retail establishment and at the same time engaging in domestic or industrial construction work). 

14. The contractor class (engaging in construction but maintaining no retail outlet) was a less favorable type and apparently included many operators who were inadequately financed to survive business adversity but temporarily offering a large volume of business during the course of a building boom. The critical condition in which the business found itself in 1928 and 1929 was due in a large measure to rapid expansion of sales to that customer class, with resulting heavy credit losses sustained with the passing of the building boom. 

15. The two factors controlling territorial distribution of sales were distance from the headquarters city and potential business avail-

able. About one-fourth of the counties served, lying on the fringe of the sales territory, produced a volume of business which seemed too small to justify cultivation. 

16. A serious small-order problem was found to exist. Over 72 per cent of the orders filled were unprofitable. 

17. The greatest need of the house seemed to be concentrated effort to secure the major business of stable and well-rounded retail electrical stores within its legitimate trade territory. The expense incurred in serving contracting units of a temporary character would probably have yielded a better return if devoted to the building up of such retail outlets in communities not adequately served. 

18. The greatest problem encountered in the cost analysis was in costing the physical handling of merchandise because of the extreme variation in types of physical units. The concept of a standard handling unit is thought to be the solution of that problem. The standard handling unit is a measure of labor requirements in handling materials of diverse physical nature based on a time study of relative difficulty in handling rather than on any attempt to balance such factors as weight and bulk.

Investment, allocated on average value of inventory, was charged most heavily against slow-moving commodities. 

Storage, allocated on floor space, also penalized the slow-moving commodities, but in relation to bulk rather than value. 

Physical handling was allocated on the basis of standard handling units and was charged most heavily against commodities which were heavy, bulky, fragile, of awkward shape, or otherwise difficult to handle.

Checking (routine buying and selling and clerical work) was allocated on frequency of sale the number of orders received in each commodity class-penalizing the commodities most responsible for the small-order problem.

Promotion, allocated on percentage of total gross margin, relieved the low-margin item from certain sales costs obviously expended for the principal purpose of selling long-margin merchandise. 

Reimbursement, allocated on volume of sales, distributed the financial costs of doing business equally to each dollar of merchandise sold and was particularly large in cases of commodities of low gross margin.

RESULTS BY COMMODITIES 

With a gross margin of 16.68 per cent and an operating cost of 16.83 per cent of sales, the business as a whole sustained a net loss of 0.15 per cent in 1927, practically "breaking even" from the standpoint of the present analysis. It must be noted, however, that in this analysis operating costs include an item for interest on investment. From the more usual point of view, which includes interest on investment with profit, the return on the business was 3.45 per cent of sales or 5.74 per cent on investment.

The purpose of considering interest on investment as a cost is to make possible a fair comparison between commodities. Indeed, the chief purpose of the survey was to measure the relative value to the business of the various commodity groups. The report deals largely with comparisons between profitable and unprofitable classes of commodities.

ANALYSIS OF SALES BY COUNTIES 

The material available permitted a distribution of sales by counties served, as well as an analysis of several types of service extended to those counties. The purpose was to determine whether some of the counties in which sales are made justify the effort expended upon them, on the basis of either present or potential business. 

The following table shows the percentage of sales volume derived from each county in the home State and from each of the other States covered.

Nearly 48 per cent of sales, it will be noted, was derived from the county of location. The percentage of sales volume covered was much less in other counties, there being only 11 out of a total of 39 counties served from which 1 per cent or more of sales volume was derived. It should be noted that the 8.6 per cent of sales shown for 

State X was sold to firms of contractors located in that State but delivered to construction jobs in near-by points. The volume of sales derived from each county is compared with the number of electrical consumers, and with the mileage range from the headquarters city. 

The counties of the home State have been ranked in order of volume of sales and are grouped into four sections. The totals for the four sections show that the potential market for electrical goods dropped sharply until the fourth section, where the total number of consumers was larger than the total for the third section. The mileage range, on the other hand, increased very gradually until the fourth group, where there was a sharp jump to more than twice the distance of the third group.

Variation in sales volume derived from the first three groups would appear to bear a much closer relationship to the potential market possibilities than to distance from headquarters; no one of the three is definitely close in as compared with the other two. There is, however, a decided break in potential business available, judging by the statistics on consumers of current. In each kind of consumers the total for the second group was approximately half as great as for the first group, and for the third group less than half as great as for the second group. The volume of business obtained from the three groups also declined steadily from one group to the next. Because of the presence of the headquarters city in the first group, however, the volume from that group was much greater than would be indicated merely by the figures indicating potential business available. 

Quite a different situation is found in comparing the third and fourth groups. Sales volume again drops off very sharply, although the potential business available is greater. The decisive factor is undoubtedly the great distance from headquarters, which averages twice as great as for the third group of counties. There seems to be a clear indication of the necessity for discontinuing all sales calls in the fourth group of counties and for accepting even such business as comes in by mail or telephone only when definite considerations of profit or policy seem to indicate that it is a wise decision. It can not be shown that the business received from the fourth group of counties is profitable.

The average orders received were progressively smaller from one group to the next. Orders were somewhat smaller in the county of location because of the volume of semiretail business which it is difficult for the wholesaler to avoid in his immediate neighborhood. 

That county, therefore, had the lowest average order in the first group of counties. Nevertheless, there was only one county in the fourth group where the average order was greater than in the headquarters county.

ANALYSIS OF ORDER SIZE 

One phase of the wholesale operations which has been much discussed is the small-order problem. Very frequently the wholesaler discovers that a very large proportion of all orders handled are so small that they do not pay their way through the establishment. 

In fact, in some houses orders are received and handled on which the operating cost computed on any equitable basis is greater than the total selling price of the order itself. 

In the house under survey there were many orders of that kind. 

The average cost of handling an order was $4.47, a figure obtained by dividing the total cost of operation by the total number of orders handled. The resulting figure is larger than the average value of a large group of orders. Such a basis of costing, however, is scarcely fair to the orders of smaller size, since they should not be called upon to bear a full share of such costs as reimbursement, promotion, and investment. A fairer scale of order cost is obtained by making each order bear an equal share of the checking cost and by charging it with the remaining cost groups on the basis of a uniform ratio to sales value. By such a computation each order would carry a cost of $1.25 for checking plus slightly more than 12 cents on the dollar for other operating expenses.

From this calculation it appears that orders for $1 would cost $1.37 to handle. Assuming the average gross margin of 16.6 per cent, the merchandise making up the order would have cost the wholesaler $0.83. The total cost to the wholesaler, therefore, of serving the customer with the dollar order would be the sum of this cost price of the merchandise and the computed operating cost on the order, or $2.20. The transaction would result in a net loss of $1.20. Orders for $2 would show an operating cost of $1.50. The gross margin to the merchant on an order of this size would be $0.35. He would, therefore, sustain a loss of $1.15 on the order.

As order size increases, the ratio of operating cost as computed gradually decreases and the amount of the net loss also becomes smaller. On a $10 order, for example, the operating cost is $2.46 and the gross margin $1.66, making a net loss of $0.80. 

The exact point at which the operating cost would be offset by gross profit on this basis of computation would be an order of $27.78. Since that amount lies near the lower limit of the $25-to-$50 order group,

that group as a whole could be considered profitable. In fact, the $25 order was only slightly less profitable than the average for the business, showing a slight net loss with operating cost of $4.25 and gross profit of $4.15. The operating cost on orders of that size is 17.32 per cent, against the 16.83 per cent for the business as a whole. It might be practical, therefore, for the wholesaler to use $25 as the approximate dividing point which sets off profitable from unprofitable orders. Probably an effort should be made to reduce the proportion of orders below the $25 mark. It is true that the merchant could never hope to do away with such orders completely. Some small orders must always be filled as an accommodation to good customers, which may be a very effective form of sales promotion. A merchant should at least attempt to decide, however, whether a given class of orders which falls below the $25 limit has any promotional value for the concern and whether that promotional value is sufficient to offset the net loss sustained on the transaction. It then becomes a question, not of refusing all orders for less than $25, but of deciding which orders in that range it may be desirable to fill because of considerations other than direct profit.

SURVEY PLAN 

For several years the Department of Commerce has been making studies of merchandising establishments, in which the central aim has been the determination of net profit by lines of merchandise handled. The inspiration for these studies lay in the pioneer effort of a New England hardware wholesaler, who set up such a scheme of analysis within his own business with astonishingly successful results. 

By allocation of all costs of operation against commodity items on the most equitable basis he could devise, that merchant found that many of the products which he had previously been featuring were actually sold at a loss. Other items which were being overlooked by salesmen were found to be the real means of sustaining the business. 

If the wholesaler had been content merely to uncover those unpleasant truths his efforts soon would have been forgotten. Instead, he at once put his method of selecting unprofitable items to the severest test conceivable. Of the 12,000 items listed in his catalogue, approximately half were indicated to be losing money; 5,500 items were immediately dropped from the line, salesmen being instructed to refuse orders for them from that time on. The first year after the change was made the net profit for the concern was three times as great as annual earnings had been previously. In other words, two-thirds of the net profit potentially available from his best items had been canceled by losses on the weaker items, as long as he carried the full line-very definite proof that the method of distinguishing profitable and unprofitable items was basically sound.

It is that method, with the refinements which have grown up in the course of several years of application to various types of business, which was applied in the survey of which this is the report. As originally devised, the method was nothing more than an ingenious system of estimate which could be operated only by executives with a full knowledge of every phase of the business and the peculiarities of every commodity handled. Revisions of the basic procedure have been designed to bring it within the field of accounting technique, so that reports on costs and profits by individual items might be prepared for the executive at regular intervals. In the meantime, while the method employed has constantly become more and more akin to ordinary accounting processes, the results achieved in the various studies in which it has been used have richly fulfilled the original promise of this type of analysis.

RELATION TO PRODUCTION-COST METHODS 

As distribution-cost accounting has developed, its fundamental resemblance to production-cost accounting has constantly become more obvious. In each case all costs which are incurred directly by a given product are charged directly to that product and other expenses allocated to commodities on the basis of whatever factors seem to give the most equitable distribution. Both are based on the certain knowledge that responsibility for operating costs may vary widely 33

between the various commodities handled in the same establishment and on the practical necessity for determining whether a given product is paying its own way. 

There are, on the other hand, several special requirements of distribution-cost accounting which are different from the requirements of production-cost accounting. These differences are principally in the character of costs to be handled, the aims of the analysis, and the details of method.

From the beginning, production costs have usually been bracketed under the three groups of direct materials, direct labor, and overhead. 

Direct materials as a manufacturing-cost division corresponds very closely to the cost of goods sold in a merchandising establishment. 

Each stands for the original outlay on the commodity, paid to the supplier of merchandise or materials, and excludes the cost of the special services by which the concern adds to the value of the product. 

This important element is very easily ascertained in a merchandising establishment, since it appears in the desired form on purchase invoices. The problem of identifying materials to finished product, confronted in costing manufacture, does not exist in costing distribution.

Direct labor, in the sense of labor which can be identified directly to a given product, is a very small element in the processes of distribution. It usually comprises a special processing or packaging operation; as, for example, the grinding of coffee or the cutting of meat in a retail grocery store. There are a few instances in which efforts of a strictly merchandising character can be attributed to a particular commodity or department, as in cases where the department employs a specialty salesman to cover a single product or a limited number of lines. Of course, where the business is completely departmentized, the opportunity for direct allocation of labor, at least to departments, is greatly increased. 

In the case of most distributors, however, direct labor is a small element, for a very fundamental reason. The end product of merchandising processes is a filled order. Analysis can not deal readily with the filled order, since scarcely any two filled orders are exactly alike. The commodities making up the order are the elements in which the interest of management rests, and the central problem of distribution accounting is to allocate to commodities the labor of order getting and order filling. Thus, while a great proportion of distribution cost, like manufacturing cost, is in the form of labor expended directly on commodities, this labor comprises an order routine in which no clearly marked division can be found as to where work on one commodity ends and work on another begins. 

The concept of overhead, as the third element in production cost, has been subjected to much scrutiny in recent years. In the less mechanized industries which were studied by the pioneers of cost accounting, direct materials and direct labor made up much the larger part of total cost of production. At that time all other expenses could be conveniently lumped and allocated in a somewhat arbitrary The increasing mechanization of industry has brought about a reconsideration of overhead cost. It is coming to be looked upon as the cost of a group of services to production which must be examined in detail in order to establish process rates for each machine or production center. In the phrase of one eminent authority, overmanner.

head is said to be the cost of maintaining capacity for work. This capacity cost can be clearly distinguished in merchandising establishments as the cost of maintaining the warehouse or salesroom and a stock of merchandise ready for sale.

It has been shown that each division of production cost has its direct parallel in distribution cost. In addition, there is an element of distribution cost which does not appear in production. Production costs cease in the plant at the point of delivery from the machine or the production center. Production costs do not reflect in any way the characteristics of the customers who buy the product. It is left to the sales and credit departments to maintain contact with the customer, and expenses involved in this contact are chargeable to the merchandising phase of the business. The business must be compensated for both the services of production and the services of distribution, and the effort of securing this compensation is in itself a part of distribution.

All of these costs are elements in the total price paid for goods by the consumer, even though the services making up this cost may have been provided by several types of concerns. A case may be assumed in which production and distribution are both handled by a single establishment. In this instance the price paid by the consumer would include, in addition to profit, the cost of materials, cost of productive capacity, and cost of productive work as production cost, and cost of distributive capacity, cost of distributive work, and cost of securing compensation as distribution cost. The first three items. make up the cost of the product at the time it becomes available for distribution-the cost of goods in a merchandising establishment that bought its commodities in finished form. The last three items are the basic divisions of distribution cost and will be designated henceforth by the terms maintenance, movement, and contact expense. 

A further contrast between costing production and costing distribution lies in the basic aims of the two. The primary reason for production-cost accounting when first established was to provide a basis for setting a price on the product which would enable the manufacturer to conduct his business at a fair profit. Distribution costing begins with the prices which are actually obtained under competitive conditions and attempts to discover the sources of profit and loss. 

Its aim is not to set prices but to eliminate waste. The latest development in production costing is a system of standard costs which allocates the expenses of production to specific jobs in advance of actual performance, but distribution costs will probably remain retrospective in method because of their very nature. Distribution includes the function of securing compensation. This function can not be performed until after the sale is made, and the cost of the function will vary with the amount of compensation sought. Another principal distributive function is to hold commodities produced in readiness for sale until demand develops. The cost of this function continues to accrue until the sale is completed. 

In production costing certain phases of operating cost, such as the cost of idle capacity, can be conveniently charged off directly against profit and loss. In distribution costing, with the emphasis on establishing a net profit rate by individual commodities, all costs must eventually be charged against commodities by whatever device seems to provide the fairest allocation. The assumption is that merchan-

dising establishments exist to make sales and that all operating costs for a given year must eventually come out of the income from sales in that year, whether charged as a direct loss or as a cost of handling commodities.

Many of the special features of method which may at first appear strange to the accountant schooled in production costing are derived from the considerations which have been set forth. The treatment of the special characteristics of distribution costs and of the purposes of distribution costing should serve to illuminate these features and help to explain the development of the procedure which is now to be described.

COST GROUPS MAINTENANCE 

The first group of distribution costs, termed maintenance costs, includes all costs of maintaining capacity for distribution. It can also be described as the cost of offering merchandise for sale. In retailing it would be the cost of maintaining an exhibit store, fully equipped and stocked, from which no sales were expected to be made. 

It includes rent of building, heat, light, and power, cleaning and repairs, depreciation on fixtures, interest on inventory investment, insurance, and taxes on merchandise stock and plant. These costs are not conditioned by the volume of sales made during the year, but by the scale of the preparations for sale; that is, by the amount of inventory carried in stock. 

Merchandise carried in stock involves maintenance costs for two reasons; first because it absorbs investment, and second because it occupies space. Maintenance is thus divided into two natural parts, investment costs and storage costs. Storage cost is computed on the basis of square feet, with the rent per square foot enchanced by a proportionate share of the utilities, such as light and cleaning, which add to its value. The space occupied by a given commodity is determined by actual measurement of the part of the establishment usually occupied by the commodity or reserved for it. In some cases an adjusted scale of space values will have to be set up, by floors or other divisions. This is particularly true where a portion of the space carries a special utility; as, for example, refrigeration. 

Investment cost includes all expenses which confront the manager as a direct result of investing in a commodity. Insurance on inventory and a portion of property taxes fall in this category. The largest element in the group is the interest on money tied up in inventory, whether owned or borrowed. It is not desired in this report to revive the controversy as to whether interest on assets owned should be set into the regular accounts as an expense. It is not proposed that such a step be taken, but merely that interest be charged against commodities for purposes of analysis in an independent study of commodity earnings. Interest income would still be shown, along with pure profit, as a part of total earnings. Equitable comparison between commodities, however, demands that a commodity which requires a large investment should be made responsible for earning interest income on that investment, and that a commodity requiring no inventory investment should be relieved from that responsibility.

MOVEMENT 

Movement costs, so called because they are involved in the flow of commodities through the establishment as contrasted with the static situation represented by maintenance, include the expense of many operations. Most fundamental in these operations, and usually most important as an element of cost, is the solicitation and assembly of orders and delivery to the customer or the common carrier. 

Since these operations deplete the stock of merchandise, they give rise directly to various replacement activities. If merchandise is sold, new merchandise must be bought, received, and placed in stock. 

There may be several processes short of manufacture involved in preparing merchandise for sale. Packing is the most common. 

Movement, then, may be defined as comprising a variety of activities. involving clerical and physical labor and the cost of materials and equipment directly assisting in the efforts of labor. 

The clerical and physical aspects of movement suggest an obvious division of movement costs, since clerical effort tends to vary with the number of times a commodity is ordered, and physical labor with the number of pieces or other measure of the quantity sold. In an establishment where packaged merchandise predominates, the number of pieces sold is a more accurate measure of physical labor than any other characteristic of the commodity, while the handling of bulk commodities would undoubtedly be measurable on a tonnage basis. 

In the more usual type of merchandising establishment, orders are filled in terms of cases or similar units. Even the physical labor of moving these units, as in order assembly or delivery, carries a certain responsibility to see that the proper number of units are moved in each instance. The case, therefore, rather than the pound, tends to be the time-consuming element in this type of effort. 

It is the time-consuming, rather than the energy-using, character of the commodity which is important from a cost standpoint, since labor of this sort is usually paid for on an hourly basis. In a given trade, time studies should be made to determine the difference in handling time between essentially different types of physical units. A barrel, for example, requires a different set of manual operations than an ordinary packing case. On the basis of studies of the actual handling time of such diverse articles carried by the same concern, it should be possible to establish ratios measuring this relative difficulty in physical handling. In this study a case of merchandise small enough to be handled readily by one man and shipped out without repacking in the same form as received was set up as the standard handling unit. 

Commodities requiring special preparation for delivery or having other unusual handling requirements were referred to this standard by means of ratios. Thus it was possible to measure the physical flow of each commodity in terms of standard handling units. 

The basic division of the movement group of expenses, it has been suggested, is into the physical and clerical aspects, which are termed "handling" and "checking," respectively. Handling is allocated to the commodity on the basis of the number of units handled or, where there is a diversity of type in physical units, on the basis of standard handling units. Checking, which includes all buying and selling that is routine in nature, in addition to what is more strictly known as clerical effort, is allocated to commodities on the basis of the number

of times each commodity is ordered. This number will correspond to the number of sales-invoice lines. Many clerical operations are directly proportional to invoice lines in the amount of time consumed, and many minor operations in which the invoice line is not the unit dealt with directly are roughly proportional to the same factor. 

It is assumed that the clerical labor involved in handling sales invoices in most houses is much larger than that involved in handling purchase invoices. Where the effort of handling purchases was especially heavy, however, there would be no objection, from the standpoint of the system here described, to setting up that group as a separate subdivision of movement expense with its own special allocation factor. In fact, the movement group would be the one most subject to refinement or adjustment to meet the peculiar conditions of various trades. In any case, whatever the number of subgroups under the general head of movement, they should be set up and allocated on the same general principle illustrated by the two groups used in this survey. This principle calls for making a distinction between groups on the basis of fundamental differences in the character of effort involved and defining for each group a standard unit of work which will measure the time consumed by each commodity. 

CONTACT 

Maintenance and movement have been described as the cost of maintaining distributive capacity and the cost of performing distributive work. The third group, contact cost, represents the cost of securing compensation from customers. It is the effort involved in maintaining the return flow of money, which must at least balance the outward flow of commodities if the business is to survive. This effort is naturally divided into the two phases of promotion and reimbursement. Reimbursement operations include all the steps involved in obtaining and receiving payment from the customer. As soon as a commodity is sold at a certain price, that price sets the task for reimbursement effort. Reimbursement is, therefore, the one group of costs in which sales in dollars is a direct measure of commodity responsibility for cost.

That part of sales price which represents the cost of goods to the dealer must be passed back to his source of supply in order to replenish his inventory. Therefore in securing compensation for that part of sale price the merchant is really acting as a collection agent for the concern which supplies him. The remaining part of sales price, the merchant's gross margin on the sale, is the portion in which he has a direct interest, since he is permitted to retain it to cover his own operating expenses and profit. It is that portion which is always tending to shrink away under the pressure of competition. It is to the maintenance of the element which represents compensation for his own efforts that all the activities known as promotion are directed. 

A few illustrations will suffice to indicate how completely promotional efforts are devoted to the extension of gross margin. One aim of promotion is to increase sales volume. The purpose of increased sales volume from the standpoint of business policy is that it constitutes an effort to provide a greater amount of gross profit against the fixed operating costs of the business. Another type of promotion is that which attempts to convince the customer of the high integrity, fairness, or ability to provide good values pertaining to the business

institution, the aim being to make the customer willing to pay prices somewhat above the competitive level. Staple commodities are frequently sold at prices which the business manager realizes will not permit them to pay their way. There is always the hope, in such cases, that it will be possible to sell items of less frequent demand at prices which bring in an adequate gross margin for the business as a whole.

Both forms of contact cost present a special problem in allocation, since the results sought are financial in character. Reimbursement is allocated to commodities on the basis of percentage of sales volume and promotion on the basis of percentage of total gross margin. Such allocation might be objectionable if the chief aim of distribution costing was the setting of prices. It is hard to conceive any other basis of allocation, however, since these activities are directly constituted by the effort to include an adequate gross profit in the price paid by the customer, and the effort to collect money from him on the basis of the stated price. Furthermore, the objection to the use of factors influenced by price does not hold, since distribution cost is the cost of disposing of commodities and can not, therefore, be determined until the sale has been completed. 

The expense items to be included in reimbursement cost would begin with bad-debt losses and direct cost of credit and collections. 

Further cost of credit reimbursement would include that part of bookkeeping cost which is involved in the accounts-receivable records and interest on the money tied up in accounts. Cash reimbursement also entails certain costs which can sometimes be shown separately, such as the cost of the cashier's department. Cash discounts taken by customers would usually be most conveniently and equitably considered as reimbursement cost. 

The most typical expense item under the promotion group would be the cost of institutional advertising. The promotional efforts of salesmen, as opposed to mere routine order taking, placed in the movement group, naturally fall there also. Where rent is paid for a prestige site which is considerably in excess of the rent actually required to secure adequate storage, display, and work space, it is apparent that such excess expenditure is promotional in character. 

The cumulative quantity discounts paid in case the customer's purchases reach a specified volume should be considered as a promotional expenditure.

Although it is not usual to think of promotion in connection with buying, there is a portion of buying expense which serves the same purpose that of extending or protecting gross margin. As in the case of selling, a considerable part of buying is routine in character, and as such is grouped under movement. Another part of buying, which may be called selective buying, is of a very different character. 

In selective buying the merchant may be selecting new products for his line, always with an eye to picking those which are likely to prove a new source of profit without injuring the showing of lines already established. Again, he may be expending buying effort in attempting to secure price concessions or favorable discounts on commodities purchased. Both types of buying activity are clearly directed toward the expansion of gross margin and can be equitably allocated to commodities on the basis of percentage of total gross margin.

If gross margin is to be used as the basis of allocation for an important group of costs, it must be recognized that this margin would sometimes be a gross loss rather than a gross profit. The manner of handling this problem would depend on the manager's reason for accepting the gross loss on the commodity. If the commodity were sold at a loss in order to clear it out, the gross margin should be considered as zero. If, on the other hand, it was sold at a loss in order to promote the sale of other commodities sold at a profit, it should receive a credit, which would add to the promotional cost to be distributed over other commodities.

SPECIAL QUESTIONS 

The effort thus far has been to sketch the main outlines of an approach to the problem of allocating distribution costs. It is inevitable that many minor questions raised by this general scheme should remain untouched in so brief an outline. There are a few points, however, which require some further explanation. Administrative expenses are to be placed in cost groups according to the activities which they promote. Where it is not possible to assign an administrative outlay as a whole to a group, it should be divided on the most equitable basis possible between the several groups. Supervision, personnel work, and employee benefits may be divided between groups on the basis of the man hours of labor used in each activity. 

Administrative costs of the financial type should be looked upon as part of the cost of securing operating funds and should be placed in groups on the basis of the amount of other expenses in each group. 

The cost of idle capacity in manufacturing is charged directly to profit and loss in some cases, where a system of standard costs of production is used. Such a method presents some difficulty for the analyst who wants to study distribution costs and determine net profits by commodities. The fairest way to distribute a cost which has been charged directly to profit and loss is to permit it to diminish each dollar of gross margin in equal measure. This amounts to the same thing, as far as the commodity is concerned, as treating the item as an expense and charging it to commodities on the basis of percentage of total gross profit, as was suggested for distributing promotional costs. Where the cost of idle capacity remains as an undistributed overhead in factory costs, this burden might be properly combined with promotion costs for purposes of allocation. Gross margin, as a basis of allocation, does not penalize any commodity because of a special characteristic. This method, therefore, accords well with the theory of standard costs, namely, to avoid penalizing commodities with costs for which they are not directly responsible. 

Since such a cost must be met from current gross earnings, it is necessary to allow it to diminish the gross profit on commodities in the way described.

Interest on investment has been listed as a part of cost. At the same time it has been pointed out that what is sought is merely a division of profits into a standard minimum return and the extra earnings known as pure profits. It is necessary to do this in order to fix the earning responsibility of various operations and commodities. It is not fair to think of warehouse labor as a cost and of the privilege of using the warehouse itself if it happens to be owned, as adding no cost to the commodity. In the same way equitable

comparisons by commodities and for firms with different types of capital_structure can not be made unless interest is included as a cost. Interest on borrowed capital would, of course, be charged also, so that cost of doing business, under the present conception, would include interest on total investment. Including interest in cost is not equivalent to making this portion of profits a first claim on the consuming public, as has been claimed by some critics, since the business manager has no real assurance that his income will cover any part of his operating costs. It merely sets a mark by which he may measure results against a minimum expectancy, which is particularly important in dealing with the individual commodity. 

The arbitrary rate of 6 per cent has been used for interest on capital owned, although it is recognized that there would be a considerable variation in the rate which different buisness enterprises would have to pay on loans. Where a more equitable rate can be established on the basis of comparison with loans actually made by the company, the adjusted rate should be used. If a considerable amount of money has been borrowed on different notes, there will probably be some difference in the interest rates. If all loans are essential to the conduct of the business, the sums of interest paid on various notes should be lumped and all assets charged with the average rate, on the theory that the same earning expectancy pertains to each dollar invested.

Another question might be raised as to the possibility of charging interest on the same property in two different establishments so that the total cost of distribution from producer to consumer would be inflated. For example, when merchandise is sold to the retailer on credit, the value of goods is listed as an asset by both the retailer and the wholesaler, in the one case as inventory and in the other as accounts receivable. What the retailer owes on the merchandise, however, is set off against the inventory asset in the form of accounts payable, thus reducing net worth. The interest charge would, therefore, appear only in the accounts of the wholesaler. Thus in each establishment, interest on total investment would have to be spread over the slightly larger figure of total assets. This device has been thought sufficiently accurate in present studies, although in the future it may become desirable to relieve from interest the specific assets covered by liabilities and to spread interest only on that part of assets which represents total investment. It will be apparent that such a procedure would somewhat reduce maintenance cost and increase contact cost in equal measure.

SUMMARY OF METHOD 

The principles of procedure here outlined may be summarized as follows: 

1. Determine sales volume, number of items sold, gross margin, and average inventory by individual commodities from the records of the company. 

2. From special studies determine the space occupied by each commodity and the average time consumed in various handling operations. 

3. In addition to expenses which represent cash outlay, include an estimated rent or equivalent expenses if the building is owned by the proprietor, estimated salary for the proprietor if no formal salary is taken, and interest on capital owned.

4. Divide expenses into as many groups as accuracy seems to require, making the groups as homogeneous as possible from the functional standpoint and preserving the basic classifications of maintenance, movement, and contact. 

5. Apportion the administrative costs among the several functional groups on the basis of an equitable measure of the service of administration to each activity. 

6. In allocating costs to commodities, begin with the direct allocation of all costs which can be conceived as direct charges. 

7. If the business is departmentized, with a clear separation of sales and expenses by departments, work out a separate set of functional groups for each department before attempting to cost commodities. 

8. Allocate expenses which can not be handled as direct charges by apportioning each functional group on the basis of an appropriate factor.

MECHANICS OF OPERATION 

The analysis contained in this report is based on sales and expense records for one calendar year in the house studied. A brief description of the various steps in this analytical procedure may be of interest to wholesale executives or accountants wishing to make similar studies in their own establishments. 

The types of information taken directly from existing records included data on sales, purchases, inventories, and expenses. The data which had to be gathered by direct investigation or observation included functional breakdown of sales and wages, allocation of space to commodity groups, and establishment of standard handling unit equivalents for types of physical units found under the different commodity groups.

Sales data were drawn directly from the file copies of the wholesalers' invoices to customers. It was necessary to have this information taken off in a very elastic form in order to permit it to be shown in a variety of ways, particularly for commodity classes, customer types, and sections of territory. The method used was the punchcard system of tabulation. Öne tabulating card was punched for each line on an invoice. The sales amount punched on this card represented the sale of a single commodity to a single customer occurring in the given transaction.

Fortunately for the purposes of the survey, the wholesaler's invoice to his customers carried the cost price as well as the selling price Gross margins shown in the tabulation for commodity classes were derived by subtracting the total cost as shown on all cards for that commodity from the total sales price as shown on the same cards.

Sales invoices in the house studied carried a great deal more information than is usually found in such transactions. All of these data were transferred to the punch cards. The information given included. designation of the customer as to type and location, a number for the identification of the invoice, the month of sale, method of sale, means of transportation, credit arrangements, f. o. b. point, class of shipment, a code number identifying the commodity, quantity sold, and the cost and selling value.

CODES USED ON TABULATING CARDS 

The accompanying chart indicates how all of this information was laid out on the punch card and indicates for some of the simpler items the nature of the code used. For example, in the column where shipment class was designated, number 1 was punched out if shipment was made to stock, number 2 if the shipment was a back order obtained especially from the factory, and number 3 if the shipment was made direct from the factory to the customer. One further column was punched indicating the type of card, this column being for the sole purpose of satisfying certain needs of mechanical tabulation. The zero was punched where the card represented a single item on an invoice carrying two or more items, number 1 where the card represented the only item on a single item invoice, and number 2 if the card represented a total for an invoice carrying two or more items. The commodity was designated only on the cards with 1 or zero punched, since the other cards represented compilations of more than one class of commodity. The tabulations showing commodity groups were, therefore, tabulated from the former. All of the tabulations involving size of order, on the other hand, were made from the two types of total cards-those with 1 or 2 punched.

The customer column of the card carried four facts about each customer; type, county and city of location, and identification within the city. This code naturally did not apply to any other wholesaler, since the counties and cities involved were those of the particular trade territory. It is very simple, however, to make up such a card for any wholesaler, given a list of his customers and their locations. 

If the wholesaler serves less than 100 counties, as in the present instance, two places on the card can be used for identifying the counties in the trade territory. Cities can ordinarily be identified by adding an additional number. For example, we may take the hypothetical case of a wholesaler who assigns number 11 to Washington County. The first city in that county alphabetically may be Arlington. Arlington might, therefore, be given a number 111, meaning that it was the first town in the eleventh county served. With locations fixed by such a system, further numbers may be assigned to identify the customer within the town and county. John Smith for example, might receive the number "11123." This would mean that Mr. Smith was the twenty-third customer in Arlington, Washington County.

As shown on the chart, a separate number was used to identify the customer type.

Analysis of invoices on merchandise received in the house from manufacturers was made on a similar punch-card basis. The same commodity code was used as in the case of sales to the wholesaler's customers, and the two sets of cards were in other ways somewhat similar. Since the house studied was affiliated with one of the national manufacturers as an authorized wholesaler, a great many of the purchases were made from that single source. Shipping points varied, however, since the manufacturer sent goods to the wholesale house studied from nine separate plants. All of these shipping points were separately recorded on the tabulating card. Another column was one used to indicate whether transportation charges were prepaid or collect.

A third set of tabulating cards was used for the analysis of inventories. Those cards carried inventory value and quantity for both the beginning and ending of the year studied. The commodity code used was the same as in analyzing purchase and sales invoices. The only tabulation drawn from the inventory cards was one used in finding the average inventory for the year on each commodity. However, the ease with which cards could be sorted and brought into proper groupings and classifications justified the punch-card method, even for that one tabulation.

The average-inventory-investment figure which was used as a basis for allocating the investment group of functional costs was a

simple average of the beginning and closing inventories for the year studied derived from the tabulation described. 

CORRECTIONS TO TABULATIONS 

Figures on sales had to be corrected for several factors in order to prepare them for the cost analysis. Returns and allowances had to be subtracted out of gross sales to yield the net amount which could actually be set down as income from sales. Consignment goods had to be measured separately since there is no inventory investment in goods so handled, although the merchandise must be given storage space just as if it were owned outright by the wholesaler and in every other way is responsible for costs in the same measure as other types of commodities. Since this is a question of agreement between manufacturer and wholesaler, the information does not appear on the sales invoice. A manager of the wholesale house was asked to designate items handled on a consignment basis, and all merchandise so designated was segregated. 

Another factor for which adjustment had to be made was that of drop shipments. From the cost standpoint, drop shipments differ from other goods in that the merchandise never actually passes though the wholesale house. Investment charges do apply, however, since the merchant can be said to be investing in consignment goods from the time they are billed to him by the manufacturer until they are received by the customer. In other words, he carries the inventory investment in drop shipments during the short period while they are in transit. All of the ordinary costs of routine selling and clerical work apply to drop shipments on the same basis as they apply to other commodity designations. The most important distinction is in the fact that drop shipments may be relieved from all charges for physical handling as far as the costs of the wholesaler are concerned. It was very easy to segregate drop shipments on the basis of the information available. The percentage of direct-fromfactory shipments was determined for each class of commodity and handling charges for that commodity were decreased in due proportion. A drop shipment, however, was likely to carry a heavier checking charge because of the small size of the units frequently found in this type of transaction.

It was possible to accept the expense records of the house very largely as they stood. The books had been closed each year under the supervision of a firm of certified public accountants. All of the ordinary schedules of expenses were carried. It was relatively easy to determine the interest on investment which had to be added to the total expenses of the concern for the purposes of this analysis. 

There were no problems of establishing a proper basis of compensation for rent and manager's salary; the business was conducted in a building which the firm did not own and for which a regular commercial rent was paid, and the manager was an employee receiving a regular salary. The records used in handling the expenses of the concern were its regular expense ledgers, with occasional journal references to determine the reason for which a particular expenditure was made. No punch cards were used in this process, since the tabulations desired were small in number and simple in character. 

ALLOCATION OF COSTS TO EXPENSE GROUPS 

The greatest problem in handling expenses was in making the functional breakdown of salaries and wages where the same employees

had two or more functions to perform. This breakdown was made by a comparison of the manager's estimates and time study and observation. Where estimates were used, the basis of the estimate is set forth in Table 1, page 51. Observation of the activities of employees in the warehouse and office was used to check these estimates at points where it was felt that the manager might not be in a position to make an accurate approximation.

In most large warehouses the assignment of salaries and wages to functional cost groups is not difficult except in the case of the higher executives. In such establishments all labor is of a clearly marked character, whether physical or clerical, and can not ordinarily be assigned to cost groups without an analysis of the activities of individual employees. In this survey, however, some clerks who were engaged in processes incident to order filling also gave some of their time to the accounts-receivable ledgers. These two activities belong, respectively, under the checking and reimbursement cost groups. 

Each employee's wage and each item of expense was considered separately and assigned to the proper cost group if the total item. could be shown to apply to a single function. Where the wage or expense item clearly covered two or more functions, the manager's estimate plus direct observation by the field staff making the study was the means by which the allocation was made. 

The allocation of warehouse space to commodities was based on actual physical measurement. The measurement was made at a time when the proportions of space devoted to various commodity classes was thought to be representative. This measurement was taken only once, however, and may not have been entirely representative for some commodities. Measurement at the end of each quarter, or at least at the time of each annual inventory, would be a more satisfactory basis for assuming cost of storage to commodities. 

Another basis which can be used with even greater facility is that of charging each commodity with the space actually reserved for it in the warehouse. In some warehouses definite space has been allocated to each commodity handled on the basis of a study of average and maximum requirements. Such an assignment of space is probably the fairest basis for allocating storage cost, since maximum requirements really determine total rent paid, and the commodity should be charged with storage costs on the basis of its maximum requirement rather than the average amount on hand. 

However, some correction for accidental variations in stock on hand for particular items was obtained under the method used. 

Actual space measurements were accepted as found only for the broader commodity groups. There was a very clear-cut assignment of floors to the uses of related commodities in the house surveyed. 

The commodities found on a given floor were made to bear the space cost for that floor, even though the floor might not be full at the time the physical measurement was made. Within the commodity group assignment of space cost to the commodity classes was made on the basis of their percentage of average inventory investment in the total inventory investment for the group. 

The establishment of standard-handling-unit equivalents for commodity groups was one of the most difficult steps in the study. It was felt that bulk and weight, which are ordinarily resorted to as a basis for this purpose, were not adequate measures in a trade handling

such diverse items as are found in a house wholesaling electrical goods. 

The physical character of items sold diverges in many ways. Some of the larger conduit sections were kept in a horizontal rack reaching nearly across one end of the warehouse. While such items are something of a problem because of the labor involved in taking them out of this rack and loading them properly on the truck, they have the advantage of not requiring packaging or careful handling. Wire and cable, when sold in lengths less than a full spool, require the extra operation of unrolling from one spool to another, measuring, and cutting. Some small items like fuses and wiring devices present little problem in the way of weight, bulk, or special handling operations except for careful packaging. This is true, particularly. with some types of goods which are highly fragile. 

The simplest item from the standpoint of physical handling is that which comes in and goes out in the same package, is light enough to permit one man to move it with ease, and is rectangular and compact in shape so that many such units can be stored in a truck with little loss of space. It is to that general type of physical unit that the term "Standard handling unit" has been applied. Where a commodity was made up entirely of standard handling units, that commodity could be assigned a handling charge based on the quantity figure applying on the invoice. With some very small items which are repacked for shipment without any particular difficulty in the packing operation, the number of sales would more nearly correspond to the concept of standard handling units. 

Whatever the operations undertaken in connection with a physical package or unit, their time-consuming character can be related to the simplest type of unit in the establishment by time study and observation. Even though this relation should be established on no more substantial basis than careful estimate, it would perhaps be more practicable than any attempt to balance such diverse items as bulk, weight, shape, and space handling requirements into a single statistical index. For example, the contrast between a package of fuses sent out as received and an item of 500 feet of rubber-covered cable is so great that it can be measured only directly. Such direct measurement consists essentially of time study to determine the relative length of time consumed in handling the items. Time study of handling operations was undertaken in another study of electricalgoods establishments. In that case, however, an effort was made to establish an actual breakdown of employees' time between all of the various departments of merchandise, even though employees might each individually be working on all of these groups simultaneously. 

This problem is simplified greatly by the concept of a standard handling unit. On this basis time study can be directed to determining the time-consuming character of distinct types of physical units. Commodity groups in which the physical unit is similar may be costed on the same basis, even though the merchandise may be utterly unlike in use or other characteristics. Ordinarily only three or four definite types of physical units are found in an establishment. 

Such definite types are somewhat more numerous in a wholesale electrical establishment, but they are not nearly so numerous as items of merchandise or even as the major merchandise classifications for which statistics are presented in the report.
