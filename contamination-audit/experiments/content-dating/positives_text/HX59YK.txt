CHAPTER I 

OR generations the Craine family had been overlords of the town of Fairview. They owned the best of the rich Georgia river bottom land for miles around. They owned the peach orchards, or held mortgages on the orchards of those who had been daring enough to defy the Fairview tradition of Craine suzerainty. The cotton mills were Craine owned and Craine operated. The front rank pew in the Methodist church was a Craine right by heredity. Old man John 

Craine frowned on dancing. Nobody danced. . in Fairview, at least. It was tacitly understood that the Craine women held undisputed social leadership and undisputed virtue. Therefore, when young Harvey Craine's mother followed his father to the family burying ground in the cemetery on the hilltop, and Harvey himself broke away from the tradition and migrated to New York, a sort of chill hush fell over a Craine family gathering when his name [I]

was mentioned. When the rumor began to float about the town . . . in cautious whispers . that he had identified himself with the theater; worse, was writing pieces for actors to speak on the stage, some of the lesser members of the clan began to mention him in the hushed tones employed in speaking of the dear departed.

Subsequent reports of the incredible sums of money earned for Harvey by these same pieces written for the theater served to soften somewhat this attitude. One of the Craines, acting as a sort of emissary for the family, even went so far as to visit the theater in New York where one of Harvey's plays was running, and on his return reported that, "it wasn't as bad as it might have been." 

And when, after Harvey's marriage to a motion picture actress and their separation, he returned to Fairview, he was treated as one who has seen the error of his ways and returned to the fold.

The house that had been his father's stood on the edge of town, between two knuckles of higher ground thrust out from the hillsides, enclosing the structure like bent and spatulate fingers. [2]

Here there was beauty. The white house, gable end to the road, had little cut-under porches on either corner, pointed arches of green blinds over each roadward window-like delicate eyebrows raised in quizzical surprise. Magnolias sprang from the bases of the enclosing slopes and made an arch of greenish yellow over a strip of marshy land that lay along the back of the property. 

There Harvey settled down. He added a flagged terrace that faced the green slopes of the country club across the road. In the spring it would be wrapped in white clouds of magnolias, with wild azealas from the nearby woods he had set out burning like fire beneath the ashes of the gray hanging moss. In the mornings he worked away at his new play in the study. Afternoons he worked in the garden he was creating. 

He was busy with the latter when the slamming of the gate notified him of a visitor. It was 

Judith Harbin, her arms full of pale-blue larkspur. She had a round, happy face, innocent of makeup, and a round healthy body. Not that 

Judith was fat. Merely endowed with curves pleasing to the eye. She had nice, dark hair and nice, dark eyes. In fact, everything about her [3]

was nice, Harvey reflected as he dropped his trowel into the bulb bed and rose . . . nice and wholesome.

She called to him as she came up the flagged walk: "Oh, Harvey. Am I late?" "Right on time," he said. In the shelter of a fragrant spirea bush, laden with blossoms, he stopped her, took her in his arms and kissed her, in his favorite spot behind her right ear. "Gosh," he said, holding her off at arm's length. "I just can't get used to it. You're a miracle, an absolute miracle. They ought to erect a monument to you in the Courthouse Square along with the monument to the Confederate Soldiers and label it "A woman who keeps her appointments on time." "Well, I said four, and I meant four. Here ." she dropped the larkspur into his hands. "I brought you these. They'll look lovely in that tall white vase in the living-room." "You mean the crackle glaze Ming jar," Harvey said, shocked. "Why . why you can't put flowers in that, Judith." "Why not?" she wanted to know as they [4]

mounted the steps to the terrace. "It doesn't leak, does it?" "No," he admitted, "but it's Ming porcelain. 

A museum piece. The most precious thing I've got. I purchased it in New York and it's crossed the continent with me four times." "That won't keep it from holding flowers," she said. Then she laughed a little indulgently. "I know how impractical you writers are, but you must really see how absurd it is not to put flowers in it just because it's Ming or something. We'll let Nude do it. Aren't you going to offer me any tea. I'm simply famished." "I'll have Nude bring it," he said, springing up from the chair alongside hers into which he had dropped. Going to the doorway he called and a moment later an enormously fat Negro woman waddled out onto the porch with a tray. 

She was an old woman without a trace of the white blood that is so often found in the South, her face seamed by myriads of tiny wrinkles, her skin holding the dull, leathery hue and look that is common in the aged of the full-blooded Negro. "Thank you, Nude," Judith said. Then she took the flowers that Harvey had laid on the table. up [5]

"Will you put these flowers in the white jar in the living-room; the jar on the small table between the windows?"

I don't think I ought to come here any more. There's certain to be a lot of gossip. Harvey Craine again." "Nonsense," he said, taking the cup of tea she poured for him. "Nude is a perfect chaperon.

A cynical bystander, one who knew Judith less than Harvey (or perhaps more) might have construed the gesture as one of deliberate intent. In another woman Harvey might have suspected the intent, but in guileless Judith, that thought was out of the question. 

With her, the momentary exposure only tended to increase his satisfaction in her entirely feminine curves, and to stimulate a tender gratitude toward her for the comfort and relaxation which life with her typified. He realized now that in this woman lay an antidote for the boyish slimness and breath-taking vitality of Holly. 

Then Judith went on: [9]

"It's darling of you to defend her. You would do a thing like that after all the things she did to you." "It's not that I'm defending her," he said. "She'd be amazed at the thought that she needed defense."

He stared out across the road to where the shirt-sleeved players made sharp spots of color against the vivid green of the golf course, his dark hair in a tumbled lock on his forehead above eyes gray and black irised. His was a face turned so brown by the Georgia sun that the eyes seemed a lighter gray than they actually were; he had big, hard cheek bones, a firm, wide mouth, a hard jaw, and his body was long and lean and hard, to match the face. Yet, inside, Harvey wasn't hard. Instead, there was something about him which made the thoughtful observer aware of a love of life. It was, possibly, a quality of vividness which illuminated him, a quality of voice that caught one up with him into the realm of his own winged thought. "Holly's idea of life," he went on after a moment, "is to spend her waking hours on studio lots, in cafés, in hotel dining rooms, dashing madly [ 10 ]

here and there, walking strips of red carpet under the glare of spotlights at the première of new pictures, surrounded by an atmosphere of press agents, catty rivals, studio gossip and motion picture producers who haven't yet learned to speak English." "But it wasn't long before I began to get the feeling of a lost soul in an inferno of strange beds, and newly decorated and unlived in living-rooms.

"She doesn't even know the meaning of the word . . of a place to be lived in and loved, a place of gardens and books and dogs and crackling fires . . . flames that crackle and leap and burn crackle and leap and burn in people's hearts.

CHAPTER II 

ARVEY watched as the trio marched up the flagged path toward the house. Holly!

Holly, the electric, the tempestuous, dropping into his quiet existence out of a blue sky. And there was nothing he could do to halt her; to fend her off. He hurried up to the terrace to where Judith was watching with a curious expression on her face. "What does it mean, Harvey?" she asked.

There was a wary light in her eyes, an expression of grim determination upon her usually placid face and a curious strained intensity in her manner that was foreign to her. Almost as if the little scene she had witnessed below at the gate had touched a hidden alarm bell somewhere in her. "It means she's coming here," he groaned helplessly. Judith demanded. [17]

"If you ask me," Judith said a trifle tartly, "I think it's quite plain. She's evidently coming here to plead with you to take her back." "Well, it won't do her any good," Harvey said firmly. "Your duty lies clear, my dear," Judith told him. "There's only one thing you can do in justice to me, in justice to yourself. If this woman insists upon swooping down upon you like this, that is no reason why you should endanger your reputation and mine by playing host to her." "But what can I do?" Harvey asked helplessly. "There's only one honorable thing to do under the circumstances. Since she has already taken possession of your home you can't very well put her out. But that is no reason why you need stay under the same roof with her. You must go to the hotel." "The hotel?" Harvey said vaguely. "Oh! Well yes. Perhaps I had better go to the hotel." "That's my sweet darling," Judith said, relief [18]

creeping into her voice. "We won't let this horrid woman interfere with what we want to do, will we, dear? We won't let her ruin our love?" "No, no, of course not," Harvey said. ARVEY was furious as he went up the stairs to his room; furious not alone at the wanton destruction of his Ming porcelain jar, but also at the unexpected return of Holly into his life, diverting its calm flow into the turbulent channel into which he now flowed.

If she had wanted a pass through the Pearly Gates, she would have grabbed up the phone after breakfast and begun calling up people she knew who knew someone who knew St. Peter.

Unseen by them her hand went out behind her and turned the key in the lock; took it out. 

And then, before a movement could be made by the startled men, or a protest uttered, she had whipped open the door; stepped outside, closed and locked it behind her . . and stood alone at the top of the little flight of steps facing the maddened mob below. [286]

HER CHAPTER XXIII 

ER Swift movement brought a startled exclamation from the men. Harvey hurled himself savagely against the door. But it was useless. It was locked and the key was on the outside. "She'll be killed," he cried hopelessly.

He sprang to the window, tugged fiercely at the iron bars. But he could not move them. Outside, just a few yards away, at the head of the steps, she stood, her slim figure seeming possessed of a sudden throbbing suspense as she faced the crowd. She had turned a trifle, so that in the bright afternoon sun the almost flowerlike whiteness of her face was apparent to him. 

The pallor of her face under the little green cap and the clearness of her eyes held him speechless for a moment. 

And under the magic of the thing that she had done, the pale candlelight of reason waned, lost in the illumination of his love for her, submerged in the flood of love, that, thwarted of [287]

late, now surged over him, all its fervor doubly intensified. 

The winter light faded suddenly from his eyes; they were suddenly lit with a new illumination; the lens of suspicion crashed into a thousand fragments. He realized now what he had known all these long hours since he had turned and left her alone in the office with Leslie; that no matter what his wife had done, no matter how much she harmed him, humiliated him, he loved her and would always love her. And the knowledge brought him such a deep inner wonder and content that the question as to whether she was or was not guilty with Leslie didn't seem to matter at all. In that breathless zero moment it swept over Harvey that it was his own life out there also. For the moment nothing mattered except the one thing; that she was out there alone . . in danger. and that he could do nothing to save her. 

He whirled away from the window. Started for the other door of the office. 

Old John caught him by the shoulder. "Where are you going, Harvey?" he demanded. "Out the back door and around the mill," came [ 288]

the frantic reply. "I've got to do something to get her away from there. Oh, why did she do it?" "You'd never get through the crowd," Old 

John told him, "and you'd only start something.

She's got a chance, out there by herself." 

Harvey recognized the force of what the old man said, and sank down in a chair helplessly. 

Old John threw an arm about his shoulder. "Boy," he said, "she didn't want me to tell you the truth about that picture. But you've got a right to know now. . . "And he went on talking in low rapid tones.

Silence fell over the muttering crowd like a pall as they saw Holly there, her green suit clearly outlined against the dark panels of the door.

A little wordless prayer went up from her as she stared out at that menacing crowd from which death might come at any moment. She had counted on that momentary silence. In it, she knew, she must win or lose. And the success or failure of her plan lay balanced upon a point of needle sharpness. If she could capture their at- [289]

tention, then she might win. If she failed . . . her mind refused to face the prospect. 

A voice in the crowd at the foot of the steps said almost inaudibly: "It's Harvey Craine's wife." "Yes," she said, and her voice was very clear for one who stood with one foot across the sable threshold of death, but it carried strongly out across the mob. "I'm Harvey Craine's wife. 

Holly Branch. You've seen me in the movies, most of you."

Then with a sudden dramatic gesture, arresting in its tensity, her hand shot aloft, holding up for them to see the key to the door behind her. "Look," she cried.

The word, the movement that followed, was as sudden, as unexpected as a striking snake. Every eye in the crowd followed the movement of her hand... saw the key slip into the neck of her dress. "The only way you can get into the mill," she said clearly, "is through this door. And to get through it you've got to thrust me aside. . . trample me underfoot to get the key." "Give her the works," a hoarse voice in the [290]

throng said, and a stone crashed against the wall at her side. 

Holly did not flinch. But a grayness crept over her face and her imagination painted a swift, vivid picture she shuddered. Whatever a person's philosophy of immortality, the near presence of death remains a shock. Then her voice rang out again, clear as a bell and as sonorous, carrying no hint of the terror that lurked behind it. "Take care," she cried, and the eyes of the crowd followed her swinging, pointing finger that indicated the windows on either side of her. "Behind those bars are motion-picture cameras. 

Every move that you make every gesture every word you utter is recorded forever on a strip of film . . . proof. . . proof in court proof that no judge or jury dare refuse to recognize." She swept off the little green hat; ran her fingers through the tangle of curls with a gesture that many of them had seen her use time and time again in her pictures. "Come on... knock me aside . . . trample on me. " she cried, her words unloosed in [291]

little gasping squadrons, yet never losing that bell-like note of clearness. "Take the key . . . it is here... inside my dress . . . and the man who knocks me down will have his act recorded on film for the world to see. . . "She touched the front of her dress. "The key is here . . come and take it . . . and let the cameras record the changing of a man to a beast. . . . . let the women and children of Fairview . . . of Georgia . . . of the United States . . . see one of you rip my dress apart and take that key."

There was an uneasy murmur from the crowd; a whispering, rustling undertone, like the sound of wind under dried leaves. There were cameras behind those barred windows. They could see them; a camera on either side of the door, with a man turning the crank. And in the face of that, each of them hesitated to be the first to make a move. 

Holly gave them no chance to recover. "And while you're deciding what to do... tell me what do you want?" she flung at them. "We wanta blow up the place," someone shouted.

A cry of assent went up to the sky from a hun- 292]

dred and fifty throats . . . a cry that had close kinship with the joyous, merciless "au-rr-ruh" of a wolf pack about to make the kill. The sound lapped about her like some soft monster, sucking her strength, her courage. She fought desperately to keep back the panic that rose in her like a tide at the sound. "Why?" she cried. "Come on, lady," someone said. “We ain't got no time for no lectures. We got work to do." "You said it," came a voice from the crowd.

The low rumbling continued. "Why?" she insisted stubbornly. "I'll tell you why," came from a little man in the forefront of the crowd . . . a man with lips of hate and eyes that dreamed of old bloody instincts. "We want to get the old man." "Why?" she repeated again. "Say, what is this?" someone asked impatiently. "The third degree?"

A power terrifying in its strength was pressing upon Holly, locking her muscles in a tension. It impelled her to press her body against the wall behind her, to attempt to crawl beneath the hard, unyielding concrete of the steps. The black vi- [293]

bration of anger was in the air. Unmistakably she felt it seething, as anyone may feel it who is receptive to impressions. But she continued to face them, her chin at a gravely defiant angle. "Give me just one good reason why you want to blow up the mill," Holly's voice rang out clear and true. "Just one good reason why you want to do this, and I'll let you in. What's more, I'll help you."

A grumble ran through the crowd, a low, menacing sound like a volcano in action. Fragments of sentences came to her through rifts in the sound. "She's nuts. . . it's a trap. . . one good reason? Jesus, we got dozens . . . . it's a trap, I tell you. Aw, shut up. . . . Give her the works." "You're a bunch of four-flushers," yelled a black-haired girl in a plaid dress. Her eyes would have been more appropriate to the zoo than to a woman. She pushed her way to the front of the crowd, and then turned and faced it. "I ain't afraid to tell her, I ain't afraid of Holly Branch or any other God-damned man or woman on earth. I'll tell her what reasons we got. I'll call her bluff." [294]

She turned to Holly, her voice ringing with the passion of a crusader. "I'll tell you what reason we got. We want to get Old John because he's been piling up money while we've been sweatin' our lives away making it for him. . . and we wanter blow up the mill because it's his, and he's in it." 

A murmur of assent ran through the crowd. "So!" Holly said.

She felt suddenly calm and sure of herself. 

Her voice rose in dramatic crescendos, never overplaying, never underplaying her points, pounding home with thrilling intensity the things that she knew she must make them understand. "And did you know that the tire fabric contract is the only thing that has saved the mill from closing down. ? Did you know that all the Craine money and all the Craine land had been pledged for the support of this mill, so you people could be kept working? And did you know that the bank was going to foreclose on the mortgage to-day because there wasn't enough money available to meet even the instalment due on the notes . . . and the reason there wasn't enough [295]

money available was because your wages hadn't been cut, because no man had been laid off, or let out; because the Craine Mills are the only organization in the state that has maintained its staff and wages intact. . . has maintained a sick benefit and accident fund for its employees, when all other factories were cutting expenses right and left . . . did you know that?” 

Her words were like tiny searing drops emerging from a vat of molten metal ... stinging.. burning. . . eating beneath the surface. . . into them she was throwing every atom of histrionic power that she possessed . using that magnificent, husky, throaty voice of hers like an organ. And under its compelling force a hushed expectancy had shut down upon the crowd like a tangible thing. "Did you know," she flung at them, "that if you strike, the mortgage will be foreclosed and you can save yourself the trouble of destroying the mill, for the Craine Mills will be bankrupt and you will all be out of jobs . . . out begging on the streets. . . your wives and children starving, until the Consolidated take the mills over and then you'll be glad enough to go back to work at [296]

half the wages, at any wages that Consolidated is willing to pay you, for hungry men do not argue what their pay is to be?" 

She paused for a moment. Her eyes flickered over the upturned faces of the crowd. A tiny thrill went through her. She was holding her audience. "You didn't know that this whole strike was a scheme of the Consolidated . . . all a scheme of the Consolidated . . . all a scheme to get the factories away from the Craines, did you? You didn't know you were being made the tools, to pull the chestnuts out of the fire for Consolidated, did you? You didn't know that you were the tools of a bunch of professional labor agitators bought by a rival concern, did you, and that if you carried out the thing they planned that you should, you would cut your own throat? Well, you know it now."

She halted abruptly. Her dramatic instinct... those thousand primitive instincts that made her genius what it was . . told her that she had gone far enough.. that anything more would be anti-climax. With a swift gesture she reached into the front [297]

of her dress, pulled out the key and unlocked the door. 

Then she turned back to the crowd. "Now," she said clearly, "come on in and blow up the place, if you still want to cut your own throats."

She hurled the key onto the ground in front of them and turning her back, strode through the door.. 

Harvey, white and strained-looking, was pressed against the bars of the nearest window. 

He rushed to the door as Holly entered and swept her into his arms. There were unashamed tears in his eyes. "Oh, darling," he cried brokenly. "Thank God you're safe." 

For a moment Holly said nothing. She felt cold and empty, as if she had suddenly become nothing but a piece of thin cloth through which life had been strained, leaving no residue. 

Then she came to with a little shock of alarm as he put her gently to one side and rushed to the half-opened door, reaching for the bolt. 

She grasped his arm with a cry of dismay. "Harvey, are you mad? Don't lock that door. [298]

You'll undo everything I did. Believe me, Harvey. They won't come in. I'll stake my life they won't come in. Only don't lock that door."

Harvey hesitated. Old John came forward, his eyes shining. "She's right, my boy. They won't come in. 

See," and he pointed toward the window. "They're already breaking up into little groups and some are starting home. Your wife has saved the Craine Mills, Harvey, and I propose a toast." 

The little frightened group gathered about her in awe and extended firm hands in friendship and grateful appreciation, while Old John opened the safe and brought forth some cherished old bottles of Bourbon and called for glasses. 

Sarah, red-eyed from weeping, took Holly silently in her arms.

The two camera men came forward, broad grins on their faces and cans of films under their arms. "Gee, Miss Branch, what a scene you put on,"

Jimmy Sanders said, his tanned face aglow. "We got it, every damn bit of it," he indicated the film. "I've shot you in some big scenes, Miss [299]

Branch, but this was the best scene I've ever seen you do. It was swell." "Thanks, Jimmy," she said, a trifle wearily. "I'd almost forgotten you were there. You say it was a swell . . ." for a moment she broke off and for a breathless instant stared at him with a strange light in her eyes. Then she gave a sudden, broken hysterical laugh, and her eyes were as wet as violets in time of rain. "A swell Ohhhhhhh!" scene. 

Her voice rose in delighted trills of ecstacy. "We've got it. Chris . . . Chris Mallory." She grasped her astonished director by the shoulders and shook him. "We've got it! Oh, don't look so utterly dumb." Her voice was suddenly edged with magic as her laugh rang out. "We've got the big scene for 'Still Waters,' you idiot. The big scene where Madge is standing before the bolted door between her lover and the men who've come to kill him. Don't you see? It's perfect!" "By George, you're right, Holly," he cried.

There swept into his face a look of intent and rapid calculation so intense that he was almost [300]

oblivious to the others about him. "It's perfect. 

And it didn't cost us a cent." "Sarah, come here, darling," Holly said, embracing the old woman. "Go home . . . go home at once, and start packing. We're going we're going back to Hollywood." 

Mallory shot a glance at Harvey. Harvey grinned sheepishly, and reached for the phone. 

Holly stopped a moment in consternation. "Nude," Harvey called into the phone, when he'd gotten his number. "Get someone to help you bring my wardrobe trunk from the attic. I'm going to California."
