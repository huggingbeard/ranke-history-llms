HE cowboy sprawled in the sand, one hand clutching spasmodically at the butt of his revolver, feet drumming against the ground. A loose spur chain tinkled against steel rowel and a tiny wave of alkali dust rose from around the body. On the back of the faded, blue shirt a dark spot appeared and spread rapidly. After a moment the convulsive movement ceased. "Skinner" Joslin stepped closer, smoking gun extended, eyes fixed on the stiffening fingers holding the six-shooter. His gaze took in the crimson smudge that had spread down the shirt to the cartridge belt, saw the dying man's struggles cease, and a wolfish snarl drew his lips still farther back from protruding snag teeth. 

He was called "Skinner," not because he skinned mules, but by virtue of his buck teeth, which always were exposed. At a distance he appeared to be perpetually smiling, but in fact he rarely smiled. Usually his pale eyes were dull and filmy. 

Now they gleamed with a hard, unnatural light. Like the man on the ground, Skinner Joslin was a cowboy.

WITH a steaming cup of coffee in his hand and a tin plate loaded with victuals placed in the sand at his elbow, Doctor Rodney W. Martin leaned back against a sand dune and watched the horsehunters' camp come to life. The horse wranglers came up driving the cavvy of saddlers. Big-hatted, bow-legged men, with wide chaps flapping, walked deliberately toward the horse herd. The mustangs, seeming to know what was wanted, headed in and moved about with the most amazing intelligence, aiding each other to escape the unerring loops that slithered and circled and dropped over their heads. 

The shouting of men, the milling of varicolored horses, sorrels and bays and claybanks and blacks and whites, the pungent cloud of dust, the constant swearing of the cook, and the sight of the nonchalant wranglers, sitting their saddles easily, undisturbed, smoking after-breakfast cigarettes, revived a flame in Rod Martin that had almost been extinguished by his grueling ride.

Rod Martin had always aspired to be a plainsman. 

Leather-fringed coat, a powder horn, a horse and a rifle these had been his first loves. Once, when thirteen, he had attempted to run away from his 

Boston home to go West, but they caught him.

Finally he settled down to his father's profession and eventually was graduated from a medical college. It

was then that his father agreed with him for the first time that he should go West. "Go out there and get somebody to practice on," said his father. "Bury your mistakes far enough away so that no one will hear of them and, when you have learned by actual hard knocks, come home and I will work you into my practice."

With this in view Martin was heading for Wild Cat town as his first location. A young interne who had settled in Jamarillo had assured him that that town was the nearest thing to the old West that he could find. So he had bought a horse and outfit and had loaded up his medical supplies and necessary belongings and had started for Wild Cat. 

A mirage had deceived him into making a detour, and so he had become lost. The day-and-night ride had taken all the romance out of his dreams and, when he rode into the horse camp that morning, he was ready to renounce his most long-standing ambition. 

Steaming hot coffee, the restful softness of the sand dune, the air which was soft and tremulous with early-morning light, and the stirring sights that greeted him, brought the boyish enthusiasm to the surface again. When a wise little buckskin headed straight into the herd and one of the cowboys missed his cast, Rod felt like cheering for the pony. He noted that the men were not saddling the mounts after they caught them, but merely cinched up a surcingle and put on a rope bridle. A dappled gray lowered its head between its legs, arched its back and began to crowhop stiff-legged while the rider clung to a buckstrap on the surcingle. It was all over in a moment, and the gray was obeying every pressure of the rider against body and neck and bit.
