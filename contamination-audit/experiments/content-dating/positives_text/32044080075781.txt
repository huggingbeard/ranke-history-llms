THE EMERGENT GENIE* Arnold Kramish The RAND Corporation, Santa Monica, California 

In his press conference of May 8, 1963, President 

Kennedy, speaking of the test ban, warned: "If we don't get it now, I would think perhaps the genie is out of the "1 bottle and we'll never get it back in again. No doubt the President was painfully aware that the genie, by which he meant proliferation of nations possessing nuclear weapons, has more than half of his body out of the bottle. 

The partial test ban treaty signed in Moscow on August 5, 1963, is, by itself, but a hindrance to the progress of the persistent genie.

Soviets, however, translated the mimeographed edition and made the translation conform to the format of the Princeton University edition. Thus, in the immediate postwar years, scientists in the Soviet Union possessed some items of information which the average American scientist did not have, unless of course he chose to check out the Soviet translation copy which reposed on the shelves of the Library of Congress. If there is any moral to this little tale, one is that ever since then more of the genie has always been out of the bottle than was usually realized.

The U.S., as other nations, is caught in a swift technological current that demands the use of new discoveries in many sectors of the economy and national defense.

And, as time went on, as we recognized that the Soviet Union, Britain and even France had progressed to certain significant stages in the development of nuclear weapons, we tended to look with scorn on the Model T technologies of the nuclear era and, with this narrow image of the other three nuclear powers, tended to be lenient in the declassification of information relating to our early bomb technologies. At least two other general histories were released which represented even more detailed guide posts to other nations than did the Smyth report. The inconsistency in policy which developed is evident. 

While our concern for the Nth country was a major reason for pressing the test ban negotiations, at the same time our information policies were such that they would have significantly advanced the state of the nuclear weapons art in any incipient Nth nation.

When first conceived, the atomic bomb was pictured to be something like an ordinary naval gun with a mass of fissionable material shot at great velocity against another mass. When separate, each of the pieces would be unable to sustain a chain reaction, but when in contact the total mass would be so great that it would become explosive. 

This is indeed a simple concept and it essentially describes the U-235 type of bomb which was exploded over Hiroshima.

But, while it may be easier to conceive, this type of bomb using the more easily obtainable material, plutonium, For had its unexpected complications for the bomb designer. 

Plutonium does have its own unique problems. example, it demands a new metallurgy; it is highly toxic and contains a melange of isotopic components which could be troublesome to the bomb designer. In detonating an atomic bomb, neutrons have to be provided at precisely the right moment to start the chain reaction. If this chain reaction is started a fraction of a millionth of a second before the design calls for it to start, a loss of efficiency is entailed. Thus, the design calls for the injection of neutrons into the system at precisely the right moment. Should spurious neutrons be injected into the system beforehand, the phenomenon of "predetonation" occurs and the bomb becomes less efficient. These stray neutrons can come from impurities in the bomb material, cosmic radiation, or from the isotope, plutonium-240. While a neutron is required to fission the major plutonium isotope, plutonium-239, the plutonium-240 isotope tends to fission in an uncontrollable manner by itself ("spontaneous fission"). In the act of fission, it releases a number of neutrons. If the amount of plutonium-240 is sufficiently high and there is a consequent high rate of spontaneous fission of that isotope, there will be a large neutron

background in the material and thus the probability of predetonation will be increased. It is this difficulty which makes the implosion process the most favored means of detonating plutonium, for in this process the effects of predetonation are diminished but not eliminated.

In the implosion configuration, a quantity of fissionable material is completely surrounded by segments of ordinary high explosives. These segments, or "lenses", are made to explode simultaneously by means of an appropriate detonation system. Before the implosion, the mass of fissionable material is not critical. That is, it will not sustain a chain reaction by itself. However, the effect of the implosion is to vastly increase the density of the fissionable material, packing its atoms closer together. This makes it more difficult for the generated neutrons to escape and thus the probability that the greater fraction of those atoms will fission within a very brief period of time is greatly increased. This, in simple terms, is what happens in the implosion type of atomic bomb.

Accordingly, bomb material should contain only minimal amounts of plutonium-240. This isotope tends to build up in relative quantity the longer the fuel is kept in a reactor. The requirement to minimize plutonium-240 tends to aggravate the economics of atomic power if one has a

dual program of producing weapons-grade plutonium and power. In order to obtain good grade plutonium such a reactor system would have to be refueled frequently But frequent refueling means significant added expense for the power obtained. One cannot have efficient bombs and low-cost power at the same time.

The Proceedings of the Second UN International Conference on the Peaceful Uses of Atomic Energy describe criticality experiments for plutonium metal and give the magnitude of quantities involved. Several other sources, including the officially released history of the Los Alamos project, describe in greater detail the theoretical and experimental difficulties encountered in developing the implosion mechanism and describe the general techniques for testing those mechanisms without the necessity of going through a full-scale nuclear test.

In this manner has the genie been handed to other nations on a plutonium platter. One may or may not mourn this, but at the minimum we must recognize that the situation is irrevocable. The Nth nation cannot, however, construct its bomb from information alone. Herman Kahn believes that in 1969 "...it may literally turn out that a Hottentot, an educated and technical Hottentot it is true, but still one who is a member of a relatively

primitive society, would be able to make bombs.

One cannot doubt the bomb-making ability of a Hottentot with an advanced technical education, but even the exceptional Hottentot would find himself frustrated in that desire when he returned to his native land. For one thing, the nation has to be able to afford the plutonium and bombmaking facilities. Certainly, any nation whose Gross 

National Product were below a certain minimum, somewhere in the lower billion dollar range, would find it exceedingly difficult to embark on such a program. It must also have the trained manpower and, more than that, must possess a certain technological maturity. One measure of this technological maturity would be the level it had maintained for a number of years on research and development expenditures related to the Gross National Product. 

Based upon what other nations have done, it appears that the "technological maturity level" necessary to embark on the independent nuclear weapons course lies somewhere around onehalf of 1%. Most of the nations of Europe qualify under this criteria, but certainly most of the other nations, in excess of a hundred and at various technological levels from the Hottentot upward, have far to go before they qualify.

This technological level, involving the ratio of research and development expenditures to Gross National Product implies that the nation possesses a certain minimal number of trained manpower and of manufacturing facilities for complex technological production, both of which are necessary for a nuclear weapons program. Of course, the basic resource, uranium is necessary. It is not hard to come by. On an average basis, it is as plentiful as zinc or lead in the earth's crust and a hundredfold more abundant than silver. Actually exploitable deposits of it are spotty, but the spots are numerous. It is found in Australia, France, Germany, Sweden, Italy, Portugal,

Brazil, the U.S., Canada, the U.S.S.R., et cetera, with new discoveries constantly being made. Thus, there is a plethora of uranium and a consequent lowering of prices. 

Present day atomic programs, including the weapons phases, are entirely too small to absorb the output of today's operating uranium mines. Consequently, if a nation is unfortunate enough not to possess uranium within its own borders, it can purchase that commodity from several sources on the world market. Even if it has its own domes tic sources, it may be more economical to import the material. 

Uranium possesses two major isotopic components. As we have indicated, it is exceedingly difficult to separate the fissionable component, U-235, from the rest. However,

by causing the unseparated U-235 to fission in an appropriately designed reactor system, it is possible to convert the more abundant isotopic component, U-238, into plutonium.

This plutonium can then be separated out chemically. All of these processes are described with detailed specifications in the open literature.

Nuclear reactors which produce power and/or plutonium can be fueled by uranium, appropriately fabricated from its natural state, or by enriched uranium. Enriched uranium is that which contains greater amounts of uranium-235 than the natural substances. The diffusion plants, in which uranium is enriched in the United States, the U.K. and the U.S.S. R., are monstrous arrays of buildings whose innards contain equipment which represents a very advanced technological development and manufacturing ability. France is also building such a plant and she is learning quite painfully, in terms of escalating costs many fold the original estimates, what the construction of this type of plant involves. She

While France is struggling to build her own diffusion plant, England has found that she now has all the enriched uranium she needs and has cut back on her production. has offered her facilities for "toll enrichment" whereby other customers can supply the raw uranium feed and pay for enrichment to the desired degree. The United States

may also offer the same services.

Thus, even though a nation is unable or unwilling to build a diffusion plant, it may in the future be able to obtain the enriched byproduct by contract arrangement with one of the larger powers. This offers a peculiar problem in control, for enriched uranium lent or sold by the United States and the United Kingdom is subject to inspection and control. If a third nation supplies its own natural uranium and receives it back containing the same atoms but in different proportions, will the third nation be willing to accept control over that enriched product? The U.S. and Britain will probably insist on control at risk of limiting sales; but, when 

France's plants are partially free for toll enrichment, will she insist?

The existence of toll enrichment facilities will undoubtedly tend to dampen, at least, any future enthusiasm for diffusion plants. It may even slow down research abroad in alternate methods of producing enriched uranium. 

The centrifuge is an example of the latter process. This process was first initiated by the Germans during World War II. It suddenly seemed to become attractive again more than a decade later and the fact that much of the new work is now classified would indicate that it is not unpromising. (However, a good fraction, if not all, of the basic centrifuge research has been declassified.)

Thorium is another material for the production of atomic energy. It is actually more than twice as abundant as uranium in the earth's crust but appears to be even more spotty as to location. It can be converted in a nuclear reactor to uranium-233. To start the cycle, at least, uranium-235 or plutonium is needed for the uranium-233 process. The latter substance has a critical mass comparable to that of plutonium. It has its own particular metallurgical and nuclear complications, but it too could be used in a bomb. Abundant deposits of thorium have been found in India, Brazil, Canada and other areas. However, the use of thorium in a weapons program would probably not occur before a successful development of plutonium weapons. 

Thus we are almost in that era where the sources of. nuclear strength are plentiful indeed. There has been a growing concern for the problem of keeping those sources unmarshaled, for controlling nuclear proliferation.

The only mechanism actually pursued has been that of achieving a test ban. Any manufacturer likes to test his product, but we must recall that the uranium-235 bomb was first tested over Hiroshima; plutonium as a device was tested at 

Alamogordo, but as a bomb over Nagasaki. Thus the partial test ban treaty is a barrier but not an absolute barrier. 

There are no absolute guarantees in this nuclear age, nor are there likely to be any.

New technological developments may or may not be favorable to nuclear aspirants as far as weapons-making abilities are concerned. For example, Kahn mentions the californium bullet, 3 an artificial element with a critical mass so small that it might be the size of a bullet. He properly expresses skepticism that this could be manufactured cheaply. In fact, since it involves all of the facilities necessary to manufacture plutonium, and more, it is difficult to see how californium could be manufactured inexpensively and in quantity by even a very technologically advanced economy.

Thus far we have spoken of the fission weapon, but there is also the fusion process, utilizing elements of the lowest atomic weights, which also releases energy. 

Thus far it has been found possible to harness the cheaper fusion reaction only by means of a fission "trigger. 

There are bombs using widely varying proportions of the fission-fusion combination and in several configurations. 

It is probably true that, in order to develop effectively any one of these designs, a minimal nuclear test program is necessary. The dream of the nuclear weapons designer would be of course to develop the ability to use only the materials of fusion in a bomb, for at least two of these

materials--deuterium and lithium--are available more cheaply and easily than are the materials of fission. Deuterium is contained in ordinary water to the extent of one part in five thousand and is easily separated. Lithium is a common chemical. The third fusion material, tritium, has its complications in manufacture, it being necessary to have neutron-producing facilities like an atomic reactor. 

If the program of harnessing the materials of fusion for peaceful purposes ever comes to fruition, there will be automatically provided an abundant source of neutrons, using a cheap fuel derived from water. With these neutrons, it will be possible to manufacture plutonium and tritium to use in appropriate bomb designs. The nature of this peaceful fusion beast is impossible to predict. 

It may be large, expensive, unwieldy, demanding an input of immense technological resources; or it could be of small, simple design, demanding the investment of perhaps only a few million dollars. Thus, although this device is not even on the horizon, it would be foolhardy to deny categorically that the distant future (and, by this, one means much beyond 1969) does not provide the way to cheap nuclear weapons.

It must also be noted that the economic and technical levels of Kahn's Hottentot nations are constantly being raised through aid programs and by the independent initiatives of those nations themselves. Thus,

while we seem to be approaching an era of nuclear plenty, defined in the narrow sense of the number of nations which have nuclear weapons, we are also approaching a wholly different kind of world as regards the distribution of economic and technological resources and their associated political problems.

As far as the ability of doing nuclear mischief is concerned, there may be several other processes by which this will be accentuated, the obvious one being the sale or transfer of weapons from a technologically advanced nation to one which is less so. The nations among which such exchanges are likely to take place are even less possible to predict than the nations which, for political

reasons, might decide to begin a nuclear weapons program. 

But we have already seen a hint of this in the form of a radioactive warhead purported in development by German scientists for Egypt.4 The best way of performing radioactive warfare is to explode a nuclear bomb; but if this is not possible one could take the radioactive products from even a small nuclear reactor, package them in a warhead and deliver them to an enemy with, at the minimum, tremendous psychological impact.

There are other ways of disseminating these radioactive warfare agents on a scale ranging from the individual to fairly large populations. Mr. Khrushchev is well aware of the former possibility.

During his visit to the U.S. in 

September 1959, his food was regularly checked by a Geiger counter--possibly for fear that there was radioactive arsenic in his caviar. The reactor, which is the main source of production for radioactive isotopes, provides the means by which one nation, not having any atomic program of its own, can conduct warfare on the nation which does go into a nuclear program. This type of warfare can be accomplished by bombing or sabotaging a nuclear reactor which has been operating for some time and therefore contains a

One could give other examples of nuclear mischief which could be perpetrated with or without nuclear weapons. 

Perhaps these few examples, however, will suffice to show that we are now well into an era where one must expect to encounter the nuclear genie with increasing frequency and in a wide variety of manifestations. It may be possible to diminish the frequency and scale of the encounters but it is impossible at this point to eliminate them. 

Another phenomenon which one may expect to become more frequent, as more nations develop their technologies and hunger for the information to feed them, is that there will be increased activity in what is now considered "technical intelligence."

The greater fraction of effort of technical intelligence operations is consumed through the systematic scanning of literature. And, as a nation becomes more developed, more wise in the ways of applying external sources of knowledge, more sophisticated in recognizing the application or the import of information beyond the context of the application it is presented in, it seems inevitable that technical information gathering is going to

become what might be described as an international industry in itself.

Thus, it will become increasingly more difficult to keep "secrets" and, via this mechanism alone, the problem of proliferation will become increasingly more aggravated. 

If we look at what complicates the proliferation problem, we find one clear factor evident. This is the aspect that technologies are imported, manpower is imported, raw materials can be imported, and even bombs can be sold by one country to another. The implication might be drawn that, while we cannot control the internal actions of a nation, perhaps we may be able to effect control mechanisms, by means of an appropriately constituted international force, over what goes on between two nations. is international precedence in this suggestion in the narcotics and slavery curbs of international agencies.5 There

Whether or not a comprehensive control system of any sort is ever achieved, it is obvious that some immediate steps have to be taken to minimize proliferation. present atomic dilemma in which we find ourselves, that its major attribute is the vitality with which it springs up again and again. We could take several steps to control the proliferation pattern, some of which are: a) The formation of a policy of information control, bilateral and multilateral materials and technological assistance consistent with measures relating to disarmament. b) The re-examination of our policy on the release of information relating to the peaceful utilization of the atom with the view of making that policy consistent with our mutual defense arrangements. The abundance of information and technologies made available thus far is such that most countries will be unable to assimilate that data, interpret it and apply it for their own peaceful needs for many, many years hence. We should examine whether or not specific advanced points of technology in the peaceful area, which might have a bearing on the expeditious production of fissionable material or which might be related to subtle points of bomb as well as peaceful technologies, should be exchanged in a semi-classified arrangement with a limited number of nations which are in a

position to use the advanced technology and are already allied by military treaty. After all, most of the countries with whom we would like to negotiate such an arrangement already exchange classified categories of information. 

And, as those nations are in a position now to contribute advanced research and development ideas, these treaties should be negotiated on a two-way contributory basis. c) Correction, especially within the academic community and within the weapons laboratories, of the scorn for some early, outmoded technologies of bomb making. They may not be of any use whatsoever to us, but we should have a firm appreciation of what they mean to emerging Nth countries. a) 

Consideration of the control of new technological breakthroughs in limited instances.

This is a difficult problem of balance. 

For example, controlled thermonuclear fusion research used to be classified on the basis that, if such a program were successful, the abundant neutrons which would be available in the operation of a fusion machine would make cheap fissionable material available. The field of fusion

is essentially now freed, partially in the belief that open programs throughout the world would stimulate scientific thought on the problem and the solution would be arrived at sooner. This has undoubtedly been true even though this ideal open fusion program is to be found only in the West. 

Soviet releases are guarded and not fully illuminating.

But suppose it should happen that in the United States a cheap workable fusion process is discovered. Do we really want to give this to the entire world? And, in answering this question, we must think not only of the Nth country problem but of the problem of economic competition with even our friends and the possible implications for the major powers of such a machine to advanced weapons technology or production. It is clear that we cannot let the momentum of a declared open field of research govern our actions ad infinitum.

At a time when many are seeking various avenues to a workable mechanism for arms control, one may stumble upon and press policies which will prove to be wrong, but a wrong policy is nowhere near as dangerous as an inconsistent policy. In the former instance, we may prove

fortunate and lose only a bit of what we had sought to protect; but with inconsistent policies we are certain to lose all.
