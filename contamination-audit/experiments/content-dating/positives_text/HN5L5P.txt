WAR MADNESS CHAPTER I 

The fifteenth of April, nineteen hundred and fifteen, dawned bright and clear, with sunlight sparkling on the water. A few robins trilled in the trees, which showed a faint tracery of green. 

The grass had lost its sere, brown look and gave promise of awakening life. Here and there on the lawn, crocuses held up their cups. A deep blue had replaced the dull grey sky of winter. Once more the earth lived.

The soft breath of the wind blowing in at an open window touched caressingly the cheek of a girl who lay sleeping. She stirred slightly, and as a sunbeam fell upon her eyelids, they fluttered open, disclosing clear blue eyes. 

Elizabeth Meadows was a pretty girl, with the delicate fragility of a woodsprite. She seemed a child in spite of her twenty years, and her smile told of the happiness that the day was bringing to her; for it was her wedding day.

WAR MADNESS 

But following her smile, an expression of wistful sadness came into her eyes. The day which had dawned so brightly held for her one cloud that dimmed her joy. Wendel Fitzpatrick, her fiancé, still held to his determination to sail for England and enlist with the Royal Flying Corps, and she was to go with him. She had begged him to change his plans, but to no purpose. He had brushed aside her fears, fears which to her were very real, for the thought of disaster haunted her waking hours. There was something intangible that oppressed her.

She had always had a strange presentiment of coming events, especially of impending danger.

Even as a child she had felt this, and when, as she grew older, many of her fears came true, she had more faith in her premonitions. There was a strong psychic influence which seemed to control her. Her friends called her a strange girl, but her very strangeness and sensitiveness added to her charm and made them love her.

In this plan of Wendel's, Elizabeth felt sure that one of them would be killed before many weeks had passed. Panic seized her. She wished 8

WAR MADNESS 

with all her soul that before the time came for them to sail, something would arise to stop them. Perhaps after their marriage she would be able to dissuade Wendel. 

Her mother interrupted her thoughts, bringing in the breakfast tray. On the tray was a note and a small package from Wendel, which contained a beautiful wrist watch with her new monogram. 

Wendel was such a dear, she thought, but so obstinate. A mental picture of him passed before her eyes; his tall, slight figure, his flashing black eyes, straight black hair and clean-cut features, his gay and winsome smile. He was a handsome man, a man of whom any girl would be proud, and one whom no girl could help loving. He could be so sweet and thoughtful, and in most things ready to follow her slightest wish. Why was he so difficult to move in this? Why, oh, why, couldn't he wait until his own country entered the war, as she felt sure it would in time? Then he might go; but she would have had him with her for a while, and could bear the separation with more fortitude. She did love 9

him so, but hers was unconsciously a selfish love.

She didn't want to give him up. 

And Wendel-what of him? He, too, was pondering, his thoughts not altogether pleas ant. How happy he could be with Elizabeth if only she would see this thing as he did. They would have the wonderful sea voyage together, and would be near each other during his training. He saw her in all her sweetness, but he realized that, little and appealing as she was, she had a stubbornness all her own. 

For a moment he wavered; he did not quite know what to do. His heart was set on going, yet he loved Elizabeth and did not want to hurt her. Why couldn't she see that he could not be happy here, even if she were with him? He had made up his mind to go after much deliberation. 

Men were needed over there, and the United States seemed so slow! He could not stand idly by, while the Germans murdered women and children! He must go. Something stronger than himself urged him on,-he couldn't help it. 

Come what might, he was going, and Elizabeth must put aside her foolish fears and go with him. 10

At that moment a terrific explosion sounded in their ears. A torpedo had entered the stoke hold and burst in the interior of the vessel. Suffocating fumes of gas and clouds of white smoke coursed through the vessel, half stifling and sickening the passengers. Gasping, they ran forward to their stations. That last embrace savored of an eternal farewell, but in the actual crisis Elizabeth seemed more calm. As she took her place in the boat, a second torpedo struck the fore part of the ship. The vessel heeled to starboard, listing at an angle of forty-five degrees. At this second impact, panic seized the passengers. Many who wore life belts broke through the restraining guard of the offi cers and jumped frantically overboard, to be picked up later by the rescue boats which hur ried to the scene.

When the ship ceased to move, the life boats were lowered. The men, knowing that their hope of rescue was small, heroically gave their places to the women and children. Ten boats filled with panic-stricken people left the ship. Most of their occupants were women.

As the boat in which Elizabeth sat was lowered, one of the falls gave way, and Wendel, to his horror, saw her thrown headlong into the sea. Knowing that she could not swim, he dashed to the rail and leaped after her. Long and wildly he swam about, searching the waters, but could find no trace of her. Then he realized that she was gone, that no effort of his could bring her back. Exhausted and heart-broken from his fran 19

True to his promise, Bob came for Wendel the next morning, and they left for Oxford, which was one of the principal ground schools in England at that time.

Young men in the uniform of the Air Service filled the train, crowding the compartments to their limit. They called friendly greetings to one another, exchanging experiences and laughing heartily over them. A spirit of comradeship and gaiety filled the air. They made light of every thing, ignoring the unpleasantness and danger of the times,

Bob knew most of them, and he seemed to be a general favorite among them. He was a part of their world, talked their language and thought as they did.
