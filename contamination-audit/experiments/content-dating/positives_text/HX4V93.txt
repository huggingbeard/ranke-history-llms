Major General Leonard Wood was born in Winchester, N. H., in 1860. 

A graduate of Harvard Medical school he was commissioned assistant medical surgeon in the army, serving with distinction in the campaign against Chief Geronimo in 1886 and receiving the Medal of Honor for his bravery. At the outbreak of the Spanish-American war he organized a regiment of western Rough-Riders, of which he was colonel and Theodore Roosevelt lieutenant-colonel, and the command distinguished itself in Cuba. After promotion to major-general, he was made Chief of Staff of the army in 1909. During the World War he directed training of the 10th division and of several regiments. In 1921, he was appointed governorgeneral of the Philippines. He died in 1927.

THE BUILDING OF FORT LEONARD WOOD 

"Even when you see it you won't believe it!" was the apt expression of one soldier after being in the Post a few days. This is almost true in as much as the enormity of the whole reservation is hard to comprehend even after seeing it all. The fort was constructed in a very rough and hilly country half way between Rolla and Lebanon, Mo., just five miles south of national highway 66.

The entire aquisition of land on which the fort is built covers more than a hundred square miles although the buildings themselves are within a rectangle that is roughly five miles long and three miles wide.

THE RAILROAD 

Through one of the roughest sections of the Missouri Ozarks a new railroad connects the Fort to the outside world. This truly masterpiece of modern engineering has a main line 19.84 miles in length with 6.62 miles of rails in five passing tracks and warehouse yards. 

This road required the movement of 1,900,000 cu. yads. of excavation and 15 bridges and trestles was completed within a period of only three months, a feat that is lauded by railroad journals and engineering magazines.

The road was designed and supervised by R. F. Bundy, employed by 

Alvord, Burdick and Howson, artitect-engineers, Chicago, with the aid of Col. Frank G. Jonah, chief engineer of the Frisco, who acted as consulting engineer. Construction work was done by a group of contractors called the K.N.W.L. Company, who were also in charge of the post construction. 

The entire reservation was planned, designed and engineered by Alvord, Burdick and Howson under the jurisdiction of Major Frank Reed, Jr., construction quartermaster in charge. D. H. Maxwell, junior partner of the above firm was resident engineer.

On April 19th, 1941, the first passenger train carrying officers of both the army and Frisco railroad was operated over the line. On May 13, the first troop train came in and the line was officially opened on May 25.

THE HOSPITAL AREA 

The ninety single-story buildings that compose the hospital are located on the northern edge of the post proper. Of these buildings, 48 are wards with a combined capacity of 1460 beds with room for 540 more in emergencies. Every building is steam-heated from one huge heating plant, the steam pipes from which extend from building to building housed within the covered walks that connect all the buildings in the area.

There are two major and two minor operating rooms complete with equipment and x-ray and fluorescopic departments the latter of which are in lead lined rooms..

Ten quarters buildings house the 48 permanent doctors and 192 nurses. Approximately 600 enlisted medics will be on duty.

There is a complete dental clinic of 15 units. 

Of the covered walks between each building, their combined length is 13,100 feet or almost 22 miles. More than 7000 feet of these walks are completely inclosed while the rest have open sides.

Of the separate components of a garrison ration the menus are planned for balanced diet and variety. A typical menu consists of: meat dish, gravy, potatoes at least once a day, a green or leafy fresh or canned vegetable, dried beans twice daily, desert of fresh vegetable or fruit salad, bread, butter at least once per day, and a beverage tea, coffee, lemonade or fruit punch. No butter substitute is used in any way. Breakfast consists of meat, bread, butter, eggs, french toast or cakes, cereal, fresh milk and fresh or canned fruit. Fish in some form must be served at least once every ten days.

When the camp is filled to capacity, it might be interesting to note just how much goods will be shipped in to supply the tables. All figures represent pounds per day for the whole comp, unless otherwise stated. 

Bacon, 4,750; Beef, 23,750; pork, 9,500; eggs, 200 cases; potatoes, 23,750. tomatoes, 4,750; coffee, 4,750; evaporated milk, 38,000; fresh milk, 9,500 qts.; lard, 1,520; butter, 4,750; flour, 7,125; sugar, 1,187; syrup, 105 gals.; salt, 1,187; vinegar, 47 gals.; pepper, 95; cheese, 593 and so on down the list of 39 different articles the army buys for their men to eat.

RELIGIOUS 

There are 15 chaplains on the post now, representing a number of denominations. The ground will soon be broken for 12 chapels to be built. 

The first religious service in post was held in a post exchange located on West 11th Street in February. All religious attendance is purely voluntary. 

Services for Negroes started the last of April. There are approximately 

4000 Negroes in the post.

Various church aids and publishing houses keep the post supplied with religious literature. # # # # #
