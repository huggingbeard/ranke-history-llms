FOREWORD 

This study was initiated in 1939 and the basic data and analysis thereof compiled during 1939 and 1940. Subsequently and up until the present time the study has remained somewhat dormant due to the press of other departmental work. The publication of a report on the subject is deemed advisable at this time in order that a permanent record may be had and the data thereby made available for future planning of rural sidewalk projects. 

There are many phases of the study which should be made the subject of further development and it is the intent of this department to do so at such time as sufficient data have been collected on "before and after" experience on the numerous sidewalk installations along rural sections of the state highways. 

Mr. James J. Walton, Engineer, Traffic Division, is responsible for the collection and compilation of data in the initial period of study during 1939 and 1940. Mr. Walton's efforts in connection with the study are to be commended. 

Acknowledgment is also made of the work of other employees of the department who have contributed to the study.

SECTION I-INTRODUCTION Article I-General 

Motor vehicle accidents involving pedestrians are usually thought of as a problem peculiar to urban areas, and few people, other than those familiar with accident statistics, realize that pedestrian fatalities are distributed almost equally between urban and rural areas. Several conditions are responsible for, or at least influence, this line of reasoning on the part of the motor vehicle operator. 

Drivers have become accustomed to or grown up with the idea of pedestrians sharing the use of city streets; the same is not true of rural highways. Sidewalk facilities are usually available in cities, while the converse is true along rural highways, and, for this reason, the pedestrian use of rural facilities is looked upon as unusual and, perhaps, intrusive.

Concentrations of traffic, particularly pedestrian traffic, are so much greater on city streets that the driver is conscious of the greater exposure or possible conflict between his motor vehicle and persons on foot.

Far from the least important of the factors influencing the motorist is the publicity given to urban accidents, especially if the city is safety-minded and attempting a reduction in accidents. Pedestrians injured or killed in urban accidents are as a rule residents of the community, and the loss to the community is made known through publicity. The pedestrian accident on a rural highway will not receive the same publicity since the accident record of a particular urban area will not be affected. 

The Oregon State Highway Commission has recognized the problem as evidenced by the allocation of certain monies with which to construct sidewalks along rural state highways. There then remains the problem of the proper expenditure of the money available such that public interests will be best served and elimination of the pedestrian hazard will provide the greatest benefit as regards accident prevention.

The construction of footpaths or sidewalks along a highway in themselves contribute little to the elimination of the hazard of crossing the highway. This report is concerned [1] 

S.g. 2

primarily with the problem of the pedestrian walking along the highway. 

It is obvious that the construction of walks or paths along every mile of state highways would do much to minimize the pedestrian hazard, but the impracticability of such a policy is obvious. The cost would be prohibitive, and many miles of walks would be built where there is absolutely no warrant. 

Sidewalks could also be built on the basis of requests from communities or individuals, but here again such basis would be unsound.

Studies, of necessity, must be made of existing conditions and pedestrian accident records to establish a priority list for the construction or improvement of pedestrian facilities. It is difficult to establish fixed warrants for construction of a particular pedestrian facility. However, it is possible to establish relative indices as between a number of projects. The purpose of this study is to develop through the inter-relation of the several influencing factors a means of computing relative indices as between a group of several projects.

In urban areas insulation is provided between vehicular and pedestrian traffic by sidewalks. In rural areas little consideration has been given to providing a separate pedestrian trafficway. 

From Table I it will be noted that urban areas experience about five and one-half times the number of pedestrian accidents as rural locations; at the same time, the number of fatalities is approximately the same. The majority of rural pedestrian accidents occur adjacent to incorporated cities, in or near communities, or built-up areas along a highway. This is logical since there is a concentration of rural vehicular and pedestrian traffic in these areas. A more important consideration is the fact that many highways in these areas accommodate a vehicular traffic volume comparable to certain urban areas which latter provide sidewalk facilities for much smaller pedestrian volumes. Furthermore, it is in these rural areas that street lighting is lacking entirely or is insufficient to provide adequate illumination of pedestrians who must use the roadway of necessity. 

Traffic law enforcement is less stringent in these rural areas. When a motorist leaves the corporate limits of a city, the task of traffic law enforcement changes hands from a fairly large personnel to a much smaller force, with the result that

the driver probably relaxes his attention to traffic, both vehicular and pedestrian, and takes more chances, a situation not favorable to pedestrian safety.

It should be mentioned at this time that little or no consideration should be given to pedestrian accidents as a warrant for sidewalk construction where walks, had they been available, would not have prevented the collision. The drunken pedestrian and his movement along a highway is unpredictable; he may suddenly reel in front of an oncoming vehicle and be entirely responsible for any mishap, and because of his condition, it is doubtful whether he would have chosen to walk on sidewalks or footpaths were they available. This travel behavior on the part of the pedestrian can be seen at numerous places along state highways, especially where holiday crowds are predominant. SECTION II-EQUITABLE DISTRIBUTION OF COST Article 3-The Road User 

Another factor in providing sidewalk facilities along rural highways is the question of equity concerning the expenditure of road user funds for this purpose. While the State Highway Commission has set aside a sum of money for the elimination of the pedestrian hazard, it does not necessarily follow that the Commission will or should be expected to follow the same policy in providing walks in any or all locations where there is a sufficient warrant or demand by citizens, and pay for them entirely from road user funds. The question must be recognized as debatable since the benefits from rural sidewalks accrue to the abutting property and the community as well as the road user and pedestrian. 

The Oregon State Highway Commission has gone on record in Technical Bulletin No. 10, "An Analysis of the Highway Tax 

Structure in Oregon", in favor of the following basic concept of highway cost distribution: "Funds for the construction, maintenance and operation of highway systems are derived, in the main, by means of assessments, taxes and other imposts, levied in various ways and against different groups. These levies are presumably in consideration for certain and sundry benefits which accrue by virtue of road and street improvement. Such benefits are various and many, and impinge in different degree and manner on each of the several groups of beneficiaries. 

There are, however, certain basic principles, a rigid adherence to which is indispensible to the formulation of an equitable and adequate program for highway taxation. Concisely stated, these are: "1. That taxes and imposts be levied against each of the various beneficiary groups in direct proportion to the benefits derived. "2. That the tax monies received from each beneficiary group be distributed for expenditure in such manner as to produce maximum benefit returns to that group. "3. That taxes and imposts be sufficient, but only sufficient, to meet such costs as are both necessary and warranted in view of the benefits."

This concept of the distribution of highway costs, if applicable to the whole structure, is certainly applicable to any

component part, and allows the question to be raised whether the provision for pedestrian traffic in rural areas is the complete responsibility of highway authorities or whether a joint responsibility should be accepted by all those benefited. If the basic principles stated above are rigidly adhered to, the road user will, at no time, be required to stand the entire cost of pedestrian facilities, and, in many cases, his share will be minor. It appears that adjacent property-especially if the type of property falls in a business classification-and the HI

Article 4-The Community 

Experience has shown that rural communities and built-up areas follow the construction of highways, and this advance of property development along highways, particularly in areas adjacent to larger cities, is undoubtedly the cause of congestion and hazards that are evidenced in many sections of the state. This condition is exemplified in Figure 6. Business establishments in such areas would definitely benefit from sidewalks and should share in their costs. Any undeveloped property would have its value enhanced by such improvements. 

The community as a whole would benefit since practically all pedestrian traffic has its origin and/or destination within the community. As mentioned before, pedestrian deaths and injuries are community losses and the minimizing of the pedestrian hazard is, in a large measure, a local problem. 

Article 5-The Railroad Grade Crossing Separation Analogy 

The problem of separating rural vehicular and pedestrian traffic along with the proper distribution of costs based on benefits received is somewhat analogous to the problem of separating highway and railroad traffic-in both instances benefits are realized by the road user. The expenditure of road user monies for either type of separation will derive the same general benefit. In the instance of the railroad grade separation the life of the road user is protected and in the case of a pedestrian facility both the pedestrian and the road user benefit. In both situations, however, there is the problem of an equitable distribution of costs.

The responsibility of providing for pedestrian safety has been mentioned. The elimination of conflicts at a railroad

grade crossing will, as in the case of vehicle-pedestrian segregation, benefit in varying degree the parties concerned. The life and property of the road user is completely protected and time is saved. The latter factor is, even though intangible, usually the more important from a monetary standpoint, since the ultimate in protection at a railroad crossing is not provided unless the volume of both vehicular and train traffic will warrant a separation. The benefits to rail traffic are the saving of time and life-schedules are not disrupted and it is not uncommon for train crews or passengers to be injured or killed in grade crossing accidents.

In recent years, fairly large sums have been set aside by the Federal Government for the elimination of grade crossing hazards, which fund can be considered as road user monies reverting to the state from the Federal tax on motor vehicle fuels. 

It then follows that other hazards due to vehicle-pedestrian conflict can be eliminated and road user funds utilized without questioning the distribution of costs on the basis of benefits derived.

The need for railroad grade crossing protection, and provision of Federal funds for this purpose, has come about through pressure of public opinion rather than from actual need when compared with other highway hazards. During the period 1936 through 1939, 80 pedestrians were killed and 241 injured while walking along urban and rural state highways in Oregon. During the same period railroad grade crossing accidents on the same roads killed 65 and injured 209 persons.

Expressed another way, railroad grade crossing fatalities and injuries were, respectively, only 31.3 and 86.7 per cent as great as fatalities and injuries to pedestrians walking along highways. This relationship is due in part to the better protection offered at grade crossings, but the cost of this protection has been quite high.

During the period for which the above comparison is made, $2,474,000 was expended for railroad grade crossing separations and only $58,000 for rural sidewalk construction. In other words, for every grade crossing casualty during the four-year period, $9,029 was expended for prevention, while for every rural pedestrian casualty of the type being discussed, an ex-

SECTION III-THE PEDESTRIAN Article 6-Use of Roadway 

As mentioned previously, pedestrian use of rural highways is unusual and, insofar as the motorist is concerned, intrusive. 

Generally speaking, the pedestrian does not concur in this thought which fact is substantiated by accident reports. 

Without attempting to settle the question as to who has the right of way along a rural highway-the driver or the pedestrian-it appears that common sense should dictate a move on the part of the pedestrian to protect his own life. He is the one who is usually injured or killed, not the driver of the vehicle. This attitude on the part of the pedestrian has at many times been nourished to the point of actual defiance of the rights of the motorist. The pedestrian is protected by law at intersections and other specified locations when he crosses a street or highway and safety campaigns or safety education should stress the responsibilities of the pedestrian as well as those of the motorist.

The statutory rights and duties of pedestrians when walking along a rural highway are as follows: "Pedestrians, when using any highway outside of incorporated cities or towns, shall use the left-hand side of such highway, so as to leave the right-hand side of the highway free for vehicles passing the same direction and for safety in meeting vehicles proceeding in the opposite direction." (L. 1931, C. 360; O. C. 1935 Supp., Pg. 1487.)

It will be noted that there is nothing in the law as stated that requires a pedestrian to leave the highway regardless of vehicular traffic. The pedestrian may be given, by law, the inference of false security thereby jeopardizing his personal safety.

Article 7-Involvement in Accidents 

The causes of pedestrian accidents are, with rare exceptions, traceable to two sources the pedestrian or the vehicle driver. 

Exceptions are instances wherein a driverless vehicle may run up on a sidewalk and hit a person looking in a store window, a pedestrian may be struck as a result of a mechanical failure

of a vehicle, or the condition of a street or highway may cause a driver to lose control of his car and run down a bystander. 

Such instances are uncommon, uncontrollable, and do not warrant consideration.

When driver behavior causes an accident, such behavior may be placed in one of several groups as follows: violations of traffic laws, improper actions other than violations, attitude toward safety of others, and driver's condition. Accident causes attributable to pedestrian behavior may be classified in much the same manner.

There are traffic laws applicable to pedestrian as well as vehicular movements. Pedestrians as well as drivers occasionally maneuver in a way, not necessarily in violation, to set the stage for an accident. As emphasized previously, any impairment of sense acuity of the pedestrian or driver nullifies in whole or part any remedial measures which may be promulgated through traffic engineering.

Observation and experience seems to indicate that the pedestrian needlessly exposes himself to injury or death while crossing or walking along a highway. He is not cognizant or fully appreciative of the limitations of the driver and his vehicle. A study of the license status of pedestrians involved in accidents reveals that a surprisingly large number of them, and particularly those in the older age groups, are not and never have been licensed drivers of motor vehicles. A pedestrian in this category does not realize that the forward motion of a vehicle cannot be stopped in a few feet nor that the direction of travel cannot always be altered in sufficient time to avoid a collision. 

The pedestrian, on the other hand, can stop in the length of a step or move in any direction-right, left, or backwards-at will.

Article II-Hazards Inherent in Night-time Traffic 

The hazards inherent in night-time traffic accidents have long been recognized by the Oregon State Highway Department. Considerable research in fields allied to this subject has been conducted. In the introduction to Technical Bulletin 

No. 12, "Light Reflecting Characteristics of Pavement Sur faces", published by the Oregon State Highway Department, the following is quoted: "The high accident rate of night driving is a matter of serious concern to all engineers and officials responsible for the construction and operation of highways. Many studies have been made of accident frequency data and the relationship between accident frequency and visibility. The conclusions reached in these studies are in substantial ac-

27 cord, and clearly show the increased hazard attendant upon night driving. "The increase in traffic fatalities has followed closely the increase in motor vehicle registration; but when these fatalities are segregated according to the hours of occurrence it is seen that, while during daylight hours the ratio of fatalities to total traffic is decreasing, the ratio and number of night-time fatalities has steadily increased. Twenty years ago only 30 per cent of such fatalities occurred at night. Now night-time fatalities constitute more than 60 per cent of all traffic fatalities. Morris, in the 1935 Highway 

Research Board Proceedings, reported on a master station at Ames, Iowa, shows only approximately 24 per cent of all highway traffic as being on the road between the hours of 

7 p. m. and 7 a. m. Other reliable estimates give 20 per cent as a figure for night-time traffic. Yet 60 per cent of the accidents occur during the hours of darkness. In 1936 almost 

38,000 traffic fatalities occurred and of these 22,000 or 58 per cent occurred at night when only 20 per cent of the traffic was moving. "While the greatest increase in traffic density has undoubtedly been on rural highways, city streets, usually illuminated in some degree, are also carrying greatly increased loads. In the last seven years fatalities on these city streets have decreased 10 per cent while fatalities were increasing 32 per cent on rural roads. "Any study of traffic accident records shows the cause of the preponderance of night-time accidents. The conditions controlling daylight operation have now changed. The driver no longer can see the hazards and prepare for them.

They are upon him instantly and a split-second response to the emergency tells the difference between safety and disaster. The most important factor in safe driving is the ability to see. The driver must see a hazard in order to avoid it. During daylight hours, signs, guardrails, centerline stripes, and pavement edges are continuously impressing upon his mind the boundaries between safety and danger. Approaching vehicles or pedestrians are visible far ahead. There is time to plan a course of action. With the coming of darkness the same obstacles are present, but they are too often not seen until too late. Visibility "It has become customary to recognize two ways of 'seeing' things. The first is known as 'discernment by surface detail'. The second is known as 'discernment by silhouette'. When light falls upon an object a portion of it,

depending on the characteristics of the surface, is reflected. 

If the reflected light which reaches the eye is of sufficient volume, the details of the object will be seen. Bright and shaded areas will contrast and colors can be recognized. 

This is discernment by surface detail. "Surfaces vary greatly in their ability to reflect light. 

An object which reflects a small amount of light, when placed against a background which reflects a relatively large amount of light, will stand out in outline against this brighter background. Recognition in this way is 'discernment by silhouette'. "During daylight hours seeing consists principally of discernment by surface detail. Sufficient light is available so that, at reasonable distances, details and colors are recognized. At great distances discernment by silhouette, by the contrasting of dark objects against light-colored backgrounds or vice versa plays a part. "At night discernment by silhoutte is by far the more important. It is almost entirely by this method that the driver, who is dependent upon his own or the approaching vehicle's headlights, sees. Where fixed lighting of sufficient intensity has been installed, some surface detail can be perceived, but even in this case the greatest reliance must be placed on silhouette. Glare "The eye can accommodate itself to only a relatively small range of light intensity. Too high an intensity causes a dazzle which submerges the object in a blaze of light and too low an intensity results in insufficient contrast. As the light intensity varies within the usable range, the eye constantly adjusts itself. This adjustment is not an instantaneous occurrence. An appreciable time is required to permit the adjustment to be made. The automobile headlight gives the equivalent of a dimly lighted tunnel down which the eye is directed. Even though the light from the lamps may be delivered uniformly, some parts of the tunnel reflect more light back to the driver than others. If the pavement is smooth, it will appear dark compared with the rougher surface of the shoulders. At the low angle at which the light is delivered only a small fraction of the light comes back to the driver from any surface. From the headlights of an oncoming car, however, the low angle causes a reflection of a great part of the light toward the driver. This reflection is a beam of relatively narrow width and high intensity. The eye cannot adjust itself to this intense beam and at the same time admit light enough to see outside

VISIBILITY 

29 the beam. An object in the path of the beam is perfectly silhouetted but nothing outside is seen at all. The oncoming car passes quickly but the eye readjusts itself slowly, the phenomena of vision being persistent on the retina for about one-sixteenth of a second after the stimulus is removed. There is always this fraction of a second of glare blindness following the meeting of an automobile. The driver is driving by guess, hoping that the road remains in the same condition as when he last saw it by the dim lights of his own vehicle. This inability to see is a major contributory cause of the high night accident rate. "Glare is, in a large measure, a function of the characteristics of the pavement surface. The driver need not look directly into the oncoming lights, but he must look at the pavement. If a great volume of light is being reflected from the surface toward him and a small volume from his own lights back to him, he can see only the approaching beam and that as a blinding glare."

SECTION V-RIGHTS AND RESPONSIBILITIES Article 12-Legal Aspects 

It is thought that there would be fewer pedestrians hit while walking along rural highways if they obeyed specifically the law relative to their rights and duties. As quoted in the law in Article 6, the pedestrian should walk along the roadway facing the traffic. This law is probably violated as often as it is obeyed, and the violation cannot always be condemned. 

Reference is made to pedestrian travel in built-up areas where strict adherence to the law would require crossing back and forth over the roadway, creating, perhaps, a greater hazard. 

Persons shopping in such an area would find it quite impractical to always walk facing the vehicular traffic. The photographs shown in Figures 17 to 19, inclusive, illustrate this. 

This condition, as mentioned before, should be included in any warrant for sidewalk construction.

The problem is different in open areas where there is little excuse for a violation of the law; but, even there, hitchhikers will not find it advantageous to be on the side of the roadway other than the direction in which they wish to travel. 

Furthermore, there are certain locations along rural highways where constrictions of the shoulder make it unsafe for the pedestrian to comply with the law. Through some cuts and fills, along sections where a guard-rail on one side is very close to the pavement edge, and in sections where shoulder constriction and conditions make the use of the shoulder impossible, the pedestrian may choose to walk with traffic, and regardless of the provisions of the law be in a safer position than if he walked against the approaching traffic with no way to step off the pavement to allow a car to pass.

SECTION VI- Article 15-General Warrant 

Having considered the general aspects of the problem, the discussion is now directed to a consideration of the warrants for rural sidewalk construction. In this vein, the following is quoted from the "Report of Project Committee on Sidewalks 

Along Rural Highways", Proceedings of the Highway Research

Board, 1939: 

"General Warrants for Sidewalk Construction 

"Based upon the data assembled so far and the experiences of members, the Committee recommends the following general principles concerning rural sidewalk construction, recognizing that further elaboration and the development of more detailed warrants are necessary. However, these more general specifications are presented to aid States and counties in identifying the locations of most imperative need for highway sidewalks and in proceeding with their construction. "1. Sidewalks should be constructed along highways where considerable numbers of pedestrians walk on the highway and the accident records show that this is resulting in frequent pedestrian accidents. In studying accident records for this purpose accidents to pedestrians crossing highways should be distinguished from accidents to persons walking along highways. Records of accidents to be used. as warrants for sidewalk construction should be confined to those of a type which such construction can conceivably prevent. Likewise, records of accidents distributed along the highway should be distinguished from those concentrated at a single point, the distributed pattern of accidents being most likely to indicate the need for sidewalks. "2. In the absence of adequate accident records for this purpose and in anticipation of accidents due to new highway construction, increased vehicular traffic, or growth in adjacent population, the need for sidewalks must be foreseen before the impending toll of pedestrian deaths and injuries starts. Sidewalks may be expected to become necessary along major highways in the transition zones between urban and rural sections, in the vicinity of rural schools, churches, and other group meeting places, and near country clubs, airports, fairgrounds, and similar locations.

"3. Walkways, separate from the roadway, should be provided on bridges and through underpasses, anticipating the need for such pedestrian facilities resulting from the growth of vehicular or pedestrian traffic if the need does not immediately exist. "4. Any walkway surface must be as comfortable and convenient to use as the roadway which it parallels or many pedestrians will use the latter, even at the risk of accidents.

Surfaces that will become muddy in wet weather or dusty in dry weather for example, are apt to be avoided at such times. "5. Proper provision should be made for continuity of walkways and for proper crossings of minor street approaches. If pedestrians must enter the roadway to pass points where the sidewalk is omitted or low grade surface is provided, they are apt to remain in the roadway. "6. In constructing walkways provisions should be made for proper maintenance and for removal of weeds and brush which may extend onto or across them if such maintenance is not provided. The growth of weeds or brush close to the sidewalk increases the opportunity for crime and discourages the use of the walkways at night."

While the foregoing includes the general warrants for rural sidewalk construction, it does not attempt to set up a method of establishing a priority of construction between individual projects which may qualify with respect to such general warrants. 

It is believed impossible to derive any exact formula for this purpose, but it is believed possible to develop a rule of thumb which, supplemented by sound judgment, will serve as an Index of Hazard until more numerous data are available.

Article 17-Relative Warrant is The foregoing discussion has dealt with the derivation of indices of hazard as between various sections; however, it does not follow that such indices may be used in determining the relative warrant of projects in the matter of construction priority. Relative warrant values or indices must recognize, in addition to inherent hazards, the item of construction costs.

Further, and as pointed out in previous discussion, when past accident records are available and show a positive value some weight must be accorded thereto notwithstanding the fact that pedestrian accidents are largely a matter of chance and subject to wide range of incidence.

There are many worthy projects that could be undertaken in the interest of safety, but they are not necessarily economically sound. If the benefits produced are not commensurate with the cost, the project is unwarranted economically. 

The benefits produced by well-placed rural sidewalks are: 

1. Added safety to both pedestrians and motorists; 

2. Time savings to motorists; and, 3. Convenience to pedestrians.

Further study may develop approximate monetary equivalents or values for these factors, but at present the data are too limited. However, even without direct computation of the warrant, relative values can be computed that will indicate the feasibility of one project when compared with that of another, by assuming that the Index of Hazard reflects the benefits. This is not unreasonable when the factors contained in the formula, I.H. V.Vp (1 + F1 + F2 + F3 + F4 + F5), are considered. Certainly the time saving to the motorist is approximately proportional to the vehicle volume (V), the convenience to the pedestrian to the number served (V₁), and the added safety is reflected not only by the volumes but by the degree of hazard represented by the factors F1, F2, F3, F4, and 

F, which is eliminated. Neglecting the actual casualties of record, the relative warrant can then be said to be directly proportional to the index of hazard and inversely proportional to the cost per mile (C…). 

The introduction of the accident factor is subject to a range of possibilities particularly as regards the weight which should be accorded this item in any computation of relative warrant. The effect of this factor on the relative warrant should undoubtedly be positive in quality. The quantitative effect-whether the factor should be direct or exponential in character—is a matter open to debate. Lacking sufficient data on which to base a detailed study, the factor of accidents has, for the purpose of

this study, been confined to one having straight line characteristics.

SECTION IX-SUMMARY 

The rural sidewalk projects in this State have proven a real benefit and have definitely indicated that rural sidewalks are worthwhile. Noteworthy is the record of highway US99E between Portland and Oregon City. This section carries a large volume of fast vehicular traffic coupled with an appreciable volume of pedestrian traffic. Prior to and inclusive of 

1939 there were no fatal accidents involving pedestrians walking along the highway and only five non-fatal accidents of the same classification. The record is more impressive in view of the fact that the walks are gravel surfaced, difficult to maintain, and not too inviting to the pedestrian. This highway, however, is of four lanes with intersectional illuminationfactors operating to decrease the pedestrian hazard. 

The oiled footpath along highway ORE11 between Freewater and the Washington State Line is another project having a good record. This walk although hard-surfaced is still not ideal, since it is not physically separated from the roadway. 

As mentioned in the preceding Section VIII, Oregon is one of the leading states in the construction of rural pedestrian facilities. This department in 1940 conducted extensive surveys of needed rural sidewalk facilities applying the Index of Hazard and Relative Warrant formulae to these studies. It was found of inestimable value in the final selection of projects to be constructed.

It is to be borne in mind that the application of the Index of Hazard and Relative Warrant to any single project is in itself of little value; however, when an extensive program has been prepared, the use of these indices enables a governmental agency to expend funds at those locations where the most good. will be derived from the expenditures.

It is the intention of this department, as mentioned hereinbefore, to make extensive "before and after" studies of all existing rural sidewalk facilities to the end that certain monetary values may be developed. These monetary values must of necessity be correlated with the values as outlined in the

SUMMARY 

61 

"Economics of Highway Planning, Technical Bulletin No. 7", as published by this department. These values or benefits could be expressed as: 

1. Mileage Element Benefits 2. Time Element Benefits 3. Accident Element Benefits 

The application of these benefit factors would of necessity entail a long-range experience before stabilized monetary values could be determined. 

Mileage Element Benefits would accrue to the motorist (primary road user) principally in the form of decreased stops. 

Time Element Benefits would accrue to the primary road user as a result of increased or at least sustained speeds that would be maintained through sections where he now must decrease his speed in the interest of safety. The secondary (pedestrian) road user, although not a direct contributor to road user funds while in the category of a secondary road user, would likewise experience a saving in time. 

Monetary savings from Accident Element Benefits would be considerable at such time as Property Damage, Personal 

Injury, and Fatal Accidents can be properly evaluated. Studies to date on the subject of accident evaluation have produced a wide range of values notwithstanding which there is definite indication that the benefits accruing to this element will assume major proportions and in many instances exceed one or both of the other component benefit elements-mileage and time. In evaluating this benefit item not only pedestrian accidents but the over-all accident experience in the section should be given consideration.

It is to be remembered that the Index of Hazard and Relative Warrant formulae are in themselves only a "means to an end" and must of necessity be correlated with a comprehensive listing of proposed sidewalk projects before their true worth and value may be obtained.

The method of analysis described in this report is based in a large part upon deduction rather than factual data and is offered only because of lack of a more exact method of deter-

mination. When rural sidewalk construction has progressed further, and "before and after" comparisons can be made, it is believed that the data necessary for a more exact mathematical evaluation will be available. It is gratifying, however, that in applying the above described formula to actual sections of highway, indices are obtained consistent with the actual accident experience.
