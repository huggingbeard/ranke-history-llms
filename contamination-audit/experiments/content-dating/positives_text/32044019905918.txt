PREFACE 

I have had the pleasure of inspecting a number of articles and have read many excellent books relative to the early Norwegian settlers in Wisconsin and Minnesota, but nowhere have I found accounts of the original Norwegian settlers or settlement in Rush Creek Valley, Winona County, Minnesota, frequently designated (especially so in former days) and known as Hardanger Dalen.

Although these settlers were not so numerous as in other places and the settlement not so extensive in area, which was on account of the limited amount of land available in this valley, nevertheless it was purely a Norwegian settlement from its very beginning and the settlers, as the name indicates, nearly all came from Hardanger, Norway. 

Besides the ten early Norwegian families who became permanent settlers in the valley, there were many others who came and lived there for some time until they found homes further west in Minnesota or other distant places.

All of these original settlers long since have passed on to their final reward. Their present posterity is now represented by only a few of their children, more of their grandchildren, but mostly by their great grandchildren to whom these sketches may be of interest. It may, therefore, not be out of place to prelude these biographic sketches with a brief repetition of the characteristics, physical dexterity, endurance, and high purpose of their ancient Nordic prodgeny in general, the Vikings. Such records, I am well aware, already have been well recorded in various previous histories. It has been my purpose here only to emphasize and to remind descendants of these early settlers of Hardanger Dalen of their inheritance and environment hoping that they may not think such delineations have been overdrawn.

It is of these original and permanent settlers, these almost forgotten men and their families that I wish especially to recall and remember in a permanent historic manner in these short biographies, for I believe they deserve as much.

FOREWORD 

Considerable difficulty and inconvenience has been encountered in collecting data for the compilation of these biographic sketches for the simple reason that these early settlers were foreign emigrants who could not write themselves, and, therefore, did not keep any written diaries, and as they were unacquainted with the language and the laws of the new land, they were handicapped in participating in politics or in holding political offices, and, therefore, their names did not appear in the permanent records, although they did participate in civic affairs. 

Their social activities consisted mainly of participation in church work or religious activities, which were conducted in the Norwegian language, and such records have not always been well kept. When many of the older permanent settlers passed away and their graves were not marked with gravestones bearing inscriptions, many dates and much interesting information was permanently lost. It therefore has been necessary to depend upon anecdotes as related by their relatives and descendants, and such information often becomes modified by age. The work has been more or less research work, extending over a considerable period of time, but has given me much satisfaction and enjoyment in its execution. CHAPTER I The Early Norwegians 

Norway is an ancient country. Historians tell us that evidences have been found indicating that at least certain parts of Norway were inhabited as early as during the stone age, and it in all probability has been continuously inhabited ever since that time. 

Rheunic stones or records have been found which were made in Palestine as early as 4000 B. C., but the Rheunics with inscriptions found in 

Norway apparently are of a much later date. Evidently the Norwegians did not make records as early as some of the people in the more southern parts of the eastern continent. Although historic records nowhere yet found does so record it, nevertheless it is quite certain that at the dawn of the Christian era, or at the time Joseph and Mary came down to Bethlehem for the purpose of registering for the census taking, Norwegians were thea tilling the soil along the Norwegian coast, sailing on and fishing in the fjords of Norway.

The present inhabitants of Norway are believed to be descendants of the Aryan race. They also are Teutons. springing from a Germanic tribe, in common with Danes and Swedes.

The earliest written records of the Norwegian nation now in existence are found in the Icelandic Sagas, as recorded in the Hauk Book and Flatey Book. These were written during the eighth and ninth century, A. D. From these Icelandic Sagas and from letters discovered at the 

Vatican in Rome, we find evidence that the Norwegian nation at one time was the most important, enterprising, prosperous, and energetic nation in all Europe.

It is now generally accepted that the Norwegians were the first to venture out upon the open seas. They had considerable knowledge of astronomy and made frequent sea voyages and discoveries. They were the first to discover Iceland, Greenland, Hebrides, Shetland and the Faroe Island, as well as the North American continent itself. They not only discovered these countries, but were the first to colonize many of them. At one time they also had colonies in Ireland, Scotland, England, France and even down in Italy. It now is conceded that the Norwegians were the first to discover and colonize the North American mainland, which they named Newfoundland and Vineland. They, therefore, not only were the first to discover the North American continent, but they also had colonized it 500 years before it was rediscovered by Columbus. Christopher Columbus visited Iceland in 1477 and rediscovered America in 1492. 

Harold Haarfoger (Harold the Fair Haired) became one of the kings in Norway when he was but 10 years old, upon the death of his father, in 863. At the time Harold became one of the Norwegian kings, Norway was ruled by several small kings called Jarls. King Harold was the first 

Norwegian king to subdue all the other small kings and bring Norway --8--

under the rule of one king. This he accomplished during his reign between 

872 and 885. He then became a dictator and having accepted the 

Christian faith demanded that all his subjects should do likewise, obey his will and be baptized. 

It was a foregone conclusion that such a democratic nation as the Norwegian people, bold Vikings as they were at that time, would not humbly submit to such arbitrary rules.

Before this time, the Norwegian nation had been a pagan nation. They had worshipped their unseen gods, Odin and Thor. They had built churches for them, made images and pictures of them, and prayed to their unseen god, Odin, who had been their god of wisdom, and who had served them well according to their belief. He had brought them safely out of bondage from other nations, and they, therefore, were reluctant to relinquish their faith in him. They were superstitious too and feared to cease their sacrifices to him for fear evil would befall them in the future, thus they clung tenaciously to their pagan faith in spite of royal decree. 

When King Harald Harfager accepted the Christian faith, demanding that all Norwegian subjects accept this faith, many refused and would not give up their God for the white Christ and the Christian faith he advocated. At first the pagans, or heathens as they were later called, persecuted the Christians, but as the Christians grew stronger and more powerful, they persecuted the pagans, burning their temples and destroying their idols. This led to much dissatisfaction and friction between the people of Norway and caused a large emigration from there to Iceland and other countries to spring up. These emigrants were from the better classes of the people, better mentally, physically and financially. With them went much of the best blood, wealth and citizenship of Norway. 

King Harald Harfager lost so many good people and wealth through this emigration that he was compelled to put a heavy tax of gold on all those who left Norway for Iceland. This emigration proved to be a great detriment to the Norwegian nation and also a great loss to the people who left Norway, for they came to much poorer countries, such as 

Iceland, Greenland, and the northern Atlantic islands.

King Harald was (according to the report of those who knew him. personally) a remarkably handsome man in appearance.

He was physically superior and a soldier of great dexterity. He possessed an abundance of light curly hair which gave him his name, "Fair Hair." He had been a great warrior since early youth. Soon after he became one of the kings of Norway, he made a vow that he would not cut or comb his hair until he had subdued all small kings in Norway and had become the ruler of the entire country. This vow was strictly adhered to. It was said that he did not cut or comb his hair for 20 years. He became ruler of all Norway between 872 and 885 and reigned as king of all Norway until his death at the age of 83, in the year 933. After his death. the Kingdom of Norway again became subdivided now between his many sons and daughters. The country again became ruled over by many -930-

small kings, as formerly. The previous raiding and marauding of the 

Vikings which was still sanctioned by the rulers continued as before. 

King Olaf Trygvason was the next Viking king who succeeded in subduing all the small kings of Norway and bring the country under the rule of one king. King Olaf continued invasions and raiding more vigorously than any of the former Viking kings, but he continued to adhere to the pagan faith.

History relates that at one time, after King Olaf had returned from a four year raiding expedition in various places, he tarried in Sicily. Here he fell in with a sorcerer or fortune teller who foretold his future, stating that he would soon fight a severe battle, that he would become wounded but would recover in seven days. After that he would accept the Christain faith and conquered the pagans, and thereby do much good for his country.

It so happened that he soon thereafter did have a battle, was wounded and recovered in seven days. This so fixed his belief in the sorcerer that he allowed himself to become baptized and accept the Christian faith. He thereupon issued the edict that all his subjects should follow his example, be baptized, accept the Christian faith or die. This so insensed many of his subjects, especially the well-to-do bondes or land owners, that they left the country.

Severe persecution and much torture of the pagans whom the Christians called "heathens" followed. Many pagans were tortured to death on account of religious beliefs. Pagan churches were burned and their idols destroyed. Their gold and silver confiscated, King Olaf was good to those who accepted the Christian faith, but very cruel to those who did. not. Still there remained many pagans who could not and would not give up their faith and the sacrifices at the temple of Thor. 

King Olaf Trygvason reigned from 995 to 1000, being killed in a battle off the coast of England during a Viking expedition. The Viking age terminated altogether during the 13th century. 103-

CHAPTER II Causes for the Early Norwegian Emigration 

Whereas the first and most important cause of the emigration from Norway was the introduction of Christianity, which resulted in a great economic loss to the nation. Nevertheless, another great calamity to occur was the pestilence called the Black Death or Bubonic plague, which made its appearance at about the same time. 

The Bubonic plague or Black Death, as it also was called, has been known to exist for 3000 years. It makes its appearance in cycles in a more or less severe pandemic form. It is a very highly infectious and deadly disease. This disease is carried and transmitted by rodents, but the Norwegian rat especially is the greatest offender. From the rat, the rat flea carries or transmits the disease to the human by its bite or sting. 

During the latter part of the sixth century and during the early part of the fourteenth century all northern Europe was hard hit by this disease, but Norway was especially so. Twenty-five per cent of the people of northern Europe succumbed and few who contracted it recovered. 

Norway and Iceland and other northern countries as high as 75 per cent, or three out of every four persons died. The people became panic stricken with fear. Some localities in Norway were completely depopulated.

In This great loss of man power, resulting from emigration extending over many years duration due to religious persecution, added to the great loss through the pestilence or Black Death, so reduced the strength of the man power of Norway that she became an easy prey for the other nations, who were none too friendly to the Norwegian Vikings. Norway soon lost all her colonial possessions, and she herself soon became subdued and a vassal of Denmark.

Conquered Norway was then bled white and remained a helpless, prostrate, ruined and bankrupt country for centuries. The Danes would send their tax collectors to Norway where the Norwegian bonde or farmer and the husman or laborer were treated with little consideration, extracting exorbitant taxes called skat, without granting them any voice in their own government, or have representation at court in law. 

The Danes prohibited the Norwegians from having universities in Norway. Their sons were compelled to go to Denmark for their higher education. The Danish language and books were ordered used in Norway, for the Norwegian language was not considered refined enough. 

It was vulgar and they also should use the Danish language in all Norway. 

Although Norway never had a true aristocracy nevertheless there came to be a great difference between the social standing of the people in Norway. 

The common people were looked down upon by the so-called upper classes who were the business and professional people. This was especially so among the learned or professional classes such as the clergy and medical -411-

men. This upper class became intolerant, arbitrary, and bigoted. Norway lived under such strained conditions for many years or until Denmark later lost in a war to England and she was forced to give up to England many of the colonies she had taken from Norway. Denmark also was forced to cede Norway to Sweden, and this resulted in the union of these two countries. All this increased further emigration from Norway which now had been directed towards North America. 

Because of this the carly part of the 19th century saw exceedingly hard times in all Norway. The farmers had large mortgages and large families. Money was scarce, business poor, and work hard to find. 

Prospects for bettering their economic conditions urged the Norwegians to emigrate. The difference between the upper and lower classes, and the distinction drawn between the wealthy and poor became so repugnant and humiliating to the poorer class that they could endure it no longer. 

Great dissatisfaction arose amongst them and they were most anxious to cmigrate. Again the military service also became so stringent that many of the younger men became anxious to avoid the service and emigrated. 

Norway also was a highly religious country. The clergy became very obstinate and over-bearing and discouraged the lay citizens from preaching and worshipping according to the dictates of their own hearts. A great deal of proselytism prevailed even among the laity. Hans Nelson 

Hague, a layman, was imprisoned for ten years for preaching. He did nothing wrong, but it was contrary to Norwegian laws for a layman to preach and interpret the Bible. Only learned men, ordained ministers, were permitted to perform the important function. 

It was a law similar to that which exists at the present time in many countries regulating the practice of medicine whose members are required to be qualified by education and license before they may legally care for those who are physically ill.

With such social and economic conditions existing in Norway during the early 19th century, it was expected that a large number of its citizens should become anxious to emigrate in hopes of improving their future conditions. -..{12}?...

CHAPTER III The Beginning of Norwegian Emigration to North America 

After Christopher Columbus had rediscovered North America in 1492, some of the most powerful European nations became very much interested in this new country. They became anxious to obtain colonies in North America, and soon set about to attain such ends. They sent envoys and explorers over to discover and take possession of large tracts of territory in various parts of the North American continent. England, France, Spain, as well as some of the other European nations soon obtained great possessions in North America. Ireland and 

Germany also contributed many immigrants to this country during the seventeenth and eighteenth century who became permanent citizens, but they obtained no colonies. Norway, being in such economic condition at this time did not consider or attempt colonization, but many Norwegian seamen were registered with other nations and sailed under foreign flags. It is said that twenty-five per cent of the Dutch seamen actually were Norwegians and many more Norsemen registered with Danish and Swedish ships. For centuries the Norsemen have been enterprising and competent sailors. They have proven themselves capable of assimilating a high degree of development, both mentally and physically, as evidenced by the Vikings.

It was during the early part of the 19th century that the Norwegian common people began to awaken to the importance of emigration and to make efforts in this direction, pointing their faces towards North America. 

In 1801 a small one-mast sailboat, which was destined to become world famous, was built on the Hardanger fjord in Norway. In all probability, it was built at the village called Oystese, for it had been the principal Norwegian shipyard for centuries. It is said that boats had been built at Oystese long before written records were made. During the first twenty years of its existence, this small ship had plied the waters along the western coast of Norway as a commercial transport. After twenty years of struggling and battling with the waves along the Norwegian sea coast, it was purchased by a man named Lars Larson. He lived at the city of Stavanger. He was known to be Quaker and had several followers. 

Quakers at that time were active in England, as well as other countries. 

He and some of his friends had notions of emigrating to North America because of greater religious freedom existing there, and also greater opportunities of economic advancement. This boat was removed to Stavanger, Norway, where it was remodeled, made more sea worthy and rechristened "Restaurationen". This reconstruction began in 1821 and was not completed until 1824. While this reconstruction work was going on a company of people were organized in Stavanger with Lars Olson as captain and Nels Erickson as first mate, while Kleng Pearson and

Knut Eide were sent to America in advance (in 1821) to find suitable locations, purchase land, and build houses for homes for the immigrants {13}-

which were to follow. Not until July 4, 1825, was this company ready and in shape to embark on this voyage to North America. After three months of a rather precarious voyage, they landed in New York City. 

After reaching this city they sailed up the Hudson River, then along the Erie Canal and finally settled on the shores of Lake Erie in western New 

York, where Kleng Pearson had found a location for them. 

This was the first Norwegian settlement in America and the beginning of the great Norwegian emigration from Norway to America that followed and which helped to spread the America fever throughout Norway. This Norwegian settlement was called the Kendall settlement and was located on the shores of Lake Erie in New York state. In this company were 52 people, six different families were represented. 

This small 45-ton Norwegian sail boat, Restaurationen, successfully crossing the Atlantic ocean in 1825, with 52 Norwegian emigrants is to Norwegian historians very interesting and in many respects, similar to the history of the Mayflower crossing the Atlantic ocean with British subjects in 1620.

Emigration from Norway began to increase following the successful voyage of this famous boat, Restaurationen. The emigrants came from various parts of Norway, but most of them came from the southwestern part of the country at first. Much has already been written by various historians about these early Norwegian immigrants to North America, about the hardships and privations which they encountered during this first year in the Kendall settlement in North America. The difficulty in obaining work and the expense and labor of clearing the land was great. The 

Kendal settlement was not altogether a success for many of the newcomers became sick, some died, others became discouraged and homesick, but nevertheless it paved the way for future immigrants which were to follow. 

Great interest in North America was created by letters sent back to friends in Norway.

Ten years later, or in 1836, two more ships arrived in North America, one called Norden and the other, Den Norske Klippe, which were fitted out and brought 200 more Norwegian immigrants to New York City. 

Many of them found their way to territories further west in the United States which had by this time begun to be open to settlers. Most of these came from Stavanger in Norway. The following year, or in 

1837, two more ships, one called Aegir, meaning Sea God, sailing from

Bergen, Norway, and the other named Eninghed, meaning Unity, sailing from Egersund Stavanger, arrived bringing a new and fresh supply of settlers. Settlements began to spring up rapidly in various parts of the country, but the destination of the immigrants invariably came to be Wisconsin.

The second Norwegian settlement of importance was called the Fox River settlement and it was located in La Salle county in Southern Illinois. 

The third Norwegian settlement was located in Chicago, and the fourth in Southern Wisconsin in Rock county. This was called the Jefferson --14--

Prairie settlement. The fifth Norwegian settlement was at Muskego, Waukesha and Racine counties, Wisconsin. The sixth and largest and most important of all the Norwegian settlements was Koshkonong or Kaskeland settlement, as it was called in Norwegian. It was located in 

Dane county, Wisconsin, and from where so many of the Norwegians came who later settled in Minnesota when it was open for settlement in the early fifties. con- The government of Norway had now become thoroughly aroused by such a great desire of its people to emigrate to North America and, scious of the evil effects which would result from such a great depopulation and its economic loss to the country, they made a desperate attempt to discourage and check such emigration. They put forth more efforts in the way of improving the economic conditions of its people at home. 

But in spite of all the efforts of the government and assisted by the clergy and others, emigration increased in great proportions among the people. 

The number of emigrants from Norway soon became greater in proportion to its population than any other European nation. ---15--

CHAPTER IV 

The Early Emigrant from Kvam Hardanger in Norway to Rush Creek Valley in Winona County, Minnesota, also Called Hardanger Dalen The Jefferson Prairie settlement, beginning in 1837, was the first permanent Norwegian settlement in Wisconsin. At first it became populated by a predominant number of immigrants from Sogn, in Norway. 

But there also were many immigrants from Kvam, in Hardanger, who settled there. Many of the Kvamingers were related to each other or at least were acquainted with each other in Norway. While there were settlers from Kvam that became members of the Koshonong settlement, there were more immigrants there from other parts of Norway. There was no definite boundary line between the settlements as there were between the counties, and as the immigrants often changed their locations from one place to another, the addresses of the early immigrants often varied or become mixed as to the settlements in which they lived. 

A man by the name of Gert Hovland is reported to be the first immigrant from Kvam Hardanger to settle permanently in all Wisconsin. 

He came there in 1831, but the first group of immigrants of any consequence coming from Kvam to Wisconsin was in 1837. They were Oddmund or Amund A Rosseland, Sjur or Syver Enderson Rosseland, Lars 

Schie, Berger Thorson, Nels Olson Alvik, Osbjorn Erickson Rykken or Oscar Erickson, as he was called later in America. These immigrants may not have come to America at the same time or together, or located in any one particular settlement there, but they were all early immigrants from Kvam, Norway, to Wisconsin. Neither does it appear that they became land owners in any particular place in Wisconsin, but later they moved farther west into Minnesota. In 1857, Lars Enderson Rosseland, a younger brother of Sjur, with his family also came from Kvam to America, stopping at first in Wisconsin, but later he located in Kandiyohi county, near Willmar, Minnesota. It was Lars Enderson Rosseland and his two sons and daughter-in-law that were murdered by the Sioux Indians during the Indian massacre of 1862. Guri Rosseland, Lars 

Rosseland's widow, it was, who became famous on account of her heroism in saving herself and bringing the bodies to the fort by ox team after the massacre. The State of Minnesota has recently erected a monument on the place in her honor, commemorating this deed.

More letters were sent back home by these first emigrants praising American laws and institutions and reporting plenty of work with good pay and good crops. All such favorable reports from America with the then existing conditions in Norway helped to increase the so-called American fever among the people of Kvam, and, as a result, in the spring of 1850 an exceptionally large group of Kvamingers, larger than ever before, came over and stopped in the Norwegian settlements of Wisconsin for at least some time or until they had become better acquainted and found -{{16} --

suitable locations in other places. Many came to the territory of Minnesota later.

In this group arriving in 1850, was Halvor Johannesen Rykkje, later called Halvor Johnson. After he arrived in America with the group 

Halvor spent a year in Chicago, which then was a village, before migrating further west into Wisconsin. Here on Jefferson Prairie, among other 

Kvemengers and countrymen he remained for two years. It was on 

Jefferson Prairie that Halvor became enthused with the great desire for traveling farther west into the territory of Minnesota for the purpose of obtaining land.

After coming to Minnesota and locating on his claim in Rush Creek valley he adopted the Norwegian style of architecture in constructing his house and stable. He also adopted Norwegian methods of farming and developing his land. In this valley he spent the best part of his life rearing a large family and developing his farm. He was a typical Nordic and Kvaminger. As his experience coicides so closely with that of the other settlers in Rush Creek Valley and also because the compiler of these historic events is more intimately acquainted with his personal history and affairs, his biography has been described more in detail than the rest of the Kvamingers who located in Hardanger Dalen. 

HALVOR, OLE, BRIGT AND JORGEN ARRIVING IN HARDANGER DALEN IN THE LATE SUMMER OF 1853 

It was in the early summer of 1853 that four young men who were employed as farm laborers by the farmers on Jefferson prairie, Rock county, Wisconsin, chanced to meet. They were Halvor Johnson Rykken, Ole Borson Ovrebirkeland, Brigt Brigtson Hellestvedt and Jorgen

Thorsen Ostenso, all emigrants from the district of Kvam in Hardanger, Norway. They had been members of the Vigor Ministerial organization (Prestejeld) in Kvam and had grown up in the same neighborhood -17-

and under similar environments, and therefore, they were more or less acquainted with one another, and each other's circumstances beforehand. 

Not all came to America at the same time for Halvor had arrived early in 1850, Ole later in 1850, Brigt in 1853, and Jorgen with his recent bride, Guro Botnen, very recently in 1853.

The present state of Minnesota was organized a territory in 1849. 

Two years later, while Minnesota was still a territory, or in 1851, the Federal government succeeded in negotiating a treaty with the Sioux Indians, in which the Indians ceded to the Federal government all the land in the Terrtory of Minnesota as far west as the Red River and Lake Traverse, with the exception of certain Indian reservations located along the Minnesota River. This agreement had opened much of southeastern Minnesota territory for settlement. 

The Federal govern ment was then encouraging emigration and granting those who were not yet naturalized the privilege of filing and settling on 160 acres of land as squatters. They could legally claim such land as their own by building a cabin on it and making certain improvements with the intention of making it their home. Later, when Minnesota would pass from a territory to a state, they could obtain legal title to their land from the Federal government by purchasing soldier script from ex-soldiers who had served in the war of 1812, and who had obtained such script as reward from the Federal government for their services. A great demand for land was created among the emigrants, and land seekers became numerous in Minnesota beginning in 1852 and much script was purchased by the immigrants.

Reports already had been brought back to Jefferson Prairie by returning prospectors, that southeastern Minnesota was a ideal country, suitable for the Norwegian farmers. Its topography was so similar to that of Norway. It had many beautiful streams coming down the valleys. flowing through rich meadows or dense forests. Many of the bluffs and the low lands were covered with large trees suitable for building material and fuel. The meadows would furnish abundant fodder for cattle. All this fulfilled the Norwegian emigrant's three prerequisites, viz., wood, water and meadow (Ve-Vatten og Slot) and furthermore, the streams were literally alive with fish. The forests were full of wild game and berries of various kinds.

The four young men above mentioned had pooled all their resources, purchased a yoke of oxen, a covered wagon or a so-called prairie schooner, and with the addition of a few farm implements, clothing, household goods, etc., they set out to find a home somewhere in southeastern Minnesota. They crossed the Wisconsin prairies in their covered wagon, reaching the Mississippi valley at McGregor, Iowa. Then, coming north through eastern Iowa, they reached the Root River valley in southeastern Minnesota about where the present city of Rushford in Fillmore county now stands. --18--

Reaching the Root River valley, they found that already many squatters had preceded them and much of the best land along the river had been claimed and filed upon by squatters. After exploring the surrounding country, they crossed the Root River at about where it is joined by Rush Creek, then penetrating up along the east side of the creek for about three miles, they encountered a sandy ridge, (later called the sandhill), where Rush Creek is joined by Pine Creek. They soon reached the top of the hill and from this vantage point they had a panoramic view of the wider and beautiful valley bordering Rush Creek up stream. 

This so pleased and enchanted them that they determined to locate here in this valley and make it their future home. Soon they selected their land and built their cabins as quatters along the banks of the creek close by the springs in the valley.

They found several Norwegians among the squatters along Root river, some of whom became permanent settlers there. These squatters had come earlier in (1853) the same year. Some of those found there (who were Norwegians) were Ole Tew, Ole Berland, Erick Glaen, Peter

Peterson Haslrude, Ole Torgeson and others who were of different nationalities. There were some Germans, a few Irish, and some from the New England states, which the Valley folks called Yankees. 

At this time there was a great deal of fraud perpetrated upon the early settlers. Fraudulent claims often were sold to the unsuspecting Norwegian newcomers. Claim jumping was a common occurrence. It, therefore, behooved the Valley folks to stick close to their claims and protect themselves in this regard, and in this, the Valley folks were determined to do, and to be of mutual assistance. 

The Valley squatters also made excursions up over the nearby ridges, inspecting the land there, and hoping, by chance, they might find even better locations. Whereas, the land on the ridges was more level and open, and easier to clear, they came back to the Valley satisfied to remain permanently there, for in the Valley there was better shelter from the cold Minnesota winters, if their houses and stables were built along the hillside among the tall and friendly trees. Also, here in the valley was found the bubbling springs of clear, cool, water in the summer, and that did not freeze up, even in winter. Again, here was close at hand tall, straight, trees from which splendid log houses and stables could be built. These settlers were not so much interested in large farms, but preferred smaller ones as those they had seen in Norway. The valley was more like Norway, more homelike to them and so they all resolved to look no further, but commenced to develop and hold fast to their claims, helping one another as best they could.

CHAPTER V 

Hardships Encountered by the Early Settlers in Hardanger Dalen For some time after the four first settlers came to the Valley, and on account of being financially embarrassed, they owned most of their personal property in common, and lived more or less together. This made them very much dependent upon each other not only for their support but also for care and help during illness or accidents, as well as protection from wild animals and the Indians who were their greatest fear. 

The Indians were not very friendly at that time. All this made the 

Valley folk good neighbors and true friends, which continued to endure the rest of the time which they spent in Hardanger Dalen. 

The land in Minnesota territory had not been surveyed by the Federal government. This did not occur until 1858, therefore, not until then could the Valley settlers obtain permanent title to their land from the government, but they possessed valid claims to it as squatters, which the government recognized. They only awaited the opportunity to purchase script from ex-soldiers, which easily was obtained for cash at $1.25 per acre. They felt safe in the possession of their claims and continued improving them as best they could, building cabins, stables, clearing land, and constructing roads from one place to another. Bridges were not built at first, but Rush creek was forded at most convenient places where the water was not too deep, and where the creek bottom was safe and solid with gravel.

Their first houses were log cabins, with log stables. They were situated on their claims on the sheltered hillside among large oak trees and in close proximity to live running springs. The buildings were constructed from logs gathered in the nearby forests. The cabins or house, as a rule, were one and a half or two stories high, having one or sometimes two windows with one door. On the first floor which always was one room, usually 12x20 feet, a bed was placed in one corner, a cupboard in the second, a table in the third, and a stove in the fourth. The upstairs or loft usually was reached by a ladder and used as a spare room for chests containing clothing, etc. The roofs of the houses invariably were thatched roofs, consisting of a layer of birch bark covered by a thick layer of earth on top of it. This made the roof impervious to water, and warm in winter, and not too hot in summer. There were no shingles obtainable at that time. As fireplaces were not in common use, chimneys were necessary. Between the logs, chunks of wood were placed, plastered over with clay or later cement. Their furniture such as beds, tables, and cupboards were homemade and benches were used for chairs. 

The stable walls mostly were made of logs, but often partly reinforced by sod turfs. The stable roofs generally were straw or hay. There were stalls for the work oxen and cows, but sheds provided for the young stock. 

Such sheds were constructed of poles covered over with straw for the --(24)3----

roofs and the walls. Into such sheds the young stock was turned loose. 

The winter supply of hay was stacked outside the stables, and had to be carried into them by the armfull and fed to the cattle.

The land out in Hardanger Dalen was partly meadow land, much of it hilly and rough, and not all fit for cultivation. Many of the hillsides were covered with large deciduous trees and hazel brush, while other hillsides were bare. The trees consisted of many white and bur oak, hickory and birch on the hillsides, while maple, walnut and elm were found along the creek bottoms. Linden, poplar, and willow also were in great abundance and many groves of wild crabapples, plums and cherries were found along the creek bottom. Smaller trees or shrubs such as gooseberries, cranberries were plentiful along the banks of the streams. 

Such were the conditions with which the Valley settlers had to contend when they first began to develop their farms out in Hardanger Dalen. 

Nevertheless, it wasn't many years before they were more comfortably situated as far as their houses and barns were concerned. 

Along the hillsides and even on top of some of the bluffs along the Valley, large quarries of lime stone deposits were found. These stones were deposited in layers of large, flat stones, superimposed one upon the other. They were from one to two feet thick. They would flake off or separate in even lines, making excellent building material. -25-

HALVOR JOHNSON'S HOUSE, NEW IN 1862 

Halvor Johnson's house, which he built in 1862, was partly constructed of such limestone rock. The basement and first story were constructed of solid stone, neatly hewn and mortered together. Later, when he built his new barn, the basement of it likewise was built of solid stone, and so was his spring house built of solid stone. Furthermore, he had constructed a retaining wall of stone around his house which was built in the hillside. A stone walk with steps was built from these large flat stones extending from the house to the spring house. This obviated a muddy path between these two places. 

All this construction has remained in fine condition after 80 years of continual use.

When Ole Axeness built his barn, it's foundation also was of solid stone. William Gaffney, living on the ridge close to the Valley, built a fine dwelling house entirely from this stone. He built a large basement to his barn, all the material taken from quarries along the hillside in the Valley. In the neighborhood of Rushford these same conditions existed along the bluffs there, and several of the largest and best store buildings in Rushford which are still standing and in good condition were constructed from such limestone in their entirety. 

As very little open land existed in the Valley at first, it required much time and labor to clear the land before the crops could be raised. 

It was necessary to grub out the trees by hand labor, therefore the fields were small and the crops meager. Little by little, as the land was cleared, 26-

oxen were employed to do the breaking or plowing, harrowing and hauling the heavy loads. They were good and strong and inexpensive to keep, but slow and cumbersome. Horses were scarce and expensive, therefore out of reach of the first settlers. These early pioneers well recall how elated they were over their first yoke of oxen and home made wagon (Buck-og-Bright med rullecubba), which served them well for several years until they were able to purchase horses. 

At first the seeding of the grain was done by hand and harrowed into the ground. The cutting of the grain was done by a hand scythe, called a cradle, then gathered up and bound by hand into bundles, and set up in shocks to dry before it was stacked and then threshed by a flail, or by having oxen walking over it on a floor and stepping out the grain. 

It then was carried in sacks on their backs to the mill or it was hauled to market by oxen requiring two days or more to take the load to market at Winona.

The Rush creek is only one of the many fine creeks flowing through the beautiful valleys in southeastern Minnesota. It has its origin in a spring about four miles south of the city of Lewiston in Winona county. 

Soon after its origin, Rush creek is crossed by the old Winona-Chatfield highway. It then courses onward down the valley, curving between hills which gradually become higher and higher on each side. After a distance of about three miles, Rush creek is joined by another creek or spring of almost its own size called the Furgison spring, which comes coursing down another valley called Furgison valley, and at about right angles to Rush creek valley. At this point the valley below becomes much wider and for a distance of about four miles it embraces several sections of good meadow land. On this wide expanse, Rush creek is joined by several fine springs, all uniting and entering the Rush creek, on what became Halvor Johnson's homestead. These springs became known as Brigtson's spring, Tew spring, Johnson spring, Logan's spring and again further down the valley the creek is joined by Lehnert's spring. Borson's spring and Moore Spring, where Rush creek is then. jointed by an almost equally large creek called Pine creek, which comes coursing down a valley called Pine creek valley. On its course down the valley, Rush creek is joined by several other springs. It reaches 

Root River at Rushford where it has become the size of a small river. 

This broad part of Rush creek valley, beginning at the junction of Rush creek and Furgison creek and ending where Pine creek joins Rush creek is the famous Hardanger Dalen, and which is the upper four miles of Rush creek valley proper.

At the time when the valley folk first came to the Valley, Rush creek was a majestic body of fresh water coursing down the valley from one side to the other, between the hills, and over the meadows. 

Now over rapids, where it is possible to ford it, and again whirling around a deep, silent eddies, where it would be dangerous for a crossing. It was the -273-

fisherman's delight for many years, for these eddies were filled with fish of various kinds, including many brook trout, which also invaded the numerous large adjacent springs. 

Rush creek was a treacherous stream subject to sudden and violent floods. When a hard rain shower would occur on the prairies north or west of the valley the water would accumulate very fast in the ravines and then suddenly find its way to Rush creek which would overflow its banks and at times fill the entire lowlands of the valley and then do a great deal of damage to the land, covering it with debris, carrying away fences, bridges, and destroying their roads. These floods often came on so suddenly that the cattle and horses were caught on the opposite side of it and would have to swim across it in order to reach home. It was bad enough when such floods occurred at daytime but much worse when it occurred at night. Often the cattle would be carried away and lost, as at one time Halvor Johnson lost a large band of sheep because they could not swim or negotiate the swift stream after getting their coats of wool completely saturated with water. It was the floods of Rush creek that caused so much damage to the mill dams by frequent washouts that the mill owners could not afford to keep up the repairs and were forced to abandon the enterprise and it was the mill dams that prevented the fish from coming up the creek from Root river to spawn which exteminated the once a fisherman's paradise.

At first there was much fencing to be done as the law at that time was such that the cattle were allowed to run at large and the farmers were required to protect their own fields by fencing around them. Such fencing required much labor to construct. The cattle would roam far away from home during the day not herded, and much trouble was had in finding them and bringing them home for the nightly milking. 

After they were brought home they were shut up in yards called cow pens for the night, and milked again in the morning before turning them loose. Bells were placed around the necks of some members in the herd so that they more easily could be found and located in the woods in the evening.

It was not customary to dehorn cattle at that time, and as there were many animals with especially long sharp horns which were a menace to their owners as well as to the other members of the herd, it became a custom for at least some of the farmers who were especially fond of their cattle, and who appreciated their value, to purchase horn buttons for them. It was not uncommon to find many mature cows in a herd wearing large brass knobs or buttons on the end of their horns. 

This was so as to make the horns more blunt and thus reduce the danger of injuring others. It also improved the general appearance of the animal, making it look more aristocratic. Many a housewife was most happy when the cows came home at dusk wearing the shiny horn buttons and brass bell a tinkling. It was customary with many housewives or milkmaids to treat their cows as they came home by giving them a pinch of salt and a bit of feed so as to make them more anxious to return home at night for the evening's milking.

All the cows were not well behaved cows, for there were those among the herd which became fence jumpers, and others who became fence breakers. Certain animals learned to jump high fences, without touching the rails at all. They would then luxuriate in the field during the day and when evening came, or if the farmer would come with a pitchfork, and his good dog Watch, they would immediately race back across the fence into the place where they belonged. Others would learn to break through the stoutest and best built fences. To obviate these annoyances the farmers invented several contrivances. Some of them would tie a rope around the horns of the animal to one of the front feet, making the animal walk with a limp. This would keep them from jumping the fence. Others would tie a board, fastened around the horns, so that it would hang over their forehead. This would keep them from jumping the fence. Another contrivance used by the farmer was a fence poker arranged around the animal's neck so that when the projected arm of the poker would strike the fence it would jab the animal in the neck. It was a cruel cumbersome and inconvenient arrangement, but quite effective. 

The principal wild animals the early settlers encountered were the deer, which were numerous, and the prairie and timber wolves, which {29}.

attacked and destroyed the deer, and also the farmer's sheep, but did not molest the cattle. Foxes, wildcats, raccoons, skunks, beavers, muskrats and minks were found. Smaller animals, such as, gophers, squirrels and weasels and so forth, were numerous. 

There were many poisonous rattle snakes in the barren hillsides.

The American buffalo was a thing of the past when the Valley settlers arrived, but evidence remained to show that they had been frequent visitors in the Valley during the winters of by-gone days. 

The Indians were the greatest menace with which the early settlers had to contend. Bands of Indians would come around, camping along the streams, trapping muskrats, beavers, and mink and often begging for food and worrying the settlers who were very much afraid of them. These Indians would steal and carry away things, too, if they could conveniently do so, but many of them were friendly when unmolested. These Indians belonged to the Chippewa and Sioux tribes. These tribes were not friendly to each other, and had often been at war with each other, nor did they look with favor upon the encroachment of their hunting grounds by the palefaces. 

Many stories are told by the early settlers of encounters with the Indians. On one occasion a band was camping in the neighborhood of the Johnson farm when it suddenly became cold, with a snow storm raging, Mr. Johnson permitted them to camp in the basement of his house. for a week, until the stormy weather abated, but they had been so filthy that it required much work to clean up and get rid of the foul odor afterwards. The Valley people had lived in Hardanger Dalen eight years when the Sioux Indian massacre occurred and which created such a great fear of the Indians among all the early settlers of southern Minnesota that it made their lives most unhappy for some time.

In December, 1856, an unusually heavy snow fall occurred over a large section of the country, and which covered the ground several feet deep. A few days later it rained some, and this again was followed by a freeze causing a slippery crust of ice to form over a deep layer of soft snow. A man could easily walk on top of this crust of hard snow without breaking through, but horses or cattle would break through, making it almost impossible for them to travel through it. 

One of the settlers who had already purchased a horse, concocted the idea of making a sort of snow shoes for his horse, which he succeeded in teaching the animal to use. During the winter this man and horse were in great demand to officiate at funerals. This horse would convey the coffin and corpse on a sled to the cemetery and the people, including the minister, would follow on snow shoes.

This winter was a most disastrous time for the wild deer. A dog or a wolf easily could run on top of the snow but the deer would break through on account of their sharp feet, and become almost submerged and helpless in the deep snow. Men could overtake a deer with a dog and kill it -30

with a club. It is said that this winter almost exterminated the wild deer in this part of the country. What the settlers did not kill were killed by the wolves. This occurrence also was a God-send to the settlers who were in great need of meat. Up to this time game was very plentiful in 

Hardanger Dalen and vicinity. 

On the 10th of February, 1858, a party of gentlemen left Chatfield for the purpose of slaughtering any game that should cross their path. 

At that date game of every species abounded in Fillmore county, and especially may this be said of the country immediately surrounding Hardanger Dalen. The gunning party was having good luck and the indications were that they would be bountifully repaid for their labor, when, by the accidental discharge of one of the guns, a member of the group was shot in the leg below the knee, breaking both bones and otherwise mangling his limb in a horrible manner. Immediately he was taken to Chatfield and placed under the treatment of a doctor of that place, but in spite of good care it became necessary to amputate the leg.

By 1861, a number of Minnesota settlers were beginning to replace their oxen with horses, and as a result of this increase in the horse population in the state, horse thieves began to make their appearance, with the result that many settlers lost their horse. Such reports made the settlers feel uneasy as to the safety of their own horses, and it became a common custom among them to place a padlock on their stable doors at night. They would not wait until the horse was stolen to lock the stable door, and they also paid strict attention to the house dog's warning at night. At this time a Root River settler lost his span of horses. He traced them up through Rush Creek valley, up into and through Furgison valley where they had been hid. Then up through Fremont township to near 

Pilot Mound, where one of the animals had been adandoned by the roadside in an exhausted condition. The thieves and other horse never were found.

Evidently, after gaining the open western prairie country, the other horse was sold. The Valley folk were more fortunate, for no record has been found showing that they lost horses. The reason for this may have been that as yet not many of the Valley farmers had been able to purchase valuable horses, and again the Valley did not afford a good place to carry out such actions successfully. This was on account of the topography of the country. Not until after a number of horses had been stolen and a most drastic law enacted, providing for the most severe punishment for horse stealing, was the menace abated.

CHAPTER VI Home Environment During the Early Days in Hardanger Dalen 

At first and for a few years following the settlement in the valley, some of the valley folk were often lonesome and occasionally afflicted with rather severe attacks of homesickness. Their thoughts would revert to their former homes in Kvam, then often wishing themselves back there. 

Such a wish they knew was out of the question of fulfillment and could not be attained. This mostly was on account of financial stringency. The only sensible thing for them to do, they knew, was to make the best of it and keep busy with the many things necessary to be done in order to improve their own homes. 

A SETTLER ESCORTING THE MINISTER IN 1861 

Sundays were especially long and lonesome days for them, for they always refrained from active work on holidays and there were only a few ways of being entertained. Again, they would recall the tolling of the church bells in Vikor at home, reminding the people there of devotional services to be held on Sundays, all of which only increased their loneliness and longings for their former homes. 

There were those among them who, when it was impossible for them to attend church services on Sunday mornings, found much comfort in reading the text at home and singing familiar religious songs, while others would amuse themselves by securing a goodly supply of fish from Rush creek, using a dip net. 32}

When night came and especially so during the long cold winter evenings the housewives were usually busy knitting or sewing by hand until late at night, while the men were busy making such necessary articles as benches, tables, chairs, beds, cupboards, etc. Many such home made articles have been preserved which showed in some instances much skill and artistic taste. CHAPTER VIII Retrospective Views 

It was frequently remarked by some of the early settlers of Hardanger Dalen after they themselves had grown old how much improved living conditions had become since the time when they began the struggle for existence, living in the valley. There was simply no comparison between. the two because it was so much better at the present day. 

They would recall that when they began living in the valley, they did not even have kerosene lamps or lanterns to assist them in doing their work in the darkness and now such were plentiful and no more were they dangerous of being explosive. At first and in early days, they often did not even have tallow candles ot use in their houses and were compelled to use grease cups with an improvised wick hanging over the side of it, and when this wick was lighted, the fire would furnish only a faint glimmer of light, or they would depend on the light from the open grate in the stove burning briskly. They, therefore, would do their chores and other work by day light as much as possible and spend the evenings in darkness or semi-darkness around the stoves in the cabins. It was wonderful to know though, how much some of them would accomplish under such handicaps. 

How well the women could knit and sew and do so many other things in darkness was remarkable, but all their time and energy was directed to useful industry.

They would generally rise at daybreak or a little before and begin work and retire at dusk. They often depended upon the crowing of the roosters at the break of day to wake them up in the morning, for they had learned by experience to know that roosters were quite dependable. 

They had no clocks or alarm clocks themselves. When the cock begins to crow, morning will soon come, they knew, but occasionally the roosters would make a mistake and crow at midnight which was often interpreted by superstitious ones that it meant there would be a death occurring in the neighborhood very soon. The poor rooster would be forgiven for disturbing their sleep and even praised for having such supernatural power of prophesying the coming events. Not always was it realized that a rooster did not distinguish between a rising moon with moonlight and a rising sun with sunlight. And the crowing began with the emergance of light regardless of the time of the day. They were guided in determining the time of day by the sun and the sun dials available, of course, only on sunny days. "Early to bed and early to rise, makes you healthy, wealthy and wise," was their motto.

All this made them accustomed to toddle around in darkness in doing their chores and other work about the place. -42-

CHAPTER X The Existing Economic Conditions 

The period beginning with the early settlement of Hardanger Dalen was a period of great financial depression and money stringency throughout the country in general. The early settlers who were compelled to borrow money were forced to pay exhorbitant interest rates. As much as 

100% per annum with elaborate mortgages to secure the debt was sometimes asked by money lenders, while 50% was more common. As Minnesota was still a territory, no legal rate of interest was yet established and the money lenders could do as they pleased about charging interest and the settlers were therefore often hard pressed, but as a rule the valley people helped each other out in this respect by loaning each other money and the necessities of life and such other articles of farm implements as was necessary. Often they did not even require a personal note for such debts, but the obligations were invariably paid in full. 

There is no record on file where the valley folks lost their homes as a result of mortgage foreclosure or being hard pressed by his neighbor nor even a lawsuit was ever instituted.

During the period while the early Norwegian settlement of Wisconsin and Minnesota were in progress, that is, during the early 1840's and '50's, a wide-spread epidemic of Asaitic cholera raged periodically over a large part of the civilized world. This disease had its origin in Asia and where it was most prevelant and severe, but it also spread to Europe and the Americas. In North America it was most severe in the seaboard cities such as New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, St. Louis and New Orleans,

In 1854 it was at its height in the United States, and here caused much sickness, with a large mortality rate among the early Norwegian immigrants. While they were on their ocean voyage and journeys across the country and before reaching their destinations many were attacked and died. It also attacked many of these early settlers while they were still living in the Middle West on their claims. This disease so often is fatal that it destroys about 50 per cent of its victims. It often causes death in a few hours but more often, a few days from its onset. It is characterized by a severe and sudden onset of abdominal cramp, profuse diarrhea and vomiting, great and rapid wasting and profound shock. It soon leaves the victim cold externally but with a high fever of the internal organs and a very rapid pulse. The patient, though at first conscious, passes into coma and dies.

This disease is caused by a germ which usually gains ingress to the stomach in the drinking water or food and causes a violent inflammation of the intestines. The disease is of a short duration and can easily be checked by proper sanitary precautions as the germs are easily destroyed. This disease was not more severe or fatal among the early 

Norwegian settlers than the people living in other localities but when they were stricken on the ocean voyage or on the journey across the country many died suddenly and before reaching their destination. Thus, many -·{45}...

men were suddenly left widowers and many women left widows with a family of children dependent upon them. There were instances where the husband left home in good health with a load of produce for market and intent to purchase supplies. Contracting the disease while in the city or on his way home and dying before he reached his journey's end. Often a most pathetic circumstance resulted for these people were often short of means, living far from neighbors and help and in poor houses with inadequate food and clothing, thus becoming acutely destitute. 

While Asaitic cholera was a severe and fatal disease, causing much distress, grief, and economic loss among the early settlers, it was not the only or the worst affliction with which they had to contend, for another disease called Ague, or Malaria, also was prevalent in many of the newly settled localities. At that time it was believed that malaria broke out in epidemic forms like other pestilence of unknown origin. This disease also was caused by a germ and is found to prevail most frequently in marshy or swampy regions or along river banks as it also did here among the early settlers. This disease is contracted in a different way from cholera for the germ gains ingress into the human body through the bite or sting of certain mosquitoes. Not until very recent years was it known that mosquitoes carried and transmitted the disease from one sick individual to one healthy and that it cannot be carried in any other way than by certain mosquito bites. Probably the most deadly enemy man has ever known is malaria. Able authorities say that in all probabilities it has done more than any other single destructive agents, floods, tempest, frost, war, famine, or earthquake, to check the progress of the human race and keep man savage, feeble, and half civilized. The real foe of the early settlers in the Middle West as well as in the Atlantic and Southern states was malaria.

Malaria has to its credit 25 per cent of all the mortality in the tropics and subtropical countries. Preventing the mosquitoes from biting a sick individual suffering from malaria will prevent his supply of germs and thus stop the spread of the disease.

Another disease quite prevalent among the early settlers was typhoid fever, often called climatic fever. They believed it was caused by the change of their climate and had no notion of the fact that it also was due to a germ gaining ingress into their stomach in the water or food supply and being brought there by a common housefly. It also was an acute infection disease which lasted from three weeks to three months or more. 

As a rule the patient would recover if properly treated but this was the days before the germ theory was understood and the disease was often improperly diagnosed and improperly treated and therefore ended fatally. 

It was often mistaken for appendicitis which was unknown to exist at hat time and many cases of the so-called climatic fever thu terminting fatally must have in reality been acute appendicitis. --(46) ----

City of Rushford, Minnesota

The city of Rushford bears the same name as the township in which is located and lies within the boundary of Fillmore county, while Rush Creek Valley lies in the Township of Hart, adjoining it, but in Winona county.

Rushford is located in the Root River valley proper at a place where Root River is joined by Rush Creek, and which is about 25 miles west of LaCrosse, Wisconsin. At this place there are several smaller valleys converging, and was in ancient times the meeting place of several Indian trails. It was well known to the Indians who inhabited this region. It was one of the finest hunting, fishing and camping grounds in all southern Minnesota. It is a most scenic place, resting as it does between the high hills or bluffs which almost surround it. THE LOGAN BLUFF AND ROCK AS SEEN FROM THE VALLEY SCHOOLHOUSE CHAPTER XII A Rush Creek Valley Legend 

Along the east side of Hardanger Dalen a prominent hill or bluff has been very noticeable ever since the valley was first inhabited, for it is a little higher and more prominent than any other bluff in the entire valley. From its very top projected a high rock, in all probability the remnant of prehistoric glacial activity. The bluff and particularly this rock always has been conspicuous. From its summit, which was often scaled by the young people especially on holidays, a splendid view could be had of the surrounding landscape and farmsteads below. Peculiarly enough the front projecting part of the rock was cleft crosswise, leaving a deep split or crack across it and through this outer projecting part was a large hole large enough for an adult person to crawl through from one side to the other, which was often done. 

This bluff was called the Logan bluff and the high rock the Logan rock for it was situated directly above the Logan house and stables. 

Mrs. Logan often expressed herself as fearing this rock would some day break loose and come tumbling down the bluff causing death and destruction in its wake. This idea was scoffed at by most all the valley people for they said it had stayed there in that position ever since the world was created. One day several years later, after the Logans had -51-

moved away and Mr. and Mrs. Logan both were dead, during a violent electrical storm the large rock did break loose and came thundering down into the valley below. Fortunately it happened when no one was present and no special damage was done but it changed the aspect of the valley's appearance and many there were who said they regretted the occurance and wondered at Mrs. Logan's prophecy which came true. 

An ancient legend has been told concerning this Logan rock and bluff, the authenticity of which is problematical, but nevertheless there are certain geological facts existing and the topography of this section of this valley country is such that it might lead one to believe that there is at least some truth connected with it.

In ancient times when southern Minnesota was under the domination of the Sioux and Chippewa Indians and when they held the entire country as their undisputed domain or hunting ground, there lived a tribe of Sioux Indians along the banks of the Mississippi with headquarters at Wabashaw and on the Minnesota prairie now called Winona This tribe was ruled over by an Indian chief named Fighting Beaver and who had a beautiful daughter named Red Bird. Although Red Bird had many suitors and had received presents a white pearl from one Indian brave and a pink pearl from another young Indian brave belonging to her own band with proposals that they wished to marry her, but her father was obstinate and would not consent to her marriage to either of them. His choice was 

Old Eagle Eye, a cross but wealthy Indian widower who had subjected his former wife to much drudgery. Eagle Eye had promised Fighting 

Beaver, Red Bird's father, many ponies for her, but Red Bird would not consent to marry him. 

An Indian trader who had chanced to see the pearls which Red Bird carried in a shammy skin bag around her neck, had offered her some beautiful red shawls for the pink pearl but did not want to give her much for the white one. She therefore came to the conclusion that the pink pearl was valuable and decided to keep it herself.

Another Indian band with headquarters out on the Root river and whose chief was Silver Cloud, annually held a celebration or pow-wow together with the Mississippi band and at such times there was much feasting, dancing and drinking amongst these two Sioux bands who were friendly but who were bitter enemies of the Chippewa tribe. Their headquarters for the celebration was in Rush Creek valley, which was midway between Root river and the Mississippi river. The best camping place was beneath the Logan bluff which the Indians called High hill. Here the Rush Creek valley is the widest and here several small valleys join the main Rush Creek valley and here several large springs also join Rush creek, making it an ideal camping place. Here the fishing and hunting was good, therefore their annual celebration was held here with signal fires placed on top of High rock to notify them if spies belonging to the Chippewa tribe should be hovering near with intent to do them harm. ---523-

Silver Cloud, the Root river chief, having lost his wife and all his children in an epidemic disease, had adopted a young brave from his own band named Fast Feet who would do all his bidding. When 

Fast Feet saw and met Red Bird, he fell in love with her and asked Silver Cloud, his superior, to be permitted to marry her and take her as one of their own into their band. To this Silver Cloud readily agreed for she was also a Sioux Indian and very beautiful, but her father, Fighting Beaver, would not consent, for he was under obligations to his old friend Eagle Eye, who would give him many ponies for her.

The result was that while Fighting Beaver, Old Eagle Eye, Silver Cloud and the rest of the braves were feasting and drinking freely of the finest fire water, Fast Feet and Red Bird eloped and hid in the large hole on High Hill rock. When the celebration was over, Fighting 

Beaver and Old Eagle Eye were ready to go back home, they missed Red Bird. When they inquired about her, they were informed that she had in all probability already gone home to her own people but when they returned home they could not find her. They were then too sick from the celebration to create much fuss. 

Later, after sobering up, Fighting Beaver threatened to send soldiers to Root River after his daughter, but as his braves did not want trouble among their own tribe for they had all they could do to keep their enemies the Chippewa in check. Therefore, Fighting Beaver gave up and agreed to let Red Bird remain with the Root River people. They were the same kind of Indian blood anyway. Red Bird and Fast Feet married and Fast Feet became the head of his band and had a large and happy family.

The two bands continued their annual celebrations in Rush Creek valley. During the elopement of Red Bird and Fast Feet and while hiding on the High Hill rock, Red Bird lost her pink pearl somewhere thereabout. Every year excursions were made to High Hill rock in search of the pink pearl which was never found.

Many Indian arrow heads were found in the vicinity of High Hill rock and also some snail shells. The Indians could understand how the arrow heads had come there, but they could not believe that a snail could have crawled to the top of High Hill rock. CHAPTER XIII Halvor Johnson and Other Early Settlers in Hardanger Dalen 

Halvor Johnson was born on May 9, 1824, on the gaar Rykken in the district of Kvam in Hardanger, southern Norway. According to the prevailing custom of that time, he was baptized shortly after his birth and given the name of Halvor Johanneson Rykkje and his name was so recorded in the church records at Ostenso. His parents were Johannes and Hildegunde Rykkje. He was the oldest son, but the third child in a family of seven children, five sisters and two brothers. 

His childhood and youth were spent at home with his parents who owned a house in the gaar or village of Rykkje and some land close by. 

At about the age of six, as was customary, he was first taught how to read by his own parents while at home. Later he was sent to the community school in common with the other children of the community for short terms each year. When about sixteen years of age he registered as a member of the confirmation class, receiving instructions principally of a religious nature from the district minister. He was then confirmed and admitted as a member of the local church organization, a Protestant Lutheran church under state supervision. The instruction in the community school consisted principally of learning how to read and committing to memory the Lord's Prayer, the Ten Commandments, the Three Articles of Faith, a few religious songs and some Bible history. The instruction in geography and arithmetic or writing was very meager or limited at this time. A large percentage of the children did not learn how to write or figure at all, but most all could read quite well. Addition, subtraction, multiplication and short division were taught in the community school but fractions were not taught regularly outside of the Seminaries, and as there were no such institution in their vicinity, instruction in these subjects were not received by him, but on account of his interest in religion and his desire of reading, he became a fairly good reader for his time. At this time Norway had not yet established public schools or school houses. The educational instruction was maintained by private contributions.

At first, Halvor intended to become a sea-faring man, but at the age of 26, or in 1850, he, together with several of the people of his community was seized with what was called the "America Fever," or a great desire to go to America. He gave up everything else and joined a company of young people who went to America where it was said the opportunities for advancement were much better than in his own country. 

In America they could obtain free land and establish a home of their own under better economic and social conditions.. Before this time (1850) not many people from Kvam had immigrated to America, but there were a few who had come over and located in Wisconsin. They had been successful financially and had written back home of the wonderful opportunities existing in America. This was what brought on the so-called →→→{55}-

intense America fever in Kvam and which caused a large number of the young people there to come on together to America. In the spring of 

1850, a company was organized which was the first company of any considerable number of Kvamingers to leave Hardanger, Norway, at one time for America, but for several years following, there were many more who followed their example.

The itinerary of their trip consisted first of a voyage from Ostenso to Bergen by a small sail boat where they were transferred to a larger sail ship across the Atlantic ocean.

Going to America at this time was an undertaking of great importance and many who went did not expect to return to see their own country and friends again, for the trip was too hazardous and too expensive. The uncertainties were so great that it was almost like going to war for them. 

Correspondence, too, was difficult and to communicate by letter was inconvenient and not often indulged in. Many could not write well enough themselves or dictate a letter. It is said that often men, after reaching America, obtained the services of some good friend to write his love letters for him and persuade some good girl to come along over to 

America as well.

Taking into consideration the economic conditions then existing in Norway, one can easily imagine the efforts it required to prepare for the undertaking of going to this new world and establish a home. There were many new home made (wadmal) suits to be made for both the men and the women, new home made shoes and so forth, besides they had to make themselves chests as they had no satchels at that time in which to carry and store their belongings. Halvor, as well as all the rest of the passengers, had provided himself with two such chests or trunks in which he stored all his worldly possessions, a small hand chest, that could be carried around like a suit case and a large storage chest, which he obtained from his sister, Kristi. These chests of Halvor's were hand made and were of good quality, very well made and provided with a large iron lock and key and decorated ornamentally by painting his name and destination on the outside. The larger chest was for clothing, while the smaller one contained the food. It was filled with flat bread, dried beef, brick cheese, sugar, coffee and such articles of food as would not spoil, for he had to provide himself with provisions on the journey and enough to last three months.

The voyage from his home port, Ostenso, to Chicago, required nine weeks and entailed great hardships. The route of their sail ship was not across the North Sea, but sailing into the Atlantic Ocean from Bergen, going north of the British Isles to New York, then up the Hudson River by steam boat and along the Erie Canal to Buffalo, and finally by boat on the Great Lakes to Chicago.

Whether Halvor remained in Chicago on his arrival in America, or if he went to stay with other Norwegian settlers over in Wisconsin at first, is not known, but shortly after reaching America we find him in Chicago. 562

which then was only a small village. Here he had obtained work in a barrel factory or copper shop. His particular work was that of making hoops for barrels from young willow saplings. He continued with his work more than one year. Barrels were in great demand at this time as they were used for packing and storing and shipping such articles as apples, meat, fish, molasses, etc. This work was not just to his liking and he, therefore, migrated further west into Wisconsin to a district situated south of Beloit and which was called Jefferson Prairie. Here a 

Norwegian settlement already had been established. Many of the settlers also were from Kvam in Norway, the same place from which he had come.

On Jefferson Prairie, Halvor remained for some time, being employed as a farm hand and learning how to farm according to American methods and saving his money. 

During the years Halvor spent working on farms down in Wisconsin on Jefferson Prairie, he had often wished he could find some suitable place in which to establish a home for himself, somewhere in these beautiful valleys in the United States. He was soon able to persuade three other young men to join him and together they set out to find new homes in the territory of Minnesota, about which they had heard so much.

Halvor Johnson's original farm consisted of a quarter of section or 160 acres of land. It was upon this land that he had filed a claim as a squatter in 1853, while Minnesota was still a territory. At this time the land had not been surveyed by the government. Minnesota became a territory in 1849 with St. Paul as the seat of government or capitol, and in 1858, it became a state, and the land was then surveyed and the settlers were granted the privilege of obtaining a patent or deed to their land directly from the government. The laws were such according to the pre-emption rights, the settler was entitled to obtain 160 acres of land by fulfilling certain requirements and paying the government $1.25 per acre. 

All these Norwegian squatters had not yet obtained their citizenship papers and they therefore purchased script issued to exsoldiers of the war of 1812 and obtained their title in that way. 

Halvor first obtained a patent or deed to 40 acres of land in Section 20 on May 3, 1859. This patent or deed was signed by President Buchanan. The same year he obtained a deed to 120 acres adjoining his 

40 acres in Section 19, also signed by President Buchanan on June 1, 1859.

He had obtained a script on this 120 acres from an ex-soldier named George Cleveland, a captain who had served the United States in the war of 1812 and had been granted this script as a bonus. This gave 

Halvor 160 acres of land in Sections 19 and 20. 

Peculiarly enough, these deeds had not been recorded until 1908, six years after Halvor's death or 49 years after it had been issued. There had been no questions that had arisen and Halvor had paid his taxes from the very beginning, therefore there must have been some record kept at the county courthouse of the legality of his legitimate claim to this 160 acres of land.

In 1870, on July 7, he purchased 40 acres of railroad land, which was joining his land, from the Winona and St. Paul railroad. For this 40 acres, he paid $280, or at the rate of $7 an acre. On January 17, 1874, he purchased 20 acres more of railroad land from Southern Minnesota Railroad company paying $6 an acre. All this railroad land was in Section 17, but joining his original quarter section. On May 27, 1876, he purchased 18 acres more of land from a neighbor named Wm. Gaffney, and for which he paid $50. This land also was located in Section 17 but joining the 80 acres of railroad land which he recently purchased. This land, he thus purchased, was called the Bluff Land, and out of the 98 acres he had puchased, he was able to clear and make about 50 acres of good field. The field was located on top of the bluff and was called the Bluff field. A very steep road had been constructed up to it a distance of almost a mile from his home buildings. It was an inconvenient and laborious road to travel and transport machinery and grain up and down this road and it represented much toil for the men and horses. -65-

In 1885, he purchased 40 acres of land from a Mr. N. D. Morse, which was known as the Logan 40, for which he paid $600. This 40 acres was located in Section 19. It made him the owner of the Northwest quarter of Section 19 as well as 136 acres in Sections 17 and 20. 

In 1890, he purchased 20 acres of land from the Southern Minnesota Railroad company which was known as the Erick Tew place. This last purchase increased the size of the farm to one-half section less 2 acres. 

About one-half of his farm was good land and contained the three requisites all the early Norwegian settlers sought to obtain and which was expressed in the Norwegian language as "Ve, vaten og slot." In the

American language this means, "Wood, water and meadow." 

By 1865, Halvor had succeeded in increasing the acreage of his field considerably and he then purchased his first harvesting machine. It was called a Kerby reaper which was propelled by horses or oxen. 

It was a heavy unhandy contrivance, but a great improvement upon the hand cradle, principally in the amount of work that could be accomplished in a day. It required two men to operate it, one to set as a driver, seated in the front seat and a second one on the rear seat who would rake the grain off the platform in sizable amounts for bundles as it was cut. The grain afterwards bound by hand into bundles and set up in shocks to dry before gathered into stacks and threshed. He continued with his Kerby reaper for a few years when he purchased a new and improved harvester. 

This was called a Champion self-raking reaper. It was a great improvement upon the Kerby as it was a better machine in every respect and could be operated by only one man as a driver, but it also was heavy and too much for a team of two horses to handle, especially on such hilly fields as most of Halvor's was. He, therefore, often hitched an extra horse in front and one of the boys could ride horseback at tandem, guiding it. It was a long hard day's work for a boy to sit on the back of a horse all day guiding it around the field, especially so in the Minnesota hot harvest weather, but it was done in that fashion. It would require five or six men to keep up binding the grain after the Champion reaper when the grain was moderately heavy. (Once while John was riding tandem in cutting grain, a heavy line pulled him off the horse and he was nearly run over by the reaper.)

In 1880, another very important and useful patent was perfected. It was a twine self-binder. In 1882, the latest and best harvesters were the twine self-binders and Halvor purchased a new Minneapolis Twine Selfbinder that year, the first one in Hardanger Dalen. This eliminated much. help during harvest and was one of the greatest advancements benefiting the farmers that had yet been invented, but this harvester also was too heavy for one team of two horses to negotiate, but it had been fixed so that three horses abreast could be hitched to it and could handle it. As the farmers increased the acreage of their fields, it was necessary to improve and increase the speed of threshing the grain. In the '60's this was a serious question for the valley farmers as a threshing rig was an expensive thing and very cumbersome to move around on such hilly and poor roads as existed in Hardanger Dalen. It also required a large crew of men to operate it This first threshing outfit the valley folk had was a J. I. C. Case threshing outfit. It was purchased by Wm. Gaffney, Ole Axeness and Halvor Johnson as partners, each one had a third interest. It consisted of two main parts, a horse power machine and a grain separator, or threshing machine proper. The power machine was put and kept in motion by five, two horse teams of horses going around in a circle. 

This power machine was connected to, the separator, not by a belt, but by a long heavy iron tumbling rod. The whole contrivance, when put in motion, made a loud, musical and exhilerating noise, stimulating all the men into great activity. The threshing was the great event of the season and all the neighbors participated in doing the work and exchanging work with each other. They would get up before daylight in the morning and groom their horses so as to be ready to start threshing by daylight, and would continue threshing as long during the evening as they could see. 

From fourteen to eighteen men were required to run a threshing rig in those days. One man would stand on the horse power with a long whip seeing to it that all the ten horses pulled in unison and did their share. 

One man, and sometimes two, was required to feed the grain into the machine, one or two band cutters, two pitchers when the grain was threshed from the stack, one to measure the grain as it was received in one-half bushel receptacles and another to hold the sacks so the grain could be emptied into it and then be lifted away, two or three to haul the grain to the grainery, one to three men to take care of the straw and besides a machinist or tender. The owners or proprietors of the machine would change about doing certain work. One would feed the separator for an hour, which was very hard work, then he would exchange and tend the machine, seeing that it was oiled, etc., for an hour or so and then he would do the driving for a turn. Often boys would be engaged to do the lighter work such as cut bands, hold sacks and at times, stand on the power and drive the horses.

This partnership lasted for several years. They did all the threshing for the valley farmers. Later, Hans Skaar purchased Wm. Gaffney's interest and continued the operations of threshing for many years taking care of the threshing for the valley residents. 

Johnnie Johnson, Halvor's oldest son, recalls and relates the following incident in connection with his early threshing experience: While he was still a lad of about ten years, he was called upon to help take care of the straw pile during threshing. Until then it had been customary to hitch one horse to each end of a long rope fastened to a heavy pole and in this way the two horses with the two drivers would haul straw down into the field away from the thresher, where it was later burned. This method -(69)

was used only in case the threshing was done out in the field and the straw was not saved or preserved. Ole Axeness had an old horse named 

Rock, which was noted for having a lot of horse sense, or more than what was usually allotted to a horse. Johnnie thought of the idea of using one horse and one driver by connecting this one horse with two long ropes, each attached to either end of the long pole and in this way hauling the straw away. Johnnie Johnson and old Rock Axeness did fine work. and succeeded very well in keeping the straw away from the threshing machine. When they came to settle the bill and paying little Johnnie for his work, his father and Ole Axeness thought that it would be just enough to pay him onehalf of the day's wages of an ordinary grown-up man as he was only a boy, but Wm. Gaffney thought differently. He claimed that as Johnnie had done the work of two men and used only one horse instead of two, he was entitled to double pay and which was granted, and which was greatly appreciated by this young thresher. Wm. 

Gaffney had a large family and until this time they were all girls. Later he had a boy of his own, which he called Johnnie. At this time Johnnie was the only boy in the neighborhood that was old enough to assist in the threshing and consequently was a favorite. 

Taking into consideration all the grubbing and clearing of the farm, the building of such buildings with stone masonry for all foundations, and the construction of roads in such a hilly country, one readily can see that it all represents an immense amount of toil. 

He could, with profit and generally did, maintain from four to six work horses, ten to twenty milk cows, forty or fifty sheep, pigs, chickens and much young stock, horses and cattle. He was especially fond of his milk cows during his entire life time and superintended the milking himself.

His farm was considered a large one, the largest in the valley and a fairly good one comparing it with adjoining farms. It was best adapted for raising small grain and stock, therefore, from the sale of wheat, cattle, pigs, dairy products and occasionally a pair of young horses he made a good living. He was considered well-to-do by his neighbors, and according to the rating of men of his time and station of life. 

At the time of his death, he had by economy and hard work, become the possessor of about one-half section of farm land unencumbered. The buildings upon it were comfortable and adequately sufficient to meet his requirements, but nothing more.

During the summer of 1892, Halvor's health gradually began to fail and by August of that year, he was unable to attend to his work and remained in bed much of the time. 

After repeated consultation with his attending physician, Doctor Heiberg of Rushford, his illness was diagnosed as cancer of the stomach and the prognosis pronounced very unfavorable as to his recovery. The peculiar and unusual thing was that this verdict of his attending physician. -70-

so thoroughly coincided with his own views and wishes that he was made extremely happy. He felt much relieved and seemed so cheerful in anticipation of the coming event.

Halvor Johnson died October 3, 1892, while at his home upon the old homestead in Hardanger Dalen where he had lived for 40 years. An exceptionally large number of friends came to attend the funeral and to pay their last respects to a man they had learned to love by long association. Rev. Arne Boyum, his old pastor, and Rev. G. O. Viks, conducted the services, eulogizing his many good qualities, his great Christian faith, a life that had not been spent in vain. He was interred in a private lot in the Hauges cemetery on the North Prairie where he had attended so many funerals during his time and where so many of his relatives and friends rested. He was next to the last one of the original settlers in the valley to pass on, but not without a distinct impression upon his community. Guro Skaas, the original Guro Thorson, now was the only surviving original settler in Hardanger Dalen.

OLE BORSON 

Ole Borson or Ole Baarsen Ovre Birkeland, as he was called while in Norway, also was one of the four first settlers in Hardanger Dalen. 

He was born in Kvam Hardanger, Norway in 1817. His parents were Baard Olson Birkeland and Kari Jensdater. He emigrated to 

America in 1850, landing on Jefferson prairie, Wisconsin, where he spent three years in the Norwegian settlement there. In 1853 he joined Halvor 

Johnson, Brigt Brigtson and Jorgen Thorson and came to the territory of Minnesota where he filed as a squatter on a claim of 160 acres of land, the same as did the rest of his companions. He was fortunate in obtaining a fine piece of valley land with a splendid spring on it, where he built his cabin late in the fall of that same year. Here he lived as a bachelor for two years. He purchased script from an ex-soldier and obtained title to his land through this usual method. 

Seri Skeie, a young girl also from Kvam and who had come to America with her brother, Knute, in 1853, and who now lived with him on his claim in Pine Creek valley, came to visit and renew old acquaintances in Hardanger Dalen with the result that Ole Borson and Seri became engaged and were married by a Justice of the Peace in Peterson, in 1856. 

The Borsons continued to live permanently on their claim. In 1860, they received title to the land which was signed by President James Buchanan. This claim was a Government grant to James Prescott for services in the war of 1812. This was redeemed and transferred to Ole 

Borson upon the payment of $1.25 per acre.

The Borsons were very industrious people, frugal and economical, who improved their farm until it became a very valuable and well built up farmstead. Soon a substantial log house was built beneath the brow of a hill where it was surrounded by larger trees and close by a fine large. spring soon to be known all over the countryside as the Borson Spring.

The Borsons early became members and communicants of the Norwegian Lutheran Church congregation at Rushford and where they attended services quite regularly. Mr. Borson became the first trustee of the congregation when the church was organized in Rushford and when the valley folk were present and participated in this organization. 

The Borson family did not participate in social or political matters to any great extent, which was because they did not learn to use the American language, but the children who soon became numerous, all attended the district school quite regularly and used the English language fluently. 

No member of the Borson family traveled to any great extent, but they all spent their entire time and energy working and improving their homestead. The extent of their journeys were visits to Rushford and Winona with farm products for sale and necessary purchases. They were a seclusive family and mingled very little with the rest of the valley folk either in a business or social way. The only one of the Borson children to marry was John. BRIGT BRIGTSON 

Bight Brigtson who was also known as Brigt Brigtson Hellestvedt, was born in Kvam Hardanger in 1807 and was one of the four original settlers in Hardanger Dalen. He came to America in 1853. Undoubtedly he had been seized with the American fever like so many of the young people at that time and came to America for the purpose of establishing a home in the new world, which was prevalent at that time. His destination was Wisconsin where a Norwegian settlement had already been made on Jefferson Prairie. In this settlement, there were several people from Kvam in Hardanger as well as from other parts of Norway. Here he found several old acquaintances from Norway. 

After remaining on Jefferson Prairie for only a short time, he, together with three young men, Halvor Johnson, Ole Borson, and Jorgen Thorson, also from Kvam, came to Rush Creek Valley. They squatted on 160 acres of land each. This was the only way they could claim land at that time for the government had not yet surveyed the land in Minnesota. It still was a territory. 

Brigt was so fortunate as to select and obtain a fine piece of land close by a good spring where there was a fine building site. He soon began to clear and improve his land, building on it a crude log house. He lived there as a bachelor and a squatter for three years. He then married 

Elsebar Sjurson Valand, who was a young woman also from Kvam, Hardanger. They were married in Rushford, Minnesota by a Justice of the Peace. They continued to live on the farm as permanent settlers and improve it. Their first child, a son, died in infancy; then two daughters were born to them. Syneva, the older one, was born on October 4, 1859. Betsey, or Bessie as she was later called, was born on 

September 21, 1861. Later another daughter was born to them, but she died in infancy. 

Susie married Sivert Moen and went to live with her husband near

Camby, Minnesota. She lived to attain old age and became the mother of a large family. Bessie married Ole Knutson and lived on near Camby, Minnesota. She also reared a large family, losing one son in the World War No. 1.

JORGEN THORSON 

Jorgen Thorson Ostenso, with his recent bride, Guro Botnen, arrived on Jefferson prairie in the spring of 1853. They both were from Kvam 

Hardanger. Shortly after their arrival Jorgen was persuaded to pool his resources and join Halvor, Ole and Brigt in their caravan trek to Minnesota territory with the intention of locating there as squatters. Jorgen left his wife with relatives living on Jefferson prairie and joined the group which later in the summer of 1853 reached Rush Creek valley. He did the same as his companions, squatted on 160 acres of excellent land. 

The next spring Jorgen's wife Guro arrived in the valley with the intention of living there permanently and in her company came Miss Aagata, Ole's daughter Ostenso, also a recent arrival from Kvam. Aagata was a relative of Jorgen and Halvor's fiance. Jorgen immediately began to improve his claim, building a fairly good log house on it which was completed in 1856. This log house is still standing in 1941, not having been remodeled or improved since it was built. 

Jorgen and his wife were the first married couple to settle permanently in the valley. Jorgen was industrious and improved him farm but he did not live to be an old man. After a few years he died being the first one of the original settlers to pass on. 

Jorgen and Guro had no children of their own, therefore, they had more liberties and became very helpful to the other settlers in case of sickness and confinements. Guro was often called to officiate at confinement cases in the valley. There was no doctor close by and very inconvenient to call one on account of poor roads and poor transportation. 

Guro became a very efficient midwife and was in great demand during the early settlement in Hardanger Dalen. 

Guro did not remain a widow long. She soon married a young man named Hans Skaar. He was much younger than herself. Hans also was from Hardanger in Norway, the same neighborhood from which 

Guro came.

SEVERT THOMPSON Severt Thompson's original name was Sjur Skjei when he came from Norway to America, but this change undoubtedly was made because his name was difficult to spell and pronounce in the American language. Such changes, the Norwegian immigrants often made at that time. 

Mr. Thompson was born on the gaar Skjei in Kvam, Hardanger, Norway, on June 24, 1834. His mother was a widow with one son at the time she married his father. Severt remained at home on the Skjei gaar until he was confirmed, which was at about the age of fifteen. He then entered the apprenticeship of a shoemaker in Bergen, learning the trade and remaining there for five years. During his apprenticeship in Bergen, he attended evening school and in this way obtained a better education, especially in reading and writing, than the average young man had at that time.

After completing his apprenticeship and at the age of twenty-one years he came to America in 1855 in company with Ole Axeness and Gutorn Amundson Lee, who were also from Kvam, Hardanger. After a long and precarious voyage over the Atlantic Ocean and a tireless journey across the country, they finally landed in the Norwegian settlement in Wisconsin, called the Koskonong, or Kaskeland settlement, as the Norwegians called it. Mr. Thompson had an uncle, his father's brother, living there whose name was Hans Hillebo. He remained only a short time in Wisconsin. He obtained work in Illinois with two American bachelors where he remained for four years. While in their employ, he learned to use the American language quite well and also became greatly interested in politics and an active Republican, sponsoring the anti-slavery party.

Mr. Thompson came to Rush Creek Valley in 1859 and at first obtained work from his countryman, Brigt Brigtson. Later he rented 

Brightson's farm for a season. It was while Mr. Thompson was employed on the Brigtson's farm that Elsebar, Brigt's wife, became seriously ill, and Mr. Thompson was delegated to go over into Pine Creek Valley or Gudbrand's Dalen, as it was called, to find a hired girl for Brigt. 

He succeeded in bringing back with him Miss Charlotte Wiik, who was a Norwegian girl living with some friends there by the name of Fugleberg. Charlotte Wiik later became Severt Thompson's wife. They were married in 1860.

The best land in Hardanger Dalen had now been filed on and it was necessary for Mr. Thompson to file on a claim located between 

Ole Borsen's claim and Jern Thorsen's claim. It was mostly hilly land. 

At first he built a house and lived for a short time on top of the hill, but this was so inconvenient for water and roads that he moved down into the valley and built a house close to Rush Creek, where they lived for many years. 993

As Mr. Thompson was more proficient in the English language than the rest of the Norwegian settlers, he became more closely identified and prominent in social and economic affairs. He held several minor offices and was very helpful in civic and economic organizations of the newly organized country. 

While living in Hardanger Dalen, the following children were born to Mr. and Mrs. Thompson: Ellen, the oldest daughter, was born April 

17, 1861; Matilda, the second daughter, was born April 14, 1863; 

Thora, the third daughter, was born May 16, 1865; and Nels was born July 20, 1867. The children all grew up and attended the country school while living in Hardanger Dalen. 

After Ellen, the oldest daughter, reached maturity, she married S. O. 

Ostenso and went west with her husband to live on a claim in Chippewa county near Montevideo, Minnesota. A few years later Mr. Thompson, with the rest of the family, also left the valley and located on a claim in Chippewa county not far from the home of his daughter, Ellen. It was on this claim that his wife, Charlotte, died. Mr. Thompson remarried, but continued to live on his claim in Chippewa county until he became an old man. He died on June 16, 1923, and was buried in the Lutheran cemetery near Watson, Minnesota, where his wife, Charlotte, had preceded him. His son Nels died in 1891. 

Mr. Thompson, although he was not one of the very first settlers in

Dear Syvert. 

Copper Creek, Arizona. 

April 30, 1938.

It was certainly a great pleasure to get your letter of the 21st, and I will hasten to answer, though it will not be possible to give all the information you wish in regard to the dates of birth, time of immigration, etc., of my parents. Some of these dates can be supplied by my sister, Mary, who has the old family Bible, in which some of these dates are recorded. 

I remember my father saying that on his arrival in Winona from the old country, he attracted attention because of the tolle-kniv (knife) which he carried in his belt. He walked from Winona to "Haringdalen," or was it to the places of his brother Ole, south of Rushford, or his brother, Even, who lived at Peterson. At any rate it was shortly afterward that he settled on the side of Rush Creek opposite your home. 

There we lived till 1875, when we all moved to Burnett county, Wisconsin, where another brother Ole (the younger of the two Oles) served several country congregations as minister. In the fall of 1876, or the spring of 1877, we all moved back to Rush Creek again and lived there until the spring of 1882, when the family moved to Swift county, western Minnesota, where father lived until his death in 1906. 

Father and mother were born in Valders, Norway, and were married there, coming to America shortly afterward. Neither had much schooling, but both wrote well, and read as much as their busy lives would permit. The home library consisted of less than a dozen books, most of them religious works. Mother had poetic gifts and wrote a number of hymns which, I remember, were highly praised by ministers who visited our home after her death, which occurred shortly after the birth of my youngest brother, Edward, who was later adopted by Mr. and Mrs. -1103-

Jerome Patterson of Luck, Wisconsin, with whom he lived until his marriage.

Our lives in the little valley were uneventful. On Sundays the family drove in a lumber wagon to attend church either in Rushford or at North Prairie. Parents and children worked hard and long hours, with no time for idleness and no thought of vacations. There was never any lack of simple wholesome food. The one-room school house, where an occasional debate was held on a Saturday evening, was our only temple of learning. 

The church influence was strong in directing the thought and conduct of members of the family. It was not unusual for some of our neighbors to come home drunk from town, but this was not considered as bad as working on Sunday. I well remember a time when you and I and several other boys were in the creek swimming, and either you or Ols Axeness accused my father of having worked in the harvest field the previous Sunday. This was more than I could stand. Such a terrible accusation meant fight, and fight we did. The foul slander was washed out in blood. Whether it was mine or my opponents' nose that bled doesn't matter now. But anyway we seemed to respect each other more and to be better friends after a good scrap.

Our house, like the others in the neighborhood, never had a lock or key. No suspicion entered our minds that a neighbor would take anything that was not his, or would cheat or defraud. Fears, doubts or worries did not distress our minds.

Neighbors were kindly, sympathetic and helpful. When the midwife brought a baby into the world all the women in the community. came with goodies and heaps for the mother to build up her physical strength; and friendship, confidence, good cheer, hope and faith for her heart and soul.

Poor in worldly wealth, as riches are measured today, these sturdy, hard working people were immensely rich in all that makes for physical and moral well being. My father never consulted a doctor or lawyer and never sat in a dentist's chair. 

In the winter of 1881-82 our family had a great tragedy. The old family home, a 2-story log house, burned with all furniture, bedding, clothes, precious keepsakes and all belongings. But we had better insurance than man ever devised. Neighbors opened their hearts and homes. 

We lacked nothing. Your home was the nearest and most roomy. There we regained courage, hope and faith, while enjoying your neighborly hospitality.

Will write my sister Mary for the facts that are set down in the family 

Bible and send you as soon as possible. 

Any help I can give you in compiling your "Who's Who of Haringdalen" will be cheerfully given. With all good wishes, Sincerely yours, Martin E. Tew.

JACOB JOHNS, SR. 

Jacob Johns, Sr., was one of the pioneer settlers in Hardanger Dalen and the only one of the pioneer settlers remaining there permanently, who was not of Norwegian descent. He was born in Germany on January 

5, 1801, and came to the valley and settled on his claim in Section 20, in 1856. He built a log cabin and a log stable on the banks of Rush 

Creek, close by the main thoroughfare down the valley to Rushford. 

On account of its central location, the district school house was soon located and built close by his home, making this farm and home one of the best known places in the valley. It was for years here, at the Johns' pump and well where the school children came with their water pails for their drinking water at the noon and recess hour. His log cabin was among the first to be built and also the last to remain occupied. 

He first was married in Germany, but his wife died on the ship crossing the Atlantic Ocean shortly after his marriage, leaving him childless and a widower. He then married a second time and became the father of three children, Jacob, Elizabeth, and Louise. His second wife soon died, leaving him a widower a second time and with three small children, all of whom lived and grew up. He married a third time and begot three more children, August, Charley and Henry. After six years his third wife died. August and Charley grew up to manhood, but Henry died at the age of 12. He then married a fourth time, but they had no children as this wife died shortly after their marriage. He then married a fifth time. This was a widow who had two sons whose names were 

Conrad and George Sherer. His fifth wife soon afterward was killed in a railroad wreck near Winona, Minnesota, on the Milwaukee railroad.

Mr. Johns did not learn to speak the English language and therefore did not fraterize much with his neighbors, who were all Norwegians and did not speak German, but they remained true friends during the many years they were neighbors.

Mr. Johns lived to be over 99 years old. He died while still occupying his original homestead, and was the oldest farmer in the valley when he died. His farm has been continually occupied by himself, his son, Charley, or his grandson, Carl, who is the present owner, for 85 years and is the only valley farm in possession of the original family owners.

JAMES LOGAN 

James Logan was born in Ireland in 1826. He came to America in 1853, landing in Jersey City, New Jersey. Here he met Miss Sarah

Drum, who also had been born in Ireland in 1824. They were married in Jersey City in 1856 and came west to Chicago in 1856, where their first son was born. Here they purchased a lot and built a small home on this, then a frontier town, intending to make Chicago their future home. 

In Chicago Mr. Logan secured employment as the brakeman on Illinois Central railroad. While in their employment he suffered a severe accident necessitating the amputation of a leg above the knee and which nearly proved fatal. After recovering, notwithstanding this great handicap, the Logans decided to sell their home in Chicago, go west and engage in farming. Having relatives named Childs living in Winona, Minnesota, at that time they came there in search of a farm. Mr. 

Childs brought them out to Rush Creek Valley in the township of Hart, Winona county, where they squatted on a 40-acre tract of what they believed to be wild land but later it was discovered to he railroad land which the government had deeded to the W. & St. Peter Railroad company and for which the Logans later were required to pay the railroad company $15.00 per acre.

This location was in what was called Hardanger Dalen in the midst of a Norwegian settlement. The Logans came to the valley in 1870, while the first Norwegian squatters had come there in 1853. These 

Norwegian settlers, as was then the custom, welcomed the new comers. and on account of Mr. Logan's handicap turned out in mass assisting in building them a log cabin and stable for their cattle. The Logans lived in Hardanger Dalen until 1882, when they sold out and moved west to Barns county, North Dakota, near Valley City. Their two sons, John and Francis, who had by this time become of age, went with their parents. 

The parents and both sons each obtained not only a homestead, but also a tree claim from the government practically free. 

Mr. Logan died while in North Dakota in 1878. His wife, who survived him, died in 1888.

Their sons, John and Francis, became school teachers as well as farmers and soon became outstanding citizens in the community. John, the elder son, was elected and re-elected to the state legislature of North 

Dakota. He was born December 3, 1858. Married Esther Philips of Iowa in 1882. They had two children, Gladys E., born in 1884, died in 1938, and Wm. Jr., who lives with his mother at Riverside, California. John Logan died in California in 1938.

Frances, the younger son, later leased his North Dakota farm and moved to Canada during the balmy days of the land boom there. After selling out his Canadian land he retired and lived at 3044 Locust street, Riverside, California, having invested in considerable income property --121-

there. Frances also was born in Chicago on May 3, 1859. He remained. single.

The Logans were the only family of Irish extraction who became permanent settlers and land owners in this valley which the Norwegian settlers far and near called Hardanger Dalen, but others would prefer to call it Rush Creek Valley.

The Logan farm with its buildings was located on level ground along the bank of a small creek or spring and close to the base of a high bluff or hill, often called the Logan Bluff. On top of this bluff, projecting out from its very top and over-hanging_its_side was a huge rock or boulder often called the Logan Rock. To the top of this bluff and to climb the rock, people would come on holidays, for it afforded a splendid panoramic view of the landscape below and of the circulating course of the Rush Creek and its many tributaries. There one could see Rush 

Creek with its numerous curves and tributaries as it came turning and twisting down the valley in its course onward to join Root River and finally the Mississippi River. The Logan cabin was located directly beneath this high cliff or rock, and Mrs. Logan often expressed her fear that some day this rock might break loose and come thundering down, causing their immediate destruction. Such a calamity was scoffed at at the time but nevertheless that was just what happened in 1930 during a severe thunder and lightning storm. Luckily no one was then living in the old Logan cabin, which had long since been razed. That mighty giant is now gone and the familiar land mark vanished, and the face of the rock continually battles the water of Rush Creek. -122-

Josiah H. Moore was one of the early settlers in Hardanger Dalen, but not one of the very first pioneers. He was born in 1840 and reared on a farm near Barnett, Vermont, attended district school and later St.

Company D, First Vermont cavalry. After serving four years in the war and suffering a knee injury, he was taken a prisoner and confined in the famous Libby prison. After the war was over he was released, recovered and pensioned. He then became a school teacher, teaching several terms of school in the East before coming west to Minnesota in 1867. He then taught school one year at Witoka, Minnesota, after which he returned East to marry Miss Anna Somers Gibson, which occurred in 1868.

Mrs. Moore also was born in Vermont, in 1847, near Rygate, where she attended the district school and McIntosh academy. She had taught school in Vermont before marrying Mr. Moore.

Mr. Moore was charmed with the opportunities in the west and soon they decided to return to Minnesota. Mr. Moore secured a position as teacher in the Rushford public school. He purchased a lot and built a house with the intention of establishing a permanent home there, but in 

1870 he sold his home in Rushford and purchased the George Hitchcock farm located in the lower end of Hardanger Dalen or Rush Creek Valley. 

The buildings on the farm at that time consisted of the usual log cabin and stable, but the Moores, by practicing economy and industry, were soon able to erect substantial buildings and making it a modern farm. 

Mr. Moore had many sugar maple trees on his farm down along the banks of the Rush Creek, and during the spring season he was kept busy tapping maple trees and cooking maple syrup, which was always in great demand.

Mr. Moore found employment teaching in the winter terms of the district school in the valley for several years. Later when he became too much occupied with his farm duties he designated this work to his sisters, Kate and Ella Moore, who taught several terms of school there.

Mr. Moore established the first Sunday School in the valley, which was held in the old school house. This Sunday School proved of great interest to the children in the valley as they were privileged to read stories written in the English language, and to sing English songs, which in turn helped these Norwegian speaking youngsters to learn to use the English language. Mr. Moore, on account of his educational advantages, his business experience and proficiences in using the English language, which the other settlers were lacking, soon became very helpful to his neighbors, who frequently sought his help and advice which he so willingly gave them without recompense. FRANCIS LEHNERTS 

Francis Lehnerts the popular valley miller came to the valley February 21, 1873, by purchasing the valley grist mill. The mill had been erected in 1860 by J. M. Hitchcock who soon afterwards transferred an interest in it to his brother, George. The mill was then a combined saw and grist mill run by water power. According to transfer records it was valued at $4,500. Six years later or in 1866 the Hitchcock mill was sold to Judson C. Hooker and Rober C. Wood for a consideration of $6,000.

The property transferred consisted of, besides the mill building proper, two frame dwellings, one an 8-room house and another a 6-room house. 

The mill then became known as the Hooker Mill but it was in charge of and run by his partner Mr. Wood. The two dwellings were occupied by the Hooker and Wood families respectively. The following year in 

1867 Mr. Wood sold his interest in the mill to Christopher Heinz and again a year later or in 1868 Mr. Hooker sold his interest to Heinz who was then a well to do farmer owning the adjoining land. For the next 10 years the mill although registered in the name of Heinz it was managed and operated by Hiram Walker a practical and experienced miller. The mill now became known as the Walker Mill instead of the 

Hooker Mill.

At this time or in 1878 it fell into the hands of Francis Lehnerts who was an experienced miller. It now was made into a grist mill, entirely but run by water power as formerly. It had no rollers but huge old fashioned mill stones which ground the grain of various kinds into flour. Mr. 

Lehnerts operated the mill for 10 years as a grist mill or from 1878 to 1888 and was then known as Lehnerts grist mill. Mr. Lehnerts sold out to Godfried Miller who took possession of it but discontinued operating the property for milling purposes. Financially it was a poor paying proposition because the mill dam and flume suffered frequent washouts on account of such frequent high water and floods caused by Rush Creek. 

The mill and site which originally included 60 acres of, timber, pasture and agricultural land has since been turned into a small dairy, poultry and fruit farm. The mill itself has been torn down and rebuilt as a barn, with the fine spring bubbling close to its side. Over the once important flume that led from Rush Creek to the mill proper an improved highway has been built, with a modern bridge crossing the treacherous creek which was the cause for abandoning this once active industry. The appearance of the place has changed from that of an active center where the early farmers came for lumber and grist to that of a quiet farm. The main dwelling house still remains on the high table land overlooking the landscape below and the fish are now allowed to come up the creek and spawn in the springs above which the former dam prevented.

REVEREND ARNE E. BOYUM 

Reverend Arne E. Boyun was born in Balestrand, Sogn, Norway, on 

April 7, 1833. His parents were Ellend Erickson Boyum and Anna

Sevatsdatter. He remained at home with his parents receiving the usual educational instructions until he was 15 years old, when he was confirmed in the Lutheran church by their regular minister, H. U. Sverdrup. 

During his childhood, his parents twice had the great misfortune of losing their home by snowslides carrying it into the fjord. This so reduced the family resources that Arne was compelled to leave home at an early age and seek employment elsewhere, which continued from his 16th to his 20th year.

At the age of 20 and in company with a sister and other relatives, he decided to come to America. He left Bergen, Norway, by sailship in May, 1853, and after seven weeks they reached their destination and other relatives in Wisconsin.

Arne Boyum was a serious minded man and soon after reaching America he became engaged in teaching a Norwegian religious school, which he continued during 1854 and 1856. He joined the so-called 

Readers Laymen's Organization, and soon took active part with the readers and in lay preaching. 

In the spring of 1858, his parents and two younger brothers, Sevat and Ole, also came to America and joined him in Wisconsin. After their arrival he, together with other friends, came to North Prairie, Fillmore county, Minnesota, where he obtained homestead land for himself, his parents, and his two brothers. He returned to Wisconsin for the winter. The following year, 1857, together with his parents and brothers and a number of other friends, came back to North Prairie and commenced building homes. At the same time he continued to preach as a lay man and hold religious services in the community in the homes of the settlers.

The following year, 1858, at the annual Synod meeting at Spring 

Prairie, Wisconsin, he, together with Gudmond Strand, was ordained as a minister by Elling Eilson. The same fall he was installed as minister by Elling Eilson in the North Prairie congregation which had been organized; this being the first Hauges Synod Congregation established in 

America, and Reverend Arne Boyum was the first active minister in the Synod except Elling Eilson. 

In the Spring of 1859 he conducted his first marriage services, which was his sister, Gertrude, who married Mons Langetieg. The same year during the summer, Arne Boyum went back to Wisconsin and was married by Reverend Lars Johnson. The wedding occurred in his brother-in-law's home, Mons Langeteig.

In 1860 a log church was built at Arendahl, 18 feet by 34 feet, which served until 1863, when a frame church was built, 30 by 40 -141

feet, which was enlarged later. The frame church was called the Meeting House and the society was legally incorporated September 29, 1863. 

The earliest trustees were Peter Nelson, Hans Hanson and Albert Anderson (Ingebrigt Anderson Romoren) with Albert Anderson as clerk and Lars Thompson Husetuft as treasurer. 

Reverend Boyum never changed his pastorate but remained pastor at Arendahl and Peterson congregation for 38 years or until 1895, when he resigned. He was president of the Hauges Synod for 11 years. At the annual yearly meeting in Red Wing in 1908, he celebrated his 50 years anniversary as a minister.

His wife died December 31, 1899, and interred in the Arendahl cemetery on North Prairie. Arne Boyum died as the result of a stroke on 

July 29, 1913, and was interred in the Arendahl cemetery, where his wife and five children also rest.

Whereas Reverend Boyum was not one of the actual Norwegian settlers in Hardanger Dalen, nevertheless he lived so close by and was SO closely identified with some of the settlers there during his lifetime that he may almost be considered one of them. 

One of the first marriage ceremonies Reverend Boyum performed was the wedding of Halvor Johnson and Gertrude Anderson Romoren, which occurred in 1859, the same year as his own wedding and also the wedding of Britha Anderson Romoren, Gertrude's sister, to Lars Thompson Husetuft.

The first baptism he performed was an infant named Arnt Arneson, the son of Torsten and Embjor Arneson, born in Hardanger Dalen at the home of Halvor Johnson who was then a widower. 

The Johnson family became members of the Boyum congregation and remained so their entire lives. Reverend Boyum had baptized and confirmed all the Johnson children. He also conducted the last rights at 

Halvor Johnson's home in 1892. -142-

GUNAR OLUAS VIK 

Gunar Oluas Vik was born on the Gaar Vik, in Kvam, Hardanger, on January 26, 1866. His parents were Hans and Ingebor Vik. His father was a cabinet maker.

Gunar, the oldest son, spent his early childhood at home with his parents was confirmed in the Ostenso church at the age of 15, after which he spent one year as a fisherman. In 1883, he came to 

America in company with Johannes Rykken, a nephew of Halvor Johnson who had sent them their fare. He spent the first year in Hardanger 

Dalen in the employment of Halvor Johnson repaying him for his ticket. 

He was employed on the farm. 

The next winter he attended the district school learning how to use the English language. Later he taught Norwegian Parochial school during the winter, and later attended the Red Wing Theological Seminary for several years. He was ordained as a Lutheran minister at Roland, Iowa, in June, 1892, and accepted a call from congregations in southern Minnesota.

Here he served as their pastor for 19 years, after which he became ill and passed on June 14, 1915. His interment was in the Elsboro cemetery in Murray county, Minnesota, on June 17, 1915. ---*143*----
