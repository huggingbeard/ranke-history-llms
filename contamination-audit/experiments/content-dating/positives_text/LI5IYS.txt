INTRODUCTION 

Country plants are important factors in the handling of dairy products in northern New England. Maino, New Hampshire, and especially 

Vermont produce milk far in excess of their local or "domostic" domends for fluid milk and cream. The surplus production is usually delivered to plants in the producing areas where it is prepared for shipment to metropolitan markets to tho south as milk or cream, or manufactured into such products as butter and choose. The following pages describo the location and size of those plants, the operations they perform, and the evolution that has occurred during the period since 1920. 

The report docs not attempt to discover the causes that have lcd to the presont organization of country dairy plants, but only to describe the organization. It is part of a more comprehensive study of plant operations and efficiencies that has boon carried on by the Now England Rosearch Council.17 This description should be of assistance to students of the cfficiency of plant operations and of the changes in operations that occur in response to changing market prices and conditions. 1/

TRENDS SINCE 1920 

The preceding section of this report has presented a detailed description of country dairy plant operation in northern New England during 1937. The situation is far from static, however, as will be indicated by a brief study of the trends and changes that have occurred in recent years. While time and data limitations preclude an elaborate analysis for past years, it is believed that the most important shifts can be revealed by a simple analysis. This is presented in the following pages and is based on two general types of data: first, historical series that indicate the broad movements; and, second, a more detailed cross-section study for the year 1925 and a comparison of that year with 1937.

The roador will find that the following discussion relates most specifically to changes for the state of Vermont. The relative importance of this state would justify such a treatment, but it must be admitted that the availability of data has boon one of the determining factors. The dairy plant reports submitted to the Vormont Department of Agriculture have provided most of the basic data. Fragmentary data are available from state and federal sources for Maine and Now Hampshire, and have been utilized where possible. While trends have not been identical in the three states, they appear to have been quite similar. 

As a rosult, the Vermont changes are rough indications of changes in Maine and New Hampshire. The fact that a large proportion of Now England's country dairy plants are located in Vormont obviously makes the situations in that state and in New England to a large extent synonomous. 

Number and Size of Plants 

During the past quarter of a contury there has been a pronounced decline in the number of country dairy plants in New England. At tho some time the volume of milk and cream delivered to plants by producers has been slowly increasing. As a consequenco, average plant size as measured by annual roccipts has increased very markedly. 

The number of plants in Vermont decreased at a rate of about three plants per year from 1920 until 1929, falling from 224 to 193. 

In the next six years, the rate of decline was much greater and the number of country plants dropped to 130 by 1935. Since that year tho number of plants scoms to have become stabilized, at least temporarily.

SUMMARY 

The material prosented in this report indicates that there have been a number of important changes in country dairy plant operation since 1920. Summarizing these changes briefly, and limiting the consideration to Vermont alone, the total number of plants decreased more than 40 percent between 1920 and 1939, and these plant consolidations plus a slight upward trend in production nearly doublod average receipts per plant. During this period, shipments of milk and cream were greatly increased while the manufacture of such products as butter and choose decreased. The enlarged volume of crean shipments, together with the narked reduction in the receipts of cream from producers, resulted in large quantities of skin milk at country plants, and consequently gavo rise to an important skim-milk nanufacturing development. The most important uses of skim milk have boon in the manufacture of casein and skin-milk powder.

In terms of the products shipped and manufactured, it is apparent that there have been marked changes in plant type. These have taken the form of an increased number of fluid-nilk plants, and a drastically curtailed number of plants engaged in the manufacture of products from whole milk and cream. The total number of plants engaged in shipping milk, cream, or both appears to have been quite stable throughout the period, but the average size of these plants more than doubled. The number of plants specializing in the manufacture of butter and cheese dropped more than 80 percent, while the total volume handled by these plants declined nearly 80 percent. The number of plants conbining nanufacture and shipment of milk and cream declined 70 percent, while the total volume handled by these plants was reduced by 50 percent between 

1925 and 1937.

-- 
- Finally, several characteristics of production that affect plant operations were changing during the period. In 1937, the seasonal variation as indicated by the ratio of daily average receipts in the high month to daily averages in the low months was about 1.8 for Vermont. 

This state ratio represented a geographical range of from 1.3 in the southeastern counties of the state to more than 2.2 in the northwestern counties. A second production characteristic concerns the form in which farmers deliver their product to country plants. In 1924 more than 40 percent of the milk was delivered to plants in the form of cream, while by 1939 this had dropped to less than six percent. It is not the purpose of this report to analyze the factors responsible for these changes. A few factors have so obviously been involved, however, that they are enumerated hero. In the first place, many of the changes have undoubtedly been associated with the increasing importance of the arca as a source of milk and cream for the metropolitan markets. 

The reported shifts in plant types and the tendencies toward a lower seasonal variation in production and a smaller amount of farm separation may be considered typical of the changes that accompany the enlargement of fluid milk and cream sheds. A second important factor has been the technological improvement in farm collection, including botter roads and improved trucks. These have made it possible for plants to expand their collection areas, and so have facilitated the consolidation of plants. 

Still another factor leading to plant consolidation has been the possibility of more efficient and less costly plants with large scale operations. In view of the fact that plant charges or allowances have been practically stable during the period12, it is oven possible that plant operators have in some cases forced plant volumes beyond the point of least cost when all factors are considered. 

12/ From 1922 until the establishment of Federal control in 1935, the country station allowance was 23 1/4 cents per cwt. for all milk.

With Federal control, the allowance has been 20 cents for Class I milk, and 26 cents for surplus milk until 1941, when the Class II allowance was reduced somewhat.
