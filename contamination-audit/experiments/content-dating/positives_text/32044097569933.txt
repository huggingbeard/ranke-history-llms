REPORT TO SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT INTRODUCTION 

1. The ten year period covered by the reports of the Executive 

Secretary to the Justices of the Supreme Judicial Court has been a time of growth and change in Massachusetts. The numbers of her people have increased; the places where they live and work, the ways they travel, their education and their government have changed radically. 

2. The judicial department of the government has moved with these changes. The improvement of the administration of the courts and of the facilities for their operation must continue. 

Although the administrative structure of the courts has changed for the better, particularly in the probate and district courts, with administrative authority and responsibility in the hands of a chief judge and a chief justice, much remains to be done in the strengthening of the administrative structure of the entire judicial system and in providing the facilities essential to the effective operation of the system. 

3. The next ten years of growth and improvement in the administration of justice require effort from within the judicial department and the support of the public, the executive and legislative departments, and the lawyers who are officers of the courts, in the decision of major problems of court organization and administration. Not only must decisions be reached but the resources to carry out the decisions must be provided. 

4. The judicial department of the Commonwealth is organized and functions as a unified, not a planetary, system of trial and appellate courts, operating under the supervision of the Justices of the Supreme Judicial Court. Every judicial officer thus must share fully the responsibility for the effective administration of justice. 

5. The most important achievement in the organization and administration of the courts in the next ten years would be the completion of the progress that has been made in the district courts. 

The goal is clear but the way to its achievement is much less clear. 

The justices of the district courts should be full-time justices forbidden to practice law, sitting in enough districts to serve the public need as to time and place for the exercise of the criminal and civil jurisdiction of their courts. The required number of justices, the times and places of their sittings and the effect of the development upon existing officials, court facilities and the habits of all those who enter the district courts raise the thorny questions which will be discussed more fully in Paragraphs 14-33.

13. In geographical size, population size and general characteristics Massachusetts is most nearly comparable to New Jersey.

DISTRICT COURTS 

14. It is easy to describe the district court system as consisting of 72 courts with administrative authority vested in a chief justice. 

A full description of the system is more difficult. 

15. There are 47 full-time justices in 47 single-justice courts, 12 full-time justices in 5 multi-justice courts and 20 part-time justices in 20 part-time courts, a total of 79 justices for the 72 courts. 

In addition to these there are 84 special justices, a few more than present statutes would require. 

16. As between the three types of justices, full-time, part-time and special, the amount and character of their compensation vary as do the limitations upon the cases which they may hear. The amount of judicial service rendered by special justices runs from zero to a very respectable amount of time and travel. 

17. The justices cover not 72 but 82 court houses, because in a few districts the court sits in more than one court house. Some towns are subject to the concurrent jurisdiction of two courts. 

The court houses, averaging in age fifty-eight years, are of all varieties. The best are modern buildings with ample facilities within and without; the worst, ancient, cramped and ill-kept quarters over a police station or a country store. 

18. The largest population figure of a district is 226,235 for 

Central Worcester; the smallest, 3,714 for Nantucket. 

19. This is not a system. It is a hodge podge. It only exists as it is because it grew up that way and because the few major attempts to improve it never fully succeeded. There is immediate need for the solution of the following problems: 

20. (a) How many districts should there be? 

21. One answer was supplied in 1962 by the Griswold Commission: 60 districts. The conditions which called for the establishment of district courts in several towns have ceased to exist. The principal reason for this is the ease of travel by automobile.

P.D. 166

REPORT TO SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

22. The answer today requires further study. Some of the very small districts by reason of their geographical location are not susceptible to merger with a larger district, but there are at least a dozen districts which would benefit by joining a larger neighbor. 

23. There will be strong local resistance to any proposal for consolidation of districts but without selective consolidation the district courts will never function efficiently. The people served by a small district court are entitled to enjoy the improvement in judicial performance which comes with the employment of a fulltime justice and with the physical facilities of a modern court house. 

24. There is present need for expansion in the numbers of persons employed in the clerks' and probation offices of many of the larger courts. The transfer of personnel from a small district would meet the need and would keep in service persons of experience in their work. A random sample shows that from 1952 to 1965 the personnel working in the clerk's office of one of the largest district courts rose from 16 to 31; in the probation office, from 12 to 22. 

25. It is a common experience to find in some of the smaller court houses inadequate working areas for the clerk's staff and little or no provision for the probation staff. 

26. Instead of the present haphazard developments in court personnel and facilities the sort of study and planning involved in decisions on consolidation proposals should provide over a period of years a far more economical and efficient district court plan than presently exists. 

27. (b) How many full-time justices are needed to operate the district courts? 

28. The answer must take into account a reasonable projection of case loads, a consideration of the amount of work that will be expected of the judges in remand cases, 6-man jury cases and in the superior court, and allowance must be made for absence by reason of illness, vacation or other good cause. 

29. The answer must also be weighed in the knowledge that some districts or even sensible consolidations of some districts cannot reach the work level of full-time courts. 

30. (c) How can the present judicial manpower be used in the period of transition to a complete full-time system? 

31. This question involves the disposition of the services of the 20 part-time justices and of the 84 special justices. The answer cannot be a simple one, such as to make all part-time courts full-time and to dispense with the services of the special justices.

P.D. 

32. Even if all of the part-time justices would accept full-time service, an incorrect assumption, so great a number of full-time justices as 79 would not be needed to man 60 districts. It is difficult, if not impossible, to determine from the present district court statistics the case load, civil and criminal, which can be handled efficiently by a full-time judge. Many full-time judges are called upon for service in courts other than their own and in the busy multi-judge courts help from visiting justices is needed to dispose of heavy civil lists. It is obvious, however, that, when 29 of the 

72 courts report trials of less than 50 civil cases in a year and when 14 report less than 1,000 criminal cases entered, careful study is necessary in order to justify the creation of additional district court judgeships. 

33. From the ranks of the part-time and special justices appointments to full-time service may appropriately be made when the proper number is determined but it is clear that no case can be made for a complete change-over of part-time to full-time and none for the present one or two a year approach.

CIVIL PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE 

39. On December 29, 1965, the Supreme Judicial Court adopted 

General Rule 15 on Depositions and Discovery. The rule became effective on April 1, 1966. The Massachusetts and Boston Bar

Associations on May 24, 1965, after many years of study and comment by the profession and with the growing experience gained from working with the Federal rules and those of most of the states, petitioned the Court for adoption of a rule to govern pre-trial oral discovery, discovery and production of documents and things, and physical and mental examination of persons. 

40. Public notice was given and September 15, 1965, was ordered to be the closing date for the filing of briefs. Six briefs were filed and other communications were received and considered. Only one brief opposed the adoption of a rule. Suggestions for changes in the proposed rule were examined and some of them as submitted or as modified appear in the rule together with certain changes and additions made by the Justices. 

41. In substantial cases the new oral discovery procedure for the deposition of parties and witnesses is available to all parties to litigation without leave of court. Leave of court must be obtained in smaller cases and for discovery and production of documents and things and physical and mental examination of persons. 

42. The benefits to be derived by both plaintiffs and defendants will appear as the rule is tried in actual practice. 

43. Although by court rules the clerks in the various courts have been required to notify counsel of the entry of conditional

non-suits or defaults for failure to answer interrogatories, St. 1966, c. 432, amending G. L., c. 231, §64, now compels the clerks to send a second notice. No judgment may be entered until five days after such notice. 

44. While the statute will furnish additional protection to lawyers and clients against inadvertence, it puts a heavy burden on the clerks. In the Suffolk county superior civil clerk's office, a clerical assistant has been added. Three assistants now devote their entire time to interrogatory notices. If the courts are required to assume increased responsibilities which lawyers or their clerks have previously undertaken, the public should know that the cost of operating the courts must increase.

CRIMINAL PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE 

45. A bill aimed at clarifying the statute which governs the granting of a new trial by a superior court justice has become law. 

St. 1966, c. 301, amending G. L., c. 278, §29. The bill was filed by this office. 

46. The amendment of General Rule 11 of the Supreme 

Judicial Court, effective June 20, 1966, enlarged the authority for legal assistance by senior law school students in criminal proceedings in the district courts to include representation of the prosecution. The rule formerly covered only representation of indigent defendants. 

47. A bill that would govern post-conviction procedure has been referred to the Judicial Council for study. Resolves 1966, c. 21. The bill is based upon the Uniform Post-Conviction Procedure 

Act and is one of at least three methods of dealing with procedure in this field. Some states have used the Uniform Post-Conviction 

Procedure Act as a model; some have copied the federal habeas corpus act; some have adopted no new legislation. Massachusetts is in the third category. 

48. Decisions of the Supreme Court of the United States in recent years which have elaborated upon the rights and privileges of persons under investigation by law enforcement officers have caused a sharp increase in the number of state and federal postconviction proceedings in addition to many more appeals in criminal cases. Unlike several of the states, Massachusetts has not been faced with an impossible burden of petitions for writs of error in the Supreme Judicial Court or of petitions by persons convicted in state courts for writs of habeas corpus in the United States courts.

P.D. 166

49. In this rapidly developing and changing area of criminal law it seems best to preserve our flexibility by the use of existing procedural devices which have demonstrated their ability to safeguard the rights of post-conviction petitioners. The Uniform Post- 

Conviction Procedure Act itself has been the subject of change and has not met with universal success. 

50. This office continued in its opposition to legislative proposals to give to counsel in criminal cases greater control over the voir dire examination of prospective jurors. This year's bill in modifying the right of counsel to the unlimited examination of prospective jurors used the elusive adverb, "reasonably," but its purpose and its probable effect would be to take from the court and to give to counsel control of the process and to invite the prolonged and unnecessary questioning of prospective jurors and to delay the conduct of criminal cases. At the end of the legislative session the bill failed to receive the Governor's approval. 

51. Bail reform in Massachusetts advanced with the enactment of a personal recognizance bill. St. 1966, c. 681. Last year by 

St. 1965, c. 396, the Legislature added a new §82A to G. L., c. 276, to provide criminal penalties for failure without reasonable excuse to appear. 

52. The new law which deals with the release on personal recognizance (r/o/r) of persons charged with an offense under the jurisdiction of the district courts requires r/o/r unless the bail official in his discretion determines that such release will not reasonably assure the appearance of the defendant. Standards to apply in reaching the determination are detailed in the statute and there is provision for speedy petition to a justice of the superior court, if the determination is adverse to the defendant. 

53. The intent of the statute is clear. It is to encourage the release of persons charged with minor offenses, if their histories indicate that they will probably appear in court. The test of the law will come in its administration by bail officials. 

54. Since 1960 the Massachusetts Defenders Committee, authorized by G. L., c. 221, §34D, as amended by St. 1962, c. 366, has been the principal instrument in providing legal services for persons accused of crime who lack the means to hire counsel. 

55. In fiscal 1966 the Legislature appropriated $254,350 for the Committee and in fiscal 1967 the appropriation is $357,335. 

With these appropriations and with very substantial contributions from private and other public sources, the work of the Committee, whose members are appointed by the justices of the Supreme Judicial Court, is constantly expanding.

P.D. 11 

56. With offices opening in six districts beyond the Boston metropolitan area the Committee will furnish legal services to indigent defendants throughout the Commonwealth. It will employ 

60 lawyers and also supporting clerical assistance. In the course of a year Defenders' attorneys have already handled 6,600 cases, both felonies and misdemeanors, in various courts. 

57. Apart from the need for voluntary defenders to satisfy constitutional guarantees, the defender movement is essential in the recruitment of young lawyers to practice in the field of criminal law, first in salaried positions as public defenders and later in private practice. The impact of the activities of the Committee is felt in the offices of the district attorneys where more assistants are needed and in the work of the criminal sessions of the courts. 

58. The defense of the indigent is still the concern of private organizations and much continues to be done by the law schools and other privately supported institutions in defender projects. 

59. There is increasing concern in the courts about the problems of alcoholism and of drug addiction. In other jurisdictions a few courts have held that chronic alcoholism is a disease and not a crime. Legal scholars are studying the question of chronic alcoholism as a defense to charges of criminal behavior. Many courts, particularly trial courts such as the district courts in Massachusetts, are questioning the efficacy, if not the legality, of commitment as a criminal of the chronic alcoholic. 

60. The eighth Report of this office dealt at length with the problems and contained valuable suggestions to the justices and the probation officers of the district courts in the establishment of rehabilitation programs for alcoholics. Much more should be done to apply modern thinking and methods to the treatment of alcoholics and drug addicts. The alcoholic and the addict will eventually be found in the clinic and not in the court room. 

DELAY IN COURT 

61. No subject in the literature of court administration has received more attention than delay in court. Every report of this office has dealt with the problem of the delay involved in reaching civil jury cases for trial in the Superior Court. Reports from other states are the same. It would be reasonable to suppose that concentration by trained judges, lawyers and legal scholars on the causes and cures of delay in court over many years would by now have produced agreement upon the causes and the cures for the malady. 

Agreement, however, is still a long way off.

P.D. 166

62. First, students differ as to what delay is and as to how it should be measured. The word, delay, seems preferable to its companion word, congestion. The busy civil and criminal sessions of 

Suffolk County will always be congested but, if the number of cases to be tried is fixed, the operation of enough sessions will eliminate delay. The qualification is essential since it assumes a uniform number of civil jury cases flowing into the sessions. Thus, if more sessions are put to work, delay is reduced. 

63. The flow of cases, however, is a variable and may increase by reason of the cure. This is the expressway theory. Convert a two-lane highway into a six-lane divided expressway, adequate for the old traffic and a reasonable increase, and, as cars drop the alternate routes and flock to the expressway, observe again delay. 

64. So with civil jury cases, if every available jury court room in Suffolk County were manned with judges, court personnel and jurors, the immediate effect would unquestionably be a reduction in delay. The long run effect is difficult to predict. 

65. What is delay in the trial of a civil jury case? This office has previously taken the position that 18 months from the date of entry in the ordinary case is not delay. The administrator of one of the busiest trial courts of general jurisdiction in the country has recently stated that a jury case is current if not more than two years have elapsed from the date of filing to the date of verdict. 

66. Delay in court is frequently attributed solely to the inability of the courts to dispose promptly of their case loads. This concept of delay is inaccurate. About 95% of civil jury entries will never be tried to a verdict. Yet most methods of measuring delay in court include many of these sleepers in the active category. 

67. An analysis of all of the many causes of delay in court is beyond the scope of this report, but it is clear that among the chief villains are the automobile and the negligence theory of liability for compensation of its victims. Obviously the courts are powerless to eliminate motor vehicle tort cases from their jury trial lists and the many plans for changing the negligence theory are still plans and not realities. 

68. The absence of a jury fee in Massachusetts encourages the insistence on a jury trial in vast numbers of cases which do not merit the time, effort and expense involved in a trial by jury. Past experience gives little hope to the advocates of the establishment of a modest jury fee. Yet in other jurisdictions jury fees are in use. 

The question of jury fees cannot be overlooked in any serious consideration of the causes and the cures for delay in court.

P.D. 13 

69. Both litigants and lawyers must share the blame with the courts for much of the delay in the disposition of civil jury cases. 

Apart from the unavoidable consequence of the illness or death of parties or of attorneys, continuances are requested for a wide variety of reasons and rarely in motor vehicle tort litigation are both sides pressing for trial. 

70. What is the proper method of measuring delay in court?

Since December 31, 1964, the Superior Court has computed time-lag by taking a mathematical average of all cases from the civil jury trial lists which were reached and held for trial and disposed of (by trial or settlement) in each three-month period ending June 30 and December 31. Date of entry is the starting point. This method, if consistently followed, will afford useful comparisons but its limitations, and for that matter the limitations of any measurement, should be recognized. 

71. First, the nature of the civil jury list must be understood. 

It is never a list of jury cases taken in the numerical order of their entries in court. There will be cases advanced for trial which will tend to reduce the time-lag figure. There will be absent old cases which have gone off the list for a variety of reasons. Their absence will also tend to reduce the time-lag figure. There will be old cases which have been continued and old cases restored to the list from a condition of inactivity. These cases will tend to increase the timelag figure. 

72. Unless over a period of years the jury lists are made up consistently, comparisons are dangerous. 

73. In order to reflect more accurately court caused delay a later date than that of entry is used in some statistical reporting. 

A date when pleadings are completed or when a certificate of readiness is filed is sometimes used. Either method shortens the time-lag figure. Thus comparisons with statistics from other states are also dangerous. 

74. In spite of the difficulties of accurate measurement, timelag is a fact of judicial administration and comparisons, if their meaning is understood, are both necessary and useful. If in Suffolk 

County a seriously injured plaintiff must wait well over three years for a jury trial, he is not receiving the prompt trial to which he is entitled. 

75. What has been and what can be done to remedy this injustice? There is no simple answer. In the last ten years the number of Superior Court judges has been increased, auditors have been extensively employed, smaller cases have been remanded to the

P.D. 166

District Courts, District Court judges have been called in to sit in jury sessions in the Superior Court, in the very busy counties judges have sat in assignment and conciliation sessions, in one county a six-man civil jury session has operated in the District Court and with the great increase in the criminal case load six-man misdemeanor sessions have been used in District Courts and District Court judges have sat in jury misdemeanor sessions in the Superior Court. 

76. The purpose of each of these measures was to supply additional tribunals for the trial of both criminal and civil cases which would require jury trial in the Superior Court before a justice of that court in the absence of a substitute procedure. Without these measures the justices of the Superior Court would have been able to operate only about 40 sessions, criminal and civil, jury and jury-waived, with little margin given for illness and for the essential work of the justices off the bench. Forty sessions are far too few for the prompt dispatch of the work of the Superior Court. 

77. Each of the measures designed to increase the number of tribunals has not been a direct answer to the difficult question: How many associate justices are needed in the Superior Court? 

78. Since the increase in the number of justices has been accompanied by the use of the other measures of relief, it is impossible to isolate the effect of the increase. The combined effect of all the measures to the extent they have been employed has failed since 1959 to keep the dockets in current condition. It follows that the work of 42 justices alone is insufficient to remedy delay in the trial of civil jury cases in the Superior Court. 

79. The use of District Court judges sitting with juries in the 

Superior Court in the trial of motor vehicle tort and misdemeanor cases is the only relief measure which is equivalent to enlargement of the Superior Court. There are, however, serious questions about its permanence. 

80. During a transition period of several years to a full-time 

District Court system it will be increasingly difficult to recruit judges for this service. The efficient administration of the District 

Court system requires the continuous employment of at least the present 59 full-time judges. The trial of civil cases remanded from the Superior Court and the use of six-man juries in misdemeanor sessions will, from present indications, continue to be a part of the work of the District Court judges in their own courts. If there is an orderly transition to a full-time District Court system which will

require the continuous employment of the judges in the work of that system, the Superior Court will cease to receive the assistance of District Court judges for Superior Court sessions. 

81. The lack of an adequate number of civil jury sessions in 

Suffolk County in recent years has resulted in a very serious increase in delay in civil jury trials. Previous reports of this office have emphasized the gravity of the problem and have commented upon the causes of delay. The two principal causes are the necessity for more criminal sessions to handle the constantly increasing criminal business and the necessity for manning the courts in other counties at the expense of the civil jury sessions in Suffolk County. 

82. In addition to the trial of civil cases remanded from the 

Superior Court, the Boston Municipal Court will be in a position to render further aid in the trial of misdemeanor cases with 12-man juries under the provisions of St. 1966, c. 697, adding a new §18A to G. L., c. 278. The statute by failing to make the necessary amendment to §18 did not substitute the new procedure for the former method of appeal to the Superior Court. Corrective legislative action to make the new procedure a substitute for, rather than an alternate to, the former procedure is necessary. 

83. A detailed discussion of the use of auditors and of the remand system is impossible within the limits of this report. Both measures have been extremely useful and yet neither is a complete substitute for a jury trial in the Superior Court. 

84. The selective use of auditors and masters in aid of the 

Superior Court will be continued but experience has demonstrated that extensive reference of cases to hearing officers will not be a permanent solution of the problem of delay. 

85. The remand of smaller cases to the District Courts will also continue and the later analysis of statistics at paragraphs 91-98 indicates that it is a more effective measure of relief to the Superior Court than has previously been suggested. 

86. The conclusion is fully justified that, unless a radical and unforeseen drop occurs in the number and weight of civil and criminal cases requiring jury trials, more justices are needed on the Superior Court bench. Since court administration is an inexact science, opinions may reasonably differ on the precise number presently needed. The suggestion of an increase of ten over a threeyear period is, however, a reasonable, if not too modest, estimate.

DATA PROCESSING 

99. The statistics published in this report lack the element of freshness which is essential to the efficient administration of the courts. The use of data processing will overcome the delay incident to the present annual reporting methods and will, in the Superior Court, for example, permit the assembly and analysis of statistical information on a current basis. Data processing will also permit the collection of more detailed information about the disposition of both civil and criminal cases than is now feasible. As experience elsewhere has demonstrated, the operation of the assignment sessions in the busier courts can be much improved with the aid of the machines. 

100. A small beginning has been made by the Massachusetts courts. Through private sponsorship experimental studies have been in progress in the office of the Suffolk Superior Court Civil Clerk. The Boston Municipal Court has for several months used data processing in the handling of traffic tickets and of complaints for failure to respond to tickets. Certain of the executive departments are rapidly expanding their use of data processing and in the case of the Registry of Motor Vehicles the machines will improve the operations of the police and the probation officers and will affect law enforcement and the courts. 

101. It is interesting to note that progress is being made in numerous legal fields to take advantage of the machines. Title registration and examination, legal research, and law office management are some of the areas in which data processing promises to provide to the legal profession more efficient service.

 THE JUDICIARY 

102. All Massachusetts judges are appointed by the Governor, by and with the advice and consent of the Executive Council. They hold their offices during good behavior. 

103. Under the Massachusetts Constitution the Governor's responsibility for judicial appointments may not be delegated. 

However, in order to assure an informed exercise of the appointing power, the last two Governors have volunteered to seek the advice of responsible representatives of the bar in the determination of the qualifications of aspirants for judicial office. 

104. The constitutional provisions governing judicial appointment and tenure have with few exceptions supplied the Commonwealth with well-qualified judges. With the example of California and some other states in mind, committees of the Massachusetts and Boston Bar Associations have been at work on a proposed rule to establish a commission to deal with problems of judicial incapacity. If created, the commission would work within the framework of existing constitutional limitations. 

JUDICIAL SALARIES 

105. The disposition of various bills which sought increases in the salaries of the justices of the Supreme Judicial Court and of the Superior Court and of the Judges of the Probate Courts was an across-the-board 10% increase for every judge in the Commonwealth. 

106. The rationale of this extraordinary legislation appears to be this. The general revision of judicial salaries in 1963 placed the salary levels in the various courts where they should be at that time. 

The cost of living has risen since 1963. Ten per cent ought to compensate for that rise and all judges are affected by it. Ergo, all judges, whether bills were filed on their behalf or not, should receive a 10% increase in salary. 

107. The false premise of this line of thought is the assumption that the 1963 revision established judicial salaries in the different courts in proper relationship to each other. The reverse is true, as was pointed out in detail in the 7th and 8th reports of this office. 

108. Due to interim increases in the salaries of probate and district court judges and to the theory that a $5,000 increase is enough for any man, the 1963 revision did not maintain judicial salaries in the relationship they occupied in 1955, the year of the next prior general revision.

22 

P.D. 166

109. The result now is that the justices of the Supreme Judicial 

Court have not yet received the salaries recommended in the 1962 Griswold Committee Report nor, obviously, those figures adjusted to 1966 levels. The salaries of the Supreme Judicial and the District 

Court justices are rapidly reaching the same level, a trend which is hard to explain in history or logic. Whether it be the new egalitarianism or the poetic view, adapted from Miss Stein, that a judge is a judge is a judge, it is a mystifying trend. A new look and a sharp one at the structure of judicial salaries is required. 

110. Shortly after passage of the judicial salary bill a 15% salary increase bill for superior court clerks and assistant clerks was passed. One need not question the justification for this bill but it is hard to see how the increase in the cost of living hits with such uneven power.

PHYSICAL FACILITIES 

111. Since my appointment I have visited all but three of the courts. There are 82 court houses in the district court system. Some district courts are accommodated in the county court houses which also house the superior and probate courts. Most of the district courts, however, are in separate buildings and the facilities for more than half of them are rented by the counties and usually owned by the cities and towns. 

112. A fair sampling of district court houses shows their average age to be 58 years. Elevators and air conditioning are almost unknown and all too many of the district court facilities, particularly the clerks' offices, lack adequate space, furnishings and equipment. 

Small law libraries, in current condition, are so rare as to be the cause of justifiable local pride. 

113. Against this dismal background appear bright figures of improvement. In Stoughton, where the court used to sit in little. dignity in rented quarters above a liquor store, it now occupies a new building of attractive design, well furnished and equipped, with excellent off-street parking. The facilities at Leominster, Nantucket, Palmer, Plymouth, Worcester and Wrentham are excellent and all are relatively new. 

114. Good renovations of old interiors have made the courtrooms, furnishings and equipment at Holyoke, Taunton and Westfield attractive and suitable for the operation of the courts. Yet at 

Holyoke and Taunton satisfactory off-street parking is lacking. 

115. The Woburn court will shortly move into its new building.

GENERAL COMMENTS 

172. Comment has already been made about the imperative need for maintaining the county law libraries. It is repeated here for emphasis. The libraries must be maintained in current condition. 

It is well known that the cost of law books is increasing but that increase must be met.

P.D. 166

173. The furnishings and equipment of the courtooms and of the offices of the court houses should be modern and adequate for the work performed in them. It is poor economy to refuse the requests of the clerks and registers for the necessary tools for efficient performance of their duties. 

174. The previous comments on court facilities show encouraging progress but it is spotty. Recognizing this, the Legislature acted upon the Governor's recommendation in establishing a special commission to investigate and study the court house facilities in the 

Commonwealth. Resolves 1965, c. 126. The Resolve calls for a membership of two senators, four representatives and seven persons to be appointed by the Governor and for the appointment by the commission of "a person outstanding in the field of judicial administration or architecture, to act as its secretary and to correlate its findings." 

175. The intent is excellent. The commission is to look both to the present and future needs of the judicial department. Without a professional staff, however, the commission members will not be able to make the investigation and study that is needed. Funds should be appropriated for this worthwhile undertaking and a realistic length of time should be provided for the completion of the commission's work. 

176. Before the advent of the automobile in overwhelming numbers the county and district court houses were built in the centres of population. Automobile parking problems were unknown, and ready access for the greatest numbers and by means of public transportation was the principal factor governing location. 

177. The transportation revolution has changed all of this. 

A downtown court house with no parking lot is generally as unacceptable as one amply supplied with space but located at a great distance from the centres of population which it serves. The advantages and disadvantages must be carefully weighed before the site for new court house construction is selected. Site selection is clearly the concern of the responsible public officials, their architects and engineers, of the court officials, their staffs, of the lawyers, and of interested members of the public. To neglect the views of any one of these groups is to invite a mistake. 

178. While aesthetic considerations of exterior appearance may be largely a matter of architectural judgment, there are situations where community opinions might be persuasive. If the old court house is a fixture in the community and is valuable for historic or aesthetic reasons, the need for more space and more modern facili-

P.D. 33 

ties may well be satisfactorily met by renovations within and expansion immediately without. This is a phase in which local judgment is of major concern and serves to emphasize the necessity of consulting local opinion. 

179. It is when the stage of interior planning is reached that the needs and views of the people who use the court house become paramount. Present use and probable future use must both be considered. 

180. The court house architects of 1900 did not anticipate the service of women on juries. When the ladies arrived in 1949, there were very few court houses equipped for them and they still are not comfortable in many. Recent years have seen the expansion of six-man jury sessions in the District Courts located in the county seats where jurors are available. Planning must take into account possible developments of this sort. 

181. Standards for the location and equipment of courtrooms, judges' chambers, jurors' rooms, clerks' offices, consultation rooms, press and stenographers' rooms, district attorneys' offices, probation offices, detention rooms, libraries and public accommodations, with all the facilities and equipment needed for their efficient use, are available. Committees of the American and other bar associations, both judges and lawyers, have produced and continue to perfect such standards. Other states are developing standards for guidance in solving their court house problems. Their experience should be helpful to us as ours will be to them. Standards for United 

States courts are published and are useful. 

182. Almost as important in court house planning as the provision of adequate total space for present and future needs is the functional arrangement of the space. It is senseless to provide an ample and efficient courtroom and to locate the judge's lobby across an open and public corridor. Jury assembly and deliberation rooms must meet the standards of both privacy and access. Many more minimum requirements could be named. 

COMMENTS ON THE VARIOUS COURTS SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

183. The increase in the business of the court continued. This year 331 cases were decided, of which 87 were by rescript opinions; last year, 318 were decided, 110 by rescript opinion. There were 

42 criminal cases. The latter presented increasingly difficult questions and the bare statistics fail to show completely the extent to which the appellate work has increased. In spite of the increase the court cleared its docket.

185. Discussed elsewhere in the report are new General Rule 15 and amended General Rule 11, adopted during the court year.

SUPERIOR COURT 

187. The problems of the great trial court of the Commonwealth have been considered at length in earlier paragraphs of this report. 

188. There can be no dispute that the number and the quality of its justices and the facilities available to them should be sufficient to permit the prompt dispatch of their work. They should deal with important issues as they are presented in both civil and criminal cases and should be relieved of the pressure caused by the accumulation of minor cases on their dockets. 

189. Progress toward this goal is evident and the goal will be reached if the resources of the various courts are fully used and the means are provided for a Superior Court adequate in strength to dispose of the litigation to which its efforts should be directed. 

LAND COURT 

190. The important work of this court fell short of previous performance with entries increasing and dispositions decreasing. 

The court was not at full strength in the reporting period and it has suffered losses in its technical staff. On January 1, 1965, Associate Judge Joseph R. Cotton retired and his successor, Joseph B.

Silverio, Esquire, was inducted on September 2, 1965. On October 

5, 1965, Judge John E. Fenton retired and was succeeded in March, 1966, by Elwood H. Hettrick, Esquire. 

PROBATE COURTS 

191. On March 31, 1966, Judge John V. Phelan, Judge of Probate in Essex County, resigned, and Albert P. Pettoruto, Esquire, of Andover, was appointed to fill the vacancy.

P.D. 35 

192. The tendency to avoid probate, marked in its extreme form by a controversial book on the subject, is not seen in the gradually increasing case loads of the probate courts. Lawyers who defend the probate system acknowledge the need for improvement, particularly in the reduction of the costs of probate appraisal and of the services of guardians ad litem. 

193. Attempts to establish conciliation divisions in the probate courts have so far not succeeded. The need for such divisions with personnel trained in family problems is apparent since the probate courts exercise wide jurisdiction in reaching marital and child welfare decisions. Also concerned are the district courts and in 

Boston the juvenile court. A period of trial is needed in order to determine what functions each of these courts can best perform. 

DISTRICT COURTS 

194. Suggestions for the future of the District Courts are contained in Paragraphs 5 and 14-33 of this report. The present operation of the courts is made possible by the devoted efforts of Chief Justice Nash, his assistant, his administrative committee and those of his justices, whether full-time, part-time or special, who are able and willing to sit not only in their own courts but in others.

The latter service is often on short notice and at considerable personal inconvenience to the judge. 

195. With the use of the justices in remand cases and in sixman jury sessions active judges are gaining experience in depth and variety. It is essential, therefore, that in the selection of the justices of these courts well-qualified candidates only be considered. 

196. Chief Justice Nash's Third Annual Letter, December 31, 

1966, to the Justices and other court officials, contains valuable and detailed information concerning the expanding work of the District Courts. These courts, standing in the front line of the administration of justice, must daily attempt to meet the demands of litigants who hope to receive prompt judgment administered in modern surroundings in accordance with the best and latest standards of judicial performance. The District Courts are responding to these demands within the limits of the resources made available to them but the justices are constantly frustrated by lack of necessary personnel, equipment and space. The District Courts are supported by the counties and from the counties much more is needed. 

197. During the year Justices Augustus Loschi of the East 

Boston District Court and William L. Macintosh of the First

District Court of Eastern Worcester died, leaving vacancies to be filled.

 THE MUNICIPAL COURT OF THE CITY OF BOSTON 

198. This court has demonstrated its ability to handle a tremendous volume of civil and criminal litigation. With its central location and with some additions to its staff of clerks and court officers, it will have the resources to make an even greater contribution to reducing the case load of the Suffolk County trial courts. 

199. Both by the assignment of justices to Suffolk Superior 

Court sessions and by perfection of the twelve-man jury proposals, the justices of the Boston Municipal Court can become very effective working partners of the justices of the Superior Court sitting in 

Suffolk County. Great administrative economies, furthermore, seem possible by modifications of the remand procedure in Suffolk County that would obviate transfers and retransfers and that would use the justices and the staff of the Boston Municipal Court as needed in superior court sessions. 

BOSTON JUVENILE COURT 

200. On September 27, 1965, St. 1965, c. 659, transferred juvenile case jurisdiction in the Roxbury district to the Boston Juvenile Court. With a heavy juvenile case load and with inadequate facilities the Roxbury Court had become seriously handicapped in its juvenile work. With nine months of the change reflected in the 1966 reports from the two courts the juvenile cases reported by the Roxbury Court declined from 715 in 1965 to 132 in 1966, a drop of 583 cases. The Boston Juvenile Court reported in those years an increase from 833 to 1,390, an increase of 557 cases. 

201. The Boston Court has added four probation officers to its staff, making a total of sixteen, and is equipped to handle the combined case load, although its facilities in the old Suffolk County court house are not adequate. There has been a sharp decline in the number of commitments of juveniles with increased placement in homes and educational institutions. 

202. The policy of the court with respect to protection of the rights of juveniles, the right to counsel for example, appears to meet modern standards. It is interesting to note that a staff member of the Massachusetts Defenders Committee is in daily attendance at the court. 

203. The work of the court's psychiatric clinic is expanding rapidly. The clinic is now served by twelve part-time psychiatrists.

 APPENDIX II REPORT OF THE STATISTICS OF THE WORK ACCOMPLISHED BY THE VARIOUS COURTS 

In nine years of reports of total civil and criminal entries in the various courts, the total entries have increased 67%. For 1958 total entries were 567,691; in 1966, 948,347.

While this does not reflect directly the number of cases, civil and criminal, which go to trial, it does demonstrate the vast increase in the work load of the judicial department: of judges, clerks, probation officers, prosecutors and all the other officers of the courts.

It does reflect indirectly but still accurately the increased work load of the judges, since the ratio of cases tried to total entries holds steady.

It should be noted that the increase in entries far exceeds the population increase of 9½ per cent in about this same period.

 SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

Three hundred and thirty-one cases were decided on appeal. 

Of these 26 cases, 17 at law and 9 in equity, originated in the single justice session. As usual the largest numbers, 260, were from the 

Superior Court, 148 law, 101 equity, and 11 Workmen's Compensation. The remainder came from the Land Court, 8; from the 

Probate Courts, 30; and from the District and Boston Municipal 

Courts, 7. The sources and their volume of cases remained substantially the same as in previous years with an increase in the totals from 318 in 1965 to 331 in 1966. All cases were decided during the year.

On the average less time elapsed between the entry of cases and the decisions than in the previous year. The average time was about four months as compared with a little over five months in the year before. The interval of time between entry and decision does not measure accurately the time required by the Court to complete the work, since postponements granted to counsel may cause a case to be argued at a later date than it would normally be heard.

Of that time almost 70% is from entry to consultation. This latter break-down has been used in prior reports and is continued in order to make comparisons. Of more interest to lawyers who wait for a decision after argument or submission on briefs, a rough average of the time from argument or submission on briefs to decision is about two months.

Again of interest to appellants and their attorneys, their chances of success are less than one to two. In 82 cases, or 24%, the lower court decision was reversed; in 198, or 60%, affirmed, in 

16, or 5%, modified and affirmed. The balance of 35 cases, or 11%, were reported without decision. Seventy-three cases, or 22%, were submitted on briefs without oral argument. This was a substantial increase from last year's 31, or 10%.

There were fewer rescript opinions, 87, as compared to 110 in 

1965. There were three advisory opinions.

Prior reports have not stated figures for total entries in the full court. A single decision may dispose of two or more cases which may involve separate records and thus involve more work in the decision making process than a single case. Also as reflecting work in the clerk's office, cases may be entered and settled before argument. It is also possible for a case to be settled after argument and before decision.

 BOSTON MUNICIPAL COURT 

The decrease in civil entries noted last year was changed to an increase from 19,944 in 1965 to 21,008 in 1966. In 1965 1,730 cases were remanded; in 1966 1,461. Remand cases tried were 584 as against 529 tried in 1965. There was a substantial decline in the number of cases removed to the Superior Court. In 1965 957 were removed; in 1966 670.

The Appellate Division heard 29 cases as compared with six in the previous year. The Supreme Judicial Court affirmed the decision in the one appeal reported.

The average of plaintiff's judgments in contract actions rose slightly to $411.20 and declined a little in tort actions to $271.45. 

On the criminal side there were sharp increases in several categories. Complaints for automobile violations rose from 5,074 to 

10,074; for parking violations, from 64,030 to 102,209. Other complaints, including drunkenness, decreased slightly. 

The police issued 605,882 parking tags, up from 431,727. Receipts from the parking tag office rose from $1,022,188 to $1,562,313. 

Fines, fees, forfeitures, etc., increased from $264,923 to $329,420.

 BOSTON JUVENILE COURT 

With the additional jurisdiction of the court in the Roxbury district, effective September 27, 1965, this year's statistics showing substantial increases in all categories must be compared with the earlier figures from the Boston Juvenile and Roxbury Municipal Courts. As noted in the earlier comments the decrease in 1966 in 

Roxbury juvenile cases is about equal to the increase in the Boston juvenile cases. There appear to be no significant changes in the total figures for both districts. 

In at least two respects the court's statistics do not fully, or clearly, reflect the work of the court. "Judicial determinations 

4,179 hearings," as the explanatory note indicates, cover a variety of actions by the court. There should be a more informative break-down of this figure. 

Second, the statistics fail to show the disposition of complaints. 

An attempt will be made to remedy these deficiencies in the future.

 APPENDIX III THE JUDICIAL SYSTEM A. THE SUPREME JUDICIAL COURT 

The chief justice and six associate justices. 

Final appellate jurisdiction in all cases. 

Original jurisdiction - mainly petitions for extraordinary writs single justice.

Advisory opinions at the request of either branch of the Legislature or the Governor or the Executive Council. 

General superintendence of all courts of inferior jurisdiction.

B. THE SUPERIOR COURT 

The chief justice and forty-one associate justices. 

General trial jurisdiction, jury and non-jury. system.

C. THE PROBATE COURTS 

Circuit The chief judge, twenty-three judges, two part-time judges, and one special judge.

Jurisdiction of probate of wills, administration of estates, appointment of guardians and conservators, divorce and annulment of marriages, separate maintenance, adoption and change of name. Equity jurisdiction except as otherwise provided by statute. County system except judges subject to assignment by chief judge.

D. THE LAND COURT 

The judge and two associate judges. 

Jurisdiction in land titles, registrations and uses. 

E. THE DISTRICT COURTS, THE BOSTON MUNICIPAL COURT, AND THE BOSTON JUVENILE COURT 

The chief justice, fifty-eight associate justices, twenty part-time justices, and eighty-four special justices. 

Jurisdiction of lesser criminal cases and of civil actions at law and of juvenile cases. Non-jury, except where six-man jury sessions being tried experimentally in certain county seats. 

Five-judge appellate divisions.

The chief justice, eight associate justices, and five special justices of the Municipal Court of the City of Boston. 

The justice and two special justices of the Boston Juvenile Court.
