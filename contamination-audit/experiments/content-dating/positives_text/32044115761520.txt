WASHINGTON STATE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL Olympia, Washington January, 1969 Letter of Transmittal 

To the Members of the 41st Legislature:

The statute which established the Washington Legislative Council (RCW 44.24.010-.070) requires the Council to file a biennial report with the Legislature. This report implements that requirement, and gives the specific proposals adopted by the Council as recommendations to this 41st Legislature. Since 1955 all such recommendations have been incorporated in actual bills and introduced in the session, identified as Legislative Council recommendations, and the 1967-1969 bills were so introduced at the beginning of the session, most being pre-filed. The study material, minutes of meetings, transcripts of hearings, and correspondence regarding the various proposals are on file in the Council office and any member of the Legislature is invited to request information on any of the bills. 

The staff of the Council is available to all members of the Legislature, and will provide research or assistance to the fullest extent possible. Respectfully,

DON ELDRIDGE, Chairman

WASHINGTON STATE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL 

The Council

The first Washington State Legislative Council, in 1947, had twenty-one members, of whom eleven were from the House and ten from the Senate. 

The statute has been amended twice since then and the present law provides for thirty-one regular members, of whom sixteen are from the House and fifteen from the Senate. The law also provides that the membership must be bipartisan and neither party may have more than seventeen members. The 

Speaker of the House and the President Pro Tem of the Senate are members by virtue of their offices and the Speaker appoints the remaining representatives while the Senate President appoints the remaining Senators. In each [ 6 ]

case the appointments must be confirmed by the respective house. In addition to the regular members, the 1967 session provided for twelve special members to serve on one Council committee only. Action by the Council provided that these members would be active both with the committee concerned and with the Council itself, but would not have a vote in the Council actions. The vacancies of Senate members are filled by appointment by the remaining Senate members and the same procedure is followed by House members. Vacancies must be filled from the same political party as that of the members whose seat was vacated. The term of office of all members is from the time of confirmation or election until their successors have been confirmed or elected, or until they cease to be members of the Legislature.

The Council's powers and duties include: 

1. Interim Studies. To perform through the Council as a whole, or through committees, duties and functions customarily delegated to special interim legislative committees. 

2. Improve State Government. To study administrative organization and procedure of all agencies of state government and to recommend improvements. 

3. Hearings. To make other studies of state government and to hear complaints, hold hearings, gather information, and make findings of fact. Prior approval of two-thirds of the membership is required to authorize public hearings. 

4. Legislative Matters. To attend generally to business addressed to, or affecting the Legislature during the interim between regular sessions. 

5. Reports. To make periodic reports to members of the Legislature and a biennial report ten days prior to each regular session. 

6. Interstate Cooperation. To cooperate and function with similar Councils in other states and with the Council of State Governments. 

The Council has operated through committees, numbering during the last few years approximately twelve, and paralleling the major standing committees of the sessions. It has a permanent research and clerical staff which prepares materials and reports for the Council and its committees and places all recommendations in actual bill form for introduction in the Legislature. 

The Council frequently utilizes other members of the Legislature as special committee members, and has on occasion established large advisory committees representing various organizations in the state to assist in its major projects. Regular meetings are held at least every three months, and committee and other meetings number in the hundreds during a biennium. As in the other forty-three states which now have Legislative Councils, it is recognized that a sixty day session, once every two years, is inadequate for the scope and complexity of present day government, and the Legislative Council, which has continuity and time to study and appraise these major problems, is one method of assisting the Legislature. In addition to the long-range development of proposed legislation, the members and special members of the Council become extremely well-informed on matters of public assistance, education, natural resources and other areas in which they work. Many of these were of real importance, including the Washington Clean Air Act, which was referred to by federal officials as possibly a guide for the other states. A special committee was set up during the biennium to study air safety matters, the assignment being initiated following the disappearance of a Seattle councilman during a flight in a small plane. Five bills proposed by this committee were approved by the Legislature and they clarified the authority of the Department of Civil Defense to carry out search and rescue missions, provide for registration of pilots for a nominal fee, with the resultant revenues being allocated to preparation of handbooks and an information program. One bill also changed the requirement for registration of private aircraft from the Aeronautics Commission to the Department of Motor Vehicles and placed them on an excise tax basis instead of the former [9]

personal property tax, which had been ignored by hundreds of plane owners. 

In addition, an excise tax on aviation fuel for general aviation (other than commercial airlines) was imposed. The revenue from this bill has already been used in an effective effort to improve the state's airports, to receive available federal funds which the state did not qualify for in previous years, and to make other improvements in the aeronautics field in the state. 

A bill to authorize world trade centers was passed and changes made in the requirements for public bids. 

The Judiciary Committee was very active and sponsored twenty bills.

Some of these dealt with the regulation of debt adjusting firms, small loans, garnishment statutes and similar areas in consumer protection fields. It also had legislation concerning the Administrative Procedures Act, retail sales, small estates, and related topics. It also developed a bill which provided that a resident receiving unsolicited merchandise through the mail was not required to accept it or to pay for it. 

In the Local Government area, a number of important bills were enacted and these included: broad authority to counties to provide water and sewer services; a boundary review board was established to deal with not only annexations as already provided, but with incorporations of various governmental entities, and guidelines for annexation by other than cities under specified proceedings; a Department of Planning and Community Affairs was established by statute, replacing the previous limited operation in the Department of Commerce; another unusual law was adopted authorizing establishment of a community municipal corporation in connection with annexations by cities or consolidation of two cities, the latter being used to bring about the consolidation of Houghton and Kirkland in King county. 

The Council's Public Assistance Committee had one bill, but a very important one, which set up clear-cut standards for licensing of child care homes or agencies.

In the area of revenue and taxation, state controls on charitable trusts were approved by the legislature, an area which had been studied extensively for a number of years without legislation being adopted. Also passed by the legislature to place on the ballot was a constitutional amendment to allow the assessment of land on the basis of use in connection with recreation areas, farms and timberlands. The voters subsequently approved this proposal at the 1968 election and the Council has proceeded to study implementing legislation with a bill being introduced in the 1969 session for this purpose.

Financial Statement 

The Council's basic appropriation for the biennium was $260,730.00. This appropriation was made before the Council law was amended to add four more members. As of December 1, 1968, $181,677.00 of this appropriation had been expended. The 1967 session also had appropriated money to continue to the end of that biennium and from this appropriation was reallocated $7,091, which was entirely expended. For the twelve special members the session provided an appropriation of $15,000.00 and as of December 1, 1968, $11,378.00 of this amount had been used. The legislature had also established a Board of Legislative Ethics to be staffed by the Legislative Council and a separate item of $10,000 was appropriated for this purpose. Of that amount, $9,072.00 had been expended by December 1, 1968, the remainder being allocated to the printing of the report of that group. Finally, a special $10,000 was allocated from the Governor's Contingency Fund after the required approval by members of the Legislative Council and the Legislative Budget Committee. This $10,000 was transferred to the University of Washington for a special research study dealing with emphysema, to be utilized with additional funds from other sources, and staff assistance at the University in working on this project. The request for such a special allocation was made by the Council's Committee on Public Assistance and Public Health and had been initiated originally by the State Labor Committee. 

INTERSTATE COOPERATION 

During the biennium the Council continued to function as the Commission on Interstate Cooperation. In this area the Council participated in development of the multi state tax compact which now has twenty-seven states as members and is intended to prevent action by the Congress which would drastically affect the states' tax structure, revenue sources, rules and audit provisions. The Council also reviewed proposals for a new interstate compact on mining and a proposed compact for mutual assistance in emergencies between the National Guards of Washington and other states. The very successful program on the interstate exchange of reports has been continued and several more states have joined this agreement. One result is the acquisition of numerous current and useful reports from the other states. 

During the biennium, the executive secretary of the Legislative Council served as president of the National Legislative Conference and Representative John L. O'Brien, a member of the Council, was elected President of the 

National Conference of State Legislative Leaders. Senator William Gissberg, vice chairman of the Council, continued as the Washington state representative on the drafting committee for suggested state legislation. The Council also cooperated with the Advisory Commission on Intergovernmental Relations and provided material to that organization, such as the Council's bill enacted in the 1967 session to authorize community councils. Other information was also transmitted to them dealing with the new Department of Planning and Community Affairs, the use of the intergovernmental contract provision and similar research study areas. 

EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE 

During this biennium, the Council amended its rules of procedure to expand the executive committee from five members to six members. This [ 15 ]

was partly to allow more representation from the increased membership of the Council. The executive committee serves as the administrative and fiscal control unit in regard to Council operations. In addition to this function, the executive committee also took jurisdiction of some of the research assignments. This activity resulted in the following recommendations to the 1969 legislature.

Rec. No. 1-Gateway Amendment. This important proposal would remedy the present problem in regard to constitutional amendments by allowing an entire subject, such as taxation, or an entire article of the constitution, such as the legislative article, to be presented to the electorate as a single ballot proposal. A similar bill will probably be introduced as an executive request and will add a provision which will allow presentation of an entire constitution to the voters as a single proposition. (H.J.R. 1)

Rec. No. 2-Convict Labor. This proposal would delete an existing provision which does not belong in the constitution and, in order to continue the fact of such provision, would be reinserted in the statute law. It later could be repealed from the Code if it appears to be entirely unnecessary. This is listed as Recommendation No. 2(a). (H.J.R. 2 and H.B. 12) 

Rec. No. 3-Statistics. This recommendation would delete from the constitution a provision which has not been used and for which it is not necessary to have a constitutional provision. (H.J.R. 7)

Rec. No. 4-Positions. This would amend the present constitution by deleting part of the prohibition against legislators holding other office. (H.J.R. 6)

Rec. No. 5-Suits Against the State. This bill would delete from the constitution a provision dealing with suits against the state, since the statutory provisions established recently give comprehensive coverage to tort liability and to suits against the state. (H.J.R. 13) 

Rec. No. 6-Census. This also is a provision which is not needed in the constitution, which has not been utilized in the past years. (H.J.R. 12) 

Rec. No. 7-Sessions. This proposal would amend the constitution to permit the legislature to convene itself if two-thirds of each house voted in favor of such a call. (H.J.R. 9)

Rec. No. 8-Railroad Passes. This bill would remove from the constitution in Article 2, a prohibition against passes or special treatment of legislators and other public officials, which is adequately covered in existing state law and is not an appropriate item for the constitution. (H.J.R. 14) 

Rec. No. 9-Compensation. This would amend Article 2 in order to clarify provisions which now are a barrier to some desirable statutes and which also could be handled by statute. (H.J.R. 8)

Rec. No. 10-Gubernatorial Veto. This recommendation would establish more clear-cut provisions in regard to the governor's veto by indicating what the terms "item" and "section" mean, so that the veto could not be applied to subsections or provisos, or individual words, and it also would prohibit veto by the governor of any appropriation to the legislature or any of the legislature's own agencies. (S.J.R. 4) 2 [ 16 ]

REPORT (1967-1969) 

Reflecting the general increase in Legislative Council activities, the Agriculture Committee was especially industrious during this interim. The committee met for a total of twenty-one days, more than twice the number of days it met during the previous interim, and in addition, sponsored nine recommendations for Legislative Council adoption, more than three times the number of recommendations sponsored by the committee during the previous interim. 

The committee's meetings were held at numerous locations throughout the state and were well attended by representatives from the Department of Agriculture and from a wide assortment of industries. 

Of the nine recommendations sponsored by the committee, eight were adopted by the Legislative Council. Two of these adopted recommendations are proposed constitutional amendments, which are aimed at deleting superfluous material from the Constitution. Of the remaining six adopted recommendations, three are agriculturally oriented measures in the area of horticulture and weed control, and three are diverse consumer protection measures dealing generally with "truth in packaging," the accuracy of certified weights and the wholesomeness of processed foods. 

A notable feature of the committee's activities over this interim has been the development of a fruitful working relationship with the Department of 

Agriculture. At least six of the committee's recommendations resulted from this relationship. In addition, by holding public meetings on a poultry inspection bill to be submitted to the legislature as a departmental request bill, the committee provided an opportunity for interested parties to voice opinions on this bill and to work out their objections to it prior to its introduction in the legislature.

In addition to its work in sponsoring nine recommended bills and in holding public meetings for the Department of Agriculture, the committee devoted much time and effort in studying and obtaining information in the areas of milk marketing, the contractual relationship between growers and processors of farm crops, meat inspection, migratory labor, and wine importation.

In 1963 the legislature enacted a law creating and defining the boundaries of eleven horticulture inspection districts. Since 1963 the locations of economic growth in the horticulture industry have shifted considerably with the [ 19 ]

result that the boundaries defined in the 1963 act are no longer reflective of the size of the horticultural industry within the districts. This imbalance coupled with the fact that each district is largely funded by means of a fee on the horticulture industry within its boundaries has created chronic financial problems in a number of districts. Recommendation No. 2 would eliminate this problem and prevent its recurrence by authorizing the Department of Agriculture to adjust or abolish district boundaries as might be needed. 

In addition, Recommendation No. 2 would make several "housekeeping" changes in the manner in which the Department of Agriculture maintains and distributes moneys from the horticulture inspection trust funds. 

Constitutional Revision: 

Recomendation Nos. 3 and 4 of the Agriculture Committee would remove 

Articles 21 and 24 from the Constitution. After much studying and hearing testimony from numerous attorneys and other interested parties, the committee determined that Articles 21 and 24, which deal respectively with water use and state boundaries, add nothing worthwhile or of any fundamental importance to the Constitution and, therefore, in the interest of having a short and meaningful Constitution should both be repealed. Weighmasters:

Recommendation No. 5 is directed at increasing the availability and convenience of a weighmaster's services for those persons and industries that have use for such services. In addition, this recommendation contains several provisions aimed at ensuring the accuracy of the certified weight tickets issued by weighmasters.

The committee has held four public meetings on this recommended bill or earlier drafts of it. There does not appear to be any industry or person having objections to this recommendation as presently drafted. 

Weights and Measures: 

Recommendation No. 6 would provide for a new Weights and Measures Act which, among other things, would authorize the director of the Department of Agriculture to make selected inspections of commercial weighing devices as he deems necessary; would require that all weights and measures used by commercial firms in repairing or servicing weighing or measuring devices be calibrated by the Department of Agriculture; and would abolish the requirement that all commercial weighing and measuring devices have attached thereto an official seal of approval. 

The primary purpose of this recommendation is to ensure the accuracy of commercial scales, and thus make more certain that the Washington consumer will, "get what he pays for".

The committee has held four public meetings on this recommendation or on earlier versions of it.

Food Processing: 

The Washington Food Processing Act, which was enacted by the 1967 

legislature, contains provisions providing for the licensing and inspecting of food processing plants. However, the act exempts from these provisions any [ 20 ]

food processing plant that is "subject to and being inspected" by the federal government. In effect, this means that of the some five hundred and fifty food processing plants in the state, only two hundred and seventy-four are licensed and inspected by the state. There would be no problem raised by these facts if federal inspections of food processing plants were uniformly frequent and if the federal inspection laws or rules contained retort standards that is standards to govern the time and temperature for the cooking of processed foods and providing for devices to govern the recording of such time and temperature. It is the position of the Department of Agriculture that federal inspections of some food processing plants are too infrequent and that the federal inspections, although otherwise comprehensive, are incomplete due to their failure to contain retort standards. 

Recommendation No. 7 of the Agriculture Committee would eliminate the present exemptions from licensing and inspecting under the Food Processing Act. In addition, this recommendation would render the rule making authority granted to the Department of Agriculture under the act commensurate with both the actual need for such authority and the physical capability of the department to carry out and enforce the rules it has adopted. 

The committee has heard extensive testimony on this recommendation or earlier versions of it from a great number of food processors, from representatives of three food processor trade associations and from representatives of the Department of Agriculture. The purpose of this recommendation is to protect the Washington consumer from purchasing food not fit for human consumption.

Noxious Weed Control: 

Recommendation No. 8 of the Agriculture Committee would provide a new program to enable counties to more effectively control noxious weed infestation. That such a new program is needed there can be little doubt for not only are the existing noxious weed laws woefully inadequate and largely unimplemented, but in addition, the problem of noxious weed infestation appears to be growing even more acute. 

The committee heard extensive testimony on the subject of noxious weed infestation and worked with a great number of interested persons in the drafting of Recommendation No. 8.

Horticultural Pests and Diseases: 

Recommendation No. 9 of the Agriculture Committee is directed at obtaining a greater degree of compliance with the requirement of existing law that the owners of horticultural plants or trees take such measures as are necessary to control or disinfect any horticultural pest or disease present on such plants or trees. Under this recommendation, a county, if it chose to, could create a board which would have the role of ensuring compliance with the horticultural pest and disease law. This role is presently vested largely in each county's prosecuting attorney. Unfortunately, due to factors beyond the control of the prosecuting attorneys, the present law has not been adequately enforced.

The committee has held three public meetings on the problem of horticultural pests and diseases. Recommendation No. 9 was drafted with the help of numerous members of the Washington State Horticultural Association. [ 21 ]

Contractual Relationship Between Processors and Growers: 

The committee has held three meetings at which it elicited testimony on contractual problems between processors of farm crops and growers of such crops. It appears that there have been abuses by some processors who have unilaterally and summarily cancelled informal agreements with a grower to purchase the grower's crops. The committee had considered a bill aimed at curbing this type of unethical contracting practice, but when it appeared that many growers did not feel that a problem existed, the committee decided to discontinue its studying in this area. 

Milk Marketing: 

The committee considered a bill defining and prohibiting unfair trade practices in the milk marketing industry. Due to the failure of any member of the milk marketing industry to show any support for the bill, the committee decided to cease its consideration of the bill.

In addition to three public meetings on the milk marketing bill, the committee held two public meetings on a bill sponsored by the Washington State Dairymen's Federation which bill provided for a state-wide milk marketing order.

At all of its meetings on milk marketing, the committee received testimony indicating that the recent federal Puget Sound Milk Marketing Order has helped considerably to stabilize milk prices in the Puget Sound market area.

Meat and Poultry Inspections: 

The committee has studied in detail the manners in which the recent federal Wholesome Meat Act and Poultry Products Inspection Act will affect this state's meat packing and poultry processing industries.

It appears that this state's meat inspection program has already been accepted by the federal government as sufficiently meeting the requirements of the federal Wholesome Meat Act. This means that the state is eligible to receive from the federal government an amount equal to one-half of the cost of the state's meat inspection program. 

As to poultry inspection, the committee held two meetings on this subject at which members of the poultry processing industry and the Director of the Department of Agriculture testified. There appeared to be a consensus at these meetings that the state should enact a poultry inspection act which would qualify the state to receive federal matching moneys for the cost of its inspection program and which would ensure that the administration of the poultry inspection program would be at the state level and not in remote 

Washington D. C.

The committee did not sponsor a poultry inspection bill for the reason that the Department of Agriculture is doing so in a departmental request bill. 

Wine Importation: 

The committee held three joint meetings with other committees of the Council at which testimony was presented concerning the marketing in this state of wine produced in other states, notably in California. [ 22 ]

Migrant Farm Labor: 

The committee devoted considerable time to studying some of the problems raised by the treatment afforded migrant farm laborers in this state.

REPORT 

In an attempt to streamline the Constitution, the Commerce, Industry, Trades and Professions Committee reviewed Article XII relating to corpora tions other than municipal. Since most of the sections within this article are covered by general statute, it was felt that the constitutional provisions were antiquated and, therefore, should be repealed. 

Another constitutional amendment which was proposed by the committee relates to harbor lines. The proposal as submitted reduces the wordage in this particular article of the Constitution and authorizes the legislature, by general law, to locate harbor lines and the usage of that area between the inner and outer harbor lines. As presently stated, the legislature is required to appoint a commission which is authorized to establish harbor lines. Since the legislature is responsible for what happens to tide lands in the State of Washington, the committee felt it essential that the legislature be authorized to establish these lines if they so desire or to appoint a commission. 

Independent of the constitutional amendment, the committee also introduced legislation which, if passed, will make the legislature the harbor line commission as required under the existing constitutional amendment. Within the proposed legislation there is also set forth the creation of harbor lines and the usage of the area between the inner and the outer harbor lines. It does not affect the commercial uses that exist between the inner and outer harbor lines under present legislation, but it does restrict the development of multi-residential units and the development of the area for purposes other than recreational or "commercial uses for the convenience of navigation.” 

Shorelands and navigable waters and contributing tributaries could not be sold under this legislation.

The 1967 legislature assigned a resolution to the Legislative Council to study whether the Liquor Board should continue to handle out-of-state wines, the comparison of the regulation of the wine industry by our sister states, and the effects of such regulations upon agricultural and manufactured products. The committee gave a cursory review to these points, however, the major thrust of the committee's investigation was the review of Substitute House Bill 635 (1967) as to the effectiveness of this measure replacing the revenue if out-of-state wine sales should be authorized in grocery stores and not restricted to liquor stores. During the course of the biennium, the 

Commerce, Industry, Trades and Professions Committee met with the Agri culture Committee and the Revenue and Regulatory Agencies Committee to consider this legislation. It was indicated that the percentage included within the bill would replace the anticipated loss of revenue which would come about by direct sale of out-of-state wine to retailers.

Other items which the committee considered were the Fair Trade and Unfair Practices Act, drug prices, private investigators, regulation of land sales, the composition of the Board of Pharmacy, air pollution, obscene literature, temporary real estate licenses, and off-shore purchases. Information is available on these subjects within the Council files if any person should desire further information regarding these subjects. [ 24 ]

Improper Venue 

It has been brought to the Committee's attention that all too often a creditor or a collection company purposely brings suit against a purported debtor in an improper county which is not the county in which the purported debtor resides. The law provides that where a defendant is sued in an improper county, he may have a change of venue to a court in the proper county, usually the county of his residence. However, because the cost of employing counsel to obtain this change of venue is considerable and might even be greater than the amount for which the defendant is being sued, there is significant pressure on the defendant to pay the amount of the claim, regardless of whether the claim is just or unjust. Recommendation No. 1 of the committee would change the venue law so that the plaintiff who selects the wrong county in which to sue would have to pay the defendant's cost in removing the suit to the proper county, should a change of venue be obtained.

Detention of Material Witnesses 

In order to ensure that a material witness to a crime will testify in the trial of the person accused of the crime, the law provides that under certain circumstances the court may incarcerate the material witness pending the trial of the case. Recommendation No. 2 would provide that where a material witness is incarcerated due to his failure to obtain surety to cover his appearance, he will receive for each day of his incarceration an amount equal to the daily juror fee of a juror serving in the superior court. This juror fee is presently ten dollars per day. Under existing law such a material witness might be eligible to receive one dollar for each day of his incarceration.

Criminal Records of Identification 

Frequently when a person is arrested and taken into police custody, the police have a photograph taken of the arrested person, and the photograph, along with other indicia of identification, is indefinitely maintained in official custody.

Recommendation No. 3 provides, with certain exceptions, for the destruction of this photograph and other records of identification, if the person so identified is found not guilty of the crime for which he was arrested or if the [ 32 ]

charges against him are dropped. The purposes of this recommendation are to protect each person's constitutional right to privacy and to prevent an innocent person's reputation from being ruined as the result of a public display of criminal arrest records, particularly photographs. 

Uninsured Motorist Proceedings 

If a party is to recover damages under the uninsured motorist clause of his motor vehicle liability insurance policy, he must prove in arbitration proceedings that the other party to the motor vehicle accident had no liability insurance. Recommendation No. 4 would lighten this frequently impossible burden of proof by providing in certain instances for a rebuttable presumption that the other party to the accident had no liability insurance. 

Interest on Judgments

Under existing law the interest rate on a judgment is six percent per annum. The fact that this rate of interest is significantly lower than the present commercial rates has resulted in the undesirable practice of many judgment debtors, particularly insurance companies, to either use their right of appeal as a means to retain the use of money at a low interest rate or use the threat of appeal as a bargaining device to reduce the amount of a judgment which a plaintiff will accept. Recommendation No. 5 would discourage this abusive use of appeal by increasing the interest rate on judgments to eight percent per annum. 

In addition, Recommendation No. 5 would make interest on a judgment accrue from the date of the verdict in any case where a trial court has been directed on appeal to enter judgment on a verdict or in any case where a judgment entered on a verdict is affirmed on appeal. 

Suspension of Driver's License

The motor vehicle financial responsibility law provides that upon receipt of notice of an unsatisfied judgment which is the result of a motor vehicle accident and which is against a licensed driver of this state, the Department of Motor Vehicles shall forthwith suspend the license of the driver. Unfortunately, there have been some instances under this law where a driver has had his license suspended even though he had never received actual notice of the suit against him and even though the efforts, if any, to so notify him were legally insufficient. The law is clear that where legally insufficient efforts are made to notify a defendant of the suit against him, then any default judgment entered against the defendant is invalid. However, the fact that the judgment is invalid may be of little consolation to a defendant who has already had his driver's license suspended and who will have to wait until the judgment is either satisfied or set aside before the suspension will be lifted. Indeed, if the defendant has great need for his driver's license, he may find it more convenient and less costly to pay the invalid judgment. 

Recommendation No. 6 would correct the financial responsibility law by precluding the Department of Motor Vehicles from suspending the driver's license of a judgment debtor who had received legally insufficient notice of the suit against him.

Extradition for Non-support 

Under the extradition provisions of the Uniform Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act, it is possible for the governor to extradite a Washington [ 33 ]

resident to a state in which he had never resided but to which his wayward and deserting wife had gone to live. This is true regardless of the fact that the husband's marital behavior may have been exemplary. Recommendation 

No. would allow for greater justice in extradition proceedings. This recom mendation would amend the Uniform Reciprocal Enforcement of Support Act so that such a Washington resident could not be extradited unless he is charged with failing to comply with a court order for support arising from a decree of divorce or separate maintenance. 

Arrest Citations 

Recommendation No. 8 would enable a police officer to issue an arrest citation at the scene of a motor vehicle accident, if the evidence at the scene of the accident indicates that a driver has committed a traffic offense. 

Presently, an arrest citation for a traffic offense may be issued only if the offense was committed in the presence of a police officer. For a traffic offense not committed in a police officer's presence, the only way to arrest the offender is through an arrest warrant, the service of which may be extremely difficult if the offender resides in a county other than where the offense was committed. The difficulties in serving arrest warrants, together with the strong evidence frequently found at the scenes of motor vehicle accidents, are the reasons why the committee sponsored Recommendation No. 8. It is noted that this recommendation would place Washington into closer conformity with the provisions on arrest citations in the Uniform Motor Vehicle Code. 

Odometer Tampering

The Committee heard much testimony indicating the considerable extent to which used car purchasers in this state, including used car dealers, are victimized by their sellers, who, prior to sale, had turned back the miles registered on the car's odometer. Recommendation No. 9 is directed at putting a stop to this pervasive and fraudulent practice. 

Custody of Prisoners

Under existing law a person appealing his conviction of a felony may spend two or three years languishing in a county jail while he waits for the

Supreme Court to act on his appeal. The county jails are not designed for holding prisoners over extended periods of time, and nor are the counties in a financial position where they can afford the drain on county funds necessary for the maintenance of such prisoners. Recommendation No. 10 would correct this existing law by providing for the transfer to a state institution of any person imprisoned in a county jail pending the appeal of his conviction of a felony.

Motor Vehicle Violations by Juveniles 

At present there is a significant backlog of cases in the juvenile courts. 

One reason for this backlog is that the juvenile courts have jurisdiction over the great number of cases involving juveniles charged with traffic offenses. 

Recommendation No. 11 would provide that juvenile traffic offenders be tried and their cases disposed of in the same manner and in the same courts as in cases involving adults charged with traffic offenses. The Committee's reasons in sponsoring this recommendation are twofold: First, it is thought that the adoption of the recommendation would reduce the case congestion in the [ 34 ]

juvenile courts. Secondly, disposing of cases involving traffic offenses does not appear to be within the purpose for which juvenile courts were created -that is, the protection of juvenile offenders from the more inflexible and formal criminal procedures designed for adult offenders.

Wage Garnishment 

The Committee held three long public meetings on the subject of wage garnishment. At these meetings the Committee heard a wide variety of testimony touching on almost every conceivable aspect of the laws relating to wage garnishment. This testimony ranged from arguments for the abolition of wage garnishment to arguments for keeping the existing laws on wage garnishment virtually intact. It was notable, however, that almost every party who testified before the Committee on this subject recognized, to some degree, that corrective legislation is needed in the following two respects: 

First, to restrict the use of the remedy of garnishment before judgment of an employee's wages and second, to make it more convenient for an employee to collect the money exemption from garnishment which he is entitled to by law. The main thrust of Recommendation No. 12 is in these two areas. 

As to the garnishment of wages before judgment, the Committee was impressed by an abundance of testimony indicating the manner in which this remedy lends itself to abusive debt collection practices and to the perpetration of consumer frauds.

In a case involving the garnishment of wages before judgment, the creditor garnishees the purported debtor's wages prior to any court judgment holding that the debt is indeed owing. In such proceedings the garnishment order is issued by the court in the form of a writ commanding the debtor's employer to withhold any money due the debtor from him. Generally speaking, if the debtor does not contest the validity of the debt upon which the writ was issued, then judgment is entered for the creditor and the amount withheld by the employer is paid to the creditor to the extent that it is necessary to satisfy the judgment. 

Complying with the provisions of a garnishment writ is considered to be a nuisance by most employers. For this reason it is a common practice among employers to discharge any employee who has sustained one or two wage garnishments. From the debtor's standpoint, however, the problem is significantly greater than a nuisance and the fact that he may lose his job, together with any job seniority, provides the background for explaining the frequent injustices caused by wage garnishments before judgment. 

Because a wage garnishment may mean the loss of employment, a mere threat of garnishment can be effectively utilized to force a debtor to pay in excess of the amount he owes, or even to pay on a debt which is fraudulent and unenforceable. The debtor who is confronted with this type of threat is placed in an excruciating dilemma. He does not want to pay on a debt, or any portion of a debt, for which he has incurred no obligation, but on the other hand, nor does he wish to lose his employment. 

Recommendation No. 12 is directed at precluding this bludgeon-like use of the remedy of garnishment before judgment. This recommendation would limit the use of garnishment before judgment to a few extraordinary circumstances, as where, for example, a debtor is about to abscond or is concealing [ 35 ]

himself in order to avoid creditors. The Committee believes that it would be a great injustice to deny a creditor the remedy of garnishment before judgment in any of these extraordinary cases where summary action, like garnishment before judgment, may be the only means available to ensure the satisfaction of a legitimate debt. 

As mentioned earlier, the other main thrust of Recommendation No. 12 is concerned with the manner in which an employee whose wages have been garnished, collects the legal amount of his wages exempt from garnishment. 

Because of the cumbersomeness of the existing procedures for obtaining this exemption, which includes taking time off from work and going to court, only an extremely slight percentage of those eligible to receive the exemption bother to claim it. Recommendation No. 12 would correct this problem by providing for the automatic payment of the exemption to the employee by his employer.

In addition to those changes dealing with garnishment before judgment and with the manner of obtaining the legal exemption from wage garnishment, Recommendation No. 12 would also increase the amount of the legal exemption from a floor of thirty-five dollars to a standard exemption of fifty dollars; would conform the garnishment laws to the investment security provisions of the Uniform Commercial Code; would update the law dealing with surety bond requirements prior to the issuance of a writ of garnishment; and finally, would broaden the jurisdiction of the courts to review a default judgment entered against a garnishee for failing to answer a writ of garnishment.

Constitutional Revision The Committee studied Articles I, IV, XIX, XXV and XXVII of the 

Constitution, which articles are captioned respectively as the Declaration of 

Rights, the Judiciary, Exemptions, Jurisdiction, and Schedule. Also, the

Committee studied Article III, Section 11, and Article XI, Section 5, of the 

Constitution, which sections deal respectively with the governor's veto power and with county officers.

The Committee determined that there is no important or sufficiently justifiable reason to revise Articles I, XIX and XXV, and therefore the Committee recommends that these articles be retained in their present language in the revised Constitution. In regard to Article XXVII, it is the 

Committee's opinion that it is premature at this time to propose any revision of this article. The Committee's reasoning is based upon the fact that the function of Article XXVII is to provide for a schedule to ensure the smooth and orderly transition from one scheme of constitutional government to another such scheme. It is evident, therefore, that this article should not be revised until such time as all of the other proposed revised articles can be studied and provided for in the proposed revised schedule article. 

The Committee studied the Judiciary article, Article IV, in great depth. It held two public meetings on this subject at which judges of the Supreme Court and Superior Court testified on numerous proposals for revision. Due to lack of time, the Committee did not have opportunity to sponsor a revised judiciary article for Legislative Council adoption.

Recommendation No. 13 is a proposed revision of Article XI, Section 5. 

Presently, this section precludes any county officer from holding office in and [ 36 ]

serving in more than one county. It is the opinion of the Committee that this preclusion is undesirable, and that in the interest of obtaining greater economies in county government and enhancing the administration of county government, there should be some legal authorization for the districting of county offices in any two or more contiguous counties where at least one of the counties, due to its slight population, is unable to support full time officers. Recommendation No. 13 would provide authority for the legislature to enact laws providing for this type of districting of county officers. 

Along with the Executive and State Government Committees, the Judiciary Committee participated in the drafting of a proposed revision of Article III, Section 11, which deals with the governor's veto power. This proposed revision, which was adopted by the Legislative Council as an Executive Committee recommendation, would limit the governor's item veto power. 

Compensating Innocent Victims of Crimes 

The 1967 report by the President's Commission on Law Enforcement and Administration of Justice indicates that the victims of violent crimes are generally those persons least able to afford the expenses of their victimization. Further compounding the plight of the victims is the fact that civil remedies are most likely to be unsuccessful because of the poor financial condition and prospects of most offenders. Indeed the state by incarcerating criminal offenders precludes such offenders from earning any money with which to make restitution.

In sum, it appears to be the case that generally the victims of violent crimes are not only those persons least able to afford their victimizations but, in addition, they suffer losses for which they are not compensated in any way. The Committee in recognition of these unfortunate facts sponsored 

Recommendation No. 14. This recommendation is directed at providing limited compensation for those victims of violent crimes who, as a result of their victimization, experience severe financial hardship. 

Jury Service 

The Committee recognizes that a juror who has served intensively over the duration of a jury term may become hardened to or bored with the judicial process. Such a juror is likely to be inattentive during the trial of a case and to play an undesirable role in the jury's deliberation. Recommendation No. 15 would provide for the discharge of this type of juror. 

Statement Taken from a Party to a Personal Injury Action 

It is almost a universal viewpoint among the expert commentators in the areas of civil procedure and evidence that in order to promote the settling of cases and to prevent unfair surprises during trials, a party to a personal injury action who has given a statement to the other party, or more likely to the insurance company of the other party, should have the right to be provided with a copy of the statement, or at least to examine the statement if he so desires.

In this state there appears to be a difference of opinion among judges on whether a party to a personal injury action has a right to examine his [ 37 ]

statement. Recommendation No. 16 would place this state in line with the thinking of the expert commentators and with the judges who do hold that such a party has a right to examine his statement. 

Appointment of a Guardian Ad Litem in Probate Proceedings 

Recommendation No. 17 would change the probate law by permitting the court, in its discretion, to waive the appointment of a guardian ad litem for a minor where the decedent's spouse, who is also a natural parent of the minor, stands to inherit all of the decedent's property under the terms of a will.

The present probate law requires the court to appoint a guardian ad litem for any minor who would inherit any property from the estate of the deceased had the deceased died intestate. It is the Committee's reasoning that this law is too inflexible and that in order to avoid the unnecessary spending of estate moneys for payment of a guardian's fee, the court should have discretionary authority to waive the appointment of a guardian for a minor whose one parent dies and leaves all his or her property to the surviving parent.

Dishonored Checks 

A 1967 statute provides certain requirements which must be satisfied before a holder of a dishonored check may recover interest and collection costs on the check. These requirements are as follows: First, notice of the dishonor shall have been sent to the drawer of the check and the drawer shall have not made the check good within fifteen days of such notice. 

Second, no attorney's fee shall be collected, except as has been set by a court.

It seems that some collection companies have the practice of collecting interest and collection costs on dishonored checks without first satisfying the statute's requirements. This practice has been especially abusive where it has been used in conjunction with a threat of garnishment if the drawer of the check does not pay the amount demanded. Recommendation No. 18 is directed at eliminating this unethical practice and obtaining greater compliance with the requirements of the 1967 statute.

Salaries of Prosecuting Attorneys 

The purpose of Recommendation No. 19 is to fulfill the legal needs of the counties. In some counties no attorney willing to run for the office of prosecuting attorney can be found. In still other counties part-time prosecutors are not able to adequately fulfill some of the most pressing legal needs of their counties.

Recommendation No. 19 would raise the salaries of prosecuting attorneys and, thereby, would make the office more attractive to attorneys. In addition, this recommendation would create full-time prosecuting attorneys in class IV counties.

Disqualification of Judges 

If a litigant has reason to believe that the judge slated to hear his case is prejudiced against him, then under existing law he has the right to have the judge transfer his case to another judge. There have been instances where [ 38 ]

litigants exercising this right in the lower courts have been greatly inconvenienced due to the judge's unreasonable delay in transferring the case. Recommendation No. 20 would authorize the Administrator for the State Courts to effectuate the transfer in the event of such unreasonable delay by a judge of a lower court.

Bill on the Retirement of Judges of the Courts of Record 

Recommendation No. 21 would provide for a new program for retirement of superior and supreme court judges and for judges of the court of appeals when such court is established. Judges retired under this new program would be eligible to receive increased benefits, but also would have the duty when called upon, to make themselves available for judicial service. It is the opinion of the Committee that this recommendation's feature of post-retirement judicial service would alleviate to some degree the case congestion in the courts of record of this state, and also would substantially reduce the need for the creation of additional superior court judgeships. 

Contracts for Dancing Instructions 

Recommendation No. 23 is directed at preventing dance studios from entering into unconscionable contracts for dancing instructions. It appears that some dance studios have been "signing up" elderly and lonely widows and widowers to contracts which are not only for huge amounts, but, in addition, are frequently for long terms or for life and are enforceable even after the death of the student or after his physical inability to continue with instructions. Illustrative of this type of abusive contract is the case of an elderly Tacoma woman who, within six months after the death of her husband, had used the proceeds from the sale of her home, which represented practically all her financial resources, to negotiate checks in excess of fifteen thousand dollars in payment of her contractual obligation with a dance studio.

Recommendation No. 22 would place a ceiling on the amount which may be contracted for dancing instructions and would provide for relief from performance of a contract for dancing instructions in the event of either the student's death or his physical inability to take instructions. 

Notice of Guardianship Proceedings 

Existing statute provides that the clerk of the court must give notice to interested parties as to any filing with the court of any one of four particular legal documents touching on a guardianship estate. It is the opinion of the 

Committee that probably inadvertently the draftsman of this statute failed to include within this notice requiring category two of the most important and far reaching legal documents that can be filed in guardianship estate proceedings-that is, petitions to restore competency or to terminate guardianship. Recommendation No. 24 would include these petitions within the notice requiring category.

Railroad Passes 

The Committee was assigned the task of redrafting a bill before the Legislative Council, which bill had the purpose of preventing public office holders from accepting any transportation not available to the general public. [ 39 ]

The Council found this bill objectionable for the reason that it was too broad and would prohibit, among other things, a public office holder, whose son works for an airline, from accepting the customary free air transportation which some airlines frequently make available to the families of their employees. Recommendation No. 25 of the Judiciary Committee would correct this problem by limiting the criminal sanction of the bill to a situation where a public office holder accepts free transportation which is made available to him only because of his status as a public office holder. 

Debt Adjusting 

The law allows a debt adjuster to recover a cancellation or default fee in the event of his client's default or cancellation of the contract agreed to between the debt adjuster and the client. There is sufficient reason to believe that some debt adjusters are more interested in collecting this fee than in liquidating their client's debts. Indeed, the Committee received testimony showing that the prospects of recovering cancellation or default fees has led to the blatently undesirable practice of some debt adjusters to actually encourage their clients to default under or cancel the debt adjusting contract. 

Recommendation No. 27, among other things, would reduce this cancellation or default fee from the present ceiling of seventy-five dollars to a ceiling of twenty-five dollars. It is the Committee's opinion that this recommendation would place the cancellation or default fee in its proper perspective. 

Liability of District Court Judges 

Presently a district justice court judge is liable for any misuse of court funds by a court employee. This is true even if the judge takes every conceivable precaution to ensure that court funds would not be misused. The main thrust of Recommendation No. 28 is to make this liability of a district justice court judge more reasonable.

Use of Dangerous Weapons by Demonstrators 

There does not appear to be any criminal statute to effectively cover a certain type of civil demonstration in which the participants display dangerous weapons as a means for both gaining the public's attention and for disrupting government or private business. In the absence of such a statute, it is conceivable that a demonstration of this nature may be held in the Legislative Building during this legislative session. Indeed, the California legislature has already suffered such a demonstration. Among other things, Recommendation No. 29 is directed at sparing the Washington Legislature from a similar experience.

Intercepting Private Communications 

The 1967 legislature enacted a law prohibiting, except under certain conditions, any intercepting of private communications whether by electronic bugging devices, wire-tapping or other means. Due to two recent United

States Supreme Court decisions and recent federal legislation, there is little doubt but that this 1967 law has been rendered unconstitutional. Recommendation No. 30 would not only eliminate the unconstitutional features of the 

1967 law, but, in addition, would maintain the restrictive policy of this state in regard to the circumstances where the intercepting of private communications is permissible. [ 40 ]

"Sitting Out" a Fine in Jail and Private Employment Agencies 

After the last regular Legislative Council meeting on November 22, 1968, the Judiciary Committee held a meeting in Olympia at which it reported out two measures, Recommendation No. 31 and Recommendation No. 32, which deal respectively with "sitting out” a fine in jail and with private employment agencies. As of the date of this report, these measures have not been voted on by the Legislative Council. A brief summary of these recommenda tions follows: 

Recommendation No. 31

The 1967 Legislature enacted a law which was aimed both at increasing the daily amount which a person "sitting out" a fine in jail has credited against the fine and at making this daily amount uniform throughout all the courts of this state. Inadvertently, the 1967 Legislature failed to include within the scope of this law one of the two types of municipal courts provided for under the 1961 Justice Court Act. Recommendation No. 31 would correct this oversight. 

Recommendation No. 32

The Committee heard much testimony indicating that in furtherance of this state's consumer protection laws, legislation should be enacted to regulate and license private employment agencies. Recommendation No. 32 would accomplish this result.

The need for regulating and licensing employment agencies is most evident when one examines the misleading contract forms which some agencies use in signing up job applicants. Not only do some of these contract forms "hide" the agencies' true fees, but in addition contain various types of misleading clauses, some of which purport to be extremely generous in one breath, but then take everything back in the second breath.

Criminal Code Revision 

The Committee has made considerable progress in its assignment to draft a revision of the penal code. It has tentatively approved a number of sections to be included within the revised code and in addition has completed substantially all of the legal research needed for the sections remaining to be drafted. The Committee hopes to continue with this project over the next interim.

VOCATIONAL REHABILITATION 

Last November the labor committee requested authorization from the

Executive Committee to study vocational rehabilitation in cooperation with the Governor's Planning Commission for vocational rehabilitation. The ap- [ 43 ]

proval paved the way for the planning commission to contract with the Council for legal and other research services of the committee's staff counsel. 

The Legislative Council received $6,000 in payment for the services. The project was successfully completed with a 231 page report presented to the Planning Commission at its meeting on September 19, 1968. The report is entitled Vocational Rehabilitation in the State of Washington: Programs 

Provided by State Agencies, now available in the State Library. 

The history of the development of vocational rehabilitation services in state government has been a story of meeting practical needs as they arose. 

When different needs arose at different times and with varied intensities, it became a matter of expediency with limited funds available to provide for these needs on the occasion. This trend is continuing and snowballing to an extreme unprecedented in the history of the country. Its importance is recognized as ranging from providing a solution to increasing the self-sufficiency of those on the list of welfare rolls, to the change in emphasis in criminal detention from punishment to prevention via rehabilitation.

From interviews conducted with state department personnel, it is apparent that generally one arm of government is not aware of what the other is doing, each department being largely content and concerned only with its own particular purposes. There seems to be a minimum of effort toward coordination of programs and activities with the division of vocational rehabilitation by some agencies providing similar services. State departments as a whole know too little of the extent of services other departments are rendering and do not appreciate the problem of duplication of services that may exist between them. Several of the departments are engaged in a planned extension of these services as they relate to their primary purpose and which serve the need of their particular client. The trend is definitely away from centralization and toward proliferation of services. 

The value of a selected service to an individual is recognized, and a number of agencies have statutory authority to provide specific services. 

There appears to be an element of truth in the idea that each department must be given the necessary authority to provide certain services to its clientele to discharge its responsibility under the law. For instance, employment security provides placement services; public assistance provides social counseling and related social services; institutions provides probation and parole and supervision while on parole. But if a galaxy of services is needed to assist an individual, one agency must be designated to put these together. 

At present the division of vocational rehabilitation is authorized by law to provide in one package the wide range of rehabilitation services which may be necessary to help an individual attain self-support. 

It needs to be said that the division utilizes services of all agencies able to provide any of the many services related to rehabilitation of the individual. 

Finding answers to the total human problem of a person trying to get back to work after years of idleness is not a simple process. Usually social services, welfare services, or medical services, by themselves, are not the answer. What is needed is a single agency capable of dealing with the total personality of the individual, able to consider all the problems with which he needs help, and equipped with the know-how to marshal and plan for [ 44 ]

community or state services to meet these needs. Interdepartmental dialogue and cooperation would minimize duplications and maintain an optimum of efficiency in statewide vocational rehabilitation. 

Hence, one could find support for special services by various agencies. 

Other departments, it may be noted, are attempting to copy important characteristics of the division program, particularly the provision of services to clients on an individual basis. The division is the one agency in the state with the authority to provide all the services needed to help an individual toward self-support.

The impression is left that if there be any lack of communication on higher levels, there is none where the work is done in the field. The coordination which exists among the caseworkers and counselors is evidence that the programs are being successfully implemented on a case by case basis at this level.

However, to meet future needs, coordination in long-range planning is needed. The problem of the jurisdiction of state agencies, and the delineation of functions in government, should be resolved at the same time the substance of these functions is determined. Recommendation

To assist the planning commission for vocational rehabilitation in its stated purpose of determining the needs for vocational rehabilitation services by 1975, it is recommended that every state department which renders specialized rehabilitation services be asked to present its program to the planning commission. It is hoped that a dialogue will be established which will provide the necessary knowledge and understanding which will give the commission the information, direction and coordination necessary to meet its purpose.

H.B. 477 (1967) OMNIBUS BILL OF THE DEPARTMENT OF LABOR AND INDUSTRIES 

Several meetings of the committee were devoted to H.B. 477, submitted by the department of labor and industries at the last session of the legislature, where it passed the House of Representatives with amendments, but the Senate refused to concur in the amendments. It never was pushed through the free conference committee.

This is an amendatory act which is omnibus in nature. Many of the amendments are mere housekeeping measures designed to either eliminate ambiguity or to update obsolete working that does not accord with actual practice under the law. Others are truly substantive and work a change in existing law.

Among other things, the proposed omnibus bill deletes the four statutory requirements governing the determination of base rates for each class, and substitutes therefor a clear mandate that base rates be established for each class on the basis of maintaining the solvency of the Accident Fund as a whole, thereby creating the fund on a unified "one-company" basis in keeping with sound, modern day insurance practice as followed by other state funds and private insurance companies who predicate their premium [ 45 ]

rates in accordance with the experience of the entire fund. This would enable the department to establish a few classes, self-sustaining within themselves, each of which would, however, include many sub-classes not subject to rigid credibility requirements, but each of which would comprise more nearly equivalent risk groupings. Further, any temporary deficiency in a given class or sub-class could be offset in part by the apportionment of a flexible general reserve in accordance with uniformly accepted insurance principles. The result would be more uniform rate structures, diminution of inequitable risk grouping, and elimination of the necessity for drastic annual rate revision for any given class or classes.

The omnibus bill would also enable the department to continue time loss compensation while the workman is being rehabilitated, vocationally. At present, we must terminate such compensation when the condition resulting from the injury, pathologically speaking, reaches a fixed or stabilized status. 

This results in cases where a workman is unable to return to his regular occupation because of his disability, and he is further unfitted for work of a different nature because of his lack of experience and training. 

Arguments against the proposed change in the omnibus bill regarding base rates based on the experience of the accident fund as a whole, centered around the lack of statutory standards other than the fund's solvency and a rating system which may encourage and stimulate accident prevention. 

The suggested amendment does not stipulate that the rating system shall be a uniform system applied uniformly, but, instead, would be that rating system which the department finds is best calculated to rate the risk equitably. 

With regard to the extension of time-loss benefits to the injured workman for the purpose of vocational rehabilitation training, the arguments against the amendment also were concerned with the lack of standards and the exercise of sole discretion by the supervisor of industrial insurance. Four standards are suggested before the extension of time-loss benefits become extended: 

1. The injury results in a permanent disability; 

2. The workman is unable to perform the work he was doing at the time of the injury; 

3. The workman's condition is evaluated by medical and vocational rehabilitation experts; and 

4. The workman is making reasonable progress. 

Additionally, the workman's permanent disability would not be rated and awarded until after the vocational rehabilitation program had been completed.

The committee took no action on the omnibus bill, preferring to wait until it was reintroduced into the next session. The ambiguities regarding the wording and its effects would first have to be cleared up. 

Industrial Insurance: House Resolution No. 67-71: 

The resolution calls for the committee to undertake a study of all aspects of the state workmen's compensation act and the present efficacy of the laws [ 46 ]

upon which they are based. The Committee held a great number of meetings on this topic, one of which was held in Salem, Oregon for the purpose of appraising the Oregon "three-way option" system of industrial insurance.

The "three-way option" system was urged as a possible improvement to the system in Washington where workmen's compensation is handled exclusively by the state through the Department of Labor and Industries. In Oregon, an employer has an option to ensure his employees either through the State Compensation Department, through private insurance carriers, or through self-insurance. The Workmen's Compensation Board, an independent agency, has administrative and regulatory authority over all of these. Organized labor generally opposes the introduction of the three-way option in Washington while employers generally are among its proponents. There still remain serious questions regarding the cost of such a system to the employer and the processing of claims and appeals. The Oregon experience is still too new to clarify many ambiguities within the workings of three-way option, although there are some trends indicated.

Those advocating the three-way option system generally cite "private enterprise" and the freedom of choice for an employer of the insurance system he wishes to ensure under. Employers also generally believe that this system will mean lower premiums for them. Apart from "three-way option," itself, but included in the package proposal for the introduction of the system into Washington, the extension of coverage of all workers is offered (in place of the coverage now in effect in only "extrahazardous employment") and an increase in the benefit rate structure now in effect. Proponents also assail the present system as too slow in the processing of claims, too cumbersome, and find fault in the appeals structure and other details in the operation of workmen's compensation as it exists. 

Opponents of three-way option are convinced that handing a social insurance system over to the "free enterprise" market will work to the detriment of injured workmen, not only because of the profit motive involved, but because the insurance company will be the agent of the employer, whose interests are inimical to the injured workman for whom the workmen's compensation act exists. They also say that three-way option is litiginous and will inherently mean delays brought by crowded dockets and the over-involvement of lawyers, which the conditions of the system will make necessary. Labor is also of the opinion that three-way option will mean higher, rather than lower premiums, a view which the Oregon experience is now beginning to indicate. 

The Committee arrived at no conclusions. [ 47 ]

Recommendation No. 1 is a sincere attempt to create a true partnership agreement between the executive and legislative branches of government to facilitate operation in all areas of automatic data processing in the state of Washington for the benefit of the citizens and to reduce the increasing cost which poor planning and duplication of equipment would bring. Statewide cooperation will help to achieve free exchange of information that is so vital in today's planning and budgeting.

It is becomining apparent that as more and more data processing installations come into being there is less and less communication between one installation and another. It is as if the industry has almost oversold the necessity and that the installation of ADP equipment had become a status symbol to department heads in state agencies. The charge that was made to my committee has been most gratifying in this respect. Every state that we have visited and every person that we have talked to have all applauded three basic intents of this suggested legislation which includes the following basic principles: (1) A true partnership between the executive and the legislature neither of which will completely dominate the ADP field; (2) A standardization of terms and forms to be used in reporting systems by all state agencies and political subdivisions supported in part or in total by state funds; and (3) The opportunity for one single source to maintain a catalogue of information with respect to all research and data which is currently being maintained in machine readable form.

This undoubtedly is one of the most ambitious attempts that has ever been made by any state to coordinate fully the total impact of the automatic data processing age. Several states have gone a long way in applying data processing to the legislative process, and other states have attempted to set up a central system for some data processing activities. However, the legislation which this committee proposes is the first attempt by any state to come to grips with the whole problem and to cover the broad spectrum of information retrieval. Manufacturers and technicians alike join us in this attempt and indicate that the situation is so grave that we must take immediate action. A delay of two to four years will create a costly situation and the problems which we would then face would be more complex and more difficult in their solution than what we are currently facing. 

Lest there by any misunderstanding it is not the hope nor the desire of the committee to create this data base within a short period of time, but only to establish the necessary standards so that any governmental agency that later is to become involved in ADP will have one set of standards by which to build their entire program. No reference is made here nor should be interpreted that this advisory committee will suggest or dictate any type of equipment to be purchased. Our main aim and objective is that the information is compatible and in such form and terms that the information exchanged can be understandable and intelligible not only from one machine to another, but also from one individual to another. All members of the legislature and the public should look upon these measures with a great deal of interest, since no piece of legislation will have such far reaching effect, giving the legislature the ability to receive necessary information that it has so long been lacking.

The 1967 legislature assigned numerous resolutions to the Local Government Committee of the Legislative Council. The Local Government 

Committee recommends that the 1969 legislature adopt legislation which would guarantee a veteran the right of reemployment until he reenlists voluntarily, at which time his rights would then be dissolved. The reason for such action is that in many instances servicemen who retire from the service then return to their former employment and request that they be employed under the rights granted them under existing statute. Such action creates hardships upon governmental agencies in that these people upon returning must be employed and also may require all the retirement rights, which places an undue hardship upon pension systems. 

The second recommendation, relating to the position of a mayor pro tem when the mayor vacates his office before the expiration of a term in third class cities, came to the Council's attention when such action occurred in Walla Walla. As a result of a death in this instance the city was effectively without a mayor, who was responsible for signing bonds, until the next municipal election which, in effect, hamstrung the city for this period of time. Since the law is not clear and definite as to the transfer of power, the 

Local Government Committee recommends legislation which would clearly delineate the same.

In 1948 a constitutional amendment was passed to authorize the consolidation of cities and counties. However, it has never been used because of the inherent limitations. In attempting to provide local units of government with the tools necessary to modernize their own local units of government, the Local Government Committee reviewed this amendment and made crucial changes and are submitting it to the 1969 legislature for their action. The three major changes between the 1948 amendment and the one now being [54]

proposed is that prior to this time such action could only be taken in counties of over 300,000. Under this proposal any county may take this course of action if so desired. Before, a city-county could not be formed until the majority of the qualified electors of the area voted favorably upon the proposition. Now, it only requires a majority of the qualified electors voting on the issue. This vote must be favorable within the proposed area and also in the remainder of the county if any should exist. Under this proposal municipal corporations may be formed, thereby allowing the city-county unit of government to form municipal corporations to handle special problems relating only to a general segment of the area within the city-county. The bonded debt limitation under the 1948 proposal would have restricted the bonded indebtedness to 5% of the taxable property. Under this proposal the city-county cannot exceed 10% of the value of taxable property within the area and municipal corporations established under the unit of government may become bonded to an amount of 5% of the value of taxable property therein. Under this proposal it would become possible for a city-county to become bonded in particular areas to provide special services such as rapid transit.

The population growth in the State of Washington has been at an increased rate and many of these families are locating around the fringes of cities. If this population becomes more dense, there is a tendency for people residing in these areas to become incorporated which leads to fragmented government and deters the growth of the existing city. The existing cities, when planning their services, generally anticipate serving the outlying areas when the growth becomes dense enough to obtain the services which cities provide. Presently, legislation exists which prohibits incorporation of an area if the area has inhabitants of less than 3,000 and is located within five miles of a city having a population of 15,000 or more in a Class AA or A county. 

The proposal submitted to the legislature would require that any area with a population of less than 5,000 would be prohibited from incorporation if the area was located within five miles of a city with 15,000 or more, regardless of the size county.

School districts were not included in the Intergovernmental Cooperation Act in the last session of the legislature and since they do plan for parks and recreational areas, in addition to the regulation of schools, it was considered essential that they also be included within this act so that they may combine with other governmental units in their purchasing and planning activities.

Boundary Review Board. Class AA indicated that their eleven-man board is operating effectively and they do not desire to have their board reduced in number, since they are able to break up into committees to provide preliminary review to any proposed Boundary Review

Board changes. Since boundary changes of governmental units at the local [55]

level have an effect upon the state's growth and development, in addition to its planning, it is also proposed with this legislation that the State Planning and Community Affairs Agency contribute to one-half of the cost of operation for the Boundary Review Boards within the individual counties. It had been stressed to the committee that while the Boundary Review Boards were performing an effective role, it was a burden for the counties to support their operation when the end results of their findings not only assisted the counties but all other local units of government within the county, in addition to the state. At the request of legislators during the biennium, the Local Government Committee was assigned the task of studying law enforcement and how it can be improved within the urban areas. After numerous hearings with law enforcement people, city officials and university people, it was decided that there was room for improvement within the area of law enforcement. Law enforcement, as it relates to the carrying out the intent of the law, did not seem to be as insufficient as did the social and humanitarian effects upon the people when carrying out such law and order. The committee also noted that law enforcement within this state is above average when considering the financial and physical facilities that are available for training law enforcement people. It was the feeling of the Local Government Committee that the law enforcement commission should be broadened so as to include academic representation in order that any research that may be conducted within the universities, colleges, and community colleges could be immediately fed into the law enforcement training courses that are presently being conducted. At the request of the Washington Association of County Commissioners, the Local Government Committee was assigned the subject of platting and subdivision. In a survey of subdivision ordinances conducted by the Puget 

Sound Governmental Conference in 1967, it was noted that county ordinances relating to subdivision were wide and varied as to the requirements and restrictions which must be complied with under the different county ordinances. The state law now is rather vague and, therefore, some counties have found it difficult to establish their authority under the existing legislation. 

Under the proposed legislation terms are defined which presently is not done in existing law. A clear statement is made as to the requirement that plats and subdivisions be approved by the governmental authorities and any subdivision of land into two parcels requires that a plat or subdivision be [56]

filed regarding that land, while under existing law the subdivision may be as high as four. In many instances, this has provided a loophole for land developers, since they would only develop four lots and then the next year develop five more, thereby constantly avoiding the platting and subdivision law. If a person purchases land under the existing law from a person who has failed to file a plat, there is no recourse. Private roads will not be authorized within subdivisions unless a non-profit, private organization is established within the proposed development for the purpose of maintaining streets or roadways. Plats will be subject to review or approval by the county road engineer, local health department, and local planning agencies.

The major work of the committee and committee staff was directed towards a total inventory of state lands and the revenues from state lands. 

This inventory was conducted by the staffs of the Legislative Council and Legislative Budget Committee and any legislator may obtain a copy from the 

Council or Budget Committee. The inventory implements HR 67-93, SR 67-25 

and SR 67-15X, all requesting a study of state lands available for recreation, revenue from these lands and open space areas which are in state ownership. 

The Senate Resolution directing the inventory of state lands and the revenues arising therefrom also requested information as to the economics of appropriating funds to acquire suitable land for parks, outdoor recreation and open space in relation to utilizing suitable public lands for such purposes.

For maximum public benefit, the finite and limited land and natural resources of this state must be conserved and fully utilized to provide the greatest good for the citizens of this state, both the generations now living and those as yet unborn. In terms of the outdoor recreation needs of our citizens, it is axiomatic that our existing state land resources be fully utilized and additional land acquisitions made in those areas where the needs are the greatest and cannot be met even by full utilization of existing state owned or controlled resources.

If state owned or managed lands can be used for several purposes which are compatible, the overall public benefits are thereby increased. It is impor- [60]

tant to realize that in the case of state owned lands as well as with land in general, that when acreage is taken out of an income producing use and is used exclusively for some other non-revenue purpose, then all future income potential, as well as current income, has been eliminated from that land. This does not necessarily mean that a different use or uses might not have a greater benefit to the people of the state. However, when the use of land is changed or restricted, consideration must be given to income which would be lost should the land use be restricted to preclude revenue producing activity. 

Lands which are producing items with a high economic value such as fish, game, food, feed and timber, a change in use which would eliminate or reduce this production should be considered as a cost item in the total price of the new use.

Land used for timber production causes special management problems. 

Current land management techniques require that the total acreage to state timber lands be considered when a future timber cut is to be scheduled. The current cut of timber is based on the future growth and production from present forest acreage. Therefore, it would be very easy to be overcutting state timber if many acres of timberlands were subsequently taken out of production, reducing what could be cut in the future and reducing the ability to reforest lands for subsequent production. Some adjustment may be possible, but when lands are taken out of a land management cycle which is planned over a period of approximately 60 to 100 years, definite problems are caused for land management. An individual tract of state-owned timber land may not produce cash dividends for periods of several years but it is growing "wood," which is "income" (and this could be valued in dollar terms on an accrual basis) and the present rate of cutting from mature timber for all state-owned forest lands is directly dependent on the growth contribution and future harvesting of each individual tract. 

Special problems also exist when trust lands are involved, since the use of these lands is severely curtailed by obligations of the trust and benefits which must be produced for the trust beneficiaries. This is especially true in the case of the school trust and lands which are controlled by the Enabling Act as well as the State Constitution. (Similar obligations exist in the case of the forest board lands which are the subject of statute law.) Assignment of state-owned lands to limited non-revenue functions could cost the state as much or more in lost income as the purchase of private land for the special use. In other words, the actual economic cost of using a state-owned, revenue producing land for recreation use or some other use exclusively will be approximately similar whether state or private lands are utilized. 

In economic terms, there is no such thing as free land. Locking up valuable lands in limited single purpose uses is expensive in terms of economic resources when other compatible beneficial public uses and benefits are thereby foregone. Choices must be made from time to time between competitive uses of all state-owned lands. Although an "income producing priority" may be placed upon certain state trust lands, other compatible uses, including outdoor recreation particularly, must be considered to allow the greatest public benefit. A single use of lands for timber, grazing, or the raising of game is becoming less and less feasible with the rising population, a greater demand for recreation facilities, rapid urbanization, and a tremendous increase in the mobility of the population. New land use techniques may [61]

make possible fully and partially compatible multiple uses, and a pure economic benefit derived from limited use may no longer be the highest and best utilization of the land. The fish, timber, food, feed, and game activities on state-owned lands may have first priority but other compatible uses must be considered to get the full value from lands. In addition, rising land costs make full utilization of state-owned lands mandatory, and while new land purchases by state agencies involved in public outdoor recreation are necessary, it would show poor planning and it would be economically foolhardy to allow lands which could be used for a variety of beneficial purposes, including outdoor recreation, to be limited to a single use. 

The conclusions of the committee, based on the inventory results were as follows: 

1. The present state land resource base is not being fully utilized for outdoor recreation purposes. As an example, over 532,917 acres of potential recreation land lack public access. 

2. Existing state land resources should be developed for full utilization of an optimum multiple use basis. Simply stated, the land resource management objective should be the long-run maximization of those uses meeting the needs of the greatest number of the citizens of this state, within a context of conserving and maintaining the basic resource. 

3. Costs to the state and its political subdivisions can be minimized by full utilization of existing land and water resources especially since much outdoor recreation use is at least partially compatible with many economic (revenue) producing uses. Any additional land acquisition needs for outdoor recreation purposes should be made in those areas where needs are the greatest and cannot be met by full utilization of existing state owned or controlled land resources. 

4. Subject to other needs and priorities, the 1969 Legislature should consider appropriating funds to purchase full ownership (i.e., fee simple title) to trust lands now leased for park purposes where the preservation of public benefits from recreation and conservation use outweighs the monetary return from other high value economic uses. In addition to the benefit of assuring the retention of these lands in park status for public use, the current returns to the trust beneficiaries would be increased. 

The committee investigated erosion problems at Tokeland and in the Willapa Bay area and it was the consensus of members of the committee that the state could not afford to undertake any extensive measures to stop the erosion in the area.

The committee studied drafts of legislation which proposed certification for recreational personnel. The unanimous feeling of the committee was that such a certification program was not presently necessary and the committee is not proposing or endorsing any legislation in this area.

The Senate Resolution called upon the Legislative Council to conduct a study on the causes of emphysema, and urged the Council to devote the sum of $10,000 to the study being conducted by the Department of Preventive 

Medicine of the School of Medicine of the University of Washington. 

The death rate for emphysema, now the fastest rising disease in the United States, has doubled every five years since 1945. The respiratory crippler ranks second only to heart disease as a cause of adult disability. 

Some experts are now estimating that up to 50% of the adult male population of the United States may be affected. 

However, emphysema is not generally regarded as an industrial disease, although the condition may be aggravated by certain industrial work conditions. It apparently does not occur with greater frequency among industrial workers than among the public in general. Smoking is considered to be the major causal factor for emphysema.

Although the Department of Labor and Industries does not maintain records on the incidence of these diseases by industry, this information could be obtained by tracing back through their records. Last year the Department received approximately 200 claims for heart disease and 25 claims for emphysema.

The Committee recommended that the Legislative Council devote $10,000 from its funds to assist the University in its study. The Council, out of budgetary limitations, on a motion taken on May 25, 1968 formerly requested of the governor a grant of $10,000 from either his emergency or contingency fund for a study on causes of emphysema, specifically the association between exposure to specific industrial pollutants and the onset of emphysema in workers exposed to them. The Legislative Budget Committee approved the action, and the Governor released the sum from his contingency fund. [64]

Dr. James McCarrol, Director of the Division of Environmental Health, 

School of Medicine, University of Washington, has already begun working on the study project and will continue to do so as funds will allow. The

District Coroner System: 

The chairman of the committee, Representative William Day, requested that the Legislative Council to defer action on the proposal of the Local Government Committee establishing a state medical examiner system, until the Public Assistance and Public Health Committee also had considered it. 

The committee, after holding a series of hearings on proposals for legislation designed to upgrade the standards of the state coroner system, proposed two recommendations. The committee heard from a wide variety of persons including county coroners, county prosecutors, doctors and funeral directors, on alternate approaches to reforming the abuses of the coroner system, and providing competent criminal-medical investigations without unnecessary and unreasonable expense to the people. 

The two recommendations, outlined below, are designed to complement each other. 

(1) Establishment of a Forensic Laboratory. 

This is mainly meant to provide back-up services for county prosecutors and local coroners, and when needed, to have available for them a centralized scientific medical investigation team and facilities located at the University hospital. a. The director of the laboratory will be a specialist in forensic pathology and will be supported by an assistant, an autopsy technician and morgue attendant, as well as clerical staff. b. The University plans to incorporate the construction of the laboratory in its previous construction plans, and the state toxicology laboratory already located there will be merged with it. c. The laboratory is designed to provide complete necropsy services for prosecutors and coroners including complete autopsies, microscopic work, photography, toxicology, x-ray, as well as acting as an avenue of consultative services. d. Benefits: Provides scientific answers in determining causes of death; eliminates the build-up of subjective and emotional feelings attending controversial deaths, e.g. racial slayings, where the laboratory acts as an impartial arbitrator; serves as a training ground for students in forensic pathology; assures a possibility of federal funding because of this educational aspect; provides the ready availability of other hospital staff and services, such as pharmacology, biochemistry, available in the University hospital; facilitates the recruiting of competent staff through identification with professional colleagues. [65]

(2) Establishment of Coroner Districts. 

a. The recommendation abolishes the county coroner system, and replaces it with a District Coroner System. The "district" approach interestingly also foresees the establishment of prosecutors and appellate courts into districts which transcend county boundaries. b. The thirteen districts are composed of contiguous counties grouped according to geography, population, location of laboratories and other facilities and based on a survey taken of county coroners. c. The coroner districts are to be funded on a per capita (population) basis within the district, thus relieving the smaller counties of maintaining separate funding. This method really means the pooling of the money, facilities and manpower of the counties comprising a district so that: (1) more is available for medical examinations; (2) duplication of services is eliminated; (3) the importance of coroner work is singled out. Local financing is flexible in that the appropriation of funds can vary where the problem varies. d. The bill requires all medical examinations and autopsies be conducted by doctors. e. The remainder of the medical-examiner bill is accepted with slight modification.

The Council adopted the recommendation for the establishment of a forensic laboratory but did not accept the proposal for the replacement of the present coroner system.

Hospital Costs: Senate Resolution 1967 Ex. 8. 

The Legislative Council, under the authority of Senate Resolution 1967 Ex. 8, was requested to conduct a study of the reasons for rising costs of hospital care within the state of Washington, and, as the resolution notes, “an analysis and evaluation of ways and means for controlling any inflationary tendencies or trends in the cost of hospital care, both to state and to private individuals."

The Committee on Public Assistance and Public Health was assigned the study by the Executive Committee of the Council. One of the startling facts that has been uncovered is the lack of any state requirements for the orderly submission of data regarding the financial operations of the hospital to the State. This is in spite of the fact that the State is the largest single purchaser of hospital care in the state. 

For example, in their report to the Secretary of State, hospitals are required to give only the names of the officers and the name of a resident agent.

The only information available at the Department of Revenue is the fact that their building is not used for profit making purposes. They do state the amount of sales tax or use tax paid.

To the Insurance Commissioner, nothing is reported. The Medical Service 

Bureau, Blue Cross, and insurance companies report their total payment for hospital care. [66]

And finally, the hospital districts do not submit an annual report. Their financial operations are audited by the state auditor but all amounts are lumped together. The only breakdown is on the auditor's work sheet. In short, there is a dearth of information submitted by the hospitals to the state. 

The committee finished work on a forty page report in compliance with Senate Resolution 1967 Ex. 8, dealing with the reasons for the cost of hospital care in Washington. Based upon the conclusions reached in the report, the committee has proposed a number of recommendations for the legislature's consideration.

The report was a culmination of two sets of questionnaires sent out to every licensed hospital in the state, both charitable and private, numbering 118. In addition, the committee held eight meetings open to the public, made a detailed tour of three hospitals, and made a survey of the laws of every state in the Union to provide meaningful ways other states were handling the problem of rising hospital costs. The report itself may be found in the committee files, but a brief summary is given below: 

Hospital costs are inextricably interwoven not only with internal problems of hospital administration, and competition and duplication of hospital services, but involve independent factors totally out of the control of hospitals themselves. The high cost and profit margin of drugs, charges for doctors' services, ambulance fees, labor costs and the general impact of inflation all contribute in a very direct way to the problem of rising hospital charges.

In addition, there are other impacts on costs and services which have indirect consequences. The involvement of insurance companies, both private, charitable and public, unintentionally generate increased costs and services to their individual beneficiaries.

Summary of Findings: 

1. After experiencing regular increases averaging about 7% a year, hospital costs in the past two years in the state of Washington have gone up about 30%, 18% in 1967, and 12% so far, this year; 

2. Labor costs constitute approximately two-thirds of all hospital operating costs; 

3. Construction costs have soared from $6,000/bed in 1940 and will amount to $30,000/bed in 1970; 

4. There is tremendous duplication of services and equipment among hospitals which compete for the health consumer dollar, as if they were private enterprises operating for profit; 

5. The cost of drugs, already high, is compounded because doctors utilizing hospitals recommend brand names instead of drugs by their generic name. 

6. Physicians' fees, after experiencing regular increases of amounting to about 3% per year from 1960 to 1965, rose 8% in 1966, compared to a rise in the consumer price index of 3.3%. 

7. Hospitals in Washington rank 27th in average cost per patient stay compared with the rest of the nation. [67]

8. The average length of stay is approximately 5½ days in Washington as compared to the national average of 8 days. 

9. Washington ranks 12th from the top nationally in labor costs, averaging approximately 3 employees/patient. 

10. Occupancy levels in hospitals vary, but some hospitals have shown a percentage of occupancy as low as 36% for 1967, and a number cluster around 45%. 

11. The extension of insurance coverage has generated increased use of hospital facilities, up approximately 25% over those without such insurance. 

12. Hospitals, being autonomous, decide for themselves what additional construction or expansion they want: new construction and services are created for reasons of prestige rather than need by hospitals with a history of low occupancy. 

CONCLUSION: The key fact about the health care industry, and hospital services in particular, is their state of disorganization. Hospitals are charitable institutions, outside the normal controls that are imposed in a free enterprise economy by the forces of supply, demand, competition and the drive for profits. Hospitals cater to the forces of need rather than demand; professional decision rather than consumer satisfaction; and quality of service rather than profits as a measure of success. Hence, without the immediate application of controls, or incentives to produce self controls, costs are going to continue to mount beyond control. Planning seems to offer the only reasonable check on spiralling costs. Lacking the market pressures of competition, the health care industry should be required to engage in the planning process to eliminate waste, duplication of services and facilities, and provide the necessary organization and integration to their programs. For example, there are possible economies and improvements in service to be anticipated from the operation of joint central services, including obstetrics, pediatrics, laboratory, research, training, radiology, pharmacy, laundry, purchasing, etc. 

The public health has historically been affected with the public interest, and is the most sacred responsibility of the state. It is incumbent upon the government, and the legislature in particular, to reverse his unfortunate trend, and rectify a dangerous problem for all health consumers.

Proposed transfer of the dairy herd from Rainier School at Buckley:

The biggest problem, and in fact one of the most controversial ones, faced by the committee this biennium involved the proposed transfer of the dairy herd from Rainier School in Buckley.

In all, four committee meetings, open to the public, were held on the matter. The proposal sparked bitter reaction from some residents of Buckley and involved the Department and, at times, the director in a bitter debate on policy objectives and the administration of the Department. 

The committee was moved by the views of alarm expressed by many of the local inhabitants of Buckley as to the potential harm which might be caused to the patients at Rainer School because of the economy move. Many questioned the value of the removal of the cattle which was being used as part of the rehabilitation program at the school. There were two issues involved. One was the issue of the value of the training program which would be interrupted by the move, which was the primary concern of the committee. The other issue was the financial one, that of the economy of the consolidation of the cattle herds.

Proposed Pasteurization Plant at Rainier School: 

The proposal for a pasteurization plant to be located at Rainier School was motivated by an announced decision by the Governor not to transfer the dairy herd from Rainier School.

The Committee thought that since the cattle would not be moved, it would present a good opportunity to gather facts about the beneficial and disadvantageous consequences both in an economic and therapeutic sense for the children it would serve. It was suggested that a pilot-study be conducted by Dr. Conte, who would keep records of the children engaged in the milk operation, the benefits accrued, the number placed in industry and on dairy farms, as well as cost of operation factors, including the number of employees used, supplied, maintenance and the like.

The legislative policy, however, had been to consolidate all milk producing plants for the purpose of economy. The Committee did recommend to the [ 78 ]

Legislative Council the establishment of such a processing plant, but the Legislative Council declined to accept the recommendation. 

Eastern State Hospital: Development of Community Mental Health 

Clinics for the Spokane Area

The Committee held a meeting at Eastern State Hospital for the purpose of reviewing and evaluating the present status of the hospital. The Committee had an opportunity to observe its operations, tour its facilities, and listen to the progress reports of the hospital administrators. 

As of April, 1967 there was an average of 55 admissions per month to the hospital, 80% of which were from Spokane County. The average length of stay was about three months, but in regards to the rehabilitation service offered by the hospital, 72% have been there over one year, with many from three to five years.

The proposed Interlake School will be in the Geriatrics Building with a 420 person capacity. There is also extensive remodeling of the second floor of the main ward of Martin Hall, a dormitory hall for male working patients. 

The State Department of Institutions is in the process of developing a 

Community Mental Health Program for Spokane. None of the city hospitals, however, are ready for it. A three-year program of after-care clinics was terminated because of lack of staffing, but the Department, in cooperation with the Office of Economic Opportunity, the Mental Health Association and the Community Action Council, is endeavoring to resurrect the program. The city of Spokane is beginning to get moving on community facilities. There are increasing requests by the local health offices for consultation services. 

There are at present approximately 200 patients in follow-up treatment. 

Much today is made up of the "missing link” between the community and the mental hospitals, which community mental health clinics could provide. 

The community itself should be directly involved, and Spokane should provide this. For this reason, the pilot program will be very important. 

The Interlake School 

The home for retarded children on the hospital grounds will be ready by September 1, 1967. It has a capacity for 400 patients and will entail the filling of 65 administrative positions by the Department of Personnel. Priority of the occupancies will be given to Spokane County. 

Historically, eastern Washington did not have a facility for severely retarded children, which the Interlake School will now provide. There are presently 1300 on the admission waiting list for all schools, of which 900 are considered emergencies. Severely retarded children in other institutions can be transferred to Interlake, thereby creating vacancies for waiting lists to those other institutions. 

Olympic Mental Health and Mental 

Retardation Center, Bremerton

The Olympic Center was authorized by House Bill 288 which became Chapter 12, Laws of 1965. The Committee met there with a view of hearing of the progress the pilot project was making. Olympic Center will be a demonstration to the state legislature and government of what can be done [79]

with mental health and retardation on the community level. The emphasis on the community is an advanced concept which has been enjoying a nationwide trend for the past six years. The program will be a comprehensive documentation of an experiment for community consultative projects across the state. 

The entire purpose of the community based operation is to provide treatment to children as well as adults in the local referral area (Olympic peninsula being the jurisdiction for Olympic Center) without creating a disruption of patient-community ties. It is an alternate approach to hospitalization. The provision for the twenty-five beds serves as free temporary hospital care for those requiring it. They serve several purposes: 

1. They present an opportunity for an individual to complete diagnostic (evaluative) work. 

2. They allow "transition" for the patient from the hospital to eventual community based treatment. 

3. They can also be used as "vacation" beds for the retarded children whose parents wish to take a break.

Reconstruction and remodeling of the Olympic Center building began January 1, 1968. The plans include facilities for twenty-five beds for the temporary care of the mentally retarded. The plans are currently being drawn up by architects, and envision a total remodeling of the old building to include plumbing, lighting and measures for the protection of the patients. 

To date, the staff complement includes a total of nine professional people (a growth of six persons as of January, 1966), which serves a total of some 900 patients in program consultation. 

School for the Blind, Vancouver

Progress in the Division of Juvenile Rehabilitation 

The main objectives of the division are two-fold, providing juvenile delinquency services and rehabilitation to youth. There are now established 

22 child guidance centers, and in the last year 1,942 children have been received through the division. The 15 year age group recorded the largest number of arrests but the Washington crime rate was coincidental with the national average. The division operates up to 94% capacity as of April 1. The department has, in accordance with Chapter 93 of the Session Laws of 1967, destroyed all juvenile crime records of the children who have reached age 21. 

The division has instituted five year plans in every section of the division and looks forward to more funds to finance three more group homes. The next session, however, the department would like to see a legal authorization for voluntary commitments without having to go through the courts. There is also a need to extend vocational services to the juvenile after he has left the division, and they would like to see central vocational training facilities near Seattle-Tacoma which will handle institutionalized children. The division also needs additional funds for in-service training of new employees. There is a need for extention of delinquency prevention facilities, also. The department would like to see a family therapy program developed which would get the families of dependent children involved in the rehabilitation program. 

Progress in the Division of Adult Correction 

The vocational training in the facility at Shelton is very comprehensive and involves training in the use of machines, electronics, data processing, auto mechanics, etc. The reception center there, of course, is the first stop for adult prisoners. The training center has a capacity of 480 beds, of which 372 are filled. The reformatory has at present 40 vacancies. The penitentiary is 28 over capacity. [81]

Parole board policies do vary and releases have increased. This is because more people are being released earlier and less people are being brought into the institution. The repeat factor, however, is pretty stable and the risks of a non-complying parolee have not been increased. The women's prison is being replaced now that the site has been selected. Bids were opened in June, 1968 and construction is expected to be completed by July 1969. The Department of Natural Resources is doing the clearing and the ground is being leveled now.

The work release program has been a most useful and progressive law. 

The department now has agreement with four local jails involving ten trainees and three in local community colleges. To date there are 51 applications for the program of which 21 have been approved. There is a dire need for placement services and transportation and housing are continuing problems. The mental health program at Walla Walla is staffed and operating. 

There are at present two hospital supervisors and inmate nurses but recruitment problems are difficult. There is a 28 bed capacity and they expect to expand it to 16 more.

Tour of Spruce Canyon Youth Rehabilitation Camp 

The Committee toured the youth rehabilitation facility near Colville, above Spokane, on May 17, and spoke with the superintendent and his staff about their problems. The camp presently houses 60 juvenile delinquents who work in cooperation with the Department of Natural Resources in forestry operations. The inmates are considered "honor" prisoners who are being prepared for release. They are under the supervision of resident counselors who also provide academic instruction. Of those who are returned to society, about 20% eventually end up in another institution. Happily, this figure also represents an 80% success rate. The camp is being used to full capacity. 

Tour of the State Penitentiary The Committee inspected the prison facilities at the penitentiary at Walla Walla on May 18, 1968. The penitentiary is the most severe of all the state's penal institutions, and houses the more serious offenders. Of those released after serving sentence, about 30% are convicted of new crimes. The population at the penitentiary is about 1,438 and is near capacity. The prison just completed installing a mental health hospital but is having problems staffing it. It hopes to renovate for modern practical purpose the original structure of the territorial prison.

Tour of Youth Corrections Center, Shelton 

The Committee had an opportunity to meet with the Board of Prison Terms and Paroles at the Shelton Center to receive a report of the problems and progress the Board was encountering in the administration of the parole system.

Mr. Bruce Johnson, Chairman of the Board, appealed for an enlargement of the Board to include another panel, which would mean an increase from the present five to nine members. This is being considered as a possible 

Executive Request by the Governor at the 1969 session of the State Legislature. [82]

The Board is saddled with a tremendous number of parole hearings a year. Washington is unique among the states of the Union in requiring the 

Parole Board to meet once a year with every inmate incarcerated in state institutions to review each person's sentence, and review and evaluate progparole violations, progress and other administrative hearings, numbered some 5,307 this year. In addition, the Board held parole review hearings which numbered as of this June some 5,192 totalling 10,499. This total was up approximately 800 over last year and has been rising steadily ever since the original Board was increased to five members, ten years ago. 

The Work Release Program has put additional burdens on the Board, but it is estimated that the state can save up to $2,000 per year by releasing a person on parole.

Tour of Veterans' Home at Retsil 

The Committee held an open meeting at the Veterans' Home at Retsil on September 14, 1968. The projected operating budget for next biennium for the veterans' homes includes funds for minor renovation of the ground floor level of the hospital which, if approved, would provide an additional 20 beds, for nursing care at the home.

WASHINGTON STATE LEGISLATIVE COUNCIL Chapter 44.24 

44.24.010 Council created-Composition. There is hereby created a "state legislative council" hereinafter referred to as the council, which shall consist of fifteen senators and sixteen representatives from the legislature of the state of Washington, including the president pro tem of the senate and the speaker of the house of representatives, said council to be appointed by the president of the senate and the speaker of the house of representatives at least ten days before the close of the 1947 session of the legislature, and at least ten days before the close of each regular session thereafter. The president of the senate and the speaker of the house of representatives shall prepare their lists of appointees so that the whole membership of the council shall include at least one individual from each United States congressional [83]

district within the state and so that the minority political party in each house shall have seven members on the council. The said lists of appointees shall be subject to confirmation as to the senate members by the senate and as to the house members by the house of representatives. In the event of a failure to appoint council members within the time above stated, or in the event of a refusal by either senate or house of representatives to confirm appointments on the council, then the members on the council from either house in which there is a failure to appoint or confirm shall be elected forthwith by the members of such house. 

44.24.020 Powers and duties. The council shall have the following pow ers and duties: (1) To perform, either through the council as a whole or through committees thereof, all duties and functions customarily delegated to special interim legislative committees: PROVIDED, That any appointments of committee chairmen shall be approved by not less than fifteen members of the council; (2) To examine and study the administrative organization and procedures of the state government, its offices, boards, committees, commissions, institutions, and other state agencies and to make recommendations, where found advisable, directed to the elimination of unnecessary overlapping or duplication of functions, procedures and expenditures, and to the promotion of economy and efficiency in state government; (3) To make such other studies and examinations of the state government and its state agencies as it may find advisable and to hear complaints, hold hearings, gather information and make findings of fact with respect thereto: PROVIDED, That no investigation shall be had or public hearing be held without prior approval of two-thirds of the membership of the council: 

PROVIDED FURTHER, That any investigation of hearing once commenced may be terminated by a majority vote of the council; (4) To receive messages and reports in person or in writing from the governor or any other state officials and to attend generally to any and all business addressed to or affecting the legislature during the interim between regular legislative sessions; (5) To make reports from time to time to the members of the legislature and to the public with respect to any of its findings or recommendations. The council shall keep complete minutes of its meetings. The council shall make and distribute its biennial report to the members of the ensuing legislature at least ten days prior to the convening of the legislature in regular session; and (6) To cooperate, act, and function with similar councils or committees of other states, with the council of state governments, and with other interstate research organizations. 

44.24.030 Examination of records-Testimony-Oaths-Compelling at tendance of witnesses. In the discharge of any duty herein imposed, the council and its committees shall have the authority to examine and inspect all files, records and accounts of any state office, department, institution, board, commission or agency, and to administer oaths, issue subpoenas, compel the attendance of witnesses and the production of any papers, books, [ 84 ]

accounts, documents and testimony, and to cause the deposition of witnesses, either residing within or without the state, to be taken in the manner prescribed by law for taking depositions in civil actions in the superior courts. In case of disobedience on the part of any person to comply with any subpoena issued in behalf of the council, or on the refusal of any witness to testify to any matters regarding which he may be lawfully interrogated, it shall be the duty of the superior court of any county, or of the judge thereof, on application of the council, to compel obedience by proceedings for contempt, as in the case of disobedience of the requirements of a subpoena issued from such court or a refusal to testify therein. Each witness who appears before the state legislative council by its order, other than a state official or employee, shall receive for his attendance the fees and mileage provided for witnesses in civil cases in courts of record, which shall be audited and paid upon the presentation of proper vouchers signed by such witness and approved by the secretary and chairman of the council. 

44.24.040 Meetings. The first meeting of the state legislative council shall be held on the third Monday in June, 1947, and thereafter meetings shall be held throughout the legislative interim at such times and at such places as the council may determine. Committees of the council may meet at such additional times and in such places as may be convenient or necessary in carrying out their delegated duties. 

44.24.050 Secretary and assistants. The council shall have authority to select and employ an executive secretary together with such other clerical, legal, accounting, research, and other assistants as it may deem desirable, whose compensation and salaries shall be fixed by the council. 

44.24.060 Expenses and mileage. The members of the council shall be reimbursed for their expenses incurred while attending sessions of the council or meetings of any committees of the council or while engaged on other council business authorized by the council in accordance with the provisions of RCW 44.04.120. All expenses incurred by the council, including salaries of employees, shall be paid upon voucher forms as provided by the budget director and signed by the chairman or vice chairman of the council and attested by the secretary of said council, or by an alternate for the secretary who shall be a member of and selected by the executive committee, and the authority of said chairman and secretary to sign vouchers shall continue until their successors are selected. Vouchers may be drawn upon funds appropriated generally by the legislature for legislative expenses or upon any special appropriation which may be provided by the legislature for the expenses of the council. 

44.24.070 Rules and regulations-Terms of office-Vacancies-Special 

Members-Minutes. The state legislative council shall have authority to make its own rules and regulations governing the conduct of its business not otherwise prescribed in this chapter. The term of office of all council members shall be from the time of confirmation or election until (1) their successors have been appointed and confirmed or elected as provided in RCW 44.24.010, or until they cease to be members of the legislature. Vacancies on the council among the senate members of the council may be filled by appointment by the remaining senate members. Vacancies on the council among the members of the house of representatives may be filled by appointment by the remaining house members. All such vacancies shall be filled [85]

from the same political party as that of the member whose seat was vacated. 

The council may appoint not more than twelve additional legislators as special members in the same ratio as membership of the respective parties in the house and senate, to serve on council committees for designated periods of time, and such special members shall be entitled to reimbursement on the same basis as council members for expenses incurred while on council business. All of the minutes, records, and files of the council shall be delivered over by the council to the speaker of the house of representatives or to the president of the senate at the convening of each regular or special session of the legislature, which minutes, records, and files shall be held subject to the order of the senate and house of representatives, and shall thereafter be redelivered to the members of the council forthwith, but in no event later than five days after adjournment sine die of the legislature.
