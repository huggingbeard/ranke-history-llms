I sing the glories of the brave; 

I sing the sorrows of the grave,

Of men who fought and are no more. 

I sing of courage at its best, The dauntless dash of British blood;

I sing of those who fetched the food, And for their comrades lie at rest.

I sing of those who held the bridge, Though shot and shell around them fell; 

Who faced the horrors of a hell, To save a comrade from the ridge!

I sing of those who worked the gun 

Until the last shell had been fired;

Who held the vantage ground acquired, And knew not what it was to run. 

I sing of courage indiscreet, That doth to human pity yield- 

Lifts wounded foemen from the field, And lays them at the foemen's feet! 

I sing of that gallant, dauntless man 

Who, seeing comrades try and fail, Yet volunteered to face the hail 

Of deadly bullets, as he ran; 1

Who felt stern duty his behest, And valued so the worth of life,

That in the grim, inhuman strife, He risked his own to save the rest!

I sing of those who take the wing 

And into boundless space ascend- 

So dauntless, when their lives depend 

On hazards of a fragile string! 

I sing of those who face so brave 

The horrors of a battlefield, With meek humanity as shield 

To save their brothers from the grave. 

I sing of Pity, in her dress 

Of gentleness beyond compare, Who tends the warrior in despair— 

And makes his pain a trifle less. 

Of noble woman at her best; 

Who soothed us in our infant days, And when affliction on us weighs,

Retakes us to her gentle breast. 

I sing of those who act like this 

Because they are of Earth the best;

Because by such have we progressed, And such made Britain what it is. 

O Britain of the free-born men: 

O Britain, diamond of the sea: 

Not till I've sung this song to thee, Can I lay down a grateful pen! 2

We stood upon Southampton's quay, All marshalled in the dead of night;

The soldiery that met my sight 

Was pregnant of dire deeds to be.

There was a movement, hushed, austere, As thousands gathered on one site; 

But, in the stillness of the night,

The orders rang out curt and clear. 

The columns swung with single swing, And filed into the waiting ship;

I felt, somehow, a Moloch's grip 

Had made our destinies one thing. 

I spoke not, neither did my mate; 

My thoughts were distant from that place; 

And his, I saw it in his face, Were musing on the moods of fate.

The transport soon was on its way; 

The waters, like the heavens, calm; 

A warship spread its guarding arm, And broke the stillness with its spray.

I thought that life was strange indeed; 

I thought that fate was stranger still: 

I wondered if the human will

Was but Annihilation's seed. 

I felt no fear, though grim the quest; 

A quick'ning but of pulse and breath: 

I had known Life, but knew not Death, And Death, perhaps, was but a rest. 3

Yet, Death was like that darksome night, Where all was hidden to the view, And every beauteous thing we knew 

Deprived was of redeeming light. 

And Life was sweet, whate'er the cost, Whate'er its sorrows and travail; 

And though from Death I did not quail, I felt that Life should not be lost.

Thus musing, I my native land 

Left sleeping by the silent sea;

And, conscious of the things to be, I knew we'd make a gallant stand.

The home-lights soon were seen no more, Which placed a weight within my heart: 

I wondered if 'twould be my part 

To see again my native shore!

II 

The first faint flush of purple dawn

Had seen us safe in friendly France, The land of sapience and romance, From which the world's finesse was drawn. 

We swung along the crowded streets, With cheer re-echoing to cheer: 

A boon the faithful fusilier

In times of trouble only, greets. 

The damsels darted to our side 

And gave us sweet things of their land;

They took brass buttons from our hand, And held them up with joy and pride!

We thought of other damsels sweet, Whose eyes were, doubtless, bathed in tears:

We drowned the pathos of those cheers 

With rhythmic song to marching feet.

Our feet in France; our hearts at home; 

Our minds revolving on the foe; 

We sang whatever seemed to go- E'en ditties of the Hippodrome. 

How oft we sang the haunting tune! 

Until, at last, it seemed a dirge

That doth from depths of pathos surge, Intoned by pipes of sorrow hewn! 

And how we stressed the simple phrase 

That told us of our home afar! 'Twas like a bright, remembered star, To shine throughout the iron days.

We marched by fields of golden corn That waved their tufts of promised good; 

By many a leafy copse and wood, Which did the hills and vales adorn. 

How sweetly Nature smiled on man, And gave him of her riches all! 

And he? Would he the gift recall, And emulate the kindly plan?

The raging, ruthless beasts of prey 

That tear each other limb from limb, Are mewing cubs that mimic him, And but his rudiments portray! 

Aye, even Nature in her ire,

That fractures rocks like fragile leaves, And to the sightless skies upheaves

A mountain base of molten fire,

Is model mount of caramel 

Compared with man's ingenious mind, That leaves no fearsome thing behind,

But counts and casts the stocks of Hell. 

Yet he he sings of God and good,

Though he of Satan is the sire, That wadeth deep in bloody mire, And doth on evil build and brood! 

The Sun was beating on our backs

For miles on miles of granite grey- 

So powerful, throughout the day,

It seemed to pierce our pressing packs. 

The moisture to our faces came, Like crystal drops of chilléd steam 

That on a heating boiler beam, And so bedew the iron frame:

The sturdy frame that strength outlined; 

The crystal dew the heavens dole:

This but the symbol of our soul, And that the measure of our mind!

My comrade striding by my side (The moisture coming through his coat), Seemed rapt in memories remote

While stepping firmly, stride on stride. 

Of home we had been speaking much, And mostly of his bonny boy A theme that used to be his joy, But had assumed a saddened touch. 

He had expressed a fervent hope 

That, for the child, he might not die; 

But had then added, with a sigh: "Good God, how we in darkness grope!"

So like my mate: a heart, a soul, A blend of pluck and tenderness; 

But wight of sorrow and distress, That never gains the yearned-for goal.

Fatigued and hot, of doubtful mood, We halted for a meal and rest. 

The Sun was sinking in the west, His outstretched arms as red as blood!

We tried in vain our thirst to quench, So lit our pipes and smoked with zeal; 

But scarce did we refreshened feel, Than we were ordered to entrench.

Soon had we on this toil begun, Our tunics off, our forearms bare- 

Though some, I fear, were heard to swear 

At soil so baked by August sun. 

But tireless men of Britain's brave

Worked staunchly at the task assigned, Though some, alas, had in their mind 

That they were digging their own grave! 

I felt it so, but laughed withal:

We joked and talked of trifling things. 

Is it a balm that Nature brings 

Ere Death doth make his dismal call? 

Quite soundly, 'neath a starry sky, That night we by our trenches slept; 

Until the dawn o'er heaven crept, To tip the trees with golden dye.

There was a freshness in the air, Which made one feel the love of life- 

That vital touch, so sweetly rife, Which wakes the sleeping daisy fair.

In tree and bush, the birds in song; 

A lark was trilling in the sky; 

And man, who had just learned to fly, Was soaring higher, droning strong.

He was the fearless, modern scout, Who flew with but imperfect means On canvas-cane-and-wire machines, With engines prone to peter out! 

And he was out to sight the foe, Who armed with cannon, shot and shell, Would turn into a raging hell 

This peaceful land, where poppies blow.

This fretful maggot of the mind, That looks on murder with dismay,

Yet urges millions on to slay 

Is it a curse on humankind?

This war, so called, this bestial fight, Where men act worse than beasts of prey, Does this a mite of good convey, Or settle what is wrong with right? 

Man, man! O what is man? A clod? 

A maniac of uncertain will? 

Or feeling flesh that can fulfill 

A righteousness to equal God?

III 

We woke to hail a lovely morn;

The Sun had risen bright and clear; 

A million blossoms, far and near,

The fields and orchards did adorn.

And those that beauty love, gave vent 

To joyous song from grateful hearts Not all the range of human arts 

Were half so blithe or eloquent. 

Just after dawn, when all was gay, A thousand cannons boomed, and, lo! 

The note of joy was turned to woe, For man averred he had to slay!

And boom on boom convulsed the air, The trees, the bushes, us, the ground- 

My God! No other dreadful sound 

Could ever with that sound compare! 

And shell on shell did shriek and wail, And burst and thunder everywhere, Whilst bullets whistled through the air, And shrapnel showered down like hail. "Keep low, keep low!" came the command: "There's time to let them have our lead." 

And low we kept, and hid our head,

Although we yearned to scan the land. 

And endless seemed the deaf'ning din, And every burst the nearer came- 

As truer grew the foemen's aim- 

But we just faced it with a grin. 

Then suddenly the barrage rose. "Get ready, boys!" the order came, And as we gripped our rifles, game, We saw the foe approach in rows.

From out a wood they rushed along, Quite closely marshalled, row on row; 

Our captain's face was all aglow 

When he beheld that herd-like throng.

"Now steady, boys!" he firmly cried: "Await my word, for this is jam! 

You cannot miss them, no, God damn, You couldn't miss them if you tried!"

And on they rushed, and nigh and nigher, Each shooting wildly as he came; 

We lay and watched their stupid game, Until our captain shouted: "Fire!” 

Then volley followed volley fast, And down the Germans fell like pins, As if some rows of mannequins 

Had tumbled over with a blast.

But still more rushed out from the wood, And still we fired with steady aim,

And still the close-packed columns came, And still our mowing was renewed. 

With writhing men the land was strewn,

With corpses stiff the land was spread, But still more Germans forged ahead, To pile platoon upon platoon! 

At last, when it was ghastly plain 

Their footmen couldn't reach our line, Like thund'ring waves upon the brine, Their cavalry came on amain. "Keep steady, boys!" the captain said: "Pick off the riders as they come!" 

And steady, like a rolling drum, Our bullets whirred and piled the dead.

The charging Uhlans, at a bound, Rushed to us like insane mustang; 

Then suddenly the order rang: "Fix bayonets and stand your ground!" 10

The remnant of the Uhlans shrank When they beheld our wall of steel- But ere they could their chargers wheel, Our horse had charged them from the flank. 

And in the melée that ensued, The Uhlans never had a chance:

Engaged with vigour à outrance, They took to flight and were pursued. 

Then "Charge!" the bugle sounded: "Charge!"

And charge we did with might and main, And o'er the corpses of the slain,

We rushed like demons set at large. 1 But as our legs were much too slow, We gripped the stirrup-leathers tight, And next the horses ran full might, To catch the now retreating foe.

We chased them for a mile or more, Amidst a din none could portray,

For as we caught them in the fray, Right through them twice we madly tore. 

And when there was no foe to fall 

Within our range of battlefield,

Against our will we had to yield, In answer to the bugle-call.

So, in the shelter of the trench, Again we waited for the foe; 

Depleted, aye, but full of go, And confident we'd make him blench.

Thrice more they tried their tactics crude, Thrice more we riddled them with shot 

And left them piled up on the spot But still we stood where we had stood. 11

Their scouts kept droning in the sky;

Their shells kept bursting all around; 

But we kept low and stood our ground; 

Just waiting, waiting to reply. 

Meanwhile, our guns did never tire

To rain the shrapnel on their ranks— 

And when they tried to turn our flanks,

We spread and raked them with our fire. 

At last, thank God, the darkness fell!

And with it, silenced were the guns 

And silenced, too, the many sons

Of Britain, who had fought so well! "Thank God for night, for we must eat!"

Such was, indeed, the reigning mood; 

But hardly had we tasted food, When we were ordered to retreat. 

So, quietly we hitched our pack, Fell into line and marched away;

We marched and ate, and had our say, Our little grouse for falling back. 

The night was starry, but behind 

A reddish glow was in the sky- 

Just blazing hamlets, to supply

That KULTUR by the Huns designed. 

By silent woods and meadows dark, Some fifteen miles we strode that night— 

Recounting each, in his own light, Experiences so ghastly stark. 

And all the way along that track, The ambulances flitted by . ... 

To Mons, as empty as the sky; 

But crammed with wounded, coming back! 121

"My God," said Fred, "the boys that fell!" "Yes, Fred," I answered, "more shall fall:

They're six to one, in guns and all . . . 

But they shall have a taste of Hell!”

And in that spirit we entrenched, We ate and sang, we joked and slept; 

And when the dawn upon us crept,

If hearts were high, our jaws were clenched. 

The Sun was up, the day was clear; 

The joyous lark was up on high, Trilling his love-song in the sky; 

And poppies red blew everywhere. 

As golden as a setting Sun,

The corn was waving in the breeze; 

Cicadas chirped in poplar trees;

All Nature seemed in unison. 

But Man . . . why, Man was there to slay- 

To blast the happy countryside, And blight with hellish homicide- 

Because the HUN had called “The Day”!

IV 

'Twas not till noon that restful spell 

Was broken by some mounted scouts,

Who searching for our whereabouts, Into a batch of troopers fell. 13

But hardly had they captured been, When our dragoons had made a swoop

Upon a gaily trotting troop Of Uhlans, that came on the scene. 

The clash was short, for in the fight Our troopers, in a manner deft, Had charged and thrust them, right and left, Excepting two, who took to flight. 

And not long after, just ahead

The close-packed columns came again; 

Again we saw the endless men 

Who rushed and tumbled o'er their dead. "Twas round on round, and at each round, Like nine-pins, down the Boches went;

But still more Huns were forward sent, To seek their graves upon that ground. 

Some guns had got our range, meanwhile; 

So, fighting hard to hold our own, We moved up forward, keeping prone, And changed our cover in defile. 

They tried to turn our flank, but failed: 

Our gallant horsemen, deftly led, With rare élan upon them sped, And ever on the wing prevailed. 

And darkness found us holding sheer, At cost of many gallant lives;

But when the cause of justice drives, There is no cost that is too dear. 

With darkness came retreat again With stifling weather to contend- 

That night we reached our journey's end

Bedraggled, in the pouring rain. 14

At Landrecies, our next set goal,

We found some shelter and some food; 

And like the famished men of Oudh, Of scanty fare took ample toll.

But hardly had we settled down, When Huns so anxious to restore 

The brutal Attila of yore, With fire and sword fell on the town.

We made a fierce and desperate stand- 

And like the hordes of River Plate

Which did bland Whitelocke's plans frustrate, From every vantage point at hand- We threw up barricades galore 

And manned them in the pouring rain, Then fired with all our might and main

From every window, attic, door. 

The burning houses gave a glare, And by that light, as heavens poured, That fiendish horde with shot we bored, And piled his dead up everywhere.

In every lane, in every street A barricade of dead was formed; 

Upon the remnant then we swarmed 

And made them quit in hot retreat.

Their cavalary then charged the town, But at close range we couldn't miss: 

As falls a rock o'er precipice, So off their steeds we shot them down. 'Twas then our horsemen charged the rest, And Landrecies was cleared of foe;

Then came the news we had to go, Because our flank was hotly pressed. 15

Exhausted as we were, we marched- We marched for hours through rain and slush; 

But nothing could our spirit crush, Not hunger, aches, nor throats so parched.

We took the wounded as we could: 

In barrows, vans, in litters, carts;

Some hobbled on with cheerful hearts, But none complained or was subdued. 

The hapless horses born to bear

The weight of man, his tragic faults, Dejected struggled on, with halts, Or fell exhausted here and there.

When eastern skies had fringed with gold 

The heavy broken clouds of storm,

And Nature did herself transform With multi-hues in wood and wold, We reached St. Quentin, that old town 

For which so many fights were fought In days when Spain its riches sought, Or when La Forge gave it renown. 

Again it was the Prussian hosts But now more shameless than before- 

They came to batter at its door 

Like Attila's revolting ghosts. 

The envious HUN was still alive 

To smash the beautiful and old, Those treasures that no God or gold 

Can ever in this world revive. 16

And knowing what this Hun could do, My heart was heavy when I cast

My eyes on relics of the past That, as we marched, came into view. 

To save the old historic place,

We dared not turn and fight, until 

We'd cleared by miles St. Quentin's hill,

Although the foe kept up apace. 

We marched right down that lovely vale, Where like a silver ribbon twined, The Oise's tranquil waters wind 

Their charming course midst wood and dale. "How beautiful!" exclaimed my mate. "Yes, Fred," I answered: ""Tis a crime 

That humans soaked in Teuton slime 

Can all this grandeur devastate!”

We crossed the Oise and took our stand 

Between old Chauny and Noyon,

For here, 'twas said, we'd hold our own 

Along the wooded, rising strand. 

The crossing saved us from attack, And gave us all a good night's rest, A bath refreshing and, the best, A hearty meal, and not a snack.

That night again the south we sought, Which caused the Germans to pursue, And made us fight the whole night through, Encounters hard, with dangers fraught.

By daylight, we were on the Aisne, In rearguard action fighting still; 

We crossed the stream and gained the hill, From where we could the Huns contain. 17

Like ants they swarmed that day, I trow, Along the river's northern side; 

Like ants we slew them, when they tried

To get across the stream somehow. 

Meantime, their guns were pounding too, To thin our ranks and stay our fire, So we were lucky to retire 

When darkness came, with losses few. 

Into the woodlands of Compiègne,

We drew our forces in the night, And thus we managed some respite 

Ere day brought battle once again. 

But thankful for that short respite

Within those cool beech woods, once more We meant to make the Huns deplore The reckless day they sought this fight. 

And daylight brought a combat fierce

Within the woods and fringes too, But though they brought up forces new, They couldn't our battalions pierce. 

No never, while our race could delve 

And find a Captain Bradbury, A Nelson and Dorrell, V.C., To serve one gun against their twelve. 

And lo, the twelve were captured, too! 

For like a blasting hurricane, Our cavalry went through amain, And coming back, brought off the coup. 

After Compiègne, we got away 

And travelled faster in retreat, Through afternoons of blazing heat, And nights as damp and cold as clay. 18

We halted here and there a while, But travelled, travelled most the time, Through fields of beet and grain in prime,

Through upland roads for mile on mile. 

We all got ragged, footsore, worn, Unkempt and dirty, bearded, gaunt; 

We had no time to sleep our want;

For marching-time, all was foresworn. 

We crossed the Ourcq, we crossed the Marne, We felt that we had crossed the Styx; 

To find ourselves in such a fix, Recalled the old satanic yarn. 

And contrast made our fix acute Our indigence was so complete, We scarce could stand upon our feet, While Nature teemed with corn and fruit.

We saw it weighing down the trees, But could not stay to have a treat; 

We crushed it dead beneath our feet, Yet could not hunger then appease. 

Sweet Nature smiled on man in flowers, Sweet Nature loaded man with fruit;

But man was so accursed a brute, He paid her back with blasting showers. 

He crushed her crops, he felled her trees; 

With passion for destruction braced,

He churned abundance into waste, And damned his riches and his ease. 

Such were the thoughts that bitter came, From time to time, in that retreat; 

I wondered why with Nature sweet, Proud man should stoop to kill or maim. 19

After the Marne, we had a rest 

At Crécy forest, and a meal;

By then we had begun to feel That, of man's blessings, these were best, From Crécy forest, though not pressed, We crossed the Grand Morin at will; 

Then, pressing onward up the hill,

Crossed open country unaggressed. 

And here the order came to stand, "And never budge a single pace!"

We all assumed a happy face, And thanked the Lord for that command! 

Behind us stood a dusty road, O'er which the traffic never ceased; 

The ambulances rushed up east, But came back slowly with full load. 

And cars and waggons swelled that train, Each jolting badly on the way, But ne'er the wounded did betray 

Their agony by groans of pain. 

That road, indeed, like hundreds more, A quaint collection saw all day: 

Mule-train so packed they whined alway, As if they did their job deplore. 

Mail vans, with dusky complement 

Of India's sons, whose pointless mien 

Spoke in a manner weird, serene, Of some Nirvana vaguely dreamt; 

And swarthy Turcos, swinging by 

With scarlet fez, bird-cages, pans,- 

A caravan of caravans, That might be counted when they die; 20

Senegalese, as black as night, With teeth as white as pearls, a-grin,

Disclosing they enjoyed the din, And e'en enjoyed the ruthless fight; 

And ever there the quick poilu, Of nimble feet and gay attire, Whose sapient gaze revealed the fire

So deep within their souls a-brew. 

O life so gay, yet sad and stern, That pleasure gives, but to reveal 

A tragedy is at our heel, So ready to chastise in turn!

VI 

God bless Smith-Dorrien, and God bless 

Field-Marshal French, whose wise retreat 

Preserved our army from defeat, And led it later to success! 

Dawn brought re-birth, and with it, lo! 

A million men had but one voice, One voice that shouted loud: "REJOICE!" 

One voice that ordered: "FORWARD, GO!" 

And go we did, as if the gods 

Had fired us with herculean might,

To labour long, endure and fight,- 

Aye, fight and win, whate'er the odds.

By noon, we'd reached an open plain, With scattered woodland all around,

In which, concealed, the Huns were found, And we each skirmish had to gain. 21

Our guns got busy, but the foe 

Rushed forward with a set attack;

This we repulsed and sent them back,— 

Then with our steel we made them go. 

And as they ran, we shot them down 

Like rabbits in an open field;

We routed them, where'er concealed; 

And triumph did our efforts crown. 

After a day of great success,— 

Esconcéd by the Grand Morin, We slept without a care or pang, Despite a night of chilliness. 

And just as dawn lit up the scene,

The shelling from our deadly guns Awoke the hills, and raked the Huns In manner they had not foreseen. 

And helped by that destructive fire,

We crossed the stream and stormed the hill; 

Our gallant lads then felt the thrill 

That did our valiant sires inspire.

Next day we still were on his track- 

He hitting back as he retired; 

But we were with one purpose fired: 

To punish him for his attack. 

But as we forward pressed and saw

The wanton devastation wrought, Our hearts were with hot anger fraught

Against this Hun of fiendish paw. 

Across the Marne and far beyond, We bore on with a mighty push; 

Despite his fire, the rain and slush, We chased this raping, looting blond. 22

These low barbarians of "Kultur," 

Would raise their hands when they were caught, Shout: "Kamerad!" as though they'd wrought 

No evil, and were bland and pure! 

And we why, we forgot our wrath: 

Our gallant natures couldn't smite

The scoundrels when they wouldn't fight- 

So, helped them to imbibe our broth! 

A purring kitten by the fire; 

A bleating lambkin in the field;

A burmese priest, so tried and old 

That nothing can arouse his ire; 

Not these nor any Christian nun 

Can be so thankful and so meek,

So bland, undignified and sleek 

As that prime sneak: a beaten Hun. 

But lo! How does his nature flow

In spirit true to native caste, When, safe within his ramparts vast, He feels he still can strike a blow!

Indeed, there are no means too low, Too brutal, fiendish, vile or mean

To satisfy the lust so keen 

That burns within this wretched foe. 

And we, o'er-anxious to be fair- 

And even generous to foes- 

Get witless o'er our ratios, And waste our caviar on a bear.

So witless that we judge this Hun- 

Who lacks our sense of right and wrong- 

An equal in the human throng, And treat him as our cultured son. 23

So, in this world of man and slug, A lesson yet have we to learn: 

However much we twist and turn, We cannot civilise the Thug. 

Beware his imperfections dire; 

Assume not that he can reform;

Make him his government transform, And strip him of his force entire. 

The Teuton, like the Muscovite,

Of moral values nothing knows; 

He's but a savage in dress-clothes, Who thinks that culture lies in might.

Such were the views that Fred and I 

Exchanged, when sitting at a meal

Within a wood near Braisne-sur-Vele, While water drenched us from on high. 

We'd seen the Teuton's turpitude;

His wanton damage showed the brute; 

His brigandage gave vent in loot, And rape displayed his nature lewd 

Determined that he should atone

For all the evil he had caused, We hardly ate and hardly paused— We'd chase him till we laid him prone. 

It rained all night, but sharp at dawn

Well drenched and muddy, we pushed on; 

Although our faces, sleepy, wan,

But mirrored that our strength was drawn. 

With heavy loss, we reached the Aisne; 

Ensconced upon the hills, the Huns 

Seemed to have placed a million guns, And ceaseless fell the iron rain. 24

Our guns with equal force replied, Until the din within that dell 

Belittled a fantastic Hell, And man's descriptive powers defied.

Eventually, in single file, Across the remnants of a bridge, We ran the gauntlet o'er a ridge, Mid bullets whistling all the while. 

Unscathed, I got across; but Fred 

Went down without a word or moan; 

Went down as would a lifeless stone; 

Went down, and lay there with the dead! 

A pang of anguish took my breath; 

A storm of anger filled my soul; 

Who shot my pal would pay the toll... 

The toll, my God. . . the toll of death!

I rushed into a village street, And with me rushed some hundreds more;

All bent on equalling the score, The score of death, until complete. 

We swept the streets with rifle fire, Then fixing bayonets on the run,

We raided houses, one by one, To see the place was cleared entire. 

We found the enemy concealed; 

A few showed fight, but one blond foe On whom I charged with eyes aglow- 

Dropped rifle, raised his hands and squealed.

He squealed, with terror in his face; 

He shouted "Don't!" in English plain,- 

But I, who'd seen my fellows slain, And saw but red, with head ablaze, 25

Thrust out with cruel, savage might, And through his heart my bayonet ran!

He dropped, as drops a sack of bran; 

He paled. ... a deathly, ghastly white. 

I drew my bayonet, dripping red,

Wiped by his tunic as I drew- 

I could not reason, but I knew 

That I his blood had wrongly shed. 

Remorse went deep into my heart; 

Remorse that is of pity born:

Pity for him, so vilely torn 

From this dear life, so young to part!

Beside my bleeding foe I knelt, And tenderly, with nervous hands,

Undid his tunic and its bands, And for his heart's pulsation felt. 

The heart was still; my foe was dead.

Dead as the steel that caused his death; 

That steel, alas, that stilled a breath 

And turned the feeling flesh to lead!

Upon his heart, and stained with blood- As who conceals a relic dear 

Next to the core of love sincere- 

A letter lay, well-thumbed with mud.

Then took High Ridge, and Pozières e’en, Although such feats were leonine. 

The cost in lives again was high,- 

But our objective was Bapaume, Which, with the aid of gun and bomb,

We aimed, with pluck, to occupy. 'Twas all imagination this, For hope had smothered any doubt; 

We e'en foresaw a German rout, Which set our reck'nings all amiss. 

We had forgotten line the third, And fourth, and fifth, and God knows what;

We got to hoping such a lot 

That reckoning became absurd.

In front of us was not a line, But twenty miles of fortress, massed With guns galore, of power so vast, That words-mere words-could ne'er define. 

Yet Haig, Lord Haig, did there aspire 

To smash right through . . . a fantasy 

Which made him mass the cavalry, To act beyond those lines of fire! 52

Fantastic blunders, such as these, Increased our warriors' sacrifice; 

And so was paid a tragic price

For unimaginative decrees. 

Five hundred thousand, more or less, Our casualties in that affray!

But that aroused no marked dismay Among the chiefs-and less distress. 

They chose to battle for each wood, Though officers and men averred 'Twas, tactically, quite absurd, And showed a "rank" decrepitude.

And so men fought because they must, Though knowing life hung by a thread; 

They might soon mingle with the dead, And all their strife and schemes be dust! 

And yet, at dawn of that grim day, The larks were singing in the blue, With mounting trills as higher they flew, Celestial music, sweet and gay. "Ye should not kill!" they seemed to say; "Ye should not kill!" for that's the law 

Which ye must nurture e'er with awe, And so, like us, be ever gay. "List, list, O man, ye stupid brute! 

Why must ye slay each other so, When ye can happiness bestow 

If ye contrive not to dispute?" "Ye must not kill," the heavens said, Displaying, 'gainst a dome so clear, Small wisps of fleece, evoking cheer 

As o'er the sunny blue they fled. 53

"Why should ye kill?" the purling corn, A-swaying negatively, asked. 

As in the glowing sun it basked: "O, why not hail the lovely morn? "For such will ripen me quite soon, And turn my green stems into gold, And ye will sing when ye behold 

How bountiful can be my boon. "Smash up your weapons, silly man, And show that sense can sway ye still; 

Ye were not born to maim and kill, But make of life a happy span." 

Aye, Nature there upon that spot,

To maddened man seemed to impart: "Take up your task with happy heart, And envy not your brothers' lot. "Behold how I return you e'er 

A hundredfold for tenfold seed, And give you everything you need, And never of my bounty spare. "You, senseless mortal, full of lust, Unto your brethren do like me; 

Give, and you'll reap in like degree, For men do honour what is just." "Look at these huddled mortals here!"

The copious yellow charlocks said- 

Amidst their lovely golden bed- "So young and dead like stricken deer! "Had they no mission in their lives? 

No need of love and happiness? 

No aim, no longing for success? 

No cheery bairns, no loving wives? 54

"Why did they come here to be killed . . . 

And end up in a rotting heap .

When they could plough and sow and reap, And see the joy of life fulfilled?" "Because they're fools!" the poppies said, And blushed deep scarlet, as they swayed; "Of enemies they're not afraid, But meekly to be slain are led. "And we that bloom for their delight, Cannot induce them to be sane . . ... 

Although they cry, but cry in vain, For homes that were so sweet and bright!" 

Look how our foes then in the fray,

Were just as sheep-like, every jot, They bleated sorrow o'er their lot, But dared not cast their guns away: "Red morning Sun, do you now glow 

To send me to an early death?” 

Such was their song at every breath, As each in woe to death did go. "The trumpet soon will sound the fray, And many brave will die with me;

I never thought that this would be 

The end of joys of yesterday! "For then a prancing steed I rode,- 

And now, right through my chest, a shot 

Foretells tomorrow 'tis my lot 

To lie in cold grave for abode!"

Such is the plaint of every heart 

That, lucklessly, is forced to face

This golgotha of deep disgrace, Which never should have had a start. 55

For war's a sin of magnitude 

So terrible to contemplate,

That words alone can ne'er relate Its horrors with exactitude. 

Not only brutal is its form, But ghastly is its every deed,- 

For men forget their Christian creed, And all morality deform.

Yet, for this crime there is no need,- 

No need to fight and kill or maim; 'Gainst governments which are to blame, Stern punishments should be decreed. 'Tis time that men en bloc should rise 

Against this human sacrifice, This crime that has one sole device: 

The golgotha that crucifies! 

That makes a martyrdom of man, Of woman and of children, too; 

Is it not barbarous, think you, To burn civilization's plan? 

Before this ghastly, savage fray,

The Somme was like a land of dreams, With wooded hills and rippling streams 

Along its vales of colours gay. 

Afar its villages were seen,

With little churches, here and there, The whole imparting that sweet air 

Of happiness throughout the scene. 

Then came this savage, mad onslaught— 

This killing monster we call “WAR,” 

And in their hearts all men abhor, Which chaos and damnation brought! 56

Aye, day by day, for months on end- 

Within an arc of forty miles,

With scarce a break-off, between whiles, Upon our lads did shells descend.

This from the left, that from the right,- 

This from the front, that echelon,

The shrieking, bursting shells kept on, And found fresh victims at each flight! 

And then, at last, it was our turn- 

The time had come to push them back, No matter at what cost, alack! 

Or what our guns would smash and churn.

The devastation they had wrought Was catastrophic, through and through, There was nought else that we could do 

But hit them till they ran distraught. 

And so our guns then opened fire 

With all their concentrated power, Until the Germans had to cower 

Where'er they could, or else expire. 

We smashed their trenches, their redoubts,- 

Their batteries to bits were blown,- 

Machine-gun nests within that zone

Collapsed, and so did small dug-outs. 

Some deep ones, even, smothered were— 

And smothered were the men inside; 

So sons and fathers choked-and died- 

Like ours, all victims of a scare! 

That scare which urged them both to fight, To tear each other limb from limb, To maim, to kill, however grim, And thus establish . . . who was right? 57

Or was it to establish might? 

Perhaps 'twas justice that both sought? 

If so, were they so far distraught 

To seek for justice in a fight? 

What were they fighting for-who knows? 

A piece of land? A mine or two? 

The right of way or such, think you? 

Or some suffused imbroglios? 

Whate'er it was, and no one's sure, Why kill four million folk, and maim 

Or ruin millions in a game 

So mad and tragic, so impure? 

Is justice dealt out with a crime? 

If so, why do we go to law? 

Could we not stretch a ripping claw, And kill our rivals every time? 

Alas, the men that don't reflect! 

Alas, the men that cannot see 

That this mad thing should never be, For all the world with this is wrecked! 

Alas, the bankruptcy of mind 

Which thinks of force, and only force,

As the sole means and last resource 

To deal out justice to mankind! 

I'll not describe the closing scenes,

For many books can give you these; 

I'll but beseech you, on my knees, To sit and think out humane means 

To right this wrong, for now and good; 

That it may never happen more, For men must never go to war, Whatever be the cause or mood. 58

The SECOND WAR has proved the FIRST 

Had taught no lesson to mankind, Who waxed up madness in his mind 

Until the world AGAIN WAS CURSED. 

And when 'twas o'er, the point remained: 

For folks we've killed or maimed or flailed,

For all we've suffered, lost or wailed, What have we gained? WHAT HAVE WE GAINED?
