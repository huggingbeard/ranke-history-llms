The Indian Army is the only entirely volunteer Army left in the world today. It numbers approximately two and one-half million officers and men. It is an army of old and fine traditions, of exemplary battle record and is invariably of an extremely high degree of training and discipline. 

Because of its fictional and motion-picture presentation in the past in the United States, it is necessary to point out most emphatically that whereas in peacetime it is an army with certain pomp of uniform and panoply of ceremony-it is in wartime indistinguishable from any other modern fighting force, except possibly in its deep esprit de its ferocity of singlemindedness in combat. corps and

The traditions of at least half of the Indian Army regiments go back as far or farther than the traditions of units in the Army of the United States. Two centuries ago, Indian Army troops helped the Spaniards take Manila. They fought the French and the Portuguese to a standstill for Ceylon-and took it. Before the turn of the nineteenth century they fought the French again in the Middle East and since then, periodically, their battle cries have been heard and their blood has been shed on battlefields in Europe, Africa, Arabia, and the Far East-in furtherance of the civilized function of bringing law and order to the world.

The modern Indian Army represents a complete cross section of these people of India, being composed of most of the races, many of the castes and practically all of the religions. But it is to the undying credit of that army that its efficiency, discipline and esprit de corps are first in its collective mind-and that racial frictions, religious differences and political discord are kept outside the gate. 

The expression "the martial races" is one that you still hear out here, but it no longer pertains to all components of the modern Indian Army. The nucleus of the Indian Army is a regular army-that is, a full, all-time, peacetime army subjected as are all such armies to wartime expansion, but it is sometimes confused with the private armies maintained by the 600-odd Indian princes who rule independent states. These private armies, after be ing trained and equipped, sometimes furnish components to the Indian Army, but they are not intrinsically a part of it -nor again are they the musical comedy armies you might think them to be. They form, in effect, a sort of Indian territorial force (our National Guard) which when souped up by well-planned training and in some cases reofficered in key positions, make an excellent fighting force.
