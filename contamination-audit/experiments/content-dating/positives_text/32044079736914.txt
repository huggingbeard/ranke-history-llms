When, then, the teacher takes upon himself to provide his students with a technique by which they may become better able to appreciate poetry, he is undertaking a no less difficult task than to define--at least for himself--such appreciation. It is not necessary that his definition find universal acceptance; it is necessary only that he have such a definition. Without it he is walking in dark and unfamiliar country--a condition not without charms of its own but one that cannot well be held desirable in him who has in trust the guidance of others.

Definitions of objectives, however, if wrongly arrived at, may be inimical to progress towards an end. I may know very clearly that my purpose is to walk from San Francisco to China, but that knowledge will help me not at all in achieving my purpose. Conditions surrounding the objective must be taken into consideration if the objective is to be reached by anything better than accident or chance. The teacher who desires to stimulate in his students an appreciation of poetry may see that aim as one thing or another, but his vision is likely to be unclear unless he first has acquainted himself thoroughly with the conditions which surround such appreciation. Appreciation is not something that takes place in a vacuum, but touches and is touched by all qualities that make human beings other than abstractions. 

It is with this thought in mind that we postpone our attempt at a definition of appreciation until such time as we have looked into the conditions under which appreciation must exist.

The Poet 

Now to look a little more closely, considering in turn each of the elements that enter into this situation. It is essential to see, first of all, that the poet is a man similar in most respects to ourselves. He is born, and lives, and dies. From the past, from his ancestors, his race, his civilization, he inherits, as we do, characteristics of body and temperament. Like us, he moves through a world of objects, and pains and pleasures, hopes, fears, tasks distasteful and pleasant, victories like an inspiriting draught, defeats bitter on the tongue. Like us, he reaches out to this world through his organs of sense, gathering through the eyes impressions of color and form, and through the other organs sounds harsh or soothing, impressions of touch and taste and smell. Like us, he thinks and feels. His perceptions are perhaps more delicate than ours, more subtle, but they are different from ours in quality only, not in kind. 

The poet's personality contains also that which we call imagination. We, too, possess it--and for the most part ignore its existence in us. The poet's imagination is fed and kept vigorously alive by the contacts which he makes with life--the worlds of sensation, feeling, thought. To us the action of our imaginations is likely to seem incidental, of little importance compared with what we call "practical concerns. 11 To the poet imagination is life. 

One other difference between us (the "average" persons) and the poet grows immediately apparent. Our attitude toward our own experiences is definitely conditioned by our concern over social, moral, or material ends. We evaluate our experiences not by what they are but by what they lead to. We are unremittingly watchful for results. 

The major portion of our energies of mind and body is dedicated to the securing of ends which we consider morally or socially desirable or materially profitable. The poet is not interested in the ends beyond experience, but in the qualities of experience. He delights in sensation for its own sake, and in thought and feeling because to him those are immediately exciting.

A last difference shows itself. We, through the importunate force of habit, remain relatively passive toward our own experiences. We see sights and forget them, hear sounds and they quickly fade out of our memories, think thoughts and feel emotions that are soon buried among our yesterdays. In the mind of the poet his experiences remain restlessly alive, exciting, disturbing. In this excitement, as an essential part of it, lies what we call the creative

The Poem 

We look next at the second element in our situation-the poem. 

The reader is looking at words. What, really, are they, and what is their meaning to him? Again the need for analysis is apparent. 

A. Words:--Change and Rest 

Words are the symbols for objects, thoughts, feelings. The meaning of any word results from agreement among the users of that word to give it a particular value. 

As we look back into the past we notice that this agreedupon value or meaning may change with the changing generations of men. The word character, to take only one of many possible examples, no longer means today what it signified several hundred years ago, and it is quite possible that several hundred years hence its meaning will have undergone further modification. The history of any language is thus in large part the story of the changing meanings of words, a story that, by revealing the causes for such change, frequently lifts into light interesting facts concerning the growth and decay of civilizations and even of individual human personalities.1

C. The Exact Word 

If we watch our own conversation or listen attentively to that of others, we shall quickly grow aware of an unpleasant slackness in word and phrase, an habitual use of bloodless and trite expressions, a slovenliness in the structure of sentences--in short, we shall have the realization forced upon us that everyday conversation concerns itself not at all with communicating experience, but merely with indicating it. I say that I saw a tree, or that a sunset was gorgeous, or that a certain person struck me as attractive. By none of these expressions have I enabled my hearer to live what I lived. At most I have indicated to him the general nature of my experiences--I have merely supplied him with a bit of information that may or may not be of interest to him.4

This indifference in everyday conversation to the exact or precise expression--the word or the group of words that will enable us to communicate the individuality of our experience to others--may be accounted for in many ways. 

Spoken words are colored and given shades of meaning by the voice of the speaker, by gestures and by changes in the features. The word "no", spoken in a particular manner may mean much more than simple negation. We speak with our whole bodies, and the aids to communication which we draw from gestures and voice modulations encourage us in habits of laziness about the actual words which we employ. 

A glance at the poem which our student has before him --let us say it is Keats' TO AUTUMN--will reveal to us, first (after we have reminded ourselves that there is no actual speaker present and therefore no direct physical aids to communication) that the words of which the poem is composed are precise and specific rather than vague and general. Autumn, the symbol or name for a particular time of year, indefinite in its connotations, almost abstract in its significance, becomes "Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness", and all the other words of the poem are equally concrete and exact.5

It will reveal to us, secondly, that the words of the poem are heavily charged with connotation. To take the first line only, mists and mellow fruitfulness are expressions rich in suggestiveness, inviting us to dream over the visions which they conjure up in our minds, flooding us with feelings of a particular quality. 

The poet, striving to catch in words the very essence of his experience, its complete truth and reality, uses such words as will stand with the fullest adequacy for that experience. The reader of poetry, if he is to enter into the experience of the poet, must grow keenly aware of the personality of individual words. Lacking such awareness, he lacks the only means by which to make contact with the poet --the contact that is at the very center of his purpose as a reader.

A poem, however, is composed not of single words, but of words in groups. What forms the relationship between word and word? What constitutes the context of the individual word?

D. Rhythm-content 

All poetry is rhythmical--though not necessarily metrical. The recurrence of stresses at more or less regular time intervals constitutes the basic rhythm-pattern of poems. This rhythmical movement naturally throws into prominence certain words in the line of poetry, particularly such words as receive stress or are related to significant pauses. A careful reading aloud of the following lines will make clear this effect of the rhythm-movement upon the individual word: 

That time of year thou may'st in me behold 

When yellow leaves, or few, or none do hang

Upon the boughs that shake against the cold 

Bare ruin'd choirs where late the sweet birds sang. 

(Shakespeare) Bonara Wilkinson (THE POETIC WAY OF RELEASE, p. 17)

The student must be helped to see the words of poetry in their rhythm-context, for in that context beats one of the pulses that give to poetry its individual life.

F. Picture-context 

Poetry is largely picture-writing--its thought and feeling are locked up or inclosed in images. The lines just quoted from Shakespeare will serve in illustration. 

Old age, the idea, the abstraction, has become a series of pictures--yellow leaves, naked boughs, etc. 

The poet characteristically speaks in pictures. Each image-bearing expression is related to every other imagebearing expression in the poem, for a poem is not a haphazard collection of colorful bits but a highly unified and artistically wrought whole.

The student must be helped to see the pictures in poetry; he must be helped, further, to see the relation of each picture to every other one and of all to the whole effect of the poem. He must be taught to read imaginatively, to penetrate through the word to the meanings which that word in its poetic context represents. He must be brought to realize that words in poetry, though to the eye they have the same appearance that they have in prose, have been transformed by certain forces of imagination, by the impact of certain kinds of rhythm--by all those factors that set poetry as an art apart from prose. Such realization, obviously, implies that the student learn to know words not only in one, but in all of their poetic meanings. 

G. The Reader 

We now look at the student. What attitude does he take toward the poem on the page before him? No teacher needs to have pointed out to him what the typical attitudes of students toward poetry are. Almost any classroom will supply in abundance students who are hostile to poetry, or merely indifferent, or lukewarm in their interest--and the few who read it with something like enjoyment. With those

last we shall concern ourselves later; at present our business is with the larger group. How is poetry to be saved for them as a source of pleasure, as a means to profound intellectual, emotional and aesthetic satisfaction? By no command of ours can their attitudes be changed. It were as sensible to command a blind man to see--and just as profitable--as to tell a student that he must appreciate poetry. 

Let us shift our ground, then. The average student is unfamiliar with words in their poetic meanings. His Vocabulary is limited, his word sense is unaroused, he is unused to looking at words imaginatively. Attempting to read poetry as he would read prose, he is baffled by the rhythmical movement, blinded by the imaginative qualities and the audacity of poetic phrasing, bewildered by the very pattern which he should delight in. Under such conditions it is inconceivable that he should enter into poetry as a world to be lived in and enjoyed with his best powers, inconceivable that poetry should mean anything to him. And that which he finds meaningless must remain to him unimportant, a thing to be avoided or attended to only under compulsion and with distaste.

We have seen the problem--we are about to attempt a solution. But, to observe the warning with which we commenced this analysis, what is this appreciation of poetry which we hope to arouse in our student? He sits there with the poem before him--what do we want him to do about it? 

H. Definition of Appreciation 

Appreciation begins when the student learns to make his own the experiences presented to him in poetry, and reaches its full growth when he learns to evaluate these experiences by sound standards of thought, feeling, and aesthetic judgment. Mature appreciation may thus be defined as intelligent, intense, and sensitive living in or through poetry.

This is the result we desire, the end we, as teachers, are striving to achieve. With this beginning, and with this fuller growth we shall concern ourselves in the pages that follow.

Chapter II Teaching Poetry as a Discovery of Self Part One: The Senses in Poetry 

"The poet writes the history of his own body." 

We have said that appreciation of poetry begins in the student when he commences actually to enter into the experiences which poetry offers him, when he learns to live through the words of a poem the life which those words represent. The problem, then, for the teacher, is to open the doors--to devise ways of presenting poetry that will invite the student to pass through words to the realities that lie behind them.

All men are aware of the functioning of their senses, all men take some measure of delight in the life of their bodies. Sounds, sights, tastes, textures, odors, muscular conditions--no normal person but finds satisfactions in some or in all of these. Whatever the degree of pleasure men may take in sensation, the awareness of sensation, is con- 1 stantly with them. 

Again, the life of the senses is that part of experience which all men have most definitely in common. 

I may think thoughts that other men do not share, steep myself in moods that others may not even guess at, but the impressions that pour in upon me from the physical world are not much different from those that pour in upon all other men in my environment. Birds and flowers and trees, mountains and sunsets, wind and running waters, the warmth of firesides, the sting of driving rain--these are mine and all men's.'

First, since the senses afford the readiest meeting-ground between man and man, the student will through them establish his most direct contact with the poet. Once this recognition of himself in the poems he reads comes to the student, any indifference that he might have felt toward poetry is likely to disappear, for poetry will then strike him as a reality as clear and as important as is that of his own existence.

Obviously, the most immediate question is one of perception--what does the student see, hear, touch, smell, taste; what muscular conditions is he aware of as he reads the poem? It must be remembered that the average person, though he is looking through the world for pleasure, attends hardly at all to his senses as a source of pleasure. 

It contents him to know that a certain object is a tree, a certain sound the song of a thrush, a certain color the blue of water. Beyond such categories he does not go, for with the categories themselves useful knowledge ends. It is again the attitude of the practical as contrasted with that of the poetic person we have here to contend with, the attitude which concerns itself not at all about the qualities of particular things, but only with the general characteristics of things.

In such soil observation finds little to thrive on, for the forms of nature, when we dispose of them through classifications, become few and repetitious. If to us a tree is merely a tree and not much different from all other objects in its class, it is not likely that we shall long remain interested in trees. Even worse than that, we shall soon cease really to look at them, for a rapid and cursory glance will be sufficient to tell us what they are. This habit of not seeing is deeply ingrained in most of us--so deeply, in fact, that only the startling or the unusual have

We are asking ourselves how we may best help him perceive images (that is, penetrate to the experiences behind the words of a poem), how we may help him savor the particular qualities, the very essence of such experiences. This esult can be achieved, I feel, only after the student has been subjected to a preliminary conditioning process.

Our ability to reintegrate the poet's experience must depend, fundamentally, upon the richness or adequacy of our experience, either direct or imaginative. A sensitive reader may reach out through his imagination to a life in poetry vastly more colorful and significant than his own personal existence. Part Two: Feeling in Poetry 

"I cannot actually describe my joy or my sorrow, my fear or my delight; I can only name the things or events that gave rise to these feelings, with the hope that, by naming the things or events, other people will have a somewhat similar feeling aroused, and SO understand mine.... "Tennyson, in Break, Break, Break, could not heave his heart into his mouth. He does not tell us directly how he felt. He could not. All he could do was to name objects the sight or sound of which, through images, directly or by contrast, gave rise to or were associated with his feeling of loneliness and grief; and, having done this, he can only trust that his reader, calling up appropriate images and grouping them in the manner suggested by the lines, will have a somewhat like feeling aroused." 

(Arthur H. R. Fairchild, THE MAKING OF POETRY, pp. 9 and 10.)

Sensation and feeling are always closely allied. Of the multitudinous impressions coming to us at every moment through our organs of sense, only a few strike deep enough into our consciousness and endure there long enough in the form of images to become surrounded or encrusted with feeling. If we pause for a minute or two and attend carefully to the sounds which assail our ears, we shall notice with surprise how numerous such sounds are, and with no less surprise, how little aware of them we have been. Clatter and whirr of cars, voices in the street, beating of hammers, shouting of children--these and a thousand more noises form a ground-swell of sound to which we, through the protective numbing of our senses by habit, pay little or no attention.

Now call up memories of sounds that have left distinct impressions on us--a cry at night, the sudden song of a meadow lark, the deepening roar of a waterfall.

Sounds of the first sort are associated in our minds with little or no feeling; those of the second sort are colored by feelings often of great intensity. We cannot call up an image of the night-cry without experiencing a cold tingling of fear and suspense; our memory of the meadow lark's song sets free in us a warm thrill of pleasure. No sensation that endures in the mind as a vivid image remains uncolored by feeling. Call up the image and you at once release the feeling with which it is associated. Such feelings are, if we care to use the term, the connotations of objects and events.1

Generally speaking, such connotations are, for most men, similar. Individual differences do exist--and they are always interesting and, to the individual at least, important--but they do not invalidate the generalization just made. An image of maple leaves unfolding in springtime will, in the minds of most men, arouse feelings of pleasure; an image of a bird with a broken wing is not likely to give rise to feelings of delight.2

Now, as Fairchild has explained, feelings can be communicated only through images with which such feelings are normally associated. If I wish to have you know my joy or my sorrow--that is, if I wish to communicate to you these feelings of mine--I must arouse in your mind images of objects and events with which you yourself will associate feelings of joy or sorrow. In other words, you can enter into my feeling only by remembering your own. 3

Part Three: Some Aspects of Imagination 

The student is likely to feel uncomfortable when confronted with the term imagination. For him it has many halfmeanings, all vague and unmanageable. He has, quite probably, a notion that the word signifies something unreal, other-worldly, something strange or even abnormal; that imagination is a possession of just a few mysteriously gifted persons; that it is a mere ornamental thing, effeminate, not to be taken seriously by people who want to "do something" or "get somewhere" in the world. His attitude toward the word, therefore, is likely to be one of scorn, or indifference, or frank bewilderment. 

In face of such conditions it is desirable to start the student thinking about imagination on the basis of the simplest possible definition. No person who really knows poetry would attempt a complete definition of the poetic imagination, a definition that should take into account all of the forms in which such imagination manifests itself. 

Even could such a definition be made, it would be of no value to anyone whose familiarity with poetry was not as great as that of the definer himself. 

At present, furthermore, we are interested in having the student discover his own imagination, so that on the basis of that discovery he may gradually develop an understanding of the poet's. Discarding subtleties, we define the imagination as that power or faculty by which we can call up in our minds images or pictures of sensations experienced in the past.

The definition is not difficult to apply to the student's own life. We name for him familiar objects--home, a pet dog, mother,--or other things from everyday experience --the taste of an apple, the odor of roses or of beef roasting in the oven, the sound of wind in pine trees, the feel of woolen blankets--and for each of these he is able to call up an image. That, to be sure, is merely memory--but memory is the mother of all imaginings, no matter how sublime.

After such exercises the student can no longer doubt that he possesses imagination or that imagination is a normal and a highly essential part of existence, his and all men's. We may remind him, now, that when he studied the sensations and the feelings in poetry, his activity was a distinctly imaginative one. 

That constitutes a beginning, a foundation. Where shall we turn next?

At the center of the poetic imagination exists a 'type of action variously described as "personalizing", "identifying", or "empathy." By empathy the individual projects himself into other objects--they may be humans like himself, or animals, birds, trees, the forces of nature, even abstract ideas--giving to these a life like his own. Later, when we discuss other aspects of the poetic imagination, and such related topics as figures of speech, we shall be aware constantly of the importance of empathy in poetry. 

For the time being we are concerned only with bringing to the student the realization that imagination is anything but foreign to his nature. 

Professor Fairchild points out that "personalizing" is a process or action characteristic of all men from the most primitive savage to the most sophisticated member of a cultured society. Chapter III Teaching Poetry as a Discovery of the World Part One: Poetic or Imaginative Thought 

Thus far we have concerned ourselves with those elements of poetry with which the student is likely to be familiar through his own experience. He possesses organs of sense, feels the tug of various emotions, enters in one degree or another into empathy. As teachers we have been striving to make him more conscious of these possessions so that by this awareness he might be able to enter into the similar elements present in poetry. The object of this training we have described as the discovery of self in poetry.

From this beginning appreciation must develop to an understanding of worlds larger and somewhat different from those which the student is likely to recognize as familiar. 

Sensation, emotion, empathy are common property. Having learned to perceive them in poetry, our recognition of them grows increasingly alert and pleasurable. We now turn to another element--thought or ideas in poetry. 

All normal people, in some measure at least, think thoughts. All have "ideas" or "opinions" about religion, conduct, manners, politics and a hundred other matters. 

All engage in some kind of thinking as they go about their practical affairs.1 

It would seem, then, that the perception of thought in poetry would be a discovery of self no less than is the perception of the life of the senses. 

It is here that the first great difference between the poetic and the non-poetic personality shows itself. The non-poetic person tends strongly to think in abstractions. 

Exercises: 

1. Write down, in single sentences, ideas (opinions, thoughts) which come to your mind as you reflect upon the following topics: true and false patriotism; education increases the individual's chances for happiness; social-mindedness; the evils of war; the blessings of peace.

The purpose of this exercise is to have students grow aware of their own ideas and of their own capacity for thought. Topics drawn directly from school-life will sometimes appeal more strongly to students than will the type of topics suggested above. It is important, whatever topics the teacher may use, that students be brought actually to think.

He will discuss, for example, the blessings of peace in such terms as prosperity, security, the advance of culture, good will among men, the refinement of man's feelings, and so forth. His chief concern is to be clear, calm, logical. If images do arise in his mind--that is, if his thought ceases to be abstract--he is likely to give himself to the emotions associated with such images, and to cease thinking. 

Poetic thought is imaginative thought. It exists always in close relation to feeling and strives, in expressing itself in words, to express at the same time the feeling with which it is associated. We have seen that feeling in poetry is communicated through pictures. It follows that the poet, in striving to communicate his emotionalized thoughts to others, will clothe such thoughts in pictures or at least relate them closely to pictures. Macbeth, one of the most poetic characters created by Shakespeare, does not state abstractly "Duncan is dead", but makes tangible or concrete his thought in a picture: 

Duncan is in his grave;

After life's fitful fever he sleeps well. 

Instead of saying "my life is ruined, my hope gone, I am ready to die", he tells us: 

I have liv'd long enough: my way of life 

Is fall'n into the sear, the yellow leaf."

Pointing out to Lady Macbeth that their plans have not yet reached completion, that the danger which threatens them as a result of their murder of Duncan has not been entirely removed, his thought and feeling burn fiercely into pictures: "We have scotch'd the snake, not kill'd it:

She'll close and be herself, whilst our poor malice 

Remains in danger of her former tooth.

But let the frame of things disjoint, both the worlds suffer, Ere we will eat our meal in fear and sleep 

In the affliction of these terrible dreams 

That shake us nightly. Better be with the dead,

Whom we, to gain our peace, have sent to peace, Than on the torture of the mind to lie 

In restless ecstasy."

If the poet does employ abstractions, he usually relates them closely to pictures, thus emotionalizing them. 

Tennyson is defending the intensity of his grief over the death of Hallam against the intended-to-be-consoling notion that death is common and intense grief, therefore, unreasonable and to be avoided. The thought of the first two stanzas is expressed abstractly. The next two stanzas present vivid pictures which serve to localize the idea of death and thus make it concrete and emotionally effective: "One writes, that 'Other friends remain,' 

That 'Loss is common to the race'- And common is the commonplace, And vacant chaff well meant for grain. 

That loss is common would not make 

My own less bitter, rather more: 

Too common! Never morning wore 

To evening, but some heart did break. 

O father, whereso'er thou be, Who pledgest now thy gallant son; 

A shot, ere half thy draught be done, Hath still'd the life that beat from thee. 

O mother, praying God will save 

Thy sailor,--while thy head is bow'd,

His heavy-shotted hammock-shroud 

Drops in his vast and wandering grave."

The sound values and the rhythm values of poetry in their relation to emotional effects will be discussed when we consider sound and rhythm. 

What, now, is the teacher's problem as he undertakes to arouse in his students perception of imaginative thought? 

Such thought, obviously, must be perceived in the pictures with which the poet has clothed it, and suffused with the emotion which is the connotation of those pictures. 

Merely intellectual recognition of the thought is worse than undesirable, for it misses entirely the imaginative qualities which make the thought poetic. Paraphrases, if taken as ends in themselves, are vicious, for they supplant, in the student's mind, the poem itself. The thought of poetry cannot be separated from the pictures which make it manifest--the pictures which are its body and life. 

Fortunately, the student has already been trained to perceive pictures and to dwell over such pictures

sufficiently long for emotion to establish itself in relation to them. It remains for the teacher to set up some technique by which the thought of poems may be discussed with students in the classroom. 

First, and at least until students have read carefully a considerable body of poetry--daily reading assignments must be short. It is impossible for anyone to read a large amount of poetry rapidly and live the experiences there presented to him. Long assignments lead directly to a kind of reading the purpose of which is simply to "get the idea" or "what it is all about." Such reading defeats at every turn the aims of the teacher who is trying to develop in his students intelligent appreciation. 

Now let us take a class which has read, say, Tennyson's ULYSSES. We may begin with questions about the mood of the poem. What feelings did we experience as we read it? To what pictures do we trace these feelings? (The students, of course, have the poem before them. It is therefore possible for them to point out the picture-arousing phrases, lines, passages.) Can we now find a term which will name or describe the mood of the whole poem? Is it a mood of hopelessness, aspiration, fear, courage, etc.?

Having named the mood we shall find that we have at the same time named the thought of the poem, for in poetry thought and feeling are one and inseparable. It is now possible to discuss that thought as thought, relating it, perhaps, to the poet's "philosophy", to the ideas current in the times and the society in which the poet lived, considering it, if we so desire, in relation to what we think at present. This, obviously, is not a discussion of the poem but of what ideas the poem has made us think of. 

We now return to the poem, reading it line by line, pausing over each picture, placing back in their poetic context the "ideas" which we have been freely considering, re-living the feelings, the emotion with which the poet has warmed those ideas into life. The last impression, then, is of the poem--of an experience or a group of experiences in sensation, emotion, thought--not of some abstraction which is not the poem at all.2

Part Two: The Creative Imagination 

With the simpler characteristics of the imagination the student is already familiar. He understands what is meant by "images" or mental pictures, and has seen how such pictures are communicated through words. The terms practical and reproductive imagination as descriptive of this simplest type of image-making are fully explained in THE 

APPRECIATION OF POETRY, pp. 75-84.1 and 2 The personaliz ing action of the imagination (empathy) is now also sufficiently understood so that the student may look for manifestations of it in the poetry he reads. We turn to a consideration of the creative imagination. 

The creative action of the imagination is that which, by synthesizing one experience and another, or one part of an experience and another part, brings into being pictures which have had no previous existence. (For a description of this process, with examples and analyses, see THE AP- 

PRECIATION OF POETRY, pp. 84-101.) 

Few students have the ability to recognize creative images, for the type of mental activity out of which such images arise is to the average person relatively foreign. 

Practical necessity demands that we see the differences rather than the resemblances between object and object, that we be careful not to confuse one thing with another. We are trained, if the expression may pass, to see realistically, to observe things as they are. Though all of us can see in experience only what our background enables us to see-that is, our perceptions are conditioned by our past experiences--yet we strive to keep that background as inactive as possible.

In this respect the attitude of the poet is distinctly different from that of the average person. Where we are concerned with differences he is interested in resemblances; where we try to see things as they are, he encourages the light of his past experiences (his background in sensation, thought, feeling) to play over them as strongly as it may.

The poet's perceptions, therefore, are much more clearly than are ours a combination of present impressions with remembered ones.

Part Four: The Worlds of Poetry I. Inner Characteristics 

The student, through his study of words in poetry, the senses in poetry, feeling in poetry, empathy, poetic thought, the creative imagination, figures of speech, has gradually acquired an understanding of certain elements of poetry which should now be given form and distinctness by having those elements brought into one clear focus. Analysis must be followed by synthesis. This synthesis must be made in the light of the inner life of the poem--not a mechanical or merely technical synthesis. For this reason the contribution of such technical devices as rime-patterns, rhythmpatterns, etc., to the world of any poem will follow the study of image-grouping, mood-unity, etc.--matters which may, at least for the teacher's purpose, be thought of as more definitely related to the inner being of any poem. 

Every good poem may be spoken of as a world in that it represents a pattern completely achieved, a oneness or unity that is clearly discernible. If the poem tells a story, all parts of that story must be related one to another, all must contribute to the final outcome, all descriptive passages, all details of incident and dialogue, must reflect and intensify the tone or mood of the whole narrative. Colloquial expressions, humorous incidents, descriptions of trivial objects, would shatter the unity of 

PARADISE LOST; heroic dignity or epic splendors of diction would be out of place in TAM O'SHANTER'S RIDE or in the "realistic" world of one of Masefield's SALT WATER BALLADS.

If the poem centers in an idea, that idea must be completely expressed (always bearing in mind that the expression is likely to be imaginative rather than direct and literal), and the emotions with which the idea is clothed (through images) must be appropriate to the idea itself. This does

not mean, of course, that a poet must always color his thinking about death in shades of gloom or melancholy; he may, as Browning did in PROSPICE, write of death in a mood of joy and exultation, or as Tennyson did in CROSSING THE 

BAR, in a mood of calm assurance that has in it no touch of sorrow or regret. It does mean that the emotions associated with the idea must be genuine and sincere. If they are such, they will be appropriate to the idea no matter in what light the poet happens to view it. If the poem centers in mood--e.g., THE LOTOS-EATERS, KUBLA KHAN--all details pre sent in the poem must contribute directly to the realization of that mood.

A poem, then, is a complete and individual world when all the elements of which it is composed operate harmoniously together to create a single distinct impression or effect. (THE APPRECIATION OF POETRY, pp. 141-145.) 

The themes of poetry and the moods of poetry are as various as the experiences of men. Poets may picture events and places much as we know them, or, searching through the more shadowy chambers of the mind, bring to us worlds that are strange and mysterious. Between the extremes of realism and the outrightly fantastic or fanciful there are innumerable kinds or qualities of mood and subject-matter, and all are the province of poetry. It need be remembered only that every poem must be read and considered in the light of its own mood. The world of Carman's VAGABOND'S SONG, for ex ample, is not the same as that of KUBLA KHAN, and the student who reads both poems must learn to perceive the dif ference. (THE APPRECIATION OF POETRY, pp. 145-152.) 

Our student has read carefully the two poems just named, dwelling over the images, looking closely at whatever similes and metaphors may be present, and is ready to talk about the feeling which the poem has evoked in him and to point out the passages which he thinks had most to do with arousing such feeling. We take the VAGABOND'S SONG first. Having given the student an opportunity to express his response to the poem, and to discuss that response in terms of particular passages, we now ask him to consider how one image is related to another, and how all the images work harmoniously together to create the mood of the poem. The mood is clearly indicated in the title--this is a Vagabond's song, a song of the open road, a song of the wanderlust that wells up in the heart of the rover. Every image, every statement brings home this mood: October calling each vagabond by name, the gypsy blood stirring, the spirit thrilling to the frosty asters and to the scarlet of the maples that shakes the wanderer "like a cry of bugles going by." In

this connection we may notice the rhythm, rapid in its movement, strong in its stresses--the rhythm of racing pulses and brisk swinging strides. We notice, further that all the images are of Autumn: "the yellow and the purple and the crimson", "the scarlet of the maples", "the frosty asters like a smoke upon the hills." This localizing of the mood, of course, gives to the whole pattern of the poem clearness and strength, and does much to establish that singleness of impression, that inner consistency, which makes the poem artistically effective and emotionally convincing.

We now study KUBLA KHAN in a similar fashion. The student will have no difficulty in perceiving the difference between the mood of this poem and that of the previous one. Again we look closely at the individual images, observing how every image has in it something of every other image, and how all the images unite in creating the central feeling. The student will thus be brought to see that, while the worlds of poetry are greatly various, the principle by which those worlds are created is the same for all poems. In other words, he will begin to understand the principle of artistic unity, which is the fundamental organizing or shaping principle in all imaginatively creative effort. II. The Study of Form 

Having studied the inner harmonies of poetry, we now turn to a consideration of that harmony or unity which is achieved in poetry by a blending of matter and form. The emphasis, which in the preceding section has been on ter, will now shift to the analysis of such elements as relate directly to form. mat- The study of rhythm, meter, rime, stanza forms, etc., presents many difficulties to the teacher who is working with students just launched on their reading of poetry. To compel students to learn the names and definitions of the various types of meter and the different stanza forms is one matter; to help them, through such learning, to quicken their appreciation of poetry is an undertaking of a more difficult sort.

The teacher interested in the development of appreciation needs to remind himself that, in the light of his objective, information about poetry cannot be accepted as an end in itself. The majority of his students are not going to become professional scholars in the field of literature. 

This being so, it is unreasonable to expect a class to show any interest in gathering information, for its own sake, about trochees, alliteration, and the rest. 

The problem is to keep the study of poetic technique clearly and steadily related to the main purpose of poetry-the communication of experience. The student should be brought to see that rhythm and the other technical or rhetorical elements of poetry are not merely poetic devices, but are an essential part of that which the poet is striving to communicate. To receive this communication, therefore, the student must acquaint himself with the media through which the communication is made.

will provide the teacher with adequate material for the study of poetic technique. To reproduce that material here is unnecessary, but some suggestions concerning the presentation of it in the classroom may be of help to teachers. 

The study of types of poetry is, I feel, in general not suited to the interests and the backgrounds of high school students. To understand, for example, the epic as a literary type demands a reading-background and a maturity of thought not possessed by the average boy or girl in high school. The study of such a type, as a type, is all too likely to become purely informational in tone (the teacher supplying the information) and mechanical in method. other hand, students will readily grasp the type-characteristics of ballads and will respond with interest to an

Two other suggestions are pertinent. It is highly desirable, wherever possible, to relate the topics discussed to the student's own life and interests. All students, for example, will recognize rhythm as a part of their own experiences. They have listened to the ticking of the clock, or have watched the waves of the sea; they have felt the beat of their own pulses and have moved their bodies to the swaying patterns of the dance. They are, then, already familiar with the delights of rhythm, and may be counted on to enter eagerly into any discussion or analysis which will sharpen their perceptions and correspondingly quicken their delight. The teacher may readily carry this interest on into the study of meter (as an ordering of rhythms into clear patterns), and into the study of stanza forms. This approach encourages the student to enter creatively into the study of elements which, considered merely as mechanics, may well appear lifeless and uninteresting. 

The second suggestion is that all technical elements present in a poem be studied as a part of the whole meaning --the complete world--of that poem. We should see in such a poem as THE LOTOS-EATERS, what the rhythms, the sound qualities of the words, the rimes, etc., add to the mood of the poem--what part they play in making that world what it is, and, therefore, what part they play in helping the poet make his particular communication. Perhaps nowhere more clearly than at this point in his teaching of poetry does the teacher need to remember that he is not a peddler of facts but a guide to his students into rich worlds of sensation, thought, emotion and imagination--into worlds where the student follows not only to see what is there, but to live in what is there with the fulness of his best powers.

Chapter IV 

Building a Critical Attitude

Obviously, it is impossible, with students young in years and largely unfamiliar with poetry, to launch into discussion of critical theories. Obviously, also, it is desirable to develop in students a taste for poetry as sound and as catholic as possible. The question confronting the teacher then is, how to make students critical in their attitude toward poetry without actually going with them into a study of criticism. The answer to that question will be found, I trust, in the paragraphs which follow. 

The poet, out of his creative urge to catch experience and pattern it in words, strives always to express a subject matter completely. If his pattern is achieved, his poem is to that extent successful. If, through lack of a proper technique, his pattern is not achieved, his expression fails of completeness and the value of his poem is thereby decreased. After his study of the technical elements of poetry, the student should be able to detect such technical flaws.

The kind of inferior poetry which constitutes a snare for the student is that in which valueless matter is disguised by a technique which in itself might seem pleasing. 

The inexperienced reader of poetry is often "taken in" by verse whose rhythms are mechanically smooth and whose rimes make a fine jingling in the ears. The teacher will find in the syndicated verse of many present-day newspapers (verse which is frequently published in book form offer its appearance in the daily or weekly column), copious examples of what is here being spoken of. The student may know that his teacher disapproves of such verse in general, yet, reading it, he finds in it a "lilt" and "swing" that please him, and, all too often, an intellectual and an emotional content which satisfy him.

The teacher must be able to show his students why any poem is unsatisfactory. Technical defects, as I have said, may be seen by the student or may be pointed out by the teacher. In all but the most technically complex or subtle poetry--and high school students will not be concerned with work of this sort--this matter presents no grave difficulties. The proper evaluation of the content of a poem is decidedly more difficult.

It must be remembered that all experience is the province of the poet, that he is not limited to any particular kind of theme but may write of all things that enter into the life of man, including his life in the imagination. is not possible, then, to dismiss any poem with the simple comment that its theme is unpoetic or unsuited to the uses of poetry. The value of a poem does not lie in its theme but in what the poet has been able to see in that theme. great poem might conceivably be written about a bathtub, provided the poet had been able to find in his contemplation of that object something that stirred his emotions and excited profoundly his powers of thought. In other words, the content-value of a poem lies in the imaginative power and intensity with which the poet has illuminated his theme. 

Where that imaginative power is lacking, the poet--he is at that moment merely versifier--can add nothing to our perceptions, either by clarifying them or by enriching them with interesting thoughts and significant feelings, but must of necessity fall into triteness of idea and superficiality of feeling. The cardinal sin of all bad poetry is that it is unimaginative.

If students have learned, through their study of the various elements of poetry and the relation of each element to the whole effect of a poem, to perceive true imagination when it is present, they have at least a means of detecting the absence of imagination or the presence of "false notes." 

They may be expected, for example, no longer to thrill to the "trees that wear, a nest of robins in their hair", and to remain just a little cold to the seer who exclaims with all the fervor of a profound discovery that "It takes a heap of living to make a house a home."

The basis for a truly critical attitude toward poetry, and therefore also the basis for a truly appreciative attitude, can be nothing other than an understanding, arrived at through study and analysis, of poetry itself. On that foundation the structure erected by the individual will be spacious or cramped and narrow as that individual has or does not have the interest or the wherewithal to build. No man can find in poetry more than he is able to bring to the reading of it--and his first and most essential equipment is the ability to perceive what its meanings are.. There is the foundation, and that the teacher must lay. What comes after will depend upon the students' growth in thought, feeling, and experience of the world, and upon the strength of the interest which supports that growth. To lay the foundation of knowledge, to fire and to make intelligent the interest, and

then to feed the interest with significant and nourishing materials--that is the whole task of the teacher of the appreciation of poetry. Having done that task he already has made his students into critics, for the aim of the critic and that of the appreciator are in the last analysis the same, to distinguish the good from the bad and to hold fast to that which is good.
