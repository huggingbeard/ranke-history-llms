FOREWORD 

An inspiring sight it is to watch an army of men as they swing forward in perfect step, the bands striking out that martial music and the people cheering wildly. The heart beats faster and sometimes tears flood the eyes, just from the sensation of majestic strength. 

War has been wise beyond its wisdom in the use of music and display. No drama matches the drama of marching men. A study of the faces of the men as they pass, is one of the moving sights of life. If they are marching away to the wars, each man responds to the emotions of his own heart. Youth is usually full of spirit, singing lustily. Drafted men, men of a fuller life in education, home, Church and state, show the strain of breaking such ties. Stern faces greet those who stand by the way, cheering. If to the wars they must go, giving up life itself for the cause of those freedoms which are worth life, such men go in consecration to the good of all mankind. "Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for a friend"; for the good of those free-

doms without which life is indeed barren and worthless.

A vastly different sight it is, when one beholds an army of veterans marching, especially if the war is still on and they are but pressing forward to new positions where they must hold or die. 

Descriptions of French forces marching on to Verdun are among the most moving words to be read by man. When one considers the millions of men who for two thousand years have marched away to the wars, one is appalled at the enormity of the losses of manhood, so needed for the upbuilding of the human race. The Earth vibrates to the marching feet of such millions of men. Could the wounded and the killed of the wars come back and enter the ranks of the marching, what a gasp would go up from the living: and even from the dead! It would take such an army maching day and night, one hundred abreast, more than a year to pass a given point. "Endless ranks of an army maching. Drum taps, drum taps, where are they marching?" 

What is it in humanity that calls for such marching, such wars, such destruction? Something is imbedded there, something that demands conflict, matching of strength and cunning; fighting to the death. Why does man encourage it, train it, honor it, call it glory to die in battle?

There is a something in man that can be satis-

Men engage in mob battles. They must settle some dispute or hate by fighting. The animals fight each other. One bull will be the leader of the herd. One stallion will lead the wild mares.

It is not an uncommon sight in the woodland to see the skeletons of two stags which had locked horns in battle and being unable to pull away from each other, have both died and given their flesh to the mountain lion or the wolves. Such animal nature is part of man's inheritance. The blood of the wild runs through his veins.

Then, too, man's innate desire to rule, to be first, to have mastery, is yet another reason for such conflicts. And when a whole nation led by an ambitious, unscrupulous man or group of men, can so arouse the people that they feel their lives are threatened by another nation or group of nations, then national and world wars ensue. 

The world is now in such a period. A global war grips humanity. Nations desiring freedom group themselves and oppose nations that seek to enslave. There are two camps of men today; the Axis group and the group of Allies. The world is at war and the nations are marching. "See the gleam of their white, sad faces, marching steadily row on row."

In our day war finds new horrors. From the heavens filled with commerce, there rains "A

ghastly dew, from the nations' airy navies grappling in the central blue." From the sea bottom there rise death wolves to pull down to the deeps great ships and brave sailors. Along the surface of the ocean there speed boats spitting death as fiery monsters. From unseen hills and valleys giant guns vomit death upon unseen peoples. 

Death, death, and men marching. "Drum taps, drum taps, where are they marching?" Marching away to eternity! 

Why are they marching? Some cannot avoid it. They must march or die by firing squads.

Some are free men and march for the love of the common good of humanity. They recognize that life without freedom is worthless. They will die that their children and their children's children may live freely in a new world. 

Freedom is not a gift; it is an acquirement.

Others have fought and died that we may have our freedoms of today. When humanity sags down under the weight of its materialism, then selfishness and dictatorship lift their horrid heads and call once again for bloodshed and war. To such wars must go even those who have striven to avert the wars. Humanity is one. No part can be saved and the rest remain lost. We live with others; we must suffer and die with each other.

CHAPTER I  

What has been called "The balder-dash of Victorianism," is two lines from Brownings' great poem, Pippa Passes. The lines read, 

"God's in His Heaven- 

All's right with the world.”

But such a statement as the above reveals the utter misreading of the poet's thought and words. He is speaking of the Physical world of man and not of his social or political world. Read the whole stanza. "The year's at the spring 

And day's at the morn; 

Morning's at seven; 

The hillside's dew-pearled;

The lark's on the wing; 

The snail's on the thorn; 

God's in His Heaven- 

All's right with the world." 17

Every word of the stanza has to do with the physical creation of God. It's a bold and hazardous adventurer who would presume to pass judgment on the physical handiwork of God! In the sense in which Browning meant his words to be understood we adjudge that all is right with the world. 

There is another world, however, the world of the social, the political, the world of human society, that is not all right. It is befogged, is in confusion and chaos, void of law, order, direction or control. Society slumps. Law appears as the force of military might. Might seems the power to control; right is dismissed as a weakling's refuge. Nietzsche's philosophy reigns. Order is dependent upon the beliefs of professional soldiers. The force of arms must control the destinies of all peoples. War is enthroned. War is the king of the years. Human blood is the oil with which to lubricate the tank wheels, rolling for mastery. All the fine spun theories of brotherhood, social amelioration, religious obligation and obedience to God, are wiped off the agenda of human thought or discussion. We seem to have come to that crisis described by Tennyson in The 

Passing of Arthur, "All whereon I leaned is traitor to my peace, and all my realm reels back into the beast, and is no more."

From the standpoint of our former thinking and belief, we are now insane and bound for the very

detention homes we built for those whom we have adjudged incapable of self care, and a menace to society. 

What has gone wrong with this human world of society, of government, of brotherhood? Why all this present confusion? What has destroyed that sense of security, permanency and peace, once regnant? What laws have been broken, what gods angered, what punishments are due? Such questions are asked wherever men meet and commingle. All seem confused, bewildered, afraid. 

Man no longer controls the society he has developed. His very creation has become a Frankenstein to destroy its creator. 

The fact is man has unwittingly built a machine which is the present master. The machine has become the ruler, and those who built it are its slaves. The machine is a runaway now, speeding down the grade with increasing momentum, at any moment liable to plunge to its destruction. 

Strangely enough, man seems to realize the awful peril, yet waits to see what will happen. He appears to be hypnotized by the dangers he is beholding. A few great souls saw the jeopardy and strove to arouse the spirit of the race, ere the crash came. The President of the United States, the Premier of Britain, the Master Mind of China, and a few such leaders, sought to gain control of the machine before the plunge of death. The

agony of such leaders in the face of such indifference and opposition, is now a matter of history. 

The declaration of war on America by Japan, Germany and Italy, cleared the air and gave direction to the energies of the American Nation. Now we go to work, though so far behind because of the appalling lack of understanding on the part of opposition leaders. But note that those opposition leaders who blocked proper preparations for the inevitable conflict, are the first to criticize the 

President, the army and navy, for any failures. "What fools we mortals be!"

What has brought about this dreadful world condition? How was control lost? How shall it be regained?

Always in past history, when the hour of destiny struck, a man has moved to the front as deliverer. 

Will history repeat itself? Will humanity now be saved? Such questions are ever present with us!

How feverish we become as the perils quicken. 

Morning and evening papers bring a rise in our temperatures with a half nervous convulsion. We are afraid! Why? Man is out of adjustment with himself, with his environment, with his fellow-men and with God. Man is adrift. Fog and darkness are thick about him. The roar of breakers on a lee shore tells what destruction awaits him. Who, who can make the winds to cease and the sea to become a calm? Who has power in these awful

times to bring humanity back to the harbor of peace and safety?

It's a long, long story, this story of man's rise, fall, redemption, and struggle forward to his pressent place. His troubles now are largely the result of his mistakes and his sins. Man is what man has made himself to be. Man is the product of his own construction. While that construction is faulty and so much less than it could have been, yet man is entitled to great credit for his achievements. When one considers all that imperfect man has wrought, since those first days of life here, one is awed to silence before the achievements. Man is a thinking, intelligent being in the midst of a vast universe, too vast for him. He cannot plumb the infinite deeps below him, nor can he guage the infinite heights above him. For instance he learns that matter is built out of electrons, so small that were he to make them into bundles of ten billions, it would take more than ten billion bundles to produce a particle of matter large enough to be seen by the strongest microscope. The infinitely small is beyond his conception.

The infinitely Large is even more appalling. 

How vast is his universe! He cannot think out to such distances. He measures the speed of light and finds that it travels at the rate of 182,000

miles per second. He finds that light comes from the sun in eight minutes.

But there are suns in vaster distances. So man must measure the speed of light in terms of years.

The distance light will travel in a year is called a light year. With that new guage man finds that there are suns and stars so far away that it takes the light a billion light years and more to reach this earth. No wonder man cries out in despair, "End is there none of the universe of God?” 

What is man in the midst of such infinity! What value has he amidst such immensity! "Would he have heart to endure for the life of the worm and the fly."

Then, too, man finds himself aware of values which seem of greatest import. He must preserve those values for they are necessary to his living. 

But a conflict is ever arising between those human values and his environment. He would have the best; environment insists on thwarting him. A strange power seems to hold the controlling interests. He searches in vain for that antagonist. He realizes that the force of power is somewhere hidden in the things he uses, yet he cannot locate it or come to grips with it. Not only is there a conflict amid his external surroundings, but more amazing still, he finds a conflict within himself. 

He wants the best, but refuses it. He fights for achievement, but often deserts during the battle.

He finds himself an enemy of himself. When he would gain, he deliberately loses. When he would do right, wrong baffles him. When he would gain the light, thick darkness settles upon him. He breaks the very laws he would uphold. Thwarted, confused, bewildered by antagonisms both within and without, he stands still crying out, "Oh, wretched man that I am who shall deliver me?" 

What is wrong, if wrong there be? Where shall we search? Was Browning false to the facts? Is the trouble with the universe in which we live! 

Science is constantly revealing new wonders of this universe. The microscope delves into the infinitely small. How one is amazed, shocked to silence as he notes the perfectness, beauty and utility of this infinite universe that dwells below the surface of the unaided eye. The structures are so fitted to the requirements of each organism. 

Note the inside braces, the curving lines, the supports. Note the perfect workmanship of each minutest part. Even the repair jobs, when accident has injured the perfect design are of the same delicate and expert pattern. Note the coloring, the blending of light and shadow, now to reveal and now to conceal. The hours go swiftly when one with his microscope, is lost on the streets of a dandelion, held motionless by the beauty of a moss frond, or making acquaintance with native insects that roam at will amid such splendors,

themselves so beautiful as to put to shame Solomon in all his glory.

The telescope, with its close associate the spectroscope, searches amid the infinite spaces, constantly enlarging one's sense of such infinity. 

We see the stars, we measure light distances and construct our language so as to convey the meaning of such distances. So many and vast are the different fields of study, that we have somewhat arbitrarily separated them, giving a name to each field. In doing this we have often done violence to the very truth we seek to reveal. We ask for the facts and truths of the universe as a whole. 

By our names we have succeeded in conveying the idea that some parts are of more value and are more necessary than some other parts; we even fancy that knowledge of one part qualifies a man to speak with authority in behalf of all.

We have named these various fields of study 

Geology, Chemistry, Physics, Botany, and such like names. The specialist in every field of study finds law, harmony, evident purpose, teleological proofs.

Our universe is evidently in harmony with itself, pursuing some plan which it is destined to pursue, each function of great and small assisting and serving every other part, and all for a final perfection which lies beyond the horizon line of our knowledge.

The geologist, noting the formation and breaking up of rock; earthquakes that damage and destroy; sea bottoms thrown to the tops of mountains; vast countries buried beneath the waters; might declare that something is wrong with the universe. The chemist who notes the destructive explosions of gases which tear asunder the habitations of men, might tell of the malignity of the universe. The biologist, discovering the trial and error method of life creation, the weaknesses and sufferings of life from the highest to the lowest forms, might utter his condemnation of the universe. The Theologian, who ever seeks an all-wise and good God, finds so much that seems to destroy his dream, may add his word of fear. But when the sum total of all the findings of all the scientists in all the fields of knowledge are given, the verdict must be rendered that the universe is right. 

Turn to the book of Job, regarded by many scholars as the oldest of all Biblical literature. 

This same question is there battled out with such fire and fury, that the reader is even now overwhelmed at the brilliancy of attack and defense.

Job, the chief character, has bitterly berated God because of disasters he has suffered. He declares that he has been upright and honest in all his ways. It is unjust of God to use His great strength to beat Job down. When all the characters in the drama have had their say, then God

speaks. There is moving majesty in the opening words, that thrill. "Gird up now thy loins like a man; for I will demand of thee and answer thou me." Then there pours forth question after question, each rising, higher in majesty until Job is crushed beneath the weight of his own ignorance and cries out, "I have uttered that I understood not; things too wonderful for me which I knew not. Therefore I abhor myself and repent in dust and ashes." 

But something is wrong! Does the wrong lie with us as human beings. We are strange beings battling strange antinomies. We find ourselves on the side of right, fighting against the right. 

We oppose a wrong with another wrong. Within ourselves the battle is ever raging. Spiritual leaders of every age have suffered in this conflict. 

St. Paul, the great gentile evangelist, one of the brainiest men of the ages and one of the most godly, writes bitterly about this conflict. He says, "When I would do good, evil is present with me.

The good that I would, I do not; but the evil which 

I would not, that I do." History tells of but one man who so mastered himself that all such conflict was banished from his heart. Jesus of Nazareth told His group of personal friends and followers that He was never troubled by this conflict. 

The evil of life found nothing temptable in Him. 

How such a word makes one cry out in agony for

a mastery like that. All realize that something is wrong with man. The question is, does that something cause all the suffering, blood-shed and horror of today, and has it been the cause of such in the long past.

We have need to go back to the creation stories as told in the Bible and in the books of science, for a foundation of belief and hope. There are two chief theories of man's creation. The one is a very literal, word-bound conception of the story as told in the first two chapters of the book of Genesis. 

We should be perfectly honest with ourselves when we come to these stories. We know they are not new stories, here written for the first time, but that they are a compilation of stories told for four thousand years preceding the life of the one or ones who wrote the book of Genesis. The growth and evolution of those Creation stories is an immortal monument to the hunger of the human spirit. Books are available today that give the enchanting history of that long, long search. 

The story of the book of Genesis is as follows: 

When God had created the universe and brought our earth to the perfection necessary for the support of human beings, He proceeded to create such beings. He took of the clay of the ground, shaped it into the fashion of a man, breathed into that form the breath of life and thus made a living, human being, called man. The story makes no

mention of the length of time necessary to perfect such a being, save as it records periods of work, as "And it was darkness and it was light, period one" or two, or three, and so on to period seven. The stories then proceed to tell that God created a woman as companion for man and to be the Mother of all living, as man was to be the Father of all such life. To the man and the woman 

God gave the free, full use of everything He had created, save only the fruit on one tree in their garden home.

The theory read into this story affirms that man and woman were created perfect physically, morally, spiritually. Such theory does not take into account the fact that imperfection was instantly revealed in disobedience to God. Had they been absolutely perfect, then could there have been no such thing as temptation to disobey God and so fall from their purity. Only Jesus of Nazareth could say, "Nothing temptable in me." 

But our Genesis theory declares that by disobeying God, man and woman fell into sin; fell from the heights of perfection. It was a long, long fall. 

Milton's description of Satan falling from heaven to lowest perdition, does not even faintly picture the depths of that fall from the garden of Eden.

The rush of the imagination burns it out like a meteor ere it reaches the earth. Think of man and woman perfect in every attribute! Think of a

state of being in which there was, nor could be sin! 

Fancy a life in all the freedom of God-likeness!

The fancy is beyond our conceiving powers. But as we meditate, we seem to miss some vital element from the dream picture. Stress and strain and struggle are not there and hence no stimulant to achievement, no self discipline in conflict, no overcoming by force of acquired will. There could be no real men and women under such circumstances. There might be angels, God-like beings in captivity to such goodness; but there could be no developed character, no sense of achievement, no encomium of "well done."

Man fell, and far down that awful precipice he came to the realization of what he had lost. Let 

Milton describe that waking: "Some natural tears they dropped, but wiped too soon.

The world was all before them, where to choose Their place of rest, and Providence their guide; 

They, hand in hand, with wandering step and slow,

Through Eden took their solitary way." 

What a long, long way that has been. What a long, long climb mankind has battled. Just a little way up the cliff has he come, this strange man. 

Sometimes the heights above are lost in earth

clouds that gather; sometimes the heights seem to yearn down, and in reaching, approach nearer to man's position. But man is climbing, climbing up. Though the way be long and steep and though his faith falter, yet a something within urges him on. What man's destiny is we may not now understand, but we know from the divine urge prompting him and the fleeting visions that now and again flash upon his spiritual sight, that he is bound for a higher destiny than earth. 

The second theory of the origin of man, is that of life reaching up to greater perfection as it moves forward into higher stages of existence. 

The process may be likened to the waters of a spring issuing from the foot of a hill and finding their way down the valley, through the gorges, across the lakes, hurrying on to the great ocean, their final home. There will be delays along that difficult course. Some of the waters will be impounded in bogs, will loose themselves in sand, will dry up by the way; but the spirit of the current seeks the sea and ere long will find it. This theory of life development is called Evolution. In that life first appearing on the earth was something demanding a greater perfection in form, beauty and expression. The colloids on the stagnant pools were a low expression. The lichens were a step upward. The mosses, ferns and flowers, successively reached to higher levers. Ere long, after

eons of struggle, life gave a new exhibition in the form of man. At first that form of man was crude. 

Of necessity it must be. Science traces that growing perfection. It begins with the first known revelation of the human. It names the first known sub-human the pithecanthropus erectus, that is, the ape-like man. A second step higher is the 

Neanderthal man. Then comes another development called the Piltdown man. So, step by step, as anthropology can trace and follow the development, it leads up to man as now we know him. 

What a long, long road that has been. What a weary climb man has made. Up from the depths has he come, but only a little way up. He is yet on the lower slopes of the great cliff. He has a long, long climb still before him. What he is today is so far below his potentialities that by comparison he seems sub-human even yet. 

A careful survey of the two theories reveals that whichever is adopted, the results are the same. Man fell and is climbing up; man began low down and is climbing up. The significant thing is, man is climbing. "Becoming," is one of the greatest words in Psychology.

Hence when we ask what is wrong with man, we discover a created being with a record reaching back into the penumbra of history, ever climbing out of his lesser or lower self into a higher and nobler self. There is then a reason for his mis-

takes, his blunders, his sins. In that fact centers all of his past wrongs. In the fact that he has always been climbing, centers his hopes for a perfect fruition, when he shall have reached the goal of his faint, far dream.

There is yet a third consideration to which much thought must be given, ere we approach any solution of the problem of what is wrong with man. 

There appears to be a maladjustment between man and his universe. Sometimes man and his universe seem at war. At all times there are conflicts. Man's will and nature's will do clash. But nature's will is always regnant. Man fights but is forced to yield to a vastly superior will. By nature's will is meant the laws that are inherent in every act of nature. Nature exists in law. 

There is law in that which at first is chaos. The molecules and atoms of a young, white-hot star, clashing, rebounding, whirling in vast confusion, produces that primordial energy which furnishes the motive power for the universe. The one great lesson of science is the lesson of a law abiding universe. Whence came these laws, is a question that carries us far beyond our present enquiry. 

They could not be self imposed. They could not proceed from lawlessness. In fact, there could be no law without a lawgiver. Just now we are noting that these laws are always master. If man

breaks them he suffers; if he puts his will and action in harmony with them he prospers.

Then we ask if there be a fundamental antagonism between man and his environment. Has this creation, this process of development, so blundered that nature demands one mode of development and man demands another? If this be true, then no possible basis of harmony is to be discovered. 

Forever must there be war between man and his environment. But to reach such conclusion is to make of all living a hell and all material development a meaningless mockery.

Before arriving at such a conclusion we need to examine most critically both the fundamental desires of man and those of nature to ascertain if the conflicts are real or seeming; to discover whether or not needless conflicts occur and whether all such conflicts are not due to man's ignorance of his universe and also to his fundamental insistence on his freedom of choice, even if the results of such choice bring him conflict and pain. If the highly suggestive story in Genesis means to convey the idea that determination to know, was the cause of man's downfall, then we have a key with which to unlock many mysteries. Man is fundamentally inquisitive. We want to know why certain things are as they are. The history of philosophy is the history of curiosity. Man will seek and find and know, or spend a lifetime in the search.

Olive Shreiner tells the story of a man who abandoned every other mode of living that he might find one feather of the wing of truth. He began to climb up the cliffs of knowledge when a young man. He meets and battles successfully every temptation to abandon his quest. We find him at last an old man, dying on the cold heights of small achievement, rejoicing that he has cut a few steps in the cliffs up which others shall come, eager students of future years, eager to climb on the knowledge he has gained-up to the heights still looming above.

Man will know. His clashes with his environment are the disclosures of his ignorance. But in his self-esteem he fancies that his will of life is superior to that of nature. So he will battle nature and defeat her. The source of man's conflicts is his battling will. "Ist Verboten," is a challenge to him for conflict. Who or what power has the right to forbid his doing as he wishes?

Hence his endless conflicts, unless he come at last to accept the dictum of Dante and find that, "In His will is our peace." So here is man this strange being, the product of forces greater than he, yet influenced by him and made to yield him strength or weakness according as he cooperates with or fights against the laws controlling him. Here he is with all his questions, fears, blunders, mysteries; here is a universe which appears now hos-

tile now friendly, but at no time to be ignored. His life depends upon his obedience to the very laws which so often appear as his enemy. Being of that inquisitive nature he turns to every source of knowledge. The discovery, the allotment and the naming of each field of knowledge, is one of the supreme achievements of man. Often great specialists in some one of these fields, have become so absorbed in their particular studies that they have lost the conception of the field as a whole. A great specialist must of necessity be a narrowminded man, imprisoned in his own concentration camp, now and again smuggling out, through books and magazines, opinions which, partly true in his own field, are even false in another field. Mr. 

Edison, a master in his field, gave out opinions regarding philosophy and religion which are false to the ascertained facts in those fields. Mr. Einstein's opinions of the Christian Religion are as worthless as John Doe's opinions of relativity.

The greater the specialist in one field of knowledge the greater his danger in all other fields. Darwin spent his working years in discovering the law of Evolution. But in doing so he lost all sense of music, harmony, beauty. Of what value were his opinions in those fields?

But man turns to every source of knowledge for his discovery of self. Philosophy the king of the sciences, opens his doors to those qualified to enter.

Crude were the first buildings, but eager students crowded in. The early peoples fancied explanations for a multitude of phenomena. The Sumarian peoples, the Brown peoples, the historic nations, all builded truth upon truth as each was discovered. The history of philosophy is one of the most fascinating of all histories. Persia,

Egypt, Greece, Rome and the later nations have made their contributions to that history. A burst of light never to dim was granted to René Descartes, when he discovered a new formula for thought. He said, "I observed that while I was resolved to feign that everything was false, I who must of necessity be somewhat; and remarking this truth, I think therefore I am-was so firm and so assured that all the most extravagant suppositions of the sceptics were unable to shake it. 

I judged that I could unhesitatingly accept it as the first principle of philosophy I was seeking." 99 This man found his identity in the midst of his material environment. Finding himself thus, he seeks for an interpretation of the phenomena he observes about him. The discovery of such phenomena becomes the field of science. Science is the assembling of facts and the arrangement of them in the order of their importance. A scientist is a seeker of facts in the realm of material phenomena. A true scientist is a man of proven facts. In his search for such facts he must ever

employ what is called "Operational Concepts."

He assumes that certain facts point to certain conclusions, but he abstains from affirming his assertions until he has an authenticated body of facts sufficient to warrant a final conclusion. The more the scientist extends his investigations the more conscious he becomes of universal laws that govern phenomena. These are the laws of physics, chemistry, biology and indeed every science. Ere long the scientist comes to affirm that man lives in a law controlled, law abiding universe. 

But whence came these laws? This question calls for another field of knowledge named Theology. If philosophy be the king of the sciences, certainly Theology is the Queen. The name Evah, given to woman in the Genesis story, means, in the 

Hebrew language, "The mother of all living."

Theology is to all the sciences what Evah is to all life. Theology attempts to reveal the source of those laws discovered by the scientists in the material universe. It attempts to reveal the source of that "I Am," who thinks, loves, aspires, and reaches for perfection beyond present achievement. Therefore Theology is the science of the knowledge of God. 

But Who or what is God? Four chief answers may be given. God is a Spirit, a Personality, a 

Soul, a Living, Intelligent Will. But all these are invisible. No one has ever seen them; yet they are

entities. No one ever saw the spirit of his mother, yet that spirit was always present. No one ever saw you; that YOU who lives within your body.

But you are there and know yourself in your communions. Then God is not more invisible than are we. Tennyson said, "Spirit with Spirit can meet." We know that to be true from our own personal experiences.

Materialism so much lauded a few years ago, died a timely death and few there were to mourn its departure. Science killed the doctrine when it discovered that those particles of electricity leaping about the nuclei of the atom were out of all control. They are as chaotic as the molecules of a young, white-hot star. Of course if there is no law in the atoms which make up the very basis of matter, there can be no self-regulation in that matter itself. At once it was seen that law and order could not come from lawlessness. With that discovery Materialism gasped out its last breath.

Reason had long known the fallacy of materialism, but the scientific proof ended all discussion. There are yet a few belated writers who seem not to know these facts. They range with the "Flat Earth" trailers of thought.

If God is a spirit and we are Spirit, and we are a Creation, then God must be our Creator. God is the Father of our spirits. Wherever spirits meet there God is. A close contact is possible

between my spirit and the spirit of God. In that contact come those spirit revelations which are the guiding, motivating power of life at one with itself, because it is at one with God. When an individual spirit is fighting the Spirit of God, then all harmony disappears. Cross purposes produce discords, rebellion, war and death. Hence when we ask what is wrong with man, the answer must include all those spirit relations existing between ourselves and other individuals, and also between ourselves and our Creator, God. We must come to understand that hostile relations between us and God are the primordial substance of all conflicts, all human disasters, all wars. The wars of today are the result of war between the human spirit and the Spirit of God. Out of harmony with 

God, we are out of harmony with man. Distrusts breed antagonisms, fruiting out into battle, bloodshed and death.

CHAPTER II  

From a train stepped a passenger and going up to a group of people standing there he said: "Will someone please tell me who I am?" Dread amnesia had imprisoned him. He was a stranger both to himself and to the group he addressed. 

In a very real sense man is asking, who am I?

Here is a being with remarkable powers to think, to plan, to execute. Dimly aware that he is meant to be the interpreter of his universe, he realizes how pitifully lacking he is in those qualities which he should possess. Aware of his limitations of sight, he has built him instruments that reveal the avenues across the infinite spaces above. Conscious of his dull ears, he has made instruments to tell him the heart beats of light. To make known to others his thinking, his visions, his beliefs, he has invented symbols of thought called words. By such words he conveys to others his visions and dreams. What a treasure house he has in words! "Words are things, and a small drop of ink 

Falling like dew upon a thought, May produce that which makes thousands, Perhaps millions think." 40

There are words like Damascus blades; words that crepitate and shock like a wildest storm; words as soft as summer showers and as full of love as the twilight hour; words as restful as old paintings; words that sing and sob and pray. Man and his words is the gigantic power of the ages. 

He speaks and around the world millions of people are moved and moulded by those words. The highest form of art is the art of speech. The orator sweeping through the gates of eloquence, reaches the highest realms of beauty and emotion -realms unknown to the common mind. 

Man gives wings to his words and like birds, they sing and fly. Melody is the catalysis of all being. Music is the combining, the resolving, the explaining element of existence. It is more than a fancy when poets have declared that the world was built to music. Browning's poem, Abt Vogler, is history as well as art. By music one rises to that height where walk and commune spirits of mortal and immortal life. "Nature in turn conceived, obeying an impulse as I; 

And the emulous heavens yearned down, made effort to reach the earth 

As the earth had done her best, in my passion to reach the sky;

Novel splendors burst forth, grew familiar and dwelt with mine,

Not a point nor peak but found, but fixed its wandering star; 

Meteor moons, balls of blaze; and they did not pale nor pine,

For earth had attained to heaven, there was no more near nor far." 

Poetry is music at anchor; singing is music under sail. The orator and oratorio singer are God's best and last gifts. If man can cause his words to sing and build with them heavenly mansions, he also can cause his words to curse and blood soak a continent. Demagogues and dictators know this well. Some of the language used to call a nation to arms is among the most noted of literary hoardings. Words and music have in them the powers of darkness and light; the challenges of heaven and hell.

Thus we find man so endowed with powers and equipped with instruments of service that he may become a saint or a fiend, a god or a devil. No wonder the Russian poet Derzhaven cries out in amazement: "What am I then?? 

Naught! but I live and on hope's pinions fly 

Eager toward thy presence; for in Thee

I live and breathe and dwell, aspiring

Even to the throne of Thy divinity. 

I am, O God, and surely Thou must be! 

Thou art directing guiding all—Thou art!

Direct my understanding, then, to Thee. 

Though but an atom midst immensity, Still I am something fashioned by Thy hand: 

I can command the lightning and am dust! 

A monarch and a slave-a worm, a God."

We are prisoners in our world of freedom. We are moulded by powers we cannot controlmoulded whether we will or no. We are governed by laws not of our making and by rulers we did not elect. We are pushed and pulled by a destiny against which we have no voice. We are but things in the hands of fate, guided hither and thither as it wills. But are these fates, these powers, hostile to us or friendly? Are these battlings meant to destroy us or to make us live? Are these disharmonies, these antagonisms, seeming or real? Is man the master and all these vaguely hostile forces but "machinery just meant to give thy soul its bent; try thee and send thee forth sufficiently impressed?"

Such questions call for a discussion of the nature of the universe, its evident purpose, its trustworthiness and its means of help or hindrance.

Ever since man began to think, his wonder regarding the universe has challenged him. What is this ever-present something called nature? Is it the source or essence of life, the power which produces existing phenomena of every variety?

Dictionary definitions are delightful in the harmonic measure of their obscurantisms. These definitions hide the very explanations we seek. They, like so many pseudo-scientists, build word bridges over the chasms of their ignorance. Take one such definition of Nature: "That which is the source and essence of life." Could there be a greater confusion? Nature possesses no powers, no life in and of itself. Nature is not a force guided and controlled by its own self-generated laws. Nature is a word we use to name an agency which carries on creative and other processes of being, without any creating or ordering intelligence of its own. Nature has no intelligence, no will, no purpose. All such statements to the contrary are but confusions of a misguided thinker. Nature is but an agent of intelligence. Before we can discuss the nature of the universe with any degree of intelligence, we must banish from our thinking the strange misconceptions foisted upon us by those who have sought to prove the non-existence of a creative, supervising intelligence, working through the given forms and processes we call nature.

With a word we endow the abstract, lifeless thing

we call nature, with creative and directional powers and then use the word as though it possessed the powers assigned to it by human speech. 

We personify an abstraction and then use it as though it were alive. 

But critics cry out in alarm! What an absurdity to say that nature has no life! Why, nature is the mother of all living! We repeat, nature has no life: nature is the mother of nothing. But that something we call nature and personally endow with directing intelligence, has life and has it more abundantly. Not nature as a thing in itself, but nature as an agent of intelligence, conveys life, sustains life, is the mother and father of all living. 

Such troubles come from our word bridges. They often deceive the very elect.

When we ask after the nature of the universe we are really asking after that Intelligence which works through and directs the universe. We ask if that intelligence is of sufficient scope to manage such a universe and if fixed purpose and definite aims can be traced in all the workings of nature. 

Janet, the great French thinker, wrote a book some hundred years ago, and it will be authoritative for some hundreds of years to come. title of the book is, "Final Causes." In that epochal work the author relentlessly traces the evidences of aim and purpose through all the unfolding of nature. He tells that the delicacy, the precision The

in the structure of the eye, for example, could never have resulted from the blind gropings of "fortuitous circumstances," as Herbert Spencer would say. To believe such a statement as that the eye is but the hit or miss of fate, is to stretch credulity to the vanishing point. Janet takes up one by one the various organs of the body and the different manifestations of life, showing how impossible it would be for a mere blind, meaningless, groping nothingness, to produce such marvels of intelligence as to cause specialists in engineering, in medicine, in surgery, in chemistry and indeed in all the sciences, to be still in the presence of such plan, such power, and such production as they behold. The Ontologist searches the very nature of being to discover, if possible, what kind of being can subsist in and of itself. He even enters the human mind to discover what sort of being would be required to satisfy intelligence. 

We might review those arguments seeking to prove the existence of intelligence in nature. 

There is cogent reasoning, irrefutable proof, logical conclusion, marking out the thought-road along which fact and faith have walked in their search. It is a high road or a system of high roads leading, like the old Roman roads, from every corner of the empire of thought to the capital city of the mind where dwell those senators of intelligence who direct the ever growing, ever expand-

ing realm of constructive belief. Said Spinoza "God is absolutely the first cause. He acts from the laws of His own nature and is compelled by no one. He is a free cause. The Divine intellect is the cause of things, both of essence and existence. The sum of the matter is that God necessarily exists; that He is one God; that He acts from the necessity of His nature; that He is a free cause of all things; that all things depend on Him, and that all things have been predestined by Him." 

Said Descartes: "I bethought me to enquire whence I had learned to think of something more perfect than myself; and it was clear to me that this must come from some nature which was in fact more perfect-I could not derive it from myself. It remained then to conclude that it was put into me by a nature more perfect than was I and possessing in itself all the perfections of which I could form an idea-in a word, by God." 

We need to remember that it is only faith that builds. Doubt may lead to constructive effort, indeed has often done so, yet doubt in itself is ever a destructive agent. Doubt never built a bank, a railroad, a ship or a nation. Faith has ever been the constructive agent. Faith must have its operational concepts, often using the trial and error method of achieving; but it is never in doubt. Faith is the fact of the thing we hope for;

it is the proof of the thing we seek to construct. 

But what is the purpose of this universe? The old and persistent belief is that the universe was constructed simply and solely as a proving ground for man. By difficulties, dangers, struggles and achievements, man would be moulded into that perfect spirit fit to enjoy the eternal realms of the blessed. In short the common belief persists that 

God built this universe to give man a place in which to prove himself divine. Such a belief opens vast fields for discussion. We may indicate those fields by a series of questions. Why was it necessary for such eons of time to pass ere man appeared? Science estimates that time in millions, perhaps billions of years. It estimates that man has been here no longer than seventy thousand years. Why did life develop on this little planet alone, if such is a fact, and not on older and greater planets? Can it be that our little earth, unseen from distances far nearer than the sun, is the only planet in all this vast galaxy of systems, which was found capable of sustaining the kind of life we call human life? Can it be that other planets have developed other systems of life fitted to their environment? Can it be that certain planets have developed certain forms of beings; permitted them to evolve to certain perfections and then, tiring of the experiment, wrecked themselves to become star dust out of which

other planets may be constructed? Are meteors the wreckage of other worlds? Is such a process now going on in this little planet of ours? From the blood and slaughter of this present day one might be led to believe that our experiment has been a failure and that our race was plunging forward to its doom. "Sees the gleam of their white, sad faces."

Whether or not the universe was created for the sole purpose of man, the fact is man is here and must use his universe as a means to develop his body, mind and spirit. If the evident purpose of the universe is to be helpful, and not "red in tooth and claw," then should man come into harmony with her laws and use them as aids to his noblest achievement.

But there are conflicting laws. There are laws of thought that prove one thing, and then by other laws prove the opposite. For instance the great philosopher Kant tells of some of those conflicts. He calls them "antinomies." They are conflicts indeed that seem to bring our thinking to a standstill. We look in vain for a way out of the dilemma. Kant gives four of these conflicts or antinomies. We use one as an example. He says:

The world has a beginning in time and is also limited in regard to space. Were the world without a time beginning we should have to ascribe a

present limit to that which can have no limit, which is absurd.

Antithesis: The world has neither beginning in time, nor limit in space, but in both regards is infinite. It must have existed from eternity, or it could never have existed at all. If we imagine it had a beginning we must imagine an anterior time when nothing was. But in such time the origin of anything is impossible. At no moment could any cause for such a beginning exist. 

It would be so interesting to quote each of these contradictions and their bearing on life. Did we take them as irrefutable, then would we be shut up to slavery in thinking and find no road to freedom. 

But are the antinomies the final result of our thinking? Do we stop with irrefutable proof? So much of our thinking is syllogistic; conclusions necessarily follow from premises. If the premises be true the conclusions must necessarily be true. 

That kind of reasoning is much like portions of river water impounded in a swamp, there to stagnate and breed disease; but the river is open still and hurries on to the sea.

Our Our mental difficulties are self-made. antinomies are self-manufactured in our own mental workshop. This is true also of our difficulties in every sphere of existence. Were we honestly to think through our present world difficulties we should find that they are of our own making. We

are not dumb driven cattle but free agents exercising our right of free will within the realm of personality. We are not in an impersonal universe, of impersonal law, impersonally administered. Omar Khayyam would have us believe that- "We are no other than a moving row 

Of magic shadow-shapes that come and go 

Round with the sun-illumined lantern held

At midnight by the master of the show. 

But helpless pieces of the game he plays

Upon this checker-board of nights and days; 

Hither and thither moves and checks and slays, And one by one back in the closet lays."

All such fatalism is of a diseased mind. Man is the measure for his universe. Man becomes cognizant of the laws of the universe, uses those laws to build himself into the optimum of his potentialities. It is man's follies, mistakes, sins if you please, that produce our present condition. The universe is not responsible for them: God is not responsible for them. The cry so often heard that there cannot be a God else He would stop this awful war, is another of those thoughtless, empty expressions issuing from vacuity. 

The worst crime that could be committed against

man would be the stopping of this war by God. If 

He should shield man from his follies, mistakes, sins and weaknesses, then would man adopt any reckless ways of living, relying on God to protect him. Human freedom would be gone and man would descend to the level of brute creation. This war and all such disasters are man made, and man must care for them. He must accept the results of his acts, good or bad, and profit by the experience. The present conditions are man made. Man must unmake or remake them. He chooses the conditions under which he will live, and he must take the responsibility for those conditions. 

Our age is the product of a technological development, unplanned, unsuited to our moral and spiritual development. Science opens vast fields of new endeavor. To find the laws in chemistry, physics and such sciences, use them in new combinations and produce invention after invention, has been the characteristic of the last century. 

Most if not all of such inventions and discoveries have been substitutes for man's personal endeavor. It is a dictum of the doctors that walking is necessary for health. But the automobile, the telephone and scores of such inventions do away with the need of walking. Therefore legs are becoming atrophied. Conscription for the war has shown that men cannot walk. They have lost the art. Fortunately modern war machinery is

doing away with the need of marching. Modern soldiers march on wheels.

The old necessity of waking early in the morning, dressing in a cold room and running downstairs to get the fires going, the water hot for the household and the house comfortable for breakfast, is now long past for modern man. But that passing has robbed man of such self-discipline, and the exercise of the will power required. Any removal of self-discipline is irreparable. Discipline is good for any man when circumstances force him to the optimum of his endeavors. 

Luxury has ever been the cause of decadence. "Yet a little slumber, a little folding of hands to sleep; so shall thy poverty come upon thee as one that travaileth, and thy want as an armed man.”

The flood of labor saving devices has inundated us. No longer do we need to wind the clock or slice the bread or even open the door. Harnessed electricity does it all for us. A push of the button and light flashes. A turn of the faucet and water gushes. A twist of the dial and music, sermon, prayer, lecture, drama, story and wanton foolishness are heard by us from every corner of the globe. There is no more near nor far. Moscow or Tokyo or London or San Francisco is equally near. The modern aeroplane is making it possible for us to dine in New York, breakfast next morning in London and sleep that same night in Cairo.

It would seem that such time-savers, such laborsavers would release man to wider fields of attainment. We would naturally expect a vast increase of human happiness together with a mighty upsurge of brotherhood. Released from such a multitude of physical burdens, surely man must increase productivity in human values, in agencies of good will, and in everything that ministers to peace and plenty. 

But such is not the case. Release from physical burdens has weakened the moral will of man. He has become flabby. His firmness has departed. 

He has lost his strength in physical ease. The reasons for this are only too apparent. Our standards of measurements have been changed from the qualitative to the quantitative. We now measure by size, weight, numbers, popular acclaim. Where before we measured by moral and spiritual worth, personal achievement and individual mastery, we now measure by things. 

Where formerly we measured by attributes, we now measure by bulk. This disease is very prevalent at this present time. The biggest, the first, the greatest, even the standing on depressed work of others, aye, even pulling others down to make one's self seem bigger-all this is the grim horror of these latter days.

So we are beholding our own moral and spiritual decay. Never before was it given to an age

to see so clearly its own descent to destruction. 

But is it destruction? Is the human race decaying? Are we bound for final disaster and together with the planet upon which we dwell, explode into nothingness and utter oblivion? Many thinkers declare that our race is deteriorating.

They base their opinions upon the deterioration observable today. They affirm that the intellectual life of the people is far below that of former days. They look to the low place of statesmanship, the rotting public life of those who pose as leaders of human society, the slaughter of this awful time, and are sure that we are growing weaker. Look at this world war and its mass murders! We are viewing mass suicide. Every nation is pouring out of its best and noblest into the caldron where brew death's decrees. Death is the master today. Death is the governor of the age. The sense of moral values is gone. Germany publicly states that the duty of every girl and woman, irrespective of the marriage vow, is to breed children for the state. That government further declares that only German children are legitimate. Hatred against all others is the natural and necessary outcome of such teaching. The 

German leaders themselves have lost all sense of moral values. Said Hitler: "I am willing to sign anything. I will do anything to facilitate the success of my policy. I shall make any treaty I

require. It will never prevent me from doing at any time what I regard as necessary for Germany's interests." So a world war is on to settle once and for all time that proposition of morals or no morals in government and life. War curses not only those who make it but also those who oppose it. If soldiers must die, let them eat, drink and be merry now. Morals are for peace times only, and not for war. There shall be no curtailment of desires. Do as we will for tomorrow we are gone. That girl, unmarried, who frankly confessed her glory in having in her arms an unwounded soldier, unmaimed and whole, was revealing the awful moral disaster overtaking millions of people in warring nations. 

But may there not be another view of the picture? May not more light bring out some hidden features, overcoming the hidden horrors? Is war all bad? Can any good come out of Nazareth?

A war of national aggression for the sole purpose of making that nation the dominant power of the world, is a crime against humanity, a disaster to itself, a prelude to its own oblivion. Germany is crucifying herself. Germany furnishes the reasons for her own destruction. The deeds of German soldiers and their leaders are unthinkable in their horrors. "Dragons of the prime that tear each other in their slime are mellow music," matched to the horrors of Poland, Czechoslovakia

and France. One shudders at the brutality revealed, outbrutalizing the wild beasts insane with hunger. O Germany, why will you destroy yourself; why will you go down to the pit of hell, unhonored and cursed by all the generations to come!

The German nation is deteriorating. The Ger man people are rotting out. The curse of both 

God and man are upon them. What a spectacle is this horror and death! And Japan is doing the same. Great possibilities were hers! She could have become one of the great forces for world betterment. Now she forfeits the confidence and esteem of all those who looked to her for real help in making a better world. But she has proven herself unworthy of any future respect or confidence. Japan will be wiped out. Her name will become a byword for treachery and deceit. She will always be "yellow." She can have no place in the family of nations. She has put herself beyond the reach of cultured brotherhood. She must be treated as a decadent and unprofitable people. 

What a curse!

But are those nations deteriorating which oppose themselves to such inhuman philosophy as that of Germany and Japan? Any nation and any individual will deteriorate that permits slavery without a struggle. There are some things worth dying for. One of those things is human free-

dom." "Tis man's perdition to be safe, when for the truth he ought to die." The battle for freedom is the most inspiring record made by man. The battle for freedom has produced the moral stimuli needed to save mankind. Those nations opposing the inhuman philosophy of the Jap and German leaders, find their noblest qualities of soul becoming regnant. The common soldier becomes a hero, and the man of former lack a giant. Old weaknesses, the results of unused energies, are left behind and social barriers are destroyed. The finest example of national regeneration ever known in history is now taking place in England. 

Hundreds of thousands of her sons and daughters are dying, and gladly that their children and their children's children may live in freedom. While we do not overlook the destruction of war, yet we see the long futures and the glory to be, "When the war drums sound no longer and the battle flags are furled." Certainly the English branch of the human family is not deteriorating now, but rather is growing in moral and spiritual stature. 

A recent book on Australia shows the deterioration of the native races in contact with the early white settlers. It also shows the deterioration of the white race as well; the one could not go down alone. If you keep a man in the mud, you must stay there with him and thus grow more muddy yourself. That book does not prove that the whole

race is deteriorating. It does show the vile effects of broken moral law on each race reviewed. 

In scores of such cases history records degeneracy of contact races, the powerful overcoming the weak, only themselves at last to succumb to the very weaknesses they had generated in others. But no book or series of books could hope to show that mankind as a whole is weaker today physically, mentally, or morally than at any former period in history. We may point out individual instances and nations even. That remarkable upsurge of genius in Greece during the third and second centuries B.C. is a case in point. Such a blossoming out of perfection in the fine arts, in government and in religion, is yet a marvel and mystery to all. Such a period, however, represents but an intellectual volcano, a tidal wave of genius, a dying earthquake of intellectuality.

Greece was a precocious child in the human family. All her years have not been as those glorious years. It is true that her magnificent stand in this war even unto death that lingers, reveals the old traits of that great age long past, and once more gives her standing in Valhalla's Halls.

All such references are but as individual achievements for brief periods. They tell not the story of humanity as a whole. When one turns to human conditions and achievements under 

Persia's early rule, or Egypt's might, or Moslem

power, or even down to Philip II and the Spanish inquisition with all the treacheries and horrors of the Duke of Alva, one sees clearly the steady advance of mankind as a whole. And when, with fearless eye, one looks out upon his world of this day beholding the regenerative forces at work even in the horror of this world war, he is compelled to declare that the forces which are for man are more than they which are against him.

CHAPTER III  

Omar, son of Ibrahim the tent maker, facing up to the whole question of life and its mysteries, cries out: 

Ah Love! could you and I with him conspire

To grasp this sorry scheme of things entire Would we not shatter it to bits-and then 

Remould it nearer to the heart's desire!

In many forms and ways this wish has been expressed, and across many centuries. Just what one would do to remould the world and these human beings, has never been published. If one has ever brought himself face to face with the problem, the difficulties have doubtless proven so overwhelming that such a man has ceased his repining and wandered out into the silences. The following question was put to a number of men, outstanding leaders in their profession: "Were you required to fashion an adult human body and give it life, what would you add to or take from 61

this present human body?" The answers are both a self and a racial revelation. The physician said, not one change! "Not one?" "No, sir, not one."

Then he added, "When I think of the abuse of this body, how men use it, what it endures, how it compensates and assists each affected organ, how it is constantly making itself immune to disease, I am more and more amazed at its perfectness. 

No, sir, not one subtraction or addition. I would like to examine the mind of that person who would otherwise speak." 99 Said the educator, thinking of the boys and girls who had come under his observation and care, "Would that something were done to prevent from being born, deformed children.' But the law is children with perfect bodies! Who breaks the law and how, that deformity follows? But did creation make impossible any deformity, how soon would mankind abuse such a blessing to the extent that even now in our laxity we have not dreamed. Carelessness and criminality rule sufficiently now in procreation. What would follow if all responsibility were taken from parents? 

Real childhood begins long before birth. Real childhood needs long preparation of parents. In nothing does mankind show its ignorance, its carelessness, its sins, as in conception, growth and birth of children. 

Said the great surgeon, "I would be presumptu-

ous, would I not, in suggesting any possible eliminations or additions?" He went on to speak of the human body as such a marvelous machine. 

Few people realize its wonderful complexity. We know the anatomy of the body. But we know almost nothing of those strange and remarkable coordinations. He spoke of the complexity of brain functions recommending a book entitled, "The biography of the human brain." He expressed his belief in a Something, a Power, a Source of being, shaping the final destiny of man. 

That led him to speak of the time element; the lengthening of human life. He half wished for more time of human activity. Then he added, "Yet I doubt if I would ask for more time.' 99 

The psychiatrist said: "From the neurological standpoint, man would be considerably benefited if the brain and spinal cord tissue had the ability to regenerate, once injured or diseased. The way it now is, brain and spinal cord tissue cannot regenerate, and once pathologically involved, it is gone forever and cannot be replaced except by a functionally useless scar. From the psychiatric standpoint, less selfishness should make for a nicer human race and a better world, although this point might be somewhat debatable.'

No true scientist frets against the construction of the physical universe. The wish is for a more thorough knowledge and understanding of it. The

scientist is allured into the halls of the unknown, ever seeking by the light of his reason to gain more knowledge of the laws governing every phase of physical development. Such men have turned the pages of the living rocks to read the story of those unknown ages long, long past. 

As they read they discover new letters of a new alphabet, and spend lifetime after lifetime learning that new language only to discover yet newer letters and stranger words. The geologist is one of the greatest of all novelists. The chemist arranges his formulae and produces results which have given humanity a new world. 

Two parts of hydrogen mixed with one part of oxygen (H2O) produces water. What would one part of hydrogen and two parts of oxygen (HO₂) produce? So the chemist has experimented with his formulae, discovering new chemicals that produce life and others that produce death. What formula produced T.N.T.? What formulae are producing those newer and more terrible explosives now dropping from British bombers? The science of chemistry is a limitless field of investigation. The great Marconi once expressed a fear that man might stumble upon that exact combination of forces which would blow the world to atoms. The same infinite reach stretches out before every science. Until man has mastered all knowledge to be obtained from the study of the

universe, he is not in a position to declare how it can be improved. Therefore any person expressing his dissatisfaction with our physical universe is but telling of his own ignorance. 

The need of remaking man seems to come nearer to our abilities. We feel sure we know ourselves so well we could suggest improvements that would greatly add to our more effective use of life. We have so many handicaps. We have so many limitations. We have so little wisdom and judgment. We are so often slaves when we should be masters. We catch visions of nobler things to do, yet permit those visions to fade out and make little or no attempt to capture them in material or spiritual forms. We feel sure we could remake man so as to overcome all these handicaps. 

But do we understand ourselves better than we understand our universe? There are scientific fields of study in man's physical, mental and spiritual constitution that outreach all studies in the mere physical world. Man is the most unknown of all the subjects to which he gives his attention. Man the unknown moves amid the mysteries of life, the greatest mystery of all. 

Dr. Carrel tells us that matter is now being investigated in our laboratories "at every level of its organization, of its ascension toward the making of man. 99

Much knowledge has been gained, but only to disclose vaster fields of the unknown.

There are as many "operational concepts" to be considered regarding the creation of man as there are regarding the creation of the world, and all of them probably equidistant from the final truth. Are we then in a better position to remake man than to remake the universe?

Man's physical body is constructed from a soft material, ever changing, and under conditions disintegrating within a few hours. Yet make him of steel and he would be destroyed before he could really live. Suppose the foot were of stone. How could it withstand the shock of constantly smiting the ground? Suppose the hand were of iron. 

How soon would it be broken to pieces? And what of man's size? Does that need to be changed? He is very small when compared to an elephant. He is very large when compared to an ant. His average height is five feet six inches. He has accustomed himself to that height, yet makes provision for an extreme height. He frames his door six feet nine inches high and two feet six inches wide.

A few people must turn sidewise to go through the doors and a few must bend their heads. The average height seems best fitted for man's work in every field of endeavor.

Time is a great factor in man's existence. Yet time is so brief for him. Could we not improve on this limitation of three score years and ten, or if by reason of strength, four score years? The

fact is, the Leader of Israel perpetrated a grim blunder on the whole human race, and it will suffer for it, time without end. He said, "The days of our years are three score years and ten." Man has accepted that as a fixed law. So often do we hear people who have passed that milestone saying, "Now I am living on borrowed time." What nonsense is that! Where got Moses the facts on which he made his statement? No one knows. 

Certainly he did not get them from Scripture.

Would you turn over to the sixth chapter of the book of Genesis and read from the third verse, you would find these words: "My spirit shall not always strive with man for that he also is flesh: yet his days shall be an hundred and twenty years."

Six score years are therefore the allotted span of man's life. The psychological influence of the words in that great song of Moses in which he used too much poetic license, has destroyed more human years than all other agents of destruction in all time and eternity. 

But let a great poem by Mrs. Anna Pierpont

Siviter tell the story of those three score years and ten. Seldom has such a voice spoken. 

My Lord, I do not understand; 

Thou givest me three score years and ten

To make and mold my life, and then- 

Dear Lord, to grow one perfect tree

A thousand years are not to Thee 

Too many years for Thy wise hand 

To make this redwood tree!

Behold this pebble at my feet, Round, smooth, and white, a perfect stone;

A thousand years to make a tree,

A million years to make a stone, And then, despite our prayers and tears, A span of three score years and ten 

Is given to men?

For me is made eternity!

That fact accounts for a certain steadying influence more and more pervading our social living. Youth is impulsive, impatient of delay, quick in attack. That is well.

Things have to be accomplished. Life is brief. 

Yet so often "haste makes waste." The steadying influence of age is as necessary to youth as the impatience of youth is to age. It is an omen of vast import to the race that the average age has so greatly increased. It is necessary to have the right proportions of youth and age. Happier and more successful will that nation be that maintains right adjustments.

The search for the fountain of youth will ever continue. The writer cherishes a bottle of water from that spring of Ponce de Leon, so eagerly sought. Yet the fountain of youth is never discovered flowing from material sources. The fountain of youth flows from within the soul of man. 

Jesus of Nazareth gave the location of that spring. 

He said that He gave the water of eternal youth. "It shall be in you a well of water fed from a spring that never runs dry.”

But size of body and time of years are not the measure of living. Man's way forward is via wider mental concepts of moral and spiritual

values. Man's mental powers must be cultivated to their optimum to make possible his largest conception of the moral and spiritual. A fool can never become a saint. Ignorance is the cesspool of crime. Yet knowledge without moral and spiritual domination is more deadly than ignorance. An educated criminal is to be feared above all others. If there must be ignorance either in the mental or the moral, let it never be in the moral. The moral sense is more important than mental discipline. When the moral sense fails, that nation permitting it, is already on the road to its destruction.

It needs no prophet, no nationality, to tell Germany her coming doom. She is telling herself by the awful atrocities she is practicing on her helpless, conquered people. Her overlords have perpetrated debauchery after debauchery, crime after crime unspeakable in horror. Murders by the hundred thousand are proven against her. Upon the Polish people, the Jewish people, the Serbs, the Yugoslavs and those of the North seacoast, have these horrors been imposed, until all the world cries out in fierce denunciation. The stain of killing the Poles by wholesale and transporting all Polish girls to German soldier brothels will grow deeper and more damning as the future years come on. As Mrs. Browning would say: the sob of such girls in the silences curses deeper than

the raucous voice of Hitler in his spasms of fury. 

Humanity will never cease hearing those sobs. It is the moral decay that is defeating Germany. 

Science declares that certain things are so. Ethics declares that certain things ought to be. Religion declares that certain things shall be. Religion, ethics and science unite in saying: "Be not deceived, God is not mocked: Whatsoever a man soweth that shall he also reap." God pity Germany in the day of her reaping. Of course there will be more of the soft heads, as at the close of the last war, who will cry out that she was not to blame for this war. She, poor nation, was put upon by England, France and a group of nations that wished to destroy her. Thank God, Mein

Kampf was written before time. It tells the whole story of plan for world conquest and reveals the preparations going on to accomplish that plan. 

When we wrench our thinking from this night of agony now upon us and permit history to speak, we find that today's deeds are but the ripened fruit of a season of immoral and unmoral growth. 

These are the most dreadful horors ever known by humanity, because they are the invention of man at his highest stage of development. We are dealing with the intellectual criminal at his most depraved moment. Hell has the most intelligent leadership today of any period since Satan fell from heaven. 1

But history through all the past centuries records the groans and agonies of great souls who have lamented the moral failures of their days.

Robert Burton of England, in his celebrated work, "The Anatomy of Melancholy," published in 1621, cries out in anguish: "Who is not brain sick? Oh, giddy-headed age! Mad endeavors! Mad actions! 

If Democritis were alive now and should but see the superstition of our age, our religious madness, so many professed Christians, yet so few imitators of Christ, so much talk and so little conscience, so many preachers and so little practice, such varieties of sects-how dost thou think he must have been affected? What would he have said to see, hear and read, so many bloody battles, such streams of blood able to run mills, to make sport for princes, without any just cause? Men well proportioned, carefully brought up, able in body and mind, led like so many beasts to slaughter in the flower of their years, without remorse and pity, killed for devil's food. These wars last always; and for many ages nothing so familiar as this hacking and hewing massacres, murders, desolations! Who made creatures so peaceable, born to love, mercy and meekness, so to rave like beasts and run furiously to their own destruction?"

It would seem that we had been listening to a story of our own day, such is the cry against mur-

ders, massacres and beast-like actions. It illustrates, however, man's struggles all down the ages, and that a few great souls have ever been the pioneers to a better day.

While one individual seems so insignificant amid the millions of people, yet one great man in each age has been the influence to turn the scale for a forward movement. The latent power in man is revealed in the achievements of a few men. We use the lazy man's excuse for non-attainment and set some men apart as geniuses. Genius is but the sweat wiped from the face of a toiler. 

When we search into the history of so-called geniuses, we discover that they have been men of work, toil, painstaking care, an indomitable purpose to do. Such men force their inherent powers to expand. Those who do not attain, have defeated themselves.

We conclude, therefore, that man does not need remaking. He already has all the powers and potentialities necessary to fulfill the great command, "Be fruitful and multiply and replenish the earth and subdue it." The process needed today is that of a thorough shaking, causing man to spring from his lethargy and apply himself to his tasks with persistent energy, growing intellect and larger over-soul. If man will not rouse himself to the effort of fulfilling his destiny, then his neglected duties and responsibilities will

gather fire damp and ere long explode. Wars are social explosions, are earthquakes. Every public man desires to own a few of them and ride up and down the earth jolting the masses out of their lethargy. Wars are sometimes a blessing in disguise. Was ever the world awake as it is today?

Did common man ever display such powers of body and mind and soul as now? Men who were very ordinary before the war are now writing their names all over history. 

We are not advocating war as a means of waking up the world; neither are we advocating earthquakes to topple down the old that the new may rise in more permanent form. The fact is, however, that we are constantly beholding "the removal of those things that are shaken as of things made, that the things that cannot be shaken may remain." That removing process is always at work. It is as unobtrusive at times, as the falling of a sunbeam; at other times it causes the earth to tremble. Would man but recognize this force, often so destructive, and do the things himself in an evolutionary way, he would draw the fire damp by constantly introducing new reforms. 

So would he save himself from such cataclysms as we are now beholding. They never could be, if man took control of his world, social, religious, financial, and as brothers.

The forecasting of dates for possible earthquakes is becoming almost a science. Man knows the causes, can calculate the strains and stresses on the earth's crust, and predict with amazing certainty the probable breaks and slips which bring on the disturbances. Certainly our sociologists should have more scientific data on which to base their prophesies, warn humanity of the disasters impending and indicate what human society must do to prevent the coming cataclysm. An encomium for the sociologists is the strenuous manner in which, for the last twenty years, they have warned the world of its coming doom. In fact, the world came to understand the approaching doom and waited with baited breath for the awful upheaval. France built a Maginot line and hid within its steel chambers. England expected that her desire for peace would be a bulwark of safety. The little countries waited in fear. All the world saw the terrible calamity coming, knew Germany was arming to the teeth, understood her determination to conquer the world, heard the rumble of her Panzer divisions, even read her plans of conquest, yet helplessly waited for the storm to break. It did break. Nations went down as a set of nine pins go down when the bowler is expert. The veins of the nations opened and the warm blood of millions is pouring out upon the land and the sea.

What paralyzed, hypotized, stupefied world action against the sure coming disaster? Why could not humanity have realized the horrors approaching and taken stern measures to prevent them? The blame must be placed on the heads of those leaders of the free governments. It is so easy to blame those who take the responsibilities of government. But they should not be there unless they have the care of the people on their shoulders. The people cannot know the inside of secret diplomacy. That is not their responsibility. 

The people seek to uphold the government while the government cares for them. Those who take the high places of government must take the responsibility of protecting the people. This is a responsibility that goes with the office. How few of our representatives take their responsibilities with such conscience. The trifling way some of them have acted while the disaster was developing, brings a blush of shame to the cheeks of millions of Americans. Pearl Harbor is an answer to their criminal neglect of the study of statesmanship, or at least their following it. So our rulers are responsible for not preventing this break up of humanity. The rulers are the watchmen on the walls. They are supposed to be on the lookout at every moment. Theirs is a responsibility that never can be delegated to another. Either they must be watchful or resign, or receive the

curse of both God and man. Listen to the Scriptures: "If the watchman see the sword come and blow not the trumpet, and the people be not warned; if the sword come and take away any person from among them, he is taken away in his iniquity; but his blood will I require at the watchman's hands." Some political leaders have an awful reckoning coming to them. Oh, it's the old story of politics, inside grab, inflated egotism and downright stupidity. When the reports came in from the secret service, and the story was told of what was drawing near, a British leader raised his eyebrows and said, "Someone must be very much mistaken." Selfishness, egotistical respect for private judgment, damnable bigotry for religious dogma-all these are the explanation of the betrayal of the people.

Our whole social and religious fabric was rotten and torn. Look at the religious situation. Here we are a body of people who believe in God and the common good. We want to live decently and in brotherly relations. We want to make the name and place of God regnant in life. So we build our churches and develop our plans of administration. Of course, we will consult with each other and do our work in cooperation with all good people! Will we? Do we? Look at the situation.

Congregationalists must live in the pride of their national evangelism. Did they not come over in

Don't find a new way as did he; just follow the way he led. We are traitors to our sires. Shall we make their creed our jailer? Episcopalians must hold by a superdignified religion of the ruling classes. Are we not of the rulers in royalty? Roman

Catholicism would not yield to God, man or the devil in its conviction that it is the only church, has the keys of the kingdom of heaven and could bind or loose as it wills. In the case of this world holocaust, it neither bound nor loosed, but remained protected by bulls of the Pope. The bulls did not prove strong enough to overcome the tanks.

A thoughtful man shudders as he contemplates the scene of a world being led like sheep to the slaughter, while shepherds flee to the cloisters of prayer. Prayer in a time like this! The rebuke to Moses from the lips of God should be thundered from every church of every land, "Wherefore criest thou unto me. Speak to the Children of

Israel that they go forward." The praying of 

Moses that night was blocking the advance.

Prayer is the greatest of powers when properly focused. Prayer is no refuge for weaklings, cowards or mere pacificists. There are glorious

times for prayer: those times are not when the sea is opening and the forces of God should be moving. 

History repeats itself today. When one considers the religious world he is stupefied with amazement. The world on fire and churches praying! Praying for what? The immediate need is to get the people out to fight the fire. "But," cries some belated comer, "Jesus would not approve of fighting." Jesus took an instrument of war, a flail, and fought the despoilers of the people, putting them to abject rout. Can you see His flashing eye! Can you hear the ring of His voice as He drove the enemies of God out of the house of God! 

His was a flame of fire that burned. After the thieves were turned out singing began in the temple. Our world can't sing today. It never will sing again until these international thieves and robbers are driven from human society. 

What must our heavenly Father think of us as we go on with our senseless bigotries, our national egotisms, our religious idolatries, our world festering in filth, and our men, women and children being slaughtered by the million. A Roman 

Catholic or Greek Catholic or Protestant Catho lic doesn't look very large in the face of our world needs. Cannot we get together! The religious people, federated, could change this hell of present living to a heaven of work, rightly directed pleasure, rightly applied knowledge, and brotherhood

rightly administered. Yet we will go on alone staying by our little and insignificant prejudices and beliefs, Baptists, Lutheran, Presbyterian, Roman Catholic, Old Catholic, Greek Catholic, or any one of the 280 sects that infest our country alone.

Possibly the most pitiful people on earth are those who live because of the sacrifices of others, even to their death, yet conscientiously object to enduring any of those sacrifices. Of all sad twists of the mind of man, this one seems the most inexplicable. An editorial in the New York Sun gives the picture in a normal and convincing light. 

It speaks of the third factor in the composition of duties. If a man tries to work out an equation involving three factors, he cannot hope for success by ignoring one of them. "The three factors are one's religious principles, his duty to himself and his duty to his fellow-man as he exists in orderly and organized society. It is this last duty that fails to grip those who would shirk the responsibility of the care and responsibility for the safety of the world." The endeavor of such people to hide from themselves in the rushes and reeds of religious verbiage, is the old trick

Knowledge of the external world may come to men through other than sense channels. 

Thought transmission without words or by no known means of contact is as real as by wave lengths of air. The radio has made us aware of wave lengths in the ether, and we communicate with others across any distances and instantaneously. But by what means we know not. 

Though man does not need remaking, he does need redeeming. Whatever it was that brought or brings him such weakness, he cries for rescue. 

Whether that something came from the "Fall," as some express it, or whether from his inheritances, man must be saved to his increasingly better self. He needs to be rescued from his selfishness, his egotisms, his sins, and saved to an unselfish devotion to the highest good of all. If his weaknesses are from his inheritances he needs a newer injection of spiritual discernment enabling him always to choose the spiritual.

Mankind has had small success in trying to save the weak. Boundless energy has been expended in guarding, shielding, pampering weak forces and weak people. Often have we thwarted the very meaning of that Gospel which we strive to emu-

late. Our major emphasis should never be upon the weak; always upon the strong. Much of our effort to help the weak has really been success in blocking the strong. Life will reach to nobler development only through those who have strength. 

Weakness is the product of broken law. We must pity the law breaker and help him all we can; yet must we give our major strength to the strong. This is not the strength of Nietzsche's superman. It is not life robbed of all mercy and grace. It is not forgetting the commands of the man of Nazareth.

There are far too many weak people; millions of them. They are the product of lawlessness. 

Vast numbers of them are the result of our criminal neglect to make and enforce wise laws. Think of permitting the weak-minded to marry, and even the insane, while they retain the power to produce children! Why is society so criminally careless?

The strong are responsible for permitting such propagation of children. When will we wake to our responsibility! Why, we even neglect the whole science of eugenics. Religious clap-trap and pink-tea sociology block the roads to such reforms, thus loading on each succeeding generation heavier and heavier burdens that never should be borne. No wonder our Christian, scientific leaders grow so discouraged. They show the way, they prove its safety and necessity; they go before and

lead the way. But the rank and file stay down in the fog-filled valleys and die amid their useless days. Even our most agnostic scientists instantly agree that matter is not the supreme force. Matter is but an agent in the production of something greater than itself. Materialism has been hopelessly defeated. The study of the mental and spiritual qualities of man has revealed the lifelessness of materialism. Not stone and steel and wood are the measure of attainment, but the mind and soul of man. Man is the measure. Man is the standard. Man is the last product of the travailing ages. Man is the success of evolution and the prophesy of final perfection.

No, we do not need to remake man. We need to have him redeemed.

CHAPTER IV  

A surface explanation for the ills, the wrongs, the horrors of this present war is easily given. 

The popular voice declares that Germany suffered under the defeat of the former war and secretly made ready for battle with the sure belief in victory. When she was all ready to the last Quisling, she launched her mechanized armies with such force and precision as to overwhelm all opposition. 

It is really appalling to review the rush of those 

Panzer divisions, the almost superhuman direction and control, and their amazing success. Nation after nation went down before that awful impact. To prepare for the success of those tanks, treachery from within and from without the ranks of the wrecked nations had its full sway. The break of the Belgian king was the bursting of the dyke. He could not see far enough into the future to know that his surrender meant for himself a prisoner's life and for his people a terrible servitude. He did not see that death in the line of duty 84

was infinitely more to be desired than surrender to his fears. It is said that he saw his armies being shot to pieces and he felt in duty bound to save them from more slaughter. But what has become of those imprisoned, enslaved, brutalized men and women of Belgium! Death in the way of freedom was a million times more preferable and honorable than such surrender. What are a few more years of living against the insults, the mockeries, the depraved situation of himself now, and his people! The Belgian people are demoralized, decimated, lost to all hope of honorable living. Their only gleam of hope flashes to them whenever they behold an Allied plane flying over. 

Hundreds of them have been shot for cheering the planes, but cheer they will. As that King sits brooding day by day and night by night, while the slow leaden-footed months and years pass, the knowledge of what his sudden surrender means to the whole world, to uncounted millions of human beings, weltering in their sorrows, their poverty and their slavery, he must be even now on the way to the asylums of the insane. But his grief and his repining can never undo what he did. He was a weakling in the place that demanded a strong man. Would that his great Father had been there, at such time of destiny!

Contrast the collapse of this man to the immortal defy of the Greeks. They knew death was their

portion as did Britain in sending aid; but they all chose death rather than disgrace. They won immortality. Their names will be enrolled on the pages of the great, " 'til time shall be no more.' 

Many years will pass ere that story of the inside betrayal of France shall be told. France had the finest army in Europe. She had freedom's passion burning in her soul. She loved her lands with the love of woman. Why did she die? Is the reason slowly coming to light in the deeds of Laval, Darlan, Petain, and such men? The world likes to feel that the old Marshal, saviour of Verdun, is not, nor ever has been a traitor to his nation. The world likes to feel that he is honestly struggling to do the best he can for France under the impossible conditions in which he finds himself. He hopes by delay in every possible way, and by every process, to keep the semblance of freedom in unoccupied France, holding this semblance as a nucleus for that great free France of tomorrow. The Marshal is an old, a worn-out man, shouldering a load too heavy for him, leading a hope too far from him, and in union with others who care only for their own safety and their future possessions, in whatever government may come, and under whatever racial rule. The world clings to the belief that Petain is motivated by his great love for Foch and his dream of the France once more to be.

The world fears the motives of the other leaders of France, prepared in heart for any betrayals of honor and country. General de Gaulle, man of ordinary ability that he is, represents the hopes of free Frenchmen. By his cooperation with the allied nations and a firm belief in a redeemed France, he keeps those fires of faith burning in the hearts of millions of his countrymen. His name will endure forever.

But a deeper reason than the one mentioned calls for consideration. Back of all German bitterness, Axis hate, and mad marching of doomed armies, lies a fundamental conflict of philosophies. Two great ideas are at war. 

Ideas are the greatest forces on earth. Ideas bring on conflicts that muscular force must often settle. Ideas are forces whether for good or ill.

But better by far to have ideas and their terrible conflicts than to live on the low level of "The man with the hoe; the emptiness of the ages in his face, and on his back the burden of the world.” 

The two battling ideas or philosophies that today fill the world with such conflict of war, cruel beyond conception, are the ideas of dictatorship and democracy. Both words have a long history. 

Dictatorship is a form of government in which one or more men determine the policies and purposes of the government, then frame and execute such laws as will make regnant the will of the dic-

tator. The dictator will have his way in every act of government. Every citizen of the nation must obey his will. The life of every citizen is in the hands of the dictator. The citizen may have no will of his own, no expressed thoughts of his own, no purposes of his own. Everything he thinks and does must be regimented to the will of the dictator.

There are great natural advantages in such a form of government. The dictator is falsely supposed to be thoroughly educated in all matters of state and of world government. His whole business is so to conduct the affairs of his nation, that it will attain to leadership in all things that pertain to human welfare. If emergencies arise in which the life or honor of the nation is at stake, the dictator can march his armies and move his fleets without the consent of any person or group of persons. He is not handicapped by sluggish brains and the selfish wills of minorities, or majorities, political parties or racial feuds. When he commands all the people obey. He knows best. 

So the best interests of the state are instantly preserved. We here in America have seen the need of such a power, as the weeks have dragged by. The selfish wills of professed patriots and the amenable desires of disgruntled disappointees have kept our President and those who saw clearly the issues, from getting the nation ready

for this supreme moment of national fate. Again we say that Pearl Harbor and even Manila are the answers to such myopic leaders of the blind. 

But dictatorship has a deadly effect upon the citizens of a nation thus ruled. They become dull, thoughtless, have no initiative, are like dumb driven cattle. The impetus to think, strive, achieve, is blunted and ere long destroyed. Such citizens are not citizens but slaves. Such a nation falters, fails and is wiped out by the onward sweep of humanity. This is the history of dictatorship.

Democracy is a word connoting all we mean by free government, maintained by the voting power of every qualified citizen. The supreme power is retained by the people and exercised by legally elected representatives of the people. It is a clumsy, cumbersome form of government, slow and at times indecisive. Great emergencies cannot be met. Great crises have often proved fatal, ere the voice of the people could be heard. That fact is so apparent here in America today that he who runs may read. Our freedoms were assailed, our way of life scorned, our President reviled and our national honor insulted, yet our Congress refused to recognize our peril and permitted our ships to be sunk without a protest. Fifth columnists, skulduggery politics, an itching for re-election-all this kept the nation from throwing her

united strength into the battle for the preservation of those freedoms entrusted to us by the nobler generations preceding ours.

A case might be cited to make all this plain. At the close of the last war, the best brains of the world gave birth to a league of nations. It was the greatest dream ever to disturb the thoughts of humanity. The time had come for the world to think in terms of humanity as a whole rather than in terms of single groups or nations of people. 

Humanity should be governed as humanity, and not as fragments of people. The League of Nations was born. It grew and began to function. By moral persuasion it settled two controversies which would have brought on war. But these differences were between small nations. When big nations sought to get something at the expense of the smaller, they withdrew rather than submit to the will of the League. By those withdrawals the

League died. It had no power to enforce its will. 

Strangely enough the United States of America would not enter the League although Woodrow Wilson was the Father of it. A personal quarrel between two men who happened to be in power at the time, frustrated our entrance into the League, and so it was sabotaged. Now we are spending men and money by the millions and billions, to fight the very thing that the League would have prevented. If Woodrow Wilson could

have been a dictator for the emergency, he could have saved us from all this present horror. The inside story of that whole period of our history has never been written. It will be many years before it is told to the astonishment of those who then will be alive. We labor in America under the strange illusion that democracy, apparently the finest form of government yet devised by man in spite of its weaknesses, has in itself the strength to endure. They have rights who dare maintain them, and only they. Democracy has no breath, turn of eye, wave of hand that keeps itself puissant. Democracy is only as strong as the majority of the people who believe in it and uphold it. 

Democracy cannot live in and of itself. Citizens who refrain from voting should be deprived of that citizenship.

Why should this government recognize those who will not uphold it by the use of their franchise. 

What right have such to criticize the government, enjoying the benefits of that government and yet too indolent, too disloyal to honor their citizenship. We made the great mistake of fancying that democracy was self-perpetuating. This world conflict is shocking us awake.

We would be much mistaken, however, if we based our conclusions on the causes of this war solely on the conflict of these two philosophies. 

Back of this great surface fact lies the reason for

the philospohies. Let Mr. B. K. Sandwell speak.

He is one of Canada's great newspaper publishers. CHAPTER VII  

Among the most fascinating stories told by history are those of man's dreams, his dreams of good and his fears of evil. His embodiment of those dreams in forms of belief, doctrine and deed, is a companion story of equal charm. In the very penumbra of human history we find hints of man's thoughts regarding himself and his position amid the other forms of life. The stone implements, the markings on caves and cliffs, the hints of burial rights, and all such discoveries, but tell us of those dreams.

Whatever the order of growth may be, we find that man has dreamed and still dreams along three ways that have no ending. Man has dreamed of his own attainments. He has never fancied himself better, wiser, more powerful, than at the present time. His moral claim has ever been foremost. 

It is the stamp of the divine on him. Low indeed were his early moral standards. He himself was always the judge and his decisions were always given according as his interests dictated. He was 163

the measure of his judgment. As his measure of himself increased, so did his judgment grow. He desired to be better than he was, even while not living up to the best he then knew. It would seem that man has always dreamed of himself as having the possibilities of the Gods. Indeed his Gods were but glorified human beings. Man then made his own Gods. All down the centuries of recorded history that dream has appeared, as shown in literature. The old Persian, Egyptian and Baby lonian records tell the story. 

When we come to Jewish history and especially Bible language, it glows from every chapter of every book. Man has dreamed that he could be a nobler being than he has yet become. Man still dreams that dream, and his vision of his own possibilities brightens as each century passes. What may a man become he has constantly asked. How great might he be? What destiny is his, personally? Ah who can tell! Truly, as Scripture says, "It does not yet appear what we shall be." 

Man has dreamed of a better world. That fascination of a "Golden age" has ever beckoned him. 

How clear cut was such a dream to those early 

Mediterranean peoples! They affirmed that there would come an age when social barriers would be levelled, war known no more and a great spiritual prince would be governor of all the provinces.

The early prophets tell of his coming. The upheavals of the centuries make a highway for his chariots. "All nations shall be blest in Him.' 99

And still a third dream has beckoned man on. A nobler man in a nobler world were but an introduction to a far, far spiritual city, where all peoples of the earth, redeemed by their visions, would gather, and would abide in peace, love, and good will forever. But the dreams for the good have far outnumbered the fears of the evil. Just as there are more fair days than foul, more days of sunshine than days of rain, so have man's hopes outnumbered his fears, and so has he subdued them. Increasing knowledge has destroyed the primitive fears of angry gods. Thunder and lightning and earthquake are now only natural phenomena, due to perfectly natural causes. It is true that every increase in man's knowledge has but opened vaster fields of his ignorance. But those fields no longer make him afraid; he welcomes them because of the new struggles they open to him. This will be the joy of humanity throughout all the unnumbered eons of its existence. The greatest joy of life will always be to work at a task it cannot master, toward a goal it cannot reach and in the presence of a Companion it cannot see.

But there is another side to the picture. There is another light flashing on its obscure images. 

There is another voice calling through the blackness. Other giant forces are being released in the very death of the thousands. Humanity is once more laying down her life that she may take it again. We are in the midst of the removal of those things that are shaken as of things made, that the things that cannot be shaken may remain. Humanity has accumulated vast stores of rubbish, during the process of her building. Millions of people had made their dwelling place in the rubbish heaps instead of the cleared lands. Indeed a fierce struggle had grown up between the rubbishheap dwellers and those who strove to move into the new buildings. The seeming had been adopted for the real. The stagings had been used instead of the dwellings. Things became gods instead of servants. Money developed effrontery in buying the judgements of religion. Churches and schools were builded by those who from the agonies of the poor. Christ in religious wrath drove them out of the Temple in His day. War, cruel, bloody, inhuman war is doing it today. 

This war is burning up the rubbish; but alas it is also burning up much else. The human temple is being cleansed; but so much of the temple is being destroyed in the process. The tares and the wheat are burning together. War destroys both the good and the bad.

Yet a careful observer must see that a remnant is being saved. The amalgam is now leaping from the gold. "Purified so as by fire," is the process we now behold. This mount of anguish reveals the fact that, while death is strong, Life is stronger. 

Not all flashes in the night of blackness are from bursting shells.

Viewed in such a light Man's human destiny appears more glorious than ever before conceived.

This willingness to give their lives for the freedoms they cherish, that those who live after, may enjoy them, is an encomium on the worth of man. 

What if they are mistaken in their belief? What if time prove their choice a wrong one? What if they die for human fiends when they thought to die for human freedom? Ah this, even this but proves the underlying stratum of human worth. 

If men will die for a great cause they may be taught to live more heroically for a great cause. 

War calls for death. Peace calls for life. The heroism of living for a great freedom is greater than that of dying for such freedom. One of the greatest problems confronting man today is that of teaching the masses the heroism of living for a great principle, a great freedom. Many young men of today will gladly die in the hell of war for the truths in which they believe. But how few of them are willing to live in the hell of politics for them. When the heroisms of death are transformed into the heroisms of life, mankind will quickly move up to higher levels.

But we cannot leave man to be annihilated in his material existence. The material great, powerful, zestful, enticing as it is and worth a life time of toil, is yet not reward enough for man. Man is born for a higher destiny than earth. He feels it. 

He knows it. He cannot confine his thinking within the circle of the earth. His loves leap over his human limitations. He wanders out to pathless realms, beyond human bounds. Preach to him all you will that he is of the earth, earthy, but he will not believe you. He may declare his last conclusions gained from both inductive and deductive reasoning, but he will laugh at those conclusions.

He will follow the road that naturally opens before him and seek God. Man is bigger than himself, bigger than his world, bigger than his fears, his hopes or his dreams. Man lives in time but outlasts it. Man is made of the clay of the earth, yet transforms it into spirit that shall develop, after the earth and all the things thereof shall cease to be. We know we have not time enough here in which to gain our goals. 

Youth reveals them, middle life clarifies them, age glorifies them. The worth of those goals grows greater as the end of our human years draws near. 

The demand for a destiny beyond the material is emphasized by the sacrifice of young men in the war. No time have they had even to approach the fulfillment of their hopes and dreams. There is a further consideration. Nature is absolutely honest. All science testifies to that fact. 

She gives oats for oats, corn for corn, wheat for wheat. "Every seed after its kind." Science discovers the unchanging laws which control the development of nature's work. The one absolutely sure thing of which the world has any acquaintance is the honesty and reliability of nature. Nature never deceives, never defrauds, never alters her fixed laws. "Whatsoever a man soweth, that shall he also reap." The Chemical formula H2SO4, will produce sulphuric acid day or night, summer or winter, in any land or any sea. We can always trust nature. IF that be true, then we know that she will not deceive us in anything. Why did she plant in us those seeds of immortality, if there were to be no crop? Why have we this ineradicable craving, if there be nothing with which to satisfy it? These seeds will fructify and bear fruit. These yearnings will not be lost. What has been given to us, is for our good. We can think no thought, we can have no desire, we can imagine no place or position that is impossible to us, if such thought, desire or position is not the outgrowth of selfishness and greed. Whatever we can imagine or fellowship in our souls, that is proof of the reality of the thing we desire. Nature never deceives.

The proof of our destiny beyond this material universe lies in the fact that we desire it. We

never could have thought of a destiny beyond, had there been none awaiting us. Therefore are we sure of our destiny beyond the material universe. 

What does that destiny connote? Who determines what that destiny shall be? Is it ever fixed or never fixed; a destiny that develops as the mind of man develops, always discovering newer and more alluring truths beyond! 

What fathomless abysses open beneath us, what inaccessible heights loom above us, as we stand on the outer brink of our human living and look off into the unknown country beyond! Somehow we dare to do this and are comforted in the thought of our future growth in a world of spirit where love never faileth and life expands into the infinite. 

Who or what determines our individual destiny?

Who assigns our place in those halls of the immortal? Who opens the doors to us, bidding us enter the society of those gathered? Such questions now demand our attention.

CHAPTER VIII  

Religion is the outward form by which man expresses his belief in and obedience to the God or 

Gods whom he seeks to honor and obey. Such forms of expression ere long become fixed in a ritual of worship. Often the worship has become lost in the ritual, and program has supplanted prayer. The prayer wheels of India tell this story in most vivid fashion. Excessive ritual has always tended to religious formalism. Since man's first appearance on earth, religion of some kind has played a leading roll in his existence. The history of religion is the history of man's growth and development. We may trace the road along which man has come and mark each great advance in his intellectual and moral development, by the prevailing religion of the time. It is a truth never to be refuted, that man can climb no higher than his religion. His religion has ever been his guide, his prophetic voice in the wilderness calling the road makers of the future. 183

This is no place for the study of those religions which humanity has developed throughout the centuries. As we have said, each new development has marked a new advance. There have been many, many adventitious growths. In every age there have been what our biologists call "Sports," or variations from the prevailing type, a mutation of the original. That is probably more true today, than in any other age of religious history. Certain cities of our own land have become hotbeds for the development of Theological "Sports."

In following the trunk line of religious development, certain great truths stand out as beacon lights. We observe that man's belief in his God or Gods has always determined his behaviour towards his fellow man. A cruel, immoral god developed a cruel immoral people. A god of vengeance, developed terrible fighters. A God of justice and mercy and truth developed people of similar characteristics. The prevailing belief in 

God set the ideals of the period of history in which people developed. The gods of the Old Persians were war gods. The gods of the early Egyptians were sensual gods. The gods of the Greeks were human gods, revelling in all the weaknesses and frailties of the human. The God of the Hebrews was a God of law, order, justice, mercy, forgiveness, truth. The God of the Christian is that same Hebrew God, interpreted in love and made

real as a loving Heavenly Father. The God of the

Christian is the latest and fullest expression of human development and divine revelation. Cried a young Jewish lawyer to Jesus, "What is the greatest commandment?" Jesus, the last and fullest revelator of God, answered: "The first of all commandments is, hear O Israel, The Lord our 

God is one Lord: and thou shalt love the Lord thy 

God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind, and with all thy strength: this is the first and greatest commandment. And the second is like, namely this, thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. There is none other commandment greater than these."

So when we declare that religion has the last word, it is necessary to ask, "What religion, of what age, and of what content? Do we mean the religion of earliest days, middle days, or these last days of man's development? Do we indeed mean any completed and final revelation of God, or do we mean the ethos of religion, the continual expansion of revelation, the belief in that God who will more and more reveal himself to man as man gains larger capacity for knowing Him?

Only a stunted thinker could proclaim that man now has the final revelation of God. Being what is popularly called a "fundamentalist," he might assert his personal knowledge, based as he believes, on Scripture, that man has the final revelation of

God, and the speaker the mouthpiece of God, in declaring it. Dogmatism has its place and its devotees. So many people must have a fixed belief, a final word of law, immutable, unchangeable. It comforts those who are religiously riding at anchor. It is a protection to those who know nothing of intellectual seamanship. The stout-hearted sailor weighs anchor and is away on the infinite sea, whispering to himself or loudly singing, “Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard, neither hath entered into the heart of man, the things that God hath prepared for them that love him." It was John

Whether or not we believe these words to be the exact words of Robinson, the truth expressed is a truth to be held for all time. 

The Bible is the instrument used to convey the revelation of God. The Bible is not the revelation, but is the container of the revelation. The statements need interpreting, and are literally true when rightly interpreted. Our modern age needs nothing more greatly than guiding principles by which to interpret this poetry, this imagery, this history, this parable, this dream, all of which become a portion of the revelation of God as given in the Bible. It is this Christian religion to which we refer when we affirm that religion has the last word. To discover that last and greatest command, we need to find the very heart of that religion and note its pulsations as does the physician seeking to diagnose the ills of his patient. 

What does the Christian religion say to humanity in this dread time? The greatest contribution ever made to human thought is that statement in the opening words of Our Lord's prayer: "Our Father." My Father!

We tell Him our thoughts, our weaknesses, our needs. Then we listen to what He would say. But all too often our prayers are our conversation, instead of God's conversation.

The third message of this Christian religion declares that Man develops by the use of his own

faculties and powers. "Grow in Grace and in the further knowledge of our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ." We are a creation of God, with infinite potentialities. Ours is the task of developing, directing and putting them into service for both God and man. There is no other way of serving God save to grow to our best and help others to their best. That is growing Grace. That is divine development. Jesus spoke a parable to His disciples on this very subject. He said that a certain man was going to a far country to receive a kingdom. 

He would need strong men and true, to aid him in the rule of that country. He must be looking for such men now. So before going away, he called about him those whom he knew, those whose abilities he partly understood, and gave each of them a task to perform while he was away. To one he gave five talents, to another two, to another one; to every man as he judged him capable. When the man came back, having received his kingdom, and now needing his men of trust, he called in those workers and asked for a report from each. It is well to note that what this man wanted was faithfulness and not money. He knew their ability and had tested them accordingly. He wanted to see who would stretch to their best. He seemed to indicate that their best would be to double their present powers.

We wonder if that is an approximate demand

The man with the two talents came, stating practically the same thing. Master you gave me two talents, and I have gained with them two more.

If you thought that I was a mere grasping man wanting that on which I had bestowed no labor, why did you not at least put the talent out to interest so that I might have had the interest on the investment? Turning to a servant standing beside him he said: “Take the gift away from this failure and give it to the man who most largely developed his trust. How that man had robbed himself! And yet the man gave the judgment by his own acts. Such a story and such a judgment is recorded by every newspaper, every day: enter thou into the sorrows of the unfaithful.

The fourth message of the Christian religion declares that our errors and sins bring their own

punishment. The parable just reviewed tells us that God never punishes people. That He does punish, is a Pre-Christian belief. God never brings woe or bloodshed or the hell of war upon us. We bring it all upon ourselves. It is the result of our unfaithfulness. Were we completely absorbed in the will of God, and striving to make His will done on the earth as it is in heaven, there would be no hell of war, no sorrows because of prodigal children, no moaning in the night watches, because of broken faith and lost life.

Did men understand this truth, never would they be heard repeating that question which is an insult to humanity: "Why doesn't God stop this war?" God did not make the war. God has warned us to avoid those sins which cause war. God has pled with His children, in words from which the tears drop, urging them to do right and really live, rather than do the deeds that bring forth death. 

The moment God stepped in to shield us from our sins, that moment our manhood would be gone.

We would at once presume on that goodness and sell our souls for a mess of potage. If we value our freedom of choice and our struggles to attain, then never would we become so lost to the deepest fact of life, namely, what we sow we must reap, even if it be war and death for millions. We make the war. We are the only power to stop the war.

Is it any wonder that this disease of war has swept the earth, engulfing both saved and sinful!

Is it any wonder that civilization plunges on to its doom, human sins having opened the flood gates of destruction. Individually our souls can be saved now, but our bodies are heaped on the funeral pyre of evil and will be consumed.

Such thinking leads to those questions with which chapter seven closed. We asked, who determines our destiny. Are we not now in a position to answer with Henley, "I am the master of my fate. With our decision goes our destiny. This fact is so proven, so sure, that we marvel at the indifference of the crowds. Why should it be?

After His resurrection Jesus called His disciples together, gave them last instructions ere he left them physically, and then said: "Go ye into all the world and disciple all nations." No land

has been neglected, no race overlooked. Certainly mistakes and blunders have been made, for all the workers have been human beings. Certainly oppositions have been met, for the old gods have fought for their very existence. But the fact remains that the finest consecration of the noblest men and women of earth has been given to the task of making known to all the world the story of God's love for people as revealed in Jesus Christ. In the homeland every agency has been employed to make known that marvelous story. Millions on millions in money and men have been spent to perfect the manner of telling the story and of nurturing youth in gospel truth. But with all the efforts of two thousand years, the gospel of Jesus Christ is not regnant on the earth. Perhaps it is because of the divisions in the ranks of Christians. Perhaps it is because of the multitude of doctrines and denominations. Perhaps it is because Christians have spent so much effort in theological discussion that they had too little strength left for world conquest. 

When we pause to consider this whole question, we are dumbfounded at this Christian waste of effort and money. True it must be, that a de-

nomination badge and a theological certificate can have no standing before God. Whether we are 

Roman Catholic or Greek Catholic or Protestant 

Catholic, will not matter in the reports of our lives, "When we stand before the king." The question will not be one of doctrine or denomination, but one of service. "I was hungry and ye gave me meat. I was thirsty and ye gave me drink. I was in prison and ye came unto me. 

Come ye blessed of my Father inherit the kingdom." Not a word of social standing, denominational bias, or racial pigmentation. Rewards will be determined by deeds. The "well done," will be heard only by those who ministered to people in the name and for the sake of our Lord Jesus. 

Notwithstanding all our Christian faults and failings the followers of Jesus have succeeded in making His judgments the standard in every court of justice, in every chancellory of the nations, in every world conflict. Not every court or chancellory or nation has yielded to His judgments, but by their refusals have they passed condemnation upon themselves. Jesus Christ is the standard. The world knows it. Those who do not follow 

Him know it.

At certain periods of history all such statements seem puerile, weak, even childish. We are now living in such a time. The standards of Jesus

have been defied. Hate and not love seems now to be the motivating force. Do evil to those who are your enemies, and curse those who despitefully use you, is the order of the day. Hitler relies upon brute force and a hunch from his madness, to make him master of mankind. What unspeakable atrocities he is measuring out to those who withstand him. What awful crimes he is committing against humanity. Hell itself built on sin and sustained by it, must shudder at these new revelations of depravity. The Japanese War Lords are apeing the horrors of Hitler. Of course they are doomed now and death will surely follow.

But "stronger than the dark the light; stronger than the wrong the right." They that be for us are more than they that be against us. We fancy that the sun sets, when it is but the roll of the earth we see. We fancy that evil rules when it is only a temporary skirmish of rebels we behold. 

We fancy that the lines of truth are broken when they are but bending back for greater resiliency. 

We do grow discouraged at times. This pall of fear settles blacker. This orgy of blood and tears grows more terrible. This strain on humanity reaches toward the breaking point. Are we to be plunged into savagery; into the bottomless pit of annihilation!

The times call not for moaning but for music.

Luther's great Hymn must be raised again on the voice of the millions: 

A Mighty fortress is our God, A bulwark never failing; 

Our helper He, amid the flood 

Of mortal ills prevailing. 

Did we in our own strength confide, Our striving would be losing; 

Were not the right man on our side,

The man of God's own choosing. 

Let goods and kindred go, The body they may kill; 

God's strength abideth still. 

His kingdom is forever. 

Saint Paul's great shout must echo over our world in clarion call: "I know whom I have believed and am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto Him." The 

Faith of the Old Prophet Habukkuk must be called from its camp and made to do sentry duty once more: "Although the fig tree shall not blossom neither shall there be fruit in the vines; the labour of the olive shall fail and the fields shall yield no meat; the flocks shall be cut off from the fold, and

there shall be no herd in the stalls; yet will I rejoice in the Lord: I will joy in the God of my salvation." 

Humanity's redemption is sure. Peace will again reign on the earth, buttressed and guarded by a nobler Democracy, all of whose judgements will be based on the principles of Jesus Christ as laid down in the sermon on the Mount. Human living will take on a newer meaning, calling for active participation in every redemptive effort. 

Evil shall not flourish because the forces of Righteousness will not slumber nor sleep. A "Newtopia" shall appear, its citizens God-loving, truth loving, home loving and strong to wage successful battle with every human enemy.
