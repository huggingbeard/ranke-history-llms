THE AUTHOR 

Mrs. Gambrell is the daughter of Rev. A. Worthington who is the guide for the Mitchells through most of the pages of this interesting little book. 

She grew up on the Indian mission fields of Oklahoma and is therefore thoroughly familiar with Indian life. 

She writes from first hand information based on experiences in her own missionary home at Pawhuska and Pawnee.

At Oklahoma Baptist University she met T. L. Gambrell, son of a preacher and a volunteer for Indian mission work. Even before their marriage they studied 

Indian mission history together and did volunteer work with the Indians. 

Together, following their marriage, they attended Baptist Bible Institute. They have remained in New Orleans where he is educational director of one of the churches. 

Mrs. Gambrell's interest in boys and grls dates from her own experiences as a Sunday School teacher, Training Union officer, and G. A. worker in the Osage church.

CHAPTER III THEIR FIRST MISSION SERVICE 

As they entered the door, the missionary paused in the little hallway and rang a large bell. "Why are you doing that?" Bill asked. "Thirty minutes before each service we always ring the bell," Brother Worthington replied. "You can hear this bell for a long distance and it reminds everyone to come to God's house."

As they entered the mission auditorium they saw there were curtains and one set of folding doors to divide the large auditorium into smaller rooms for Sunday School classes. Even more room was needed for the Sunday 

School, they were told, especially in the winter months when all the children from the Government school could come to the mission on Sunday morning. The auditorium needed some repairs which the members hoped to make soon, the missionary said.

The Pawnee Church, he told the Mitchells, is the second largest Indian Baptist church in the South. The mission work was started in 1906 by J. S. Brendel, and in 1908 a church was organized. It has grown greatly since then and now there is Sunday School, Training Union, W.M.U., and a full working program. "Now," said Brother Worthington, "I want you to see 22 22

The dining room, they saw, was also used as the Sunday School class room for the Primaries, who sit on the benches and use the tables for their handwork.

As the group returned to the church, they saw that the people were beginning to come, so they quietly sat down and waited for the services to begin. They noted that most of the older people sat on one side and the younger ones on the other side. Several of the men wore long braids wrapped with gayly colored yarn, and blankets covered the shoulders of the older women. The young people on the other side were smartly dressed in the fashions of today.

They were particularly interested in a Junior girl who came leading a blind man into the service. They noticed how carefully the girl saw that the blind man was seated and made comfortable before she turned to be with her friend who had come with her.
