INTRODUCTION 

NLY those of us who tie our own flies experience the supreme thrill of fishing. This thrill comes not when we have just executed a perfect cast, or landed a high-voltage old sockdolager on super-light tackle, or stalked a timid fish from afar, but when, in addition to any or all of these, we have completed the triumph by tricking the fish to strike at a lure of our own making. That, my friends, is the supreme thrill of fishing! After all, of what avail the long and patient stalk, the perfect cast, the strong but sensitive. wrist, if the fish scorns our hook?

But fly tying goes even further than this in bringing pleasure to the fisherman. There is yet another sensation in store for him - the deep thrill of creation which he feels when he turns out a neat, delicate bit of feathered flimsy which he knows, as he takes it from the jaws of the vise and turns it over in his fingers, will certainly take fish. There is also the fact that the fishing season is extended to the full twelve months of the year five or six on the streams and the remainder at the bench, fashioning lures for the next outdoor season. 

And because fly tying is so fascinating and so worthwhile, I often wonder why more fishermen don't do it. Perhaps the reason lies in the common fallacy that tying flies is a difficult art. But it isn't. If you have a full complement of fingers in good working order and enough perseverance to see a thing through, you can learn to tie surprisingly good flies in a surprisingly short period of time.

Your first flies may seem coarse and ugly, but each one you tie is certain to be an improvement over the last. And however crude your early output may be, the chances are that they will take fish, all the same. A trout is much less particular than an angler who selects his stock from a tackle dealer's trays. By fishing with your own flies, even the first ones you tie, you will stimulate your own interest in tying and your work is bound to improve thereby. 

This book is written exclusively for the beginner. It attempts to reduce fly tying to its simplest possible form. No doubt many experts will take exception to some of the tying methods I advocate, [3]

but I sincerely believe that these methods will make the beginner's task easier. And after he has learned the mechanics of fly tying, he can lay this book aside and carry on under his own power, so to speak. 

A word about the pictures. The reader will note that the size of the hooks and the thickness of the tying thread are far out of proportion to that of the vise and some of the materials used. As a matter of fact, the hooks are tinned O'Shaughnessy 6/0, which I use surf casting for striped bass, and the thread is coarse white linen. After some experimenting I found that bronzed hooks of the size used for tying trout and bass flies would not "show up" in the pictures clearly enough for my purposes, and fine silk thread was totally lost in the prints. Hence, I advise readers to overlook the lack of proportion between, for instance, the size of the hook and the jaw of the vise that holds it, remembering that the purpose of the pictures is to illustrate as clearly and simply as possible the several steps entailed in tying a fly.

Before committing the reader to the body of this book, I should like to give him Edmund Ware Smith's classic definition of a fly. 

A well-known angling authority and writer on angling subjects, he has written a paragraph that should hang framed over every fly tyer's bench: "A fly is not a fly at all. It is something which, in the conversation of a fisherman, is referred to as such - but nothing is further from the truth! The standard materials used in fly tying are, first, the hook, consisting of three prime parts: the eye (to which the leader is attached), the shank (to which the body or general dressing is attached), and the barb, to which, it is hoped, a large fish will eventually be attached; second, the dressing, consisting of the hair of many mammals, the feathers of many fowls, and tinsel of many varieties. These, and sometimes a great many other materials, are wound with silk thread upon the hook, and the result is beautiful to behold."

That the reader's results may all be beautiful to behold is my sincere wish.

Son CHAPTER I Fly Tying Tools and Equipment 

OMEONE once said that a workman is no better than his tools. In no field of endeavor is that more true than in fly tying. If your vise will not hold a hook firmly in its jaws, if your scissors won't cut and your hackle pliers won't hold, you cannot expect to tie a good fly. It would be well nigh impossible, even for the expert. 

Unfortunately, most beginners are reluctant to invest much money in fly tying equipment until they are sure that they are going to stay with it. After a few unsuccessful attempts to tie a fly with clumsy, inefficient tools, they usually give up the whole idea as a bad job. In doing so they've not only wasted the money they spent on equipment, but they have also economized themselves out of the most fascinating hobby a man can have. 

No, spend an extra dollar or two and get the best. Good equipment will soon pay for itself with dividends of pleasant hours at the tying bench and flies you can be proud to call your own.

This may sound as though the original investment in fly tying tools and equipment were a costly one. But it isn't. In this chapter are given the prices at which good, serviceable equipment can be bought. You will be surprised to see how little it will cost you – so little, in fact, that there is really no need at all of trying to save the odd penny.

The Vise 

Without doubt the most important single item of fly tying equipment is the vise. A good one that will hold hooks of all sizes without slipping and permit you to work on a fly from all angles is a joy forever; a poor one is the heaviest cross a fly tyer can bear. [5]

On the market now are several good fly tying vises ranging in price from around two dollars up to five. Personally, I feel that it is poor economy to save money when buying your vise, because you'll use one that is unsatisfactory only as long as your temper lasts, and then you'll junk it and buy a better one anyway. The vise I use (1A) cost $2.50 and has been worth every cent I paid for it. 1A When you're looking 'em over with intent to buy, test the gripping power of the jaws with an assortment of hooks of different sizes, ranging from No. 18 dry fly hooks up to No. 2's for large streamers and bucktails. If the vise has strong, firm jaws which can be handily adjusted, it 

I should hold both No. 18's and No. 2's equally well. If you can move the hook in the tightened vise jaws with your fingers, you can do the same when you are making a painstaking operation on a fly.

Another important feature to look for is the position of the jaws. 

If they are on the end of a horizontal rod it will be almost impossible to get the left hand around it so that material can be held in place. 

The rod or bar should be set at an angle, slanting upward and to the right (if you are right-handed) so that it will not interfere with the use of the left hand.

Gadgets attached to the vise for holding working thread between operations are not important. Clips should be attached to the edge. of the bench, where they are out of the way, rather than to the vise itself, where they will catch loose thread and materials more frequently when you don't want them to than when you do. 

Although it is not absolutely essential, it is better to obtain a vise which can be adjusted for height and position. If you pay more than three dollars for one, the chances are that it will provide the necessary adjustments. Usually the beginner becomes so accustomed to the first vise he uses that he does not find it necessary to alter the position of the jaws. [6]

It is possible to obtain magnifying mirrors which can be attached to the vise in such a way that both sides of the fly are visible to the tyer. Many good tyers swear that these mirrors are indispensable, but relatively few tyers use them, and I believe they are more of a nuisance than an aid to a beginner. 

When buying a vise, make sure that the jaws are closed by a clamp, rather than a thumb screw. The clamp is quicker and easier to use, and eliminates the possibility of crossing threads which happens occasionally with set-screw vises. 

Hackle Pliers

This is another "must" item for the fly tyer. Hackle pliers are used to grip either the tip or butt of a feather when hackling both wet and dry flies, and also as a weight to hold thread taut between operations (1B). When buying pliers, test them to make sure there is plenty of strength and spring in the body, and look closely at the jaws, which should be narrow, flat, smooth-edged, and in perfect alignment. 

Inexpensive hackle pliers cost from 18 ten cents to a quarter, and are usually quite satisfactory after the gripping surfaces of the jaws have been slightly corrugated with a file, and the sharp edges of the jaws rounded so that delicate materials will not be cut. There is now on the market hackle pliers which have a pad of soft corrugated rubber covering the jaw surfaces. These constitute deluxe equipment to the writer, who has always used 25-cent pliers with perfect satisfaction. Scissors As you will use scissors from ten to twenty times in the tying of a single fly, a sharp, strong, fine-pointed pair will soon pay their way. [7]

As a matter of fact, I keep two pairs on my fly tying bench at all times, one for fine, delicate work and the other for heavy duty, such as cutting tinsel, feather butts and coarse bucktail. Personally, I prefer curved blades for the fine work and straight blades for heavy duty use.

Curved-blade fingernail scissors (1C) which will be quite satis- 1C factory for all-around fly tying use can be bought at the five-and-ten, and to tell the truth, these are the same that are listed in some catalogs as fly tyers' scissors. The cost is usually 20 cents in the five-and-ten; from a quarter to a halfdollar from the supply houses. However, there are many good fly tying scissors listed by reputable dealers which are especially adapted for close, fine work, and a dollar spent on these will be a dollar well invested. Inexpensive five-and-ten scissors will be perfectly satisfactory, however, for coarse work. 

Dubbing Needle 

Used mostly to pick out hackle that has been doubled under, and to provide a long, fine point for separating strands of silk floss, to divide wings, etc. These bodkins (1D) can be 10 bought for a dime from most supply houses, but it is a simple matter to make one yourself. Merely sink the eye of a long darning needle into a cork stopper, making sure it is seated firmly, and you have a dubbing needle that will do anything the "boughten" ones will. Wax Although it is not necessary to wax all the thread used to tie flies, it certainly does no harm, and probably adds to the life of the [8]

fly by partially waterproofing the bindings and preventing rot from dampness. It is necessary, however, to wax thread used in tying wool or fur bodies, as the material must adhere to the thread. In addition, it prevents thread from slipping when tied to the bare hook shank. Most tyers use a small cake, drawing the thread through it (1E) once or twice. Others, however, have found that furniture and automobile wax paste serves the purpose equally well when applied with the finger tips. 

Cobblers' wax costs from 5 to 10 cents a cake, and will last almost indefinitely. Lacquer 

Most supply houses list lacquer which is presumably made specifically for fly tyers. The writer, however, has found that ordinary household lacquer which 1E can be bought in the five-and-ten is perfectly satisfactory - and much, much cheaper. A ten-cent can will last two or three seasons if it is kept covered so that contact with air does not harden it. Clear lacquer should, of course, be used; rarely is it necessary to color the head of a fly.

To give the head of a fly a finish that is both durable and smooth, I have found it best to give it two coats, the first with thin lacquer and, after that has thoroughly dried, the second with thick lacquer. 

The first coat soaks into the thread, cementing it solidly and waterproofing it through the first several layers, and the second covers it with a smooth glossy finish that is impervious to water. To thicken lacquer, let it stand uncovered overnight, then stir it thoroughly. 

The longer it is left uncovered, the thicker it will become. If it becomes too thick, mix thin lacquer until the proper consistency is obtained. The thin lacquer will remain so as long as it is kept tightly covered. [9]

Apply lacquer with a sharpened toothpick (1F) except for the smallest flies, when it is better to use the point of a round-headed pin. 

Wipe off the point of the pin or toothpick immediately after use so it will not become gummed with old lacquer. 

Thread Clips 

Quite frequently when tying a fly it becomes necessary to keep the working thread taut and out of the way while some other operation is being finished. 

Many tyers clip hackle pliers to the end of the thread and let it dangle, but many times it hangs in your way, particularly when working under the hook. 1F A better way to dispose of the thread is to half-hitch it and clip it either to some part of the vise or to the edge of the work bench. For this purpose many supply houses advertise metal thread clips, but since metal edges often cut through fine working thread, I have found homemade clips of split cork much more satisfactory. Using a sharp knife or 1G razor blade, cut a narrow wedge of cork completely around an ordinary bottle stopper and nail or screw it to the edge of the work bench. Pull the thread tightly halfway around the stopper (1G) and it will bite into the cork, holding firmly without danger of fraying or cutting the thread. If you are right-handed, fasten the cork thread clip to the edge of the bench on your right. After some experimenting, I found that placing my thread clip about 12 inches from the vise enables me to keep the thread out of my way while I am doing something else on the fly. [ 10 ]

Thread 

Working thread should be both strong and fine - not one or the other, but both. Nothing is more exasperating than to have the thread break just as you are mid-way through a particularly painstaking bit of work, as will inevitably happen to experienced tyers as well as to beginners. For this reason it is not good economy to buy thread at the five-and-ten unless you know what you are getting. And only a few threads manufactured for sewing and stitching purposes are suited to fly tying. It will be much safer for the beginner to buy his working thread from a supply house, and thus be sure he is getting materials which are adapted to fly tying use. 

The two most commonly used thread sizes are 00 and 000, the latter being the finer of the two. Some experts use thread even finer than this, but I would not advise it for beginners, as it will break too often under inexpert fingers. 

Only one color is necessary-black. Use 00 for streamers, bucktails, and wet flies larger than No. 8. For smaller wets and all dry flies the triple-0 size is preferable, as it does not bulk on a hook and enables the tyer to finish off with smaller, neater heads. 

The cost of working thread is so low that any tyer can afford the best. Fifty-yard spools of both 00 and 000, either black or in any colors you choose, can be bought for a dime. This thread is also spooled in 100-yard lengths to sell for twenty cents. The colored thread in 00 size is also used for rod winding. 

Hooks 

There are so many different types of hooks that can be used for fly tying that to discuss them all would only serve to confuse the reader. For the purpose of this book, which after all is to show beginners how to tie flies in the easiest possible manner, it will be necessary to mention only three: Limerick, Sproat, and Model Perfect. The first two are the types most commonly used for streamers, bucktails, wet flies, and nymphs. Model Perfects are used by most tyers for dry flies, as the wire is fine and light. Short-shank Sproats [11]

are excellent for wet flies. A glance at the illustrations will show the difference among the three types. 

For all streamers and bucktails I use Limericks exclusively. The straight stem of the barb and the narrow bend seem to hook and hold fish better than any other. The principal advantage of a narrowbend hook like the Limerick lies in the 23 fact that during a long fight with a fish the hook cannot saw back and forth in the flesh or cartilage of a fish's mouth, cutting a hole which eventually becomes so large that the hook may fall out. When buying

Limerick hooks specify that you want turned down, looped eyes. The looped eye has no gap like that of the ringed eye in which a leader knot may slip or become cut on the sharp edge of the wire. And the turned down eye makes it possible for the fisherman to pass his leader through the loop and turle-knot it around the neck of the shank, making a more secure tie and putting the strain of a heavy fish on the strongest part of the hook, which is the shank, rather than on the eye. 

Most popular hooks for fly tying. 

Left to right: Long-shank Sproat,

Long-shank Limerick, Model Per fect, and Hump-shank for cork bodied bass bugs.

Sproat hooks differ from Limericks in that the bend is wide, forming almost a complete half-circle. Aside from this feature there is little difference between Sproats and Limericks, as both may be obtained with looped turned-down eyes in a range of sizes. 

Model Perfect hooks are used by most tyers for dry flies. This style bends entirely from shank to barb, and is one of the suresthooking of all the different types. Buy turned-down eyes for all except the smallest flies, in which the eye may interfere with the "bite" of the hook, or the distance between shank and hook-point, in which case turned-up-eye hooks should be used. 

Do not buy offset-bend hooks for fly tying. While this type has some advantages for bait fishing, it is inferior to the straight bend. 

And don't use the same hooks for wet and dry flies. Heavy, thick [12]

hooks are best for wet flies, as the additional weight sinks the lure, while fine wire hooks which are light in weight make a more floatable dry fly.

Hooks are sized by numbers; the smaller the number the larger the hook. For instance, a No. 4 hook is larger than a No. 6, the difference being a little less than 1/16 of an inch. The following table will serve as a rough guide to beginners for selecting the proper hook sizes.

Hooks cost from 20 to 35 cents a dozen, but considerable money can be saved if two or more fly tyers chip in and buy hooks by the hundred. Hooks cost much more in small quantities because of the time and work required to sort and count dozen lots. [13]

CHAPTER II Fly Tying Materials 

T WOULD be utterly impossible for an amateur fly tyer to maintain a complete stock of fly tying materials. The different kinds of feathers and fur used for fly making are countless. They come from birds and animals of every land and some are priced in proportion to the rarity of the species from which they are plucked. For the purposes of this book, which is to help the beginner to learn fly tying in the easiest and least expensive manner, it will suffice to list only those materials which will be necessary for normal purposes. 2A Bear in mind that suitable substitutes are available for some materials that are too costly for the amateur. Wood ducks, for instance, enjoy complete protection from the Federal government, and the feathers from this handsome fellow are difficult to obtain. But except for certain flies which call for wood duck feathers and nothing else, the amateur tyer can get along very well with substitutes - and I doubt that the fish will ever discover the deception. 

The following materials, most of which are illustrated in 2A, are those which are most useful to fly tyers. In listing them, I have added some suggestions for selecting the best materials, and have indicated the range in which the material will be priced: 

Silk Floss: This material is very useful for making bodies. Floss silk is sold either by the spool or skein, and can be obtained in a variety of colors. Black, yellow and red are the most commonly used. 

If possible, obtain the single-strand floss, as it lays flatter and makes [ 14 ]

a smoother body. Two- and three-strand floss is perfectly satisfactory, however, if the tyer will carefully separate the strands before starting to wind the body.

Silk floss costs about 10 cents for a 5-yard skein. This floss can also be obtained from dry goods stores, but be sure to accept nothing. but pure silk.

Chenille: One of the most useful body materials, especially for wet flies and bucktails. May be obtained in a variety of colors, of which yellow, white, red and black will be the most useful, and in four sizes: very fine, fine, medium, and large. Buy "fine" for flies of size 8 to 12, and medium for all others except No. 2 and larger. 

Chenille costs about 5 cents a yard or from 12 to 20 cents for 5-yard skeins, depending upon size. Most supply houses also offer an assortment of sizes and colors for about 25 cents a package. 

Tinsel: Metal tape used either to make a full silver or gold body or to rib over other material. Tinsel is made in only two colors for fly tying - gold and silver - but may be obtained in wide, medium or narrow width, flat or oval, and either smooth or embossed. The embossed tinsel makes an attractive body as its surface will be broken up, catching and reflecting the light better, but it is not as strong as the smooth tinsel.

Many supply houses advertise their tinsel as non-tarnishing, but the statement is nothing more than a snare and a delusion, as every type of tinsel I have used has tarnished unless it has been previously lacquered.

Tinsel costs about 10 cents for a card of from 5 to 10 yards, depending upon the width. It may also be bought by the spool at a slight saving.

Bucktail: The best hair for bucktail flies is obtained from the tails of young deer and does. This hair is finer, softer, and easier to handle than the stiffer tail hair of a buck. Many tyers hunt deer in season and so get at least one tail for their own use; others have friends who hunt, and who can be persuaded to save the flag in exchange for a few trout flies.

When buying bucktail, notice the texture of the hair, and take only those which are not brittle or stiff. Some dyes stiffen the hair, [15]

so examine all artificially colored tails carefully. Bucktails cost an average of $1 each. Most tyers prefer to buy the natural tails and dye the hair themselves as an economy measure. 

Squirreltail: Obtained from gray, red, and fox squirrels. Squirreltail is used like bucktail, and makes excellent flies for trout, bass and panfish. As the squirreltail hairs are not hollow like deer hair, and does not compress, the beginner should be careful to avoid making a large bulky head. Use less hair and when ready to finish off, trim the surplus carefully to a taper to form a smooth base for the head. 

Squirreltails are relatively inexpensive, being listed by most supply houses at 10 or 15 cents each. 

Maribou: These feathers, used by dressmakers for years to make fans and ornaments for milady's hats and slippers, make excellent wings for streamer-type flies. The fibres are extremely soft and fluffy, and when drawn through the water have a flirting, sinuous action that is most seductive to game fish. 

Maribou flies may be winged with either one or two feathers; one usually being sufficient except for extremely large hooks. Maribou wings are simple to tie. Strip off the fibres at the base of the stem (2B) and lay it over the shank of the hook, tying it in place firmly. Junglecock shoulders dress up a maribou fly very effectively and serve the extra purpose of wedging the maribou into shape. 28 Maribou can be obtained in a variety of colors, white, red and yellow being the most useful. The feathers are usually sold in small quantities at about a nickel a pair and fifty cents a dozen. When selecting maribou, select those with the straightest, finest stems and the softest fibres. 

Hackle Feathers: These feathers, which serve a multitude of fly-tying purposes, may come from two parts of a bird: the neck, which supplies the best hackles for dry flies, and the back or saddle, which many tyers prefer for streamers and wet flies. [ 16 ]

-- Hackle feathers may be purchased in bulk by the pound, strung - that is, sewn together at the butts and roughly sorted for size and the complete neck on the skin (2C). By all means buy the complete neck. It will be easier to select feathers of the size and shape required, and will be much cheaper in the long run. 

For dry fly tying, hackle should be stiff and glossy; for wet flies the softer 2C hackles are preferable. If you can afford only one neck, by all means select one with stiff hackles, as these are ideal for dry flies and can be used for wets, while soft hackles are useless for dry flies. 

Hackle necks with soft feathers suitable for wet flies and streamers can be bought for around a dollar, both dyed and natural. For dry fly hackles a much higher grade of neck is required. The 

birds which supply these feathers are usually fed a special diet and killed in the winter when the plumage is in prime condition; hence good hackle necks suitable for dry flies will cost more two dollars being about average. You can save money by buying a neck in partnership with another tyer and dividing it with a razor blade, cutting from top to bottom so that each gets a full selection of feathers. 

Breast Feathers: Used principally for making fanwings. These are the soft, curved feathers that grow on the breasts of all ducks, the most useful being from white domestic ducks, mallards, and teal. 

Most fly tyers' supply houses package these feathers in small quantities and list them at from five to ten cents a dozen. The fibres from these feathers are also useful for making throats, as they are much longer and therefore easier to handle than the shorter hackle fibres. 

Wing Feathers: The large flight feathers from big birds are used principally for winging both wet and dry flies. Buy only in matched pairs - one from the left wing and one from the right and select only broad, stiff, straight-edged feathers. 

[ 17 ]

Many different birds supply feathers suitable for winging flies, but the most useful come from geese, turkeys (2D), and ducks. Other wing materials are made from the flight feathers of pheasants, crow, guinea hen, and sea gulls.

The tyer can buy complete matched wings or the flight feathers only, and my advice is to get only the flight feathers, as these are the ones that are most useful. The complete wings cost around 25 cents a pair while the big flight feathers can be obtained for about a nickel. 2D 

When buying dyed feathers, examine them closely to ascertain whether or not the dye has stiffened the fibres so much that they are brittle, in which case they are almost useless. 

Junglecock: This is one of the most expensive materials the fly tyer can buy, yet I use it on all streamers and bucktails and firmly believe that it adds much to the attractiveness of the fly, not only to the fisherman but to the fish as well. 

When buying junglecock, it will be cheaper in the long run to obtain a complete neck, even though the original outlay of cash seems staggering. With the complete neck the tyer has a full assortment of sizes and can match two "eyes," or feathers, closely. And it is well to shop around when you buy a junglecock neck, as otherwise you may be stuck with a neck of split feathers. Examine the eyes carefully to see that the fibres form a smooth, glossy surface just as though they had been lacquered, and of course, try to find a neck with as many feathers as possible. 

Complete junglecock necks cost from $2.50 to $3.50 for "second quality," while the better grades run from $4.50 up to $6. It is good business to buy the first quality necks, even if doing so means that the neck must be shared with another tyer. By splitting the neck from top to bottom with a razor blade each partner will get a complete assortment of sizes, from the very smallest feathers at the top of the neck to the largest at the bottom. [ 18 ]

Pheasant Crests: The topnotch of both the golden and Lady 

Amherst pheasants is a crest of curved lustrous feathers which are very useful to the fly tyer for use as overlay on wings of both wet flies and streamers, and for tails. Golden pheasant crests are bright yellow, and each individual crest is curved so that it lays well over the wings of a streamer. The crest of the Lady Amherst is bright red, and shaped like that of the golden pheasant. 

Single crest feathers may be bought for 15 or 20 cents a dozen, but money will be saved by buying the entire head and neck, thus getting the complete crest and the tippet feathers besides. 

Tippets: These come from the neck of both the Lady Amherst and golden pheasants. The individual tippets are stiff-fibred, making ideal tails for dry flies, and when trimmed down to small sizes make excellent wings for trout and bass flies. Golden pheasant tippets are yellow barred with black, the Lady Amherst white barred with black (2E). 2E A dozen tippets in assorted sizes cost 10 or 15 cents, but a complete pheasant neck with enough tippets to last a full season and the complete crest besides costs only about a dollar, and I advise readers to buy the complete neck, even if it must be shared with another tyer. I do not, however, advise readers to buy the whole pheasant skin, as there is little useful material on it except for the crest, tippets, and flight feathers.

Peacock Herl and Sword: The tail plumes of the peacock are very useful to fly tyers, who use the fibres for making. herl and quill bodies and for striping or overlaying streamer and bucktail flies.

Although herl can be bought cheaply by the package, a better quality can be obtained by buying the complete plume. This provides a large number of herl fibres of various length and thickness, and in addition the tyer gets the eye of the feather which is useful for making quill bodies. A single eyed tail plume costs about 10 cents. [ 19 ]

Peacock sword is a thin, pointed feather with blue-green herl which is excellent for overlaying both streamer and standard wet fly wings and making tails and throats. The sword is not so satisfactory for bodies, however, as the individual fibres are not solidly filled out like the eyed plumes. When buying, select plumes that are bushy and well filled out. [ 20 ]

CHAPTER III 

Tricks that Make Fly-tying Easier

HERE ARE no short cuts to fly-tying skill. The tyer must learn through experience and trial and error. He will get no sudden blinding flash which will reveal how to whip-finish a head with a simple twist of the fingers. There is no absurdly simple trick which will enable him to tie perfectly balanced fanwings time after time without the slightest difficulty. Even the most expert tyer still has his troubles, but those same experts will admit that the more flies they tied, the easier and quicker and better they tied them.

No. Fly tying must be learned the hard way - through practice. 

There are, however, a few methods and kinks that will be of immense help to the beginner. That is why I believe this chapter should be the most valuable of the entire book; learning how to make fly tying easier by using the simplest and most amateur-proof methods will save the beginner much discouragement and start him tying creditable flies in a reasonably short time. 

Tying the Half-hitch

Although many, and probably most, fly-tyers use few or no half-hitches in tying a fly, this simple little knot is a great boon to the beginner. Its most important use is to secure the work at different stages of the fly-tying operation while the material to be used next is prepared. It is not necessary to use half-hitches, as the working thread can be thoroughly waxed and kept taut between operations. by clipping hackle pliers to the loose end and letting it dangle. However, the beginner will find that occasionally the thread becomes loose and the work for instance, a nicely cocked pair of wings over which he has labored for a full half hour - either falls off the hook [ 21 ]

3C A 3 B entirely or drops out of position. In addition, a loose end of thread with hackle pliers clipped to the end will get in the way as often as not. Beginners will be better off using the half-hitch. By using it they can be sure that an operation once finished will not have to be done over again and the finished fly will be more durable. 

Tying the half-hitch is simple. Assuming that your working thread is attached to the hook, hold the end of the thread with your left hand - if you are right-handed and pull the thread taut toward you. Press the middle and forefinger of the right hand down on the taut thread (3A). Bring the thread in your left hand over to a point just under the eye of the hook, at the same time spreading the middle and forefinger of the right hand, forming a triangle with the thread (3B). Now turn the right hand palm up, bringing the loop thus formed over the hook (3C). This will cross the thread under the eye of the hook. Holding the thread taut with your forefinger, take the middle finger from the loop. Use the middle finger and thumb to pinch the crossed threads against the hook (3D). Remove your forefinger from the loop and pull down with the left hand. If you have followed these directions, you have tied a half-hitch which will hold much better than a taut but untied thread. And with a little practice making a quick half-hitch [ 22 ]

will become an unconscious motion like shifting gears in your car or tying a bow knot in your shoelaces.

The Whip-finish 

At the outset of their fly-tying career many beginners finish off the heads of their 3D flies with a series of half-hitches. Cemented with lacquer, this head may last," but like a chain and its weak link, a series of half-hitches is no stronger than the last half-hitch, and the head may in time unravel. Far better to learn the proper whip-finish, which is neater, more durable, and when once learned no harder to tie than a half-hitch.

There are several ways to make a whip finish, all giving the same result in the end. Some tiers hold a dubbing needle with the left hand a couple of inches over the head of the fly parallel with the shank and wind the thread around needle and hook, forming five or six tight loops through which the end of the thread is then passed (3E) and pulled tight, drawing the loops together. The surplus thread is then clipped off, and you 3E have a whip-finished head. 

Although one is tempted to advise this method, since it is much simpler to explain (and many good fly tyers use it, besides), I strongly recommend that the beginner learn the method which follows, as it is easier to use, once learned, is much quicker, more accident-proof, and makes it possible to form a smooth, neatly tapered head not possible with the other method. 

This may sound complicated, but if you put a hook in a vise, secure the thread to the shank just behind the eye, and follow each step carefully, I'm sure you will get it first try: [ 23 ]

As I write this, with my fly-tying vise at my elbow, a hook in it and a thread attached with the whip finish I made as I wrote the last few paragraphs, I am wondering just how long it takes to make this whip-finish. I've never timed myself before, and this seems a good time to do it.

Five seconds flat. That was for a whip-finish with five complete wrappings and the surplus thread broken off clean. It would take a little longer when shaping up the head of a fly; the experiment was on a bare hook. The short period of time required to tie the whipfinish (even by someone who has had the advantage of practicing on thousands of flies) is in marked contrast to the long one required to describe it. 

Dyeing Your Own 

As a fly tyer you will use feathers and hair of many different colors, most of them dyed. Whether to dye your own depends largely upon whether you depend upon fly tyers' supply houses or the marksmanship of yourself or your friends for your materials. If the former, my advice is to buy dyed materials, which cost very little more and are worth the difference, as amateur dyeing often ruins both the material to be dyed and the conjugal happiness of the family in which the attempt is made. Dyes discolor cooking utensils, spot linoleum and rugs, splatter over the stove, and stain porcelain sinks. It is a tolerant and loving wife who will permit her husband to dye fly tying material elsewhere than in the cellar or garage. [26]

-- 
If you do want to try dyeing — and all tyers do at least once wash all dirt and natural oil from the material thoroughly in warm soapy water. Rinse several times in clean water and dry. Buy a small quantity of dye powder of the desired color and from then on follow directions on the package carefully. Frankly, I have found that dyeing is bothersome, results uncertain, and that in the end the dyed materials have cost me as much and possibly more than if I had bought them ready dyed from a supply house. Only if you use a large amount of colored material would it pay you to dye it yourself. 

I make one exception to this. Feathers and bucktail can be dyed a rich golden yellow by using picric acid powder, obtainable at any drugstore. Stir the powder into a quart of warm - not hot water and test frequently with pieces of white cloth until the desired shade is obtained. Naturally, the more powder used in a given quantity of water, the deeper the shade. Using this powder you can dye feathers and bucktails all shades of yellow from light canary to brilliant gold. 

This dye will remain fast through the normal life of a fly, and does not seem to affect the texture of the material. Many commercial dyes make the material stiff and brittle. 

Holding Junglecock in Place 

Maybe this one isn't cricket, but it's a trick well known to all fly tyers and under certain maddening circumstances quite permissable. 

Junglecock eyes, for example, sometimes stubbornly refuse to lie flatly in place. The thin stems of the feather twist, throwing the junglecock out of line when you tie them in place, and all the patient plucking and patting in the world won't make 'em lie down. In that event, before making another try at tying them in, daub the inside of the feathers lightly with thin lacquer, press them in their proper place, and hold them pinched tightly between thumb and forefinger while they are tied securely in position. The lacquer will hold them in flat alignment, and if too much lacquer is not used, the deception will never be noticed. [ 27 ]

Tying Off Small Dry Flies 

After you have given a small dry fly a bristling hackle collar, you often find that you have not left enough "neck" to the fly to tie off the head without tying down the hackle also. Trouble in tying off can be eliminated by punching a small round hole just large enough to pass over the eye of the hook in a square card of isinglass or thin cardboard. When you are ready to tie off the head, pass the thread through the hole and bring the card back over the eye of the hook so it presses the hackle back out of your way. This leaves. the neck of the hook free to be finished off. 

Tarnish-proof Tinsel

Tinsel tarnishes after exposure to water and air unless it is protected. For this reason, it is advisable to give the tinsel a coat of thin lacquer and permit it to dry before being used. Some tyers paint the whole spool, letting the lacquer soak through the first few layers, and when this is used apply another dressing. Better than this method, however, is the simpler one of cutting an evening's supply of tinsel to the most commonly used length and using the thumb and forefingers to coat the strips individually. 

Feather Curvature

Occasionally in an unguarded moment a tyer will buy a hackle neck of feathers which are curved excessively. Such feathers will be very difficult to handle, particularly if they are to be used for streamer flies. They can be easily flattened, however, by pressing them under a damp cloth with a hot iron. Moths 

Moths are the fly tyer's greatest and most active enemy. No feather or strand of hair is safe from their ravages unless properly protected. 

And moth balls scattered through boxes and drawers of materials are [ 28 ]

not enough. Your furs and feathers will never be safe from moths unless you dust them liberally with moth flakes so that the flakes work. through the outer layer of hair or feathers. Keeping materials in airtight cedar boxes will help. But even so, it will pay you to inspect your supply of materials at least once a week – winter, summer, spring and fall.

Finding Lost Hooks 

Every once in a while a hook will tinkle to the floor and defy all efforts to find it until the next time you pass that way in your bare feet. A small magnet costs little, and will soon pay for itself by finding lost hooks (at 25 to 35 cents a dozen) and saving iodine for punctured feet. 

Envelope for Feathers 

Much of the material used in fly tying is bought in small quantities pheasant crests, tippets, etc. To have this material where you can always put your hand on it, keep it in envelopes, each marked on the outside as to its contents and filed alphabetically in a narrow drawer or box. Envelopes of the No. 10 size - 9½" wide by 4" deep - are best for small quantities; the larger brown paper envelopes 6" wide by 9" deep are also useful for storing hackle necks and bucktail. [ 29 ]

CHAPTER IV 

Streamers and Bucktails

N TYING streamers and bucktails, the tyer should keep in mind the fact that the finished product should represent a small food fish in the water. It must be long, thin, sinuous, streamlined. It must also be approximately the same shape and size as small fry upon which game fish feed. Compared with these two qualities, color is of relatively little importance, except that a touch of tinsel, which adds flash to the fly and makes it more readily noticed, is never out of place. 

Streamers and bucktails, being long flies, are tied on long-shanked or extra-long-shanked hooks, the latter usually for bucktails and the former for streamers, although satisfactory flies of the streamer type may be tied with either. 

The first and most obvious step in fly-tying is to insert the hook in the vise an act, however, that is not quite so simple as it sounds. Place only the bend of the hook in the vise never the straight shank or the barb, for it is the bend itself that offers the most gripping surface. The shank of the hook should be level, and if you are right handed, the eye should be at your right. Make sure that the hook is firm in the grip of the vise before you start work there is nothing so maddening as a hook that begins to slip about just as you reach a particularly tricky part of the fly-tying operation. 

Many fly-tyers insist that the working thread should be thoroughly waxed before you start, but I have never found this necessary except when tying dry flies. Instead, coat the shank of the hook liberally with thick lacquer, so that some of it will be absorbed by the body that will later cover it. This makes the body of the fly stronger, more durable, and will prevent it from slipping around the shank as you work. [ 30 ]

Now comes the first step of actual fly-tying. Cut from your spool of working thread a length of about two feet. This is more than you will need, but thread is cheap, and you'll want some surplus to work with when you finish off the head of the fly. Holding the end of the thread in your left hand, use your right to lay it over the shank of the hook about a quarter-inch ahead of the point where the bend starts, and make three or four turns, wrapping the thread over itself, and securing it with a half-hitch. Clip off the short end as close to the hook as possible, and let the remainder hang until needed for the next operation. 

Tying the Streamer

Let us say we are tying a Black Ghost streamer, a popular pattern and a good example of its kind. For the Ghost, and all flies the bodies of which are to be ribbed, the first material used is tinsel - silver for the Ghost. From the spool clip off about five inches of ribbing, taper one end to a point, and use the left hand to lay the tapered end at an angle over the wrapping you 4A have already made on the hook (4A), allowing the long end to hang to one side of the bend. Take two or three tight turns about the tinsel, secure with a halfhitch, and leave it hanging there. You are now ready to tie on the tail of the fly.

The tail of the Black Ghost is yellow. It is properly made with a single crest feather from a golden pheasant, but I find a small section cut from a yellow wing feather serves equally as well. Lay the section over the tail of the hook so that it curves upward over and beyond the bend. Pinch tightly while binding the material in place so that it will not twist to one side or the other (4B). Halfhitch in place, and touch the winding with a drop of lacquer. [ 31 ]

The next operation is winding on the body of silk floss, and a much neater job will result if you start this at the head rather than the tail, where it will bunch, so wind your working silk up to the head of the hook, half-hitching it in place about a quarter-inch behind. the eye. Now clip off about eight inches of black silk floss, which 4B will be plenty long enough for longshanked hooks in No. 6 and 8 size. Bind the end of this to the hook where you made your last tie, half-hitch it securely in place, and clip off the short end of surplus floss as closely as possible. 

To make a smooth, shiny body, the strands of floss silk should be unraveled, dividing it into separate strands. Insert the point of your stiletto scissors between them, and strip it down. Do this several times, until the floss is entirely unraveled. 

You are now ready to lay on the body.

Wind the silk floss evenly toward the bend of the hook, passing it from one hand to the other and laying it on as thinly and smoothly as possible. Bring it back to the tail fibres, winding a thin layer of floss over the thread that holds the tail, and start the floss forward again. Work carefully now; if the floss bunches, unwind that part of the body and try it again until you have built up the shank with a thick, smooth layer of silk slightly tapered at both ends. 

When you have wound the silk up to the working thread, take two or three firm turns around the floss with the thread, pulling the surplus floss under the shank past the eye of the hook. Be sure that your work is securely half-hitched at this point, and a drop of lacquer on the last knot will serve to make the body more secure. Clip off the surplus floss as close to the tie as possible. 

The body is now complete except for ribbing the tinsel, which has been hanging from the tail of the hook and doubtless getting in your way as you worked. Ribbing the body is quite important, in that a poor job will show up, but is not at all difficult. [32]

First, lift up the tail and make a wind or two with the tinsel around the hook under the tail. Then hold the tail firmly while you wrap the tinsel two or three times over the base of the tail fibres, thus wrapping them in a neat bunch and hiding up any of the working silk that may not have been covered by floss. The rest of the ribbing operation is simple; wind the tinsel spirally forward to the head of the hook, spacing the ribs evenly and not too closely (4C). When you reach the end of the body, tie off the tinsel with one or two winds of the working thread, half-hitch it securely, and clip off the surplus. 

Next comes a really tricky operation - the one at which most beginners make their most obvious mistakes: tying on the wings of the streamer. 4C The success of this particular job depends much upon the quality of the hackle feathers you use. Select four white feathers from a neck - feathers that are of the same length and width and of uniform but not excessive curvature. 

Lay one feather over the shank of the hook to get the desired length, keeping in mind that the wings of a streamer fly should be about one-third again as long as the hook, and strip off the surplus hackles at the butt. This feather can now be used for measuring the others. Strip 4.D the other hackle feathers so that all are of the same length and shape. 

To make a neat fly, the wings must be matched and laid one against the other so that the four will look like a single heavy feather. 

The beginner may have a bit of trouble getting this effect, but will get onto the knack of it in a short time. [ 33 ]

The feathers are matched one at a time. Hold one flat between the thumb and forefingers of the right hand, rough or concave side up, with the butt pinched firmly. With your left hand take another feather and lay it, rough side down, over the first (4D), making sure the tips are even, and pinch this also between the fingers. Use the left hand to hold the feather by the shoulders (the broad part of the feather near the butt end) while you get a better "pinch" on the butts, and also to turn the feathers over so that additional ones may be laid on. When you have finished you will have laid the feathers, two to a side, evenly together so they look like one. During this, keep the butts lying side by side between your "pinching" fingers, else the feathers will twist and spring out of place in a most disconcerting manner. Now that they are all laid on evenly, however, shift your "pinching" fingers so you can hold the feathers at the butts between the thumbnail and the side of your forefinger - horizontally rather than flat and dig the nail into the butts. This makes the feathers stand up better when they have been tied on the hook. 

And tying these wing feathers properly will no doubt cause the beginner more anguish than any other tying operation. Under unpracticed fingers the feathers will fall apart, bulge out, or otherwise become separated, and when this happens there is nothing to do but take them off, sigh profoundly, and start the wings all over again. 

But with a little practice this apparently difficult feat will become as simple as any other operation in fly tying.

The trick is to lay the butts of the feathers evenly on the last windings of working thread so that they are flat against the top of the hook, and to hold them firmly in place between the pinching fingers of the left hand. Be sure the feathers are lying in the right position before you tie them on. To do this you will have swapped the feathers from your right to left hand, so that you now hold them at the shoulders with the fingers pinching both feathers and hook. 

The operation is now the same as that by which you tied on the tail. Holding the working thread with the right hand, bring it up between your pinching thumb and the hook, loop it over, and bring it down again on the other side of the shank, between the hook and [ 34 ]

4E forefinger, forming a loose winding or loop over the work (4E). Pull the thread downward until it tightens about the feathers at the butt, repeat the loop, make two or three turns, and fasten with a half-hitch. 

At this point you may find that one of the wing feathers has decided to strike out for itself, and has wandered from the others to flare defiantly up, down, or outward. This rebel may be put in place by pulling its butt while holding the feather in its proper place. If this doesn't work, you may find that it can be cut free and never missed. 

The beginner should expect some trouble on this score, but after he has tied a few streamers he will find that the wing feathers have decided to behave themselves.

When the wings have been placed to your satisfaction, bind them firmly in place with three or four turns of thread and a couple of half-hitches. Many tyers, the writer included, daub this winding with a drop of lacquer, which serves not only to make the tie more binding, but to waterproof the thread and prevent dampness from rotting the thread after use. 

The next step in tying the Black

Ghost is setting the junglecock shoulders in position. Select from the neck two "eyes" which match in size and marking, and strip off the soft fibres at the base so that both are exactly the same length and shape. Now place one of the eyes, glossy side out, against the shoulders of the wing feathers facing you and use the 4F thumb and forefinger of the left hand to hold it securely in place. 

With the right hand wind a couple of turns of thread around the butt of the junglecock (4F). If all goes well, it will lie flat against the wing [35]

feathers; if not, it may be straightened by pulling or twisting the butt. 

If the beginner runs into much trouble laying junglecock in place, he can avoid some of it by daubing a drop of lacquer on the inside of the eye the side which will be laid against the wing feathers. 

This will make it adhere to the wings, even if the tie should not hold it in its proper place. 

Putting on the second junglecock on the opposite side of the fly is a little more difficult, since the tyer must work over the fly.

However, the same procedure used to tie the first eye in place is followed. I do not advise trying to tie both junglecock eyes in place at once, although I have seen some fly tyers do it. And it will be much easier to convince the eye feathers to remain in place if the butt of the feather is not cut off until the very final operation. Later, when tying in the throat, the junglecock may spring out of place, and it will be much easier to twist it back into line if the butt is available to work with.

Now for the throat which, in the Black Ghost, is comprised of yellow hackle fibres. Pluck a hank of fibres from a hackle feather, roll it between the fingers to form a compact bunch, and tie it in under the neck of the hook at the same place where the wings and junglecock were tied. The procedure is the same: pinch fibers and hook-shank between the thumb and forefinger and loop the thread around the material, drawing tight and tying off with a half-hitch. 

Now for the final operation, and the one that can make your fly a graceful success or a grotesque failure: building the head. 

Your fly is virtually complete from the head back, but at the head you have the butts of four hackle feathers and two junglecock eyes. These butts should be clipped off as close as possible, but not so close that there is not enough to be held permanently by the working thread.

Start the head by covering the feather butts completely with thread, binding it quite tightly and winding back and forth until it presents a smooth surface that tapers down to the eye of the hook. 

Look under the fly to be sure you have covered the short ends of the throat fibres. When the head is formed to your satisfaction, tie off with the whip finish described in Chapter III. Coat the completed [36]

head first with thin lacquer, and after that has dried, another application of thick lacquer. The first will soak into the thread, making the tie permanent and virtually waterproof, while the second will give the head a smooth, glossy surface that is both attractive and durable. 

Tying the Bucktail

The procedure followed to tie bucktails differs little from that used with streamers. The various types of tail, body, and throat are described elsewhere in this book, but to make sure that the reader is off on the right foot, let's tie up a Dark Edson Tiger. The Tiger is a typical bucktail and in addition the most consistently effective lure the writer has ever used, and thus his favorite. 

Although few tyers do it, the writer always uses a band of silver tinsel in his Tigers, tying it in at the end of the shank just ahead of the bend, and continuing it halfway down the bend, winding in tight spirals down and back and tying off. This "tag" gives the fly a little sparkle that never seems amiss, although the tinsel band is not necessary, and, as a matter of fact, is somewhat of a nuisance to tie. 

The tail of the Tiger is made of fibres from a barred or mottled feather - Speckled Mandarin, Silver Pheasant Tippet, or even Guinea hackles. Tie in the tail by the same procedure used in tying the 

Black Ghost, and rather than clip off the excess, wind it to the shank of the hook so that the body will be a little thicker on top of the shank than underneath it.

The body of the Tiger is made of yellow chenille. Clip off a four-inch length, and strip the fuzz off one end for a half inch, baring the two threads that form the core of the chenille. Hold the chenille in your left hand and with your right tie the stripped ends in place just ahead of the bend with the working thread. Stripping the chenille in this manner eliminates a bulge in the body which results when the whole chenille is bound to the hook. 

Binding the stripped ends of the chenille tightly in place, wind the working thread spirally forward to the neck of the hook and [ 37 ]

secure it there so it will not get in your way. Then wind the chenille forward tightly so that it forms a smooth, solid body, and tie off at the neck (4G). Cut off the unused chenille, and the body is complete. Incidentally, the writer considers a chenille body the easiest and most fool-proof of all to tie. 4G Next, the wings, which in the Dark 

Tiger are brown bucktail. After experiments the writer has proven to his own satisfaction, if not to some of the skeptics with whom he fishes, that the dark hair clipped from a bucktail that has been dyed yellow is best for the Tiger. However, undyed brown will serve well enough, and most tyers use it. 

Clip from the bucktail about twice as much hair as you think you will use on the fly, cutting it close to the skin (4H). About half of this will be waste, as many of the hairs will be either too long or too short, and should be stripped out so that those which will be used will be of uniform length. The best way to strip out surplus hair is to grasp the hank between thumb and forefinger at one end, and pull out loose hair from the other. Then reverse the procedure, holding it by the other end and plucking. 

You will find that about half of the hank has been stripped out, leaving only hairs that match in length. 4H 

The length of the bucktail "wings" is a matter for you to decide for yourself, although the writer likes them just long enough to reach about a half inch beyond the bend of the hook. 

Tying in the bucktail is not a difficult job. Hold the hank between the thumb and forefingers of the left hand and place over the neck of the hook so that the two fingers are pinching both the bucktail and the hook shank. The binding should [ 38 ]

be made at the point where the body ends at the neck of the hook. 

Tie in the bucktail by the same method that was employed in tying on the wings of the Black Ghost, bringing the thread up between thumb and hook, over, and down between hook and forefinger. 

Tighten the loop by pulling straight downward (41), and make three or four additional turns before securing with a half-hitch. This will leave a large amount of surplus protruding over the eye of the hook, but this should not be trimmed off until all other operations on the fly are completed. 

Next the junglecock eyes. Tie the eyes in by the same method used for the Ghost, except that it is not advisable to use lacquer to cement them in place. Do not trim off the butts of the junglecock until later. 

The throat of the Tiger is a thin section of goose wing feather dyed red although almost any red material can be 

41 used, such as dyed hackle or red yarn. This can be tied in almost any old way for a passable job, but to make a really tidy fly of it, a little extra care should be used. Clip from the wing feather a section about a quarter-inch wide, flatten it so that it assumes a rectangular shape, and fold it lengthwise with the glossy sides out. Now pinch this firmly between the thumb and forefinger and bring it up under the hook by passing the eye of the hook through the loop formed by the two fingers. Now bring your fingers up so that they are pinching both feathers and hook, and use the right hand to make the tie.

Here again the thread is brought up between thumb and hook, down the other side between hook and forefinger, and up again the other side before being tightened. This will give a curved backward sweep to the throat which makes a very attractive fly.

With the throat in place, the excess bucktail and junglecock butts can be trimmed. Do not trim the bucktail off bluntly, or it will be almost impossible to make a small, neat head. Trim the buck- [39]

tail by tapering it toward the eye of the hook, in the same shape that you want the finished head to assume. If you have been working with fairly heavy thread up to this point, it will be a good idea to tie it off and use 00 or even 000 to finish it off with. Cover all the bucktail ends with thread, and when you have shaped it properly, tie off with the whip-finish and apply lacquer, a thin coat first, and after that has dried, use the thick to give the head a smooth, glossy finish. CHAPTER V 

"Standard" Wet Flies and Nymphs 

ECHNICALLY, all flies which are fished under the surface of the water streamers, bucktails, and nymphs included are wet flies, hence the author prefers to describe the orthodox short-shanked, winged lures as “standard” wet flies. And the term seems appropriate, as the other types are later forms which seem to have evolved from the original idea of a wet fly lure.

Whether standard wet flies are tied to represent drowned insects or insect life in one of its larval stages is a question on which the angling authorities have argued since the days of old Ike Walton. 

Obviously, you should know what you are making before you attempt to make it, whether it be trout flies or shoelaces. But if you want to know whether your finished wet fly should represent a drowned and waterlogged insect or some form of life that has never left the water and taken wing, I can't help you. For the purposes of this chapter it will suffice to say that the general form of the standard wet fly is looked upon with favor by fish. So for the present we'll let the fish decide for themselves what a wet fly represents, and go ahead and tie one.

Probably the most popular wet fly for all-around use is the Coachman, originated many long years ago by Tom Bosworth, who was coachman to Her Majesty Queen Victoria when he wasn't out on the nearby streams showing the local angling talent how to catch trout. And as the Coachman is a simple fly, not too difficult to tie, it will serve well as an example for beginners. 

Materials: Tail, none; body, peacock herl; wings, white; hackle, brown or dark red.

Start the working thread at the base of the shank just at the bend. Pluck two fronds of peacock herl of equal length and tie in [ 41 ]

both butts at the same time, allowing the butts to lay on top of the shank of the hook and pointing toward the eye. Wind thread forward to neck of the hook, binding in butts to form a base for the body, and clip off excess butts. 

The beginner will not find it advisable to use hackle pliers to wind on the herl, as the fronds are not strong, and the pliers will often cut through and break them. Hold both close together between the finger tips and wind forward toward the eye of the hock, winding the herl as close as possible to build up a solid, fuzzy body. When the neck of the hook is reached, tie with a half-hitch and clip off excess. 

If you wish, you can waterproof this tie with a drop of lacquer, although it is not necessary to do so. 

Now for the wings. These are made from white goose wing feathers flight feathers, they are called - which are quite stiff. It is necessary to have a pair of these feathers, one from each wing, which match closely. Cut from opposite sides of each of the wing feathers a section of the fibres between 4 and ½ inch wide. Cut 5A cial fly tying houses. close to the quill, or backbone, of the feather to obtain pieces that are as long as possible (5A). Then place the two pieces back to back, each curving in toward the other, or cupping, with the tips and butts together. If the two sections are not exactly the same width, pick out the surplus with the nails of your thumb and forefinger, and don't be afraid of making the two sections too narrow, as most wet flies are too heavily winged, especially those made by the big commer- When matching the two sections, if you find that they are not the same length from tip to butt, match the tips, placing the unevenness in the butts, which will later be clipped off as surplus. 

Now to tie the wings in place. Pinch them between the thumb. and forefinger of the left hand and lay them on the shank so that the butts extend over the eye of the hook, and the tips bend downward [ 42 ]

5B over the bend. Slide the pinching fingers down so they are holding both the neck of the hook and the wings. With the right hand bring the working thread up between thumb and hook, form a loose loop over the work, and bring the thread down the other side between forefinger and hook (5B). Pull this loop down so that it is just resting over the wings, and make another. Now, while still pinching firmly, pull steadily down on the working thread, tightening the two loops. When it is tight, make a few additional turns and half-hitch the tie in place. If all has gone well, the wing will be lying along the top of the hook and flaring slightly upward on each side of the tie. If all has not gone well, you may discover that the wing has buckled, or doubled, which probably means that the wings were too wide, or you did not hold your "pinch" while making the tie. Or possibly that the material was too soft, in which case nothing much can be done about it except to try again.

If the wing lies too close to the body, lift the tips and make a couple of turns with the thread around the neck of the hook and under the wings where they are tied, thus forcing them up. 

If all has gone well, clip off the excess butts as closely to the tie as you can, and half-hitch securely. A drop of thin lacquer on the winding at this point will make a more durable job. 

And now for the hackle. You can hackle the fly in one of two ways, depending upon the size of the hook. One is to strip a tuft of fibres from a hackle feather and tie it in as a hank, while the other is to wind a whole hackle feather around the neck of the hook, forming a collar which sweeps back toward the bend of the hook. 

Since my advice to beginners is to use fairly large hooks - No. 8 is a good size for wet flies to begin with - we will finish off this Coachman by tying in a tuft of hackle, rather than spinning on a feather as would be necessary with smaller hooks. [43]

From a soft brown hackle feather, strip from each side of the quill enough fibre to make a thin hank, making sure that the fibres are all the same length. Roll this hank between your fingers to make it as compact as possible, and then pinch it between the thumb and forefinger of the left hand, with the fine tips pointing to the left and the coarse butts to the right, toward the eye of the hook.

Now bring the hackle fibres under the hook, catching the neck of the hook between the fingers that are pinching the hackle. Loop the thread under the hackle, passing it between hook and fingers as described in tying on the wings - except, of course, that you are working under the hook instead of over it - and make your tie. The tips of the hackle should just extend back almost to the point of the hook, and should flare out from the shank (5C). When this material is firmly tied in place, clip off the excess, and you are ready for the final operation of finishing off the head. 

Unless you have been working with very fine thread, it is advisable to tie off with a couple of half-hitches and finish off with the finest you have, as a small, neat head makes for a neat, workmanlike fly, and small heads cannot be made with heavy thread. 5C 

Wind from neck to eye of the hook a distance of not more than 1/16 of an inch, and even less is preferable - to cover the excess butts of wings and hackle, tapering down toward the eye and building a smooth surface. Complete the head with the whip-finish described in Chapter III and cement with a drop of lacquer.

Now for hackling small wet flies. Instead of stripping off hackle fibres and tying in as a hank, use a complete hackle feather. Strip off the fuzzy fibres at the butt, spread the fibres by stroking the feather against the grain, and tie in at the tip, which is pointing toward the eye of the hook with the glossy side of the feather toward you. Grip the butt of the feather with hackle pliers and wind the [ 44 ]

feather around the neck of the fly for three or four turns (5D), forming a thin collar that bristles out at right angles to the hook.

Tie off the feather and clip the surplus. 

As wet flies should be somewhat streamlined - that is, all parts pointing toward the back of the hook - the next step is to bind the hackle back. This is done by sliding the thumb and forefinger of the left hand from the eye of the hook toward the bend, until the hackle is laid back. Then with the right hand wind the working thread over the hackle just enough to hold it back. Finish off as described previously. Nymphs

A nymph is little more than a glorified - or perhaps I should say modified wet fly. Any wet fly can be fished as a nymph. Clip the wings off it and it is a nymph. 5D Every insect, as far as I know, goes through a nymph stage, at the end of which it works its way through the water toward the surface, where it will break open a thin outer shell, spread its wings for the first time, and fly off on the glorious adventure of mating.

It is then a "dry fly," dipping briefly to the water's surface and dropping its eggs back into the stream; next a "spent wing," dead and floating inert on the current, and then 

Whether it then becomes a "wet fly" is a debatable point. Personally, I've never seen a drowned insect under the surface of the water. So if that's what a wet fly represents, then give me nymphs every time. Neither have I seen a living, winged insect struggling under water, so if that's what a wet fly represents, I'll still take nymphs. And if, as some say, wet flies more properly represent small crustacea, such as shrimp, then I don't believe we've given much credit to the intelligence of the fish we try so hard, and sometimes so unsuccessfully, to catch. I think wet flies are tied to represent [ 45 ]

nymphs, and therefore are nymphs and shouldn't be called wet flies at all.

One good thing about nymphs, though, is that the tyer has a world of latitude. Any lure with a body, hackle, and tail he can call a nymph - and he can probably catch fish with it, too. (The secret of success with nymphs lies in the way you fish them, rather than the way you tie them. A discussion of this subject has no place in a fly tyer's book.)

Chenille-bodied nymphs have proven very popular, and as they are very simple to tie, the beginner would be well advised to try one first. Use medium long-shanked hooks in No. 10 size, heavy wire and either Limerick or Sproat. A couple of strands of fairly stiff peacock herl make an excellent tail, and if the two strands fork, so much the better. Tie in the chenille at the tail and wind it forward, tying off at the neck of the hook. To obtain a two-color body, tie off about a third of the way up the shank and finish with chenille of a contrasting color. 

Most tyers seem to prefer side whiskers of hackle rather than the usual collar, although it is doubtful that this makes much difference to the fish. To obtain the side-whisker effect, make a hackle collar in the usual way and split it by wrapping figure-eights with the working thread.

Another popular type of nymph is the Strawman, which has a body of closely-clipped deer hair. The method of making the clippedhair body is described in detail in Chapter VII, but it might be well to describe it briefly here as well: 

Clip small hanks of hair from the hide of a deer, and starting at the bend of the hook, tie in each individual hank at the middle, pulling the thread tightly so that the hair springs upright. Continue up the entire length of the hook until it is completely hidden in hair-ends. Then with scissors clip the hair down to a small, cylindrical body, which may be then banded with silver or gold tinsel, very thin strips of cellophane, silk floss, or very heavy thread. Whiskers or hackle is optional. 

NOTE: To obtain a closer imitation of a real nymph, make your lures hump-backed, or at least larger at the head end. [ 46 ]

Another good nymph can be made by tying in a hank of bucktail at the head of the fly, tying off, and then gathering the ends of the bucktail at the bend so that it makes a smooth hair body. Use the surplus bucktail that protrudes beyond the bend for a tail, clipping off all except that which protrudes over the bend. This body can then be divided into segments by spiraling from the tail forward with strong thread, making the hair bunch up between winds. 

One of the finest nymphs I have ever fished with was the simplest: short tail of fibres stripped from a black hackle feather, body of black floss silk tapered from thin at the tail to thick at the neck, and black whiskers. The silk body, however, was dressed thoroughly once with thin lacquer, and after that had dried the belly of the fly was lacquered yellow and the top black. 

Another material that should not be overlooked is fur, particularly from animals that live much in the water, such as beaver and muskrat. Spun on a waxed thread and wound on the hook to form a tapered body, this material is easy to handle and when wet has a juicy texture which seems most attractive to fish. Chapter VIII tells you how to make fur bodies in detail. 

Nymphs will, I believe, play an increasingly important part in American trout fishing as time goes on, and I advise readers not to overlook nymphs when they start tying next year's supply of flies. 

Because there are few standards of nymph design to follow, it is one type of lure with which they can give their imagination full play. [ 47 ]

CHAPTER VI Dry Flies 

O FORM of fly tying holds quite so much interest or causes so much N controversy as the dry fly. The writer has tall causes so much of expert fly tyers, and although many will agree on certain methods of dressing wets and streamers and nymphs, few will agree on the proper procedure for setting wings, spinning hackle, or making a quill body.

While the experts argue the merits of their respective procedure, let's go ahead and tie a couple of dries by the simplest method the writer knows and dry flies are far from hard to tie after a small amount of practice. In fact, many good tyers assert that dries are much easier and quicker to tie than any other form of feathered lure. 

So we'll start with the simplest form of the dry fly: bivisibles.

Once we've learned how to spin a hackle on a bivisible, the setting of wings and body building will be easier when we tackle the winged dries. 

Tying the Bivisible Flies of this type are made completely of hackle, without body or wings. The hackle is wound palmer style - that is, along the entire length of the hook, and the last feather to be tied in at the head of the fly is of a contrasting color so that the lure will be more easily visible when it is floating on the surface of the water. Hence the term "bivisible."

Tie in the working thread at the shank of the hook just above the bend and follow the customary procedure to make the tail, using such stiff material as fibres from a golden or silver pheasant tippet. 

Now pluck from a stiff, glossy hackle neck four or five small feathers slightly graduated in size, and strip off all the soft, fluffy [ 48 ]

fibres at the butt. Pull the feathers between your fingers against the grain of the fibres so that they stand out at right angles to the stem. 

Tie in the smallest hackle feather first. Lay the tip over the winding that holds the tail in place and bind it in place securely, fastening with a half-hitch. When tying in the feather, and those that will follow, fasten them in place so that the rough or under surface is facing you and the butt to your left. This is important to make a floatable fly, as the slight curve of the hackle will be forward toward the eye of the hook, resulting in a fly that will both ride and float better. 

With the first feather tied in, grip its butt with hackle pliers and wind it around the hook in spirals, each spiral as 6A close to the other as possible. When all the hackle on the feather is used, tie firmly with a half-hitch and clip off the unused stem. Then tie in the tip of the next largest feather (6A) and repeat, gradually working toward the eye of the hook until you have formed a solid, bristling surface of hackle that is slightly. tapered with the greatest width at the head of the fly. The last feather to be tied in is, of course, the white or yellow one (6B) which gives the bivisible its name. This last feather will give the fly a thin collar which will be in contrast to the remainder of the fly.

When the last feather is in place, tie off firmly and whip-finish the head, applying lacquer with a pin-point or sharpened toothpick. You will usually find, after 6B the fly is completed, that many fibres have been tied under, and these should be straightened by plucking them out with a dubbing needle. The fly then looks like 6C - a large, bushy ball of stiff fibres [ 49 ]

that, as one fisherman of my acquaintance described it, will "bounce when it hits the water."

Winged Dry Flies 

The increasing popularity of dry fly fishing in the last couple of 6C decades has brought such refinements to this field of angling that it no longer suffices to describe a dry fly as such. It might be a bivisible, or a fanwing, spentwing, goose wing (that is, a "standard" dry fly with erectile wings of goose-quill), spider, variant or one of the new dries developed by Lee Wulff, who makes the wings and tails of his Wulff Series with hair. Except for the wings, however, the procedure in tying dry flies is fairly well standardized.

Let's select a common pattern which is simple to tie and see how it's done. The Coachman, popular in both dry and wet forms, and a consistent fish-killer, is easy to tie and will make a good pattern for beginners to try. Since this pattern was also used as an example in the chapter on wet flies, the beginner will be better able to learn the different methods of tying wets and dries using the same materials. 

The Coachman is dressed with fibres from the tippet of a golden pheasant, peacock herl, two sections cut from white goose wing feathers, and brown hackle.

Pluck from a golden pheasant tippet a half-dozen fibres and tie in at the tail of the fly, allowing the fibres to project approximately a quarter inch beyond the bend of the hook. If the fibres slant down over the bend, lift them and make a couple of turns with the working thread underneath, forcing them back into position. These fibres will bear some of the fly's weight on the water, and this fact should be kept in mind when determining not only the length of the tail, but the materials of which it is to be made. [ 50 ]

When the tail is properly placed and half-hitched firmly in position, clip the excess close to the tie. A touch of lacquer to the winding at this point will cement it permanently, and prevent the tail from pulling out when the fly is in use. At this stage, incidentally, it is a good idea to coat the entire length of the shank with lacquer, wiping off the excess with the fingers, leaving only enough to make the shank sticky. This will make a foundation for the body and prevent it from twisting around the shank later. 

Now for the body, which in the case of the Coachman is peacock herl. On small flies of size 10 and smaller, only a single herl will be necessary. Tie in the herl by the thickest or butt end at the point. where the tail is fastened, and before proceeding further with it wind the working thread forward to the neck of the hook and secure it in a thread clip. Then wind the herl forward around the shank of the hook to a point just behind the eye, making a solid fuzzy body which covers the shank of the hook completely. Tie at the head and clip off the remaining unused herl. Herl, incidentally, has little or no tensile strength, particularly at the tip, and must be handled carefully else it will break and uncoil its entire length, making it necessary to do the entire operation over again with another herl. 

When tying off the herl, make sure that enough space is left between body and eye of the hook for wings and hackle. - 1/16 of an inch should be enough. 

Now for the wings. From two white goose wing feathers, cut from the broadest side of each a section of web approximately 

3/16 of an inch wide. Place these two 60 sections back to back so that they flare outward, matching tips carefully. Pinch the two sections together with the thumb and forefinger of the left hand, tips to the right and pointing down, and lay them on edge over the shank of the hook, including the neck of the hook between your pinching fingers (6D). About a quarter-inch of the [51]

tips should extend beyond the eye of the hook; it is not important how much of the butts protrude as they will be clipped off close later. 6E Pinching the two wings and the shank of the hook firmly, use the right hand to bring the working thread up between thumb and hook, over the work, and down the other side between hook and forefinger. Slowly pull the loop tight, forcing both ends of the feather upward (6E). Repeat with another firm loop and half-hitch the tie. 

Trim off the excess butt close to the tic. 

Then, holding the wing tips in a vertical position, make a few turns of thread ahead of them to hold them more nearly erect. 

It is not necessary to have them perfectly cocked at this point, as they can be placed in better position when you wind on the hackle, which comes next.

Select from a hackle neck a small, thin feather and strip from the butt of its stem all the soft downy fibres, leaving only those that are glossy and stiff. Run the feather between your fingers against 6F the grain of the fibres, making them stand out at right angles from the stem. Now tie in the feather by the tip, grip the butt with hackle pliers, and wind it around the hook first ahead of and then behind the wings, making a bristling collar of stiff hackle fibres, with the wings sticking up in the middle (6F). If there does not seem to be enough "legs" on the fly to float it, tie in another hackle, although this should not be necessary if you have hackle of good quality and wind it on so that all of the fibres are used. 

When the hackle feather is tied in place, clip off the excess stem as closely as possible and whip-finish the head. If you find that some | 52 |

of the hackle is caught under, pick it out with a dubbing needle. A touch of lacquer applied either with a pin or sharpened toothpick will both waterproof the tie, preventing rot from dampness after use, and give the head a neater appearance. If any lacquer adheres to the hackle, wipe it off immediately with the fingers. [ 53 ]

Bass Bugs 

N LATE YEARS the sport of "bass buggin' 

at night has become increasingly popular, and its popularity is well deserved as the bass bug is a deadly instrument of destruction for the so-called "civilized” bass especially smallmouths of heavily fished lakes and streams. 

In general, there are two types of bass bugs: cork-bodied and clipped-hair. Of the two, I prefer the latter. I find the clipped-hair bugs easier to cast, particularly with light rods, and usually hook a larger percentage of strikes than with the more bulky cork-bodied lures. 

Tying the hair bugs is an easy but somewhat tedious job. You will need strong thread for this, and personally I prefer a reasonably stout linen thread for all operations except tying off the head, where linen is too bulky. Linen does not break so easily, withstands water rot better, and since the thread is hidden in the bulky body, its size does not show in the finished product. 

As there are no really orthodox forms which bass bugs may take, you can give your imagination full play in tying them. The usual fixtures are a tail (optional with the tyer), body (necessary) and wings, fins, or arms, also optional.

Begin with the tail. After starting your working thread on the hook just above the bend, tie in a generous tuft of bucktail and clip the butts close to the tie. The body itself is made of coarse hair from the deer hide, rather than the tail, although the coarser hair at the base of the tail may be used if the other is not available. 

From the hide clip off a small tuft of hair – one trial will give you an idea of how much you can easily handle – remembering to cut it as close to the hide as possible. Holding the hair between thumb and forefinger of your left hand (still assuming that you are [54]

right-handed), lay it over the shank of the hook so that you are pinching both the hair and the shank at the point where the tail was tied. Then with your right hand bring the working thread up between thumb and shank (7A), forming a loop over the middle of the hair and bringing it down between forefinger and shank.

Pull down firmly, and the coarse hair will spring upward in pompadour effect (7B).

Make another turn with the thread to anchor the hair firmly; then use the left hand to hold the hair back while you make one or two turns ahead of the work. 

Half-hitch, and press the hair back toward the bend of the hook as far as it will go. 

The next tuft of hair should be laid on the side of the shank and tied in place the same way. From this point on, continue this operation, placing the hair as close together as possible and filling the length of the shank solid, making sure it is covered evenly on all sides. When you cannot possibly find room for another tuft, tie off with a couple of half-hitches, and then whip-finish the head with finer thread.

The hook will now be completely hidden in a ball of bristling hair nearly a third as large as your fist (7C), and from this point on the form which the lure will take depends entirely upon what you can do with a pair of strong, fine-pointed scissors. 7A 7B 7c [55]

Clip the hair fairly close to the shank, making a surface of cropped, stiff hair-ends, which, if properly tied, will present a smooth, solid surface. When clipping, be sure to leave plenty of "bite" between barb and shank, as otherwise you will have difficulty hooking your fish. When clipping the body, tufts can be left at the sides to simulate the arms of a frog or the wings of a bug (7D). 7D Mottled and striped body effects can be obtained by using dyed hair of different colors. After a little practice you will pick up the trick of twisting the hair as you tie it on so that one tuft will form a collar completely around the hook. Successive. collars of different hues result in a striped lure that is very effective. Mottled bodies are obtained by using smaller tufts of hair and holding the hair firmly as it is being tied in place, so it does not fan completely around the hook shank.

When tying clipped hair bass bugs for your own use, it is a good idea to soak them thoroughly in liquid fly oil, letting them dry completely before use. The hair will absorb and hold the oil and will float much better.

The writer prefers long-shanked Limerick hooks in sizes 4 and 6 for bass bugs, but this is a personal preference and not a rule which all should follow. A hook with a reasonably long shank should, of course, be used so that the lure will have the maximum amount of floating surface. 

Incidentally, clipped-hair nymphs are used with some success in trout fishing. The method of tying is, in general, the same, although of course small hooks are used, and tails are usually made of feather hackle. 

Cork-bodied Bugs

Bass bugs depend upon two properties for their success as fishtakers: floatability and "pop"-ability. By the latter term I mean [56]

that they should pop along the surface of the water when retrieved, burbling and kicking up such a fuss that any bass in the vicinity would feel impelled to investigate the disturbance. Cork bugs, while more awkward to cast than hair lures, float better, and, because of the larger body, make more disturbance on the water when retrieved. 

Cork-bodied bass bugs should be made with hump-shanked hooks, else the body will invariably turn around the shank of the hook. The hump holds the cork in one position, and nothing else will unless you take the trouble to solder a short, stiff cross-wire to the shank, and even that is not as satisfactory as the hump. 

But first start with the tail. Fastern the working thread to the base of the shank just ahead of the bend, and tie in bucktail. It will do no harm to use plenty of tail, and make it long- at least an inch (7E). Tie off the tail firmly and cement the tie with thin lacquer. Next, coat the entire length of the shank with thick, gooey lacquer or, preferably, ambroid cement and allow it to become semidry before putting on the cork body. 7E Suitable bodies for this type of bug can be home made from cork bottle stoppers, although most supply houses catalog the cylinders at a very reasonable price. To make your own, obtain cork cylinders about 3/4" long and a half-inch or less in diameter at the thickest end, depending, of course, on the size of the hook you are using. With a razor blade slice a wedge off this thick end, or face, to make it oblique. Again using a razor blade, cut a slot lengthwise half the thickness of the cork on the side of the cylinder which is shortest. Push the shank of the hook into this slot, with the largest end of the cylinder toward the eye of the hook and with the face slanting back from top to bottom, so that it gives the effect of a bug with a protruding forehead and a receding chin. A few tight turns of thread [57]

around the thick or front end of the body will hold it in place while the lacquer dries (7F). tates At this stage the body of the fly should be painted with colored enamel - black, yellow, red, white, or whatever hue your fancy dicand allowed to dry thoroughly. If you plan to tie more than one bug, you will find it better to affix the bodies and paint several at once, finishing them a day or two later when they have dried. 7F From this point on you can dress the bug any way you choose, following your own ideas for tying on bucktail or feathers. 

One bug that I have used with considerable success, however, is dressed as follows: 

A generous quantity of green bucktail is laid over the top of the body and wrapped in place so that the hair flows back to the length of the tail. 

Over this the tip of a large black breast feather is laid, and tied in place by the butt. The winding is then whip-finished and the tie coated with thin lacquer. Additional dressing can be placed on the bottom of the fly, giving a touch of color to contrast with the body, or using stiff bucktail that will make even more commotion in the water when the bug is retrieved.

To do a really workmanlike job, you can paint eyes on the face of the body, using either red enamel encircled with white, or any colors that will be in contrast to the body. Excellent eyes can also be made from round-headed pins by clipping off all but 4 of an inch of the pin and pushing them into their proper place on the face of the bug. In my mind, however, these add unnecessary weight and do not attract any more fish than do bugs that have no eyes. [58]

CHAPTER VIII Body and Wing Materials . . . and How to Use Them 

HERE ARE SO many different materials that can be used for fly bodies and wings that I felt they deserved a chapter of their own. 

Following is the list of materials and instructions for using them in both dry and wet flies. There are others, but these are the most commonly used and the ones which I believe will be of most interest to the beginner.

Body Materials 

Cellophane: May be obtained in a variety of colors, is tough, and has a distinct glint in the water. Roll a sheet of cellophane, press it flat, and use large, sharp scissors to cut off bands of various widths. Excellent for ribbing fly bodies, and better than tinsel 8A on dry flies, as it is lighter in weight. To rib, tie in at tail before the body is made. 

When body is completed, wind spirally forward and tie off. To make a body of solid cellophane, build a smooth surface with working thread after tying cellophane in at tail, and then wind forward.

Chenille: Easiest of all body materials to handle. Strip fibres from one end of a strand of chenille for about a quarter-inch, and tie in at tail of hook. Stripping the fibres makes it possible to build a body that does not bulge at the tail, where the chenille is started. Wind forward in close spirals, tie and clip off surplus (8A). 

NOTE: When using chenille for bodies, it is not advisable to coat the shank of the hook with lacquer, as the chenille will absorb some of it and become stiff. [59]

When using two colors of chenille, as in the McGinty wet fly, tie in both strands at the same time and wind both together. This gives the body a striped effect. 

Fur: This material is used widely for making bodies for dries, wets, and nymphs. Fur is the short wool that grows close to the hide of rabbits, moles, mice, red foxes, and several other soft-furred animals. After the tail of the fly is tied in place, take a small quantity of fur and roll it between the fingers around the waxed working thread, or lay a small quantity of it on your trouser leg and roll the waxed thread over it. When the thread has picked up a heavy coating of wool, it can be wound on the body. Fur makes a fuzzy body that is very attractive to fish when wet. It is necessary to wax the working thread thoroughly before attempting to spin fur on it. 

Hair: Instructions for making a clipped hair body were given fully in Chapter VII, which described the making of hair-body bugs. 

The coarse hair from the hide (not tail) is used. Clip a small quantity close to the skin, tie to hook tightly, so that the hair flares up on 8B each side of the tie (8B), and continue until the body is entirely covered. Then with small scissors, clip the hair close. 

Mottled and striped effects can be obtained by using hair of different colors. 

Herl: Peacock herl is used in many flies, and although it seems attractive to fish, it does not make a durable body. 

The herl can be tied in either by tip or tail, but after some experimenting I have found it better to tie in at the butt, as fine thread will not cut through it so easily, and doing so brings the broadest part of the herl, which is at the tip, toward the head of the fly.

Herl is very springy, and if it breaks or slips from fingers or hackle pliers while being wound around a hook, it will promptly uncoil itself. To avoid these troubles, don't try to use too much of a single herl. Tie it in at the butt where the fibres begin to grow thickly and [ 60 ]

use only about half of the remainder. The closer you wind toward the tip of the herl, the greater are the chances of breaking it, which means starting all over again. 

Ostrich plumes also furnish herl which can be used for making fly bodies, but this herl is much coarser than peacock's, and for most. uses is not as satisfactory. Herl fibres can also be obtained from peacock sword-feathers, so called, but the fibres on this herl are not as dense as on the eyed tail feathers, and are usually too short to handle conveniently.

Quill: The most popular material for quill bodies is the fibre. from eyed peacock tail feathers. Only the quill or backbone, however, is used, the fibres being stripped off either by scraping it with the finger nails or with a dull razor blade. When using the razor, lay the her on a blotter or telephone book, which offers a resisting surface; otherwise you may cut through the quill. Tie in the stripped quill and wind forward in close spirals. 

Another useful quill body material is made of the individual fibres of a goose wing. The wing itself is composed of a succession of fibres, or filaments, each attached to the next by a row of jagged teeth which interlock. From a large, broad feather pick out a single fibre, tie it in at the tip, or thinnest end, and wind forward. Striped bodies can be made with this material by selecting fibres from dyed wings and winding both on at the same time. 

Silk Floss: Sometimes this material comes in twisted strands, in which event the strands must be separated, as otherwise it will be almost impossible to make a smooth body. If making a small fly 

No. 8 or smaller · use not more than half a single strand. Tie in at the head of the fly, wind back to the tail and forward again, building up the body slightly in the middle and tapering it at both ends. The floss will form itself into a flat tape as you wind, making it very easy to build a smooth, even body. 

NOTE: Most silk floss bodies are ribbed with tinsel, which is described in the following section. 

Tinsel: Used two ways for ribbing, and for a full tinsel body.

For ribbing, cut a piece from four to six inches long, depending upon the size of the hook. Taper one end down to a point. Tie this [61]

end in at the tail of the fly before the body is built up. The taper will eliminate a bulge where the tinsel will be bent at the beginning of the winding process. Lift up the tail of the fly and wind the tinsel down the bend of the hook for a couple of turns, then wind it back to the end of the body and begin to spiral it, spacing the windings evenly, up to the neck of the fly. Tie off, taking care not to cut the thread on the sharp edge of the tinsel. 

NOTE: As tinsel tarnishes, it is advisable to treat it with thin lacquer before use. This can be done either by painting the entire spool of tinsel, allowing the lacquer to soak through the first three or four layers, and repeating when that amount has been used, or better, by cutting several strands and applying the lacquer to each individual one with the thumb and forefinger.

Making a neat all-tinsel body is a little more difficult than ribbing. 

If the fly has a tail, tie in first a long strand of flat tinsel at the base of the tail, and then a shorter strand of oval tinsel wire. Then wind the working thread up the entire length of the body solidly, to make a smooth foundation for the tinsel. Next, wind the tinsel forward so that it does not overlap or leave gaps, and tie off, again taking care that you do not cut the working thread on the sharp edge of the tinsel.

Now spiral the oval wire forward and tie off. This wire breaks up the surface of the fly body, giving it more glitter in the water, and also makes the body stronger and more durable. Even the sharp teeth of a pickerel will not be able to tear a tinsel and wire body under ordinary conditions. 

After the body is completed, it is best to coat it thoroughly with thin lacquer and lay it aside to dry before finishing the fly. 

Tinsel makes the most attractive of all wet fly bodies so long as it is shiny; if it becomes tarnished it is no longer worth the time you put into making it.

If tinsel becomes tarnished on the spool, before you use it, it can be brightened with metal polish which the women-folks use on their silverware. [ 62 ]

Some of my friends and I have experimented with flies consisting of only a long tail and built-up tinsel body, as pictured in SC. A deep-running, flashy lure, it has taken many fish that refused to look at anything else.

Wool: Unwashed, unspun wool as it comes from the shearing pen is very useful to fly tyers, especially for dry flies. If you can get unwashed wool you will find it impregnated with oil and will float until doomsday. Don't be fastidious with unwashed wool; it may smell unpleasantly and feel greasy to your fingers, but nevertheless it rates tops as dry fly body material. 

Wax your working thread thoroughly, and when the fly is ready for the body, 8C spin a small quantity of wool on the thread by rolling it between the thumb and forefingers. Don't use too much; a small pinch of wool

BD will make a body fat enough for the largest fly you will want to tie. When the thread is coated with wool, wind forward to the neck (8D) and tie, stripping off the surplus wool. 

Woolen yarn may also be used for body. material, but it is difficult to make a smooth job of it, and since yarn has been washed and either bleached or dyed, does not float too well. However, if use it you must, tie in at the tail, bring the working thread ahead to the neck of the hook, wind yarn forward, and tie off.

Wing Materials 

Goose Quill Wings-Dry Fly: These wings, which were accepted as standard in the old days, but not so popular now, are made [63 |

of twin sections cut from matched goose wing feathers - o one from each side of the bird. Cut a section about a quarter inch wide from the broad half of each wing and lay the two sections together with the convex sides facing. Now with your fingernails pluck out surplus fibres until the two sections match exactly, and are about 3/16 of an inch wide.

Pinching the two sections together tightly between the thumb and forefinger of your left hand, lay them on the shank of the hook with the tips pointing forward, and let your fingers slide down until they are pinching not only the wings but the hook shank as well. 

Take the working thread in your right hand and bring it up between shank and thumb, and down on the other side of the hook between shank and forefinger, leaving a loop over your work. Make a lastsecond check-up to make sure you are holding the wings in the proper position, and then pull down on the thread, drawing the loop between thumb and forefinger and tightly over the wings. This will pull the wing tips and butts up, although it would not be noticed 8E is hackled. unless you released your hold. But still pinching the work firmly, make another strong turn, bringing the thread up one side and down the other between your pinching fingers. Let your thumb and forefinger then slide forward far enough to grasp the tips of the wings and pull them back so that you can make a couple of turns and a half-hitch ahead of them. 

This should bring the wing-tips very nearly perpendicular (8E), but if not they can be cocked later when the fly If the wings have crumpled or bent, you have probably used too much wing material. Better start over again and use thinner sections, says inch wide. If the wings have fallen off center they can be easily twisted into place and held there with another turn or two with the working thread. [64]

With the wings properly placed and bound into position, clip the surplus close to the tie. 

NOTE: Many tyers tie in the wings of a dry fly first and do not clip off the wing-butts, but build the fly body over them. This makes a stronger fly, but adds bulk to the body and is really unnecessary. 

Goose Quill Wings-Wet Fly: Although the softer flight feathers from a duck wing may be used for winging wet flies, I believe that goose quill is easier to handle and makes a neater fly. 

Most commercial fly-tying houses dress wet flies too heavily, evidently for the purpose of catching the fisherman's eye rather than the fish's. It is not advisable, therefore, to follow a commerciallytied sample too closely, particularly in winging and hackling wet flies. Whereas the commercial tyers will use wings a quarter or even a half an inch wide, the amateur is well advised to make his wet fly wings not more than %- or at the most, 3/16of an inch in width. The narrow wings also have the virtue of being easier to handle and to tie neatly. 

Cut and match two pieces as described in the foregoing section, but when laying them on the book, point the tips to the left so they extend back to the bend of the hook, allowing the butts to protrude over the eye. Place the wing so that the pointed edges are down, in which position the material will curve in a sweeping arc from front to back, giving the wings a stream-lined appearance. 

Holding the wing sections pinched tightly between thumb and forefinger, tie in by the same procedure used for dry fly wings, making a loop over the quill sections and drawing it tight, causing the wings to spring up on each side of the tie. If the wings lie too close to the shank of the hook, lift them by the tips and make a couple of turns. underneath with the working thread, which will hold them up. When the wing is in place, clip off the butts, half-hitch, and cement with a drop of lacquer.

Fanwings: Tying a fanwing fly on which the wings are properly balanced and in their proper place can be one of the most exasperating jobs the fly-tyer will undertake, yet when the operation is learned you will probably wonder why you used to make such a fuss about it. [ 65 ]

Fanwings are made from breast feathers which are curved or, to describe it better, cupped. It is important that the two feathers you select to make the wings are closely matched. If you have a whole breast to choose from, select two feathers that grow very close together, or one over the other. Match them carefully for length of quill, width of hackle, and degree of curvature. 

Now pluck from each the fuzzy fibres at the butt, stripping both to match. Don't use fanwings that are too large for the hook, as they will make the completed fly top-heavy and look more like a butterfly on the water than a live insect in which a trout would presumably be interested. The fanwing wants enough wing to be visible to the angler when riding the water and to parachute the fly down to a two-point landing on tail and hackle.

To tie the wings in place, hold them back to back with thumb and forefingers of the left hand and set them down over the neck of the hook so that the butts straddle it and the wings ride upright. 

Bind them firmly in place with a series of figure-of-eights around the butts and shank while still holding the wings in place. If the wings 8F are not set properly now, they can be bound into position with a turn of thread here or there. When they are properly set (8F), bend the butts back toward the bend of the hook, under the shank, and bind it in, before clipping off the excess. 

Incidentally, for this reason most fly tyers tie in the fanwings before the body of the fly, thus enabling them to build the body over the wing butts. But as the beginner will have trouble enough making a dry fly without having to work around a pair of wings while tying in tail and body, his work will be easier if he saves the wings until the tail and body are completed, and then cut off the wing butts close to the tie. 

If the wings are too erect and close together, they can be separated by a turn of thread, or if they are set too far apart, they can be brought together with a turn of thread. When the wings are | 66 |

set to satisfaction, hold them apart and daub the crotch with a drop of thin lacquer or varnish.

Setting a pair of fanwings is a difficult operation to describe, and the only way to learn how is to keep trying until you have discovered the method that works best for you. One word of advice, however: if, despite your supreme efforts, the wings do not set straight and in perfect alignment, pull 'em off and start over again with a new pair, because if the fanwings are not perfectly set at this point, the chances are they never will be. Some adjustment can be made with working thread, but the "set" of the wings is made when the first few turns of thread seat them in place. 

Hackle Tip and Spent Wings: Except for spent wing flies, hackle tip wings are little used, for which fact this fly tyer offers a fervent prayer of thanksgiving. Hackle tip wings are a nuisance to tie, and serve no important purpose, as they do not give the fly visibility, are too flimsy to balance the fly on the water, and I doubt that they be seen by a fish. However, if tie them you must, follow directions given for tying fanwings. Incidentally, hackle tip wings can be made by using the tips of the feathers used to make the hackle collar of the fly. Take a little more care when tying in the tip of the hackle feather, setting it in place before winding the collar, and of course, using two feathers. Winding ahead of, behind, and between the wings with the hackle feathers will push them into place. 

Spent wing flies, while not so popular in this country as they are in England, sometimes take fish when other types of floating flies won't and so deserve mention in this chapter. To tie, follow directions for making fanwings, but bind the wings down flat, first with working thread and later with hackle feathers, by making several turns between the wings. 

Other Dry Fly Wings: Excellent dry fly wings can be made from a breast feather of the teal, mandarin, mallard, or other species. of wildfowl with barred or mottled plumage. Bunch the fibres of a single feather by rolling it between the fingers and lay it over the shank of the hook, feather tips forward over the eye, and bind it [ 67 ]

in place (SG). This will leave all the butt and much of the feather itself unused. Make a firm tie, then lift the tip and make a few winds. ahead of it to set it upright. Divide the tips exactly in half with a dubbing needle and pass the thread through the split, forming two wings. Clip off the excess, and when winding the hackle you can set the wings in final position. 8G 

Streamer Wings: The procedure for tying this type of wing was described fully in Chapter 1. These wings are made with matched hackle feathers, either four or six to a fly, which are placed face to face so that the curved feathers press against each other making one flat, heavy wing.

Soft-fibred feathers should be used for streamers, to obtain the maximum amount of action when the fly is twitched through the water.

When tying the streamer wings in place, hold between the thumb and forefinger at the shoulders, or broadest part of the feathers, as close as possible to the neck of the fly where the tie will be made. 

The pinching method, in which the thread is drawn up between thumb and hook, over the work and down between hook and forefinger, will enable the tyer to set the wings with the least trouble. Do not clip off the butts until the fly is completed, as they may be used later to straighten feathers that have gone awry during a later operation. 

Bucktail Wings: Properly, these are not wings at all, but are called that for want of a more nearly correct designation. 

For bucktail flies, doe and fawn tails are superior to buck, the hair being finer. But since bucktails are easier to obtain, most of us have to do the best we can with what the game laws enable us to procure.

The method of tying bucktails is described fully in Chapter IV. 

However, here are a few hints that will prove useful: [68]

Don't cut hair off a bucktail willy nilly. With your fingers, separate a hank of the size, length and color needed for the particular fly you are tying, twisting it so that it makes a separate tuft, and then cut it off as close as possible to the hide. 

Almost fifty per cent of every hank of bucktail will be waste, as not all the hairs are of uniform length. Hold the hank first by the tips and pluck loose hair from the butts, and then reverse the procedure until all short and broken hairs have been stripped out. 

If this leaves you with more than you will use, take only as much as you will need, and lay the remainder aside for later use. 

Tie in the bucktail by the pinching method, and after the hair has been firmly half-hitched in place, cement the tie with a drop of lacquer which will waterproof this operation and hold the bucktail more firmly in place. 

It is difficult to make a small, neat tie-off on bucktail flies, as the butt of the hair is coarse and builds up to a bulky head. However, by trimming the butts to a taper and using the finest possible thread, a smooth and not over-large head can be made. [ 69 ]

CHAPTER IX Twenty-five Favorite Flies 

VERY fly fisherman will confess to partiality toward certain patterns. My own experience bears this out. The first fish I ever caught with a fly torpedoed from an undercut embankment and nailed a No. 10 wet Coachman. And although this was many years ago, and although I have tied and tried hundreds of patterns since then, I still look upon the Coachman as my "Old Reliable” among standard wet fly patterns. 

Novice fly fishermen and the fly tying beginners will undoubtedly have the same experience. We all have our prejudices, which accounts for the fact that any two fishermen of equal skill may have angling theories that are in direct opposition. The Coachman may be one man's meat and another's poison. Angler A may swear by the Quill 

Gordon, and Angler B swear at it. In the last analysis, it is the fly that is most used that catches the most fish. I personally believe that I would have caught just as many trout on a Dark Montreal, for instance, as on a Coachman - if I'd used the Montreal as much. 

In our anxiety to raise fish we are tempted to tie on a fly that we know from past experience fish will take, rather than to experiment with a pattern that is to us untried and unproven. 

So any list of favorite flies that I suggest should not be accepted, necessarily, as the most productive for all fisherman under all conditions. Rather, they are flies that my friends and I have used with consistent results over a long period of time. I list them because they are all proven fish-takers, all standard, well-known patterns which every novice fly tyer should know how to tie. [70]
