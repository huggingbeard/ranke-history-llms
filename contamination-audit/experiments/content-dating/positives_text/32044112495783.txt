FOREWORD 

A portion of this brochure was given as an address under the same title before the 1940 Institute of the Women's Alliance of the First Parish Church. It has since been rewritten and greatly expanded. In collecting the material I received assistance from countless "natives" and from some "foreigners" -to all, many thanks. But for the most part the paper relies, not upon verbal but upon written records,-statute books, state and town reports, pamphlets and scrap books, and above all on the Brunswick Telegraph, the weekly newspaper of that era. 

Luckily its editor, A. G. Tenney, "handsome brother Tenney”, as he was somewhat condescendingly called by the metropolitan press of Portland, happened to see the passing events of the village, small as well as great, with the eye of the future. But he leaves, as well, the impression of a flavorsome personality. 

His style had an earthy and sometimes extravagant vigor; his thought defied the conventional conservatism of the small town and the proprieties of the smug and the cautious. Nature meant much to him. The seasonal procession of blossoms from mayflower and wild strawberry to goldenrod, the vivid display of northern lights, a rainbow, sunsets from Powderhouse Hill, the springtime flight of wild geese,-all these almost turned his prose into poetry. Above all, man was his study. Catholic in interests, liberal in outlook, he was equally at home at a golden wedding anniversary or an oyster supper of the Niagara hand tub; a Democratic flag raising by the French-Canadians or the graduating exercises of the Brunswick grammar school. As he poked about town on foot or drawn by his faithful little mare in carriage or sleigh, he pieced together an unforgettable mosaic of village life. I have been fortunate to scan it through the eyes of a man, so honest, courageous, and educated.

In 

Brunswick celebrated the one hundred and fiftieth anniversary of its incorporation. There was a variety of entertainment and excitement. In the morning a parade of horribles and fantastics passed through the streets of the village and later, after a second and still longer procession, four hundred citizens sat down in the new town hall to a dinner that included boned turkey, larded prairie chicken, Philadelphia capon, sliced ham, sliced tongue, lobster salad, chicken salad, ice cream, strawberries and cream, and five kinds of cake, not including lady fingers, kisses, and macaroons. Amidst so much marching and eating perhaps they neglected to pay proper attention to the address of C. C. Everett, the orator of the day. His was an impressive performance. For one thing the choice of Everett was a suitable one. Brunswick born, Bowdoin educated, teacher and librarian at his alma mater, as long as the boards could tolerate his Unitarian principles, he later received the final consecration of a call to Harvard University. A loyal lieutenant of Charles W. Eliot in the latter's attempt to modernize Harvard, he brought his own school, the Divinity School, to a high level of scholarly excellence. Now in his Brunswick oration he essayed an evaluation of the town, clearly in his mind not to be confused with a mere geographical area, as distinguished on

the one hand from the country and on the other from the city. 

In his opinion the institutions which had determined the character of the town were first the church, then the college, and finally the factory. But to him this sequence seemed “an anticlimax". I can hardly agree with this last assertion. Whatever might once have been true, the factory by the eighties was the cornerstone of the distinctive position of the town. For by then the town, or village if you will, had outgrown its rural chrysalis; yet it still avoided a complete surrender to the centralizing tendencies of business and metropolitan life. This passing moment in history deserves to be called Brunswick's 

Golden Age. And at this moment of distinctiveness, this “embryonic city", as one correspondent to the Telegraph put it, took on the paraphernalia of modern civilization and assumed essentially its present form. 

There was of course no sharp break with the past. The traditional occupations still persisted. Every week the Telegraph published a section devoted to "Farm, Home, and Garden," and paragraphs in the news columns chronicled the first peas and the doings of Topsham Fair. Some farmers still celebrated the end of the haying season with decorated hayracks, cockaded horses, and the magnificent dinner when everyone was paid off. 

In winter they dragged into town their sleds of hay and cordwood and displayed them for sale on the down town streets. 

When the 2800 pound bell was raised to the tower of the new town hall, not barking tractors but the slow, powerful pull of oxen and of human hands brought it to position. But by the eighties, although gardens still stretched into town, rural landholders were moving west or into the village. No longer were the words of farmers law. When in 1883 the nation was divided into time zones, the selectmen decided to run the town clock on railroad time, as it was called, although there was plenty of 10

grumbling at this surrender of "stay-at-home" time to Philadelphia time, that of the Eastern time zone, 19 minutes later than Brunswick clocks. And in 1886 the grocery and butcher stores, which once had kept open in the evening so that farmers could drive in for supplies after tea, decided to close two nights a week. "It is a good move," said Tenney, "the only parties affected being the standard shop loafers. They are like the engaged fellow who was advised to marry and who replied with the question 'where under heaven shall I spend my evenings?'” And finally a board of selectmen was chosen that contained no representative of the rural voters. 

The maritime trades followed the same decline. Shipbuilding had ceased in the Pennell yards in 1874 with the launching of the Benjamin Serwall. 

No one heard the whisper passed, "The loveliest, and it is last,

Last of the ships of Pennellville, Last of the ships to climb the hill, The blue Atlantic hill, and go 

Around the globe in sun and snow.”

But the Skolfields launched vessels till the middle eighties and in 1887 on his arrival at Calcutta, Captain George Skolfield counted three Skolfield vessels, including his own, in its harbor. 

Ship captains still returned to Brunswick from distant voyages and showed their curios along the streets. But they came by train from Boston or New York. Far more typical were the captains who held directorships in local banks or managed estates as trustees or like Captain Jordan, a profane old ship captain, raised corn and ran a cannery. Although the sea no longer gave so many livelihoods, it still flavored the life of the town. For their Christmas Eve party the Universalist Sunday 11

School had on one occasion in place of a tree, a boat trimmed to represent a ship, and Tenney, in describing the frequent runaways of the village never had the wagons overturn, they always "capsized".

Surely by the eighties the factory had become the core of the town. Better, I should say factories, for more than one enterprise utilized the water powers of the Androscoggin. The arrangement of the dams and the location and variety of the mills reinforces my point that the town, even in the economic sense, still had a considerable independence. The lower dam on the Brunswick side provided power for two sawmills, a grist mill, a pulp mill, and also the Dennison Manufacturing Company. The saw and grist mills were the traditional representatives of a simple localized economy; the latter two enterprises, although more novel, were also of local origin. The more famous was the Dennison Manufacturing Company. This concern was the offspring of the business enterprise and imagination of two brothers,-A. L. Dennison, more the inventor, and 

E. W. Dennison, more the salesman. While living in Boston, the former resolved to undertake in this country the manufacture of the jewelers' boxes commonly imported from abroad and he invented machinery to do it; the other, also in Boston, applied manufacturing technique to other paper products, notably tags. In the sixties some of these manufacturing operations were returned to Brunswick where they had been sporadically carried on, and by the mid-eighties the Dennison 

Manufacturing Company was employing over four hundred people and shipping its jewelers' and wooden mailing boxes all over the United States. "No man was more responsible for 

Brunswick's business revival and her prosperity in the eighties than was E. W. Dennison," asserted Tenney.

The exclusive owner of the water power at the upper dam 12

was the Cabot Manufacturing Company. After nearly sixty years of recurrent failures a cotton mill at this site had at last succeeded in manufacturing cotton fabrics with some regularity of profits. Unlike the mills below, it was a "foreign" enterprise. A series of reorganizations had early squeezed out the local capital; in organization and operation it eventually resembled the standard cotton mill in the north of Boston area. 

Thus it had a large capitalization, $600,000, nearly fourteen times that of the Dennison Company, and it employed over seven hundred hands. In the decade of the eighties the mill increased its spindles nearly 30% and in the nineties replaced its patchwork buildings by the present substantial brick battlements, spangled with windows. It was one of the ten great cotton mills of the state, rivaled or surpassed only by the huge enterprises of Lewiston, Waterville, Augusta, Biddeford and Saco. Whining in public over its competitive disadvantages and engaged in a running controversy with town and county assessors for lower appraisal of its property, it, nonetheless, averaged 8% dividends from 1880-85 and its $500 par-value stock never sank below par and once enjoyed a premium on the Boston market of $240. The popularity which attached to the mills along the lower dam was not extended to this other enterprise. On one occasion the town meeting tartly instructed officials to assess the "Cabot Mill Property upon the same principle and basis that they do upon property of other taxpayers throughout the town", and Tenney, hardly a stone's throw up Maine Street, blasted away at "the grasping Shylocks of the cotton and woolen mills of Maine." 

Hardly adding to its popularity were the racial changes taking place in what had been an Anglo-Saxon town. For it was the cotton mill that drew hither the French-Canadians. Their migration, starting after 1865 as a dépopulation en masse in 13

the eastern townships of Quebec, was like all migrations to America. It was set in motion by the conditions at home, in this case the depressed state of agriculture in the St. Lawrence 

Valley; and by the hope for improvement in a new land, in this instance the wages from industrial employment in New England. News of the good tidings was carried back to Quebec by the frequent return of those who had come south, worked, and saved money, or by the letters and later the newspapers from the New France in New England. In case these stimuli were not enough, railroads spurred migration for the sake of passenger fares and factories, the Cabot among them, sent agents to Quebec to recruit workers, as long as Federal law permitted. 

For the French habitant, although an industrial amateur, learned quickly. Frugal, docile, and eager to get ahead, he accepted conditions and wages which the native American was too restive to endure. Critics were not slow to point out that the high profits to the mills were due to the exploitation of these newcomers although the proof for such an assertion is an impossibility. In any case the foreign population of Brunswick roughly doubled every ten years between 1870 and 1890. In 

1891 there were somewhat less than 2500 French-Canadians in town. 

No Mayflower brought them to our shores; no Plymouth

Rock has been enshrined as their landing place. "Le Grand 

Tronc", the means of their arrival, yields ancestral symbols with difficulty. Nonetheless it required courage for them to sever home ties and settle a strange land, cleared, to be sure, of Indians and forests, but occupied by a dominant race, different in civilization and suspicious of intruders. Their migration was not an individual one. Occasionally a young man would come alone and put up in the boarding houses which the mill ran for unattached men and women, French, American, English, or 14

Irish. But generally they came as families and crowded into the tenements along Mill Street. The mill owned over a hundred of them, building some for this special purpose, or else buying houses elsewhere for $75 to $125 and moving them to vacancies in its property. Apparently the returns at a $7 a month rent were so lucrative that private owners in the neighborhood hastened to follow this example. The customary type was two or three stories high and was divided into eight tenements. Each tenement had from five to seven rooms and accommodated about twelve people. In a country village there thus developed a congestion comparable to the city slums. The immediate surroundings reinforced the comparison. Anxious to save money, the new arrivals turned naturally to the background of their agricultural way of life. Pigs were penned between the tenements, cows were kept in the sheds and fed like the pigs with the garbage from the kitchen. As for sanitary arrangements each block of tenements, roughly a hundred people depended upon four privies. Water was drawn from wells dug in the yards, dirty with filth, and constantly dampened by discharges from the sink-spouts just carried through the walls of the houses. In addition to its rents the Cabot had another auxiliary source of income in the company store at which the management expected its employees to trade. Nine out of ten saw the wisdom of obeying this unwritten law.

As for working conditions they were far different than in the Dennison factory upstreet where twice a day a flood of girls poured forth, pretty enough in their gay clothes "to charm the heart of a wheelbarrow". And where skilled workers made the unheard-of sum of $20 a week. In the Cabot the whole family worked. Mulespinners, the best paid male workers with the exception of a tiny aristocracy of loom fixers, might average between $9 and $10 a week; women working 15

looms received $7 or $8, and in the spinning room a dollar or two less. But the costs of living were much lower than now, with stove coal $6.50 a ton, eggs never more than 25c a dozen, milk 6c a quart, and pork at 10c a pound. Even top notch board in the company houses was only $3 a week. All in all the cotton mill workers made in a year somewhat more than the teachers and their assistants in the village schools who averaged $270.31 and decidedly less than full professors at Bowdoin who received $1800.

Although the wages of children have not been chronicled they often began to work in the mill at seven years of age. The 

Brunswick Telegraph was vaguely disquieted by the picture they presented at the funeral of one of the room overseers. "It was an interesting sight to watch the varying expressions upon the faces of the children as for an instant they looked upon the countenance of their old but now dead overseer." Those in his room marched two by two to the grave, “it was almost a sad sight to watch these little ones, mere tots many of them-as they tramped through the dust, eager to see the body of him they had loved so well deposited in the grave.” Hours for all were eleven a day; and conditions of work in the long, stone two story mill with its many small rooms, dimly lit and ill ventilated, do not meet the conceptions of the present for decency, sanitation or health. But in 1887 the long agitation for effective state regulation of laboring conditions finally overcame the opposition of the "tyrants of the cotton and woolen mills" to use Tenney's phrase. An act of that year for all practical purposes established the ten hour day in manufacturing and mechanical establishments, forbade completely the employment of children under twelve, and required of those under fifteen school attendance for a portion of the year as a pre-requisite for employment. It is only fair to add that many parents, 16

dependent for a livelihood upon the wages of their children, were as opposed to the act as any cotton "tyrant". "But,” as 

Tenney remarks, "if the receiver is as bad as the thief why should not the tempter (the mill owner) come in for his share of punishment."

Undoubtedly the motives for the French migration were economic. But the migration itself transcended economic limitations. These people like all uprooted peoples sought to recreate as closely as they could the civilization they had left behind. Gradually the town grew conscious of the process. 

From time to time the Telegraph noted the arrival of frozen herring, the favorite French diet during Lent, the gaiety of French weddings-"On Monday morning as frequently happens there was a French wedding, a part of the fun always being a ride, after the ceremony is over. Three sleighs driven abreast, as rapidly as livery horses can travel, were going down Federal Street, the occupants being all in high glee,”—and the funeral processions of distinguished dead headed by brass bands. For already there was a French band giving concerts and dances and a French club giving plays. On the whole few 

Frenchmen escaped the province of the mill. To be sure there was a French doctor, Doctor Paré, who had come as a young man to Kents Hill to learn English and had received his degree in medicine from the University of Michigan. There were only a few French-Canadians in trade for the company store controlled the clientele they would logically have served and the initial capital was hard to accumulate. But their youngsters were entering the older stores as clerks; in time after training and thrift they became their owners and managers. Their forays into politics were humiliatingly unsuccessful. 

The real nucleus of this new civilization was the institution, which aside from language, most set them apart from the 17

-- Protestant community, the Catholic Church. Since this church had become essentially Irish in character the growth of catholicism in Brunswick inevitably involved internal tensions. Fortunately the parish was led in the eighties by Father Gorman. 

His name was as Irish as Kelly but his collegiate and theological training had been in Canadian institutions. Young, he was in his late twenties when he came here,-handsome, charming, energetic, he successfully surmounted the divergencies of his congregation, preaching both in French and in English, importing a French-speaking priest for great festivals like Easter, and, perhaps with the aid of his sister, arranging that at the weeklong parish fair the Irish ladies would have the art booth while the French ladies and the young French ladies sold other articles at different stands. In his pastorate the church ceased to be a hand-me-down denomination. He moved his congregation from the second-hand "Protestant temple" they had occupied on Federal Street to an immense new edifice on Pleasant, though it still had an organ purchased from the Congregationalists; he acquired land for a cemetery and the reverent transfer of the dead from the town lot, where they had been buried, to consecrated soil began; he established a school in French, taught by "an accomplished scholar" of one of the teaching orders to supplant the French school which the Cabot Mill had run ostensibly as an accommodation for the children in its French labor force and financed, it was suspected, by increased rents collected from the employees. In the mideighties he had 1400 parishioners, French and Irish alike devoted to him.

The body of Dr. Paré on his untimely death at the age of 

32 was carried back to Canada for burial. There was something symbolical for the whole French-Canadian community in this last journey. The question was always before them: 18

Were they to be a permanent part of the new community or were they simply on a sojourn in les États-Unis to acquire the means to make living endurable on their return to the old country? During the seventies and early eighties the Catholic 

Church and the Province of Quebec joined a vigorous campaign of repatriation, urging the exiles to return and promising them opportunities made ready in Quebec.. To such appeals the answer of the Brunswick community was not unanimous. 

On the one hand the French school was justified by the assertion that the children would never need English instruction since they would return to Canada with their parents. On the other hand it was clear that the majority had determined to remain. The Telegraph notes in 1885 that "The French-Canadians are putting up several dwelling houses on land recently purchased in the northwestern part of the village and the way they put together a wooden building is something of a marvel." 

A year earlier a group of French-Canadians met in the town hall. "The question of naturalization was discussed, the question being should the French take out naturalization papers or remain foreigners. It was unanimously decided to naturalize it was decided that all possible means of information should be resorted to to gain political information." Such decisions hardly endeared the French to the native Americans who had formerly denounced them as aliens and complained because they sent money out of the state. Now their competition and divergencies from type threatened to become permanent. C. C. Everett might regard the situation with a patronizing detachment. He observed in his anniversary address that "It is very pleasant early in a summer evening, to pass from the classic shades of the college to the lower end of the town, in which one finds oneself as if in another world. The bright faces and lively jargon of the French create for the moment 19

the illusion of being in some foreign land." Lesser breeds threw stones through the windows of French tenements and picked street quarrels. Undoubtedly the most scholarly summary of the native attitude was expressed by William MacDonald, then professor of history at Bowdoin. "As a class they are treated considerately in public because of their votes, disparaged in private because of general dislike, and sought by all for the work they do and the money they spend." 

That the village had reached maturity in the eighties was shown by the necessity of taking measures to protect the health of its inhabitants from disease and prevent the destruction of property by fire. Village life, like urban life, depended upon water. As long as Brunswick was only a settled country-side this local problem seemed easy of solution. Although in dry times high winds carried dust typhoons the length of Maine Street from the Congregational Church to the Androscoggin, Brunswick in reality was built, in Tenney's phrase, “over a lake". In most places only "a thin crust of land" overlaid the water level, and in spots even it gave way to marshes and pools of stagnant water. McKeen's swamp and peat bog lay south and west of the town, the area east of Union Street and south of Pleasant was a moist lowland kept dry by drains or brooks that ran northward into the Androscoggin or flowed east to the Mall, emptied into a shallow pool, and finally made their way across Federal Street into the immense marsh that lay near Jordan Avenue. Every householder found it a simple matter to run down a well, fifteen feet or more to the water under his property and the town for fire protection had dug thirty huge cisterns, lined them with brick, and relied upon surface water or seepage from below to fill them. The business enterprises of the town first sensed the inadequacy of such arrangements. 

The Cabot Mill installed its own pumps; the Dennisons pumped 20

water from the river to their factory, in arrangement with the town placed a few hydrants along lower Maine Street and extended the water pipe into the town hall building; the Maine 

Central Railroad, which had once filled its tanks from a neighboring brook with the aid of a windmill, laid pipes to the river and installed a pumping apparatus. Some private houses, stores and offices had secured connections with these piecemeal systems.

No town sewerage system existed. Sink-spouts flowed their discharge over the ground or were, with the occasional connivance of the selectmen, connected with the gutters in the streets or the brooks and drains that sluggishly moved to swamp or river. In some instances privies emptied into the last two channels. Short of a town system it was realized that cesspools should be constructed to catch these wastes. But they cost money and besides it was clearly recognized that the "fouling of wells" was hastened by their proximity. Seepage from surface drainage was slower, some thought it non-existent. People prayed for rain, not for their lawns' sake, but to flush the gutters and drains clear of odors and filth. In 1880 a man from the 

Middle Ages would have felt at home amidst the dirt and smells of Brunswick.

The situation had become intolerable. For a portion of the year the village was under mud and water. Cellars were flooded, streets were impassable quagmires, or else lagoons from which hapless teamsters, especially those under the influence of "old tanglefoot", were occasionally saved from drowning. In all seasons disease was at hand. Dysentery and diarrhea were endemic; every summer doctors when pressed had to admit they were carrying ten or twenty typhoid cases on their lists; and other ailments,-smallpox, scarlet fever, and diphtheriaperiodically ravaged the population. In 1886 pestilence reached 21

a peak. The Village Improvement Society anticipated an outbreak of cholera; smallpox, raging in Quebec and Montreal, was turned back only by the heroic efforts of the recently appointed State Board of Health, and the compulsory vaccination of all employees in the Cabot Mill; scarlet fever and typhoid claimed their usual quota of victims; and a diphtheria epidemic fastened itself upon the town. The first public announcement of its existence was the quiet statement of Father Gorman in May that since the first of the year he had buried more children than he had baptized; soon Dr. Paré was giving flaming figures; and editor Tenney swung into action with the demand that the owners of private tenements and especially the Cabot Company clean up the cesspools, privies and drains they had compelled their French tenants to accept. The sights along Mill Street and the Cove are “enough to make a Christian swear", stormed Tenney. When the company proved obdurate and dilatory the Telegraph announced it had "shown a degree of brutality almost inconceivable in a civilized community". The company imported three outside doctors who, after an examination of a few cases, diagnosed the disease as not diphtheria but "follicular tonsilitis"; the State

Board of Health, after investigation, contradicted this opinion and supported Paré. Before the year ended Dr. Paré had had 

152 cases of diphtheria with 19 deaths, 78 cases of typhoid with 11 deaths, and 16 fatal cases of diarrhea. The State Board of 

Health commented, "Brunswick's record for illness probably will not be equalled in New England." Undoubtedly some shrugged the disaster aside; the calamity had fallen chiefly upon 

Frenchtown. Of such as these Tenney sarcastically inquired "if a French-Canadian had any rights which his Yankee brother is bound to protect." But disease didn't stay at Mill Street. It entered the big houses along the Mall and even the fraternities 22

in college. Prospective students stayed away because of Brunswick's reputation as an unhealthful village. 

The remedy was clear to the discerning. Drainage pipes should be installed to lower the subsurface water level; a water system should introduce pure water into the village; and sewers should carry away the waste. But the problem was a staggering one. At the outset there was a question of expense. The town, already burdened with a debt of $90,000, would have to expend $100,000 alone to build a water system. Then there was human selfishness and inertia. Those with means could fetch or purchase water from Paradise Spring, one of the few springs outside ancient Greece ever specifically celebrated in a Phi Beta Kappa poem, whose water was endorsed by the college chemists and practically the entire faculty. Still others had wells driven through hard clay or rock to pure water. Still others were content with their wells, they had used them for sixty years, and what did it matter if they analyzed forty to sixty grams of organic or inorganic matter per gallon. Tenney thus addressed them: "I would rather drink whiskey. For a man can well count upon the form of disease which may attack him from an indiscriminate use of whiskey, while heaven can only tell of what he may die if he uses filthy well water." There was truth in the latter part of this utterance. For one of the great barriers to improvement were the rudimentary and inchoate notions about contagion. Pasteur had in 1878 definitely announced that infections and contagious diseases were propagated by micro-organisms, bacteria, and seven years later the first annual report of the State Board of Health summarized admirably the modern knowledge of contagion. Naturally these radical scientific ideas were hardly the stock in trade of the average Brunswickian, but it is surprising to find Tenney, always alert to scientific advance and the constant companion 23

of the teachers in the Maine Medical School, writing a strange confusion on the subject. He advanced at the same time the theory that disease was due to the germs in the excreta of the sick and that it might be caused by rotting vegetables in the cellar and the "peculiar tone of the atmosphere". 

Perplexed by the tug of interest and theories the words of an early nineteenth century ballad certainly applied: 

Brunswick like mackerel in the spring

Knew not which way to run. 

When the project to introduce water into the town ran into difficulties it was natural that the citizens should resort first to the cheaper task, that of under-drainage. After the proper engineering studies a network of tile drains was laid under the area the other side of the track. For a while the scoffers rode high, but the next spring cellars were drier, road surfaces harder, and the areas of damp ground greatly diminished. In spite of these gains the situation was only the more complicated. Surface drains and streams were now more clogged and noisome than ever, in well after well water disappeared while disgruntled owners chased after it with deeper excavations, and the cisterns for the fire department went dry. Once a house caught fire it was doomed. The hand tubs would throw a brief spray on the conflagration and in the next issue of the Telegraph the owners might insert a card worded like that of the Bryants, whose home on Federal Street was completely consumed. "Mr. and Mrs. L. H. Bryant extend their thanks to the 

Fire Department and citizens of this town for their earnest and sympathetic endeavors to save their home from the late fire on Federal Street." In his next annual report the Chief Engineer of the Fire Department made the sage observation, “The fire department is useless without water." 24

Four years earlier some enterprising capitalists in Brunswick and other places had obtained from the legislature a charter for the Pejepscot Water Company, authorizing them in Brunswick, Topsham and Bath "to provide, from the Androscoggin River, pure water for domestic and municipal purposes, including the extinguishment of fires". Many, including the editor of the Telegraph, detected an incompatibility between the objectives sought, "pure water", and the means chosen, "the Androscoggin River", for "the notion was now dead", Tenney assured his readers, that running water purified itself and that in the instance at hand the contamination from Lewiston and Lisbon had disappeared by the time the river reached Brunswick. 

But by the mid-eighties the town was more interested in "the great boon of a full supply of water" than in its purity, and many were hopeful that half a loaf might yet prove a whole one. The annual town meeting voted to pay the company $2,000 a year in return for a stated number of hydrants and drinking fountains. Gangs of French-Canadians began digging trench, pipe was laid, the standpipe rose, ring on ring, on Standpipe Hill, and a pumping station was built at the edge of the river above the upper dam and supplied with filters to remove the visible debris from the stream. By the autumn of 1886 the water was turned into the mains; from the hydrants streams were shot high into the air; the fireman's muster at the end of 

September had plenty of water for its playing contest; and more private householders than had been expected sought connections at the base rate of $8 a family, with an additional $5 for a bath tub, $6 for a water closet, and $5 for a stable. The following year the Telegraph chronicled the arrival of typhoid and dysentery attacks at an unusually early date. For years the 

Pejepscot Water Company or its successors sought other 25

sources of supply; no adequate response to the quest came in 

Brunswick's Golden Age.

Eventually the influx of water compelled the abandonment of Brunswick's primitive sewerage arrangements. This last step was, however, retarded, as so many other betterments had been, by the question of expense; and the question of expense was entangled with the unwillingness of the town as a whole to finance improvements which apparently benefited the village alone. It was an old quarrel and led on occasion to efforts to separate the town from the village and incorporate the latter as a city. These proposals always collapsed for, though the rural districts looked with suspicion on expenditures for village schools, water supply, and fire department, they did not wish to sacrifice the bulk of taxes that maintained the country roads. 

But to meet this latest emergency, that of sewerage disposal, the village had to become of political age. A charter for a village corporation to comprise all of the territory within one mile of the town hall was introduced into the legislature and was to go into effect when accepted by two-thirds of the town's voters. At first rejected, the Brunswickians finally braced themselves to ratify it. One provision of the charter authorized the corporation to issue its bonds "for the construction and maintenance of sewers and drains." And at a corporation meeting in 1894 the first appropriations were voted. 

Within three years the sewer committee had spent approximately $88,000, laid nearly nine miles of pipe, and confessed "not so many house connections have been made as could be wished . . . but the time will come when joining it will be thought quite as desirable as having glass in the windows, or chimneys for the fire places."

Even if the factory stood at the center of the town's new civilization, the older institutions which Everett had men- 26

tioned-the college and the church-still shaped its culture. 

The college, indeed, gave a peculiar distinction to the village. 

It made it, if not the Athens, at least the Cambridge of Maine and enabled an author in 1891 to observe, "Naturally the society of Brunswick is of a much higher tone than in most towns of seven thousand people." In Brunswick's Golden Age the college was at last quickened by change. It was high time. 

Originally started as a frontier institution, it was after threequarters of a century a provincial institution still, ridden with denominationalism, starved for funds, limited in curriculum. 

Only the talent of a few great teachers and the sturdiness of the undergraduate material entrusted to its care had saved it from oblivion. Nor had the harassed administration of General 

Joshua L. Chamberlain, ending in 1883, bettered matters. Indeed the establishment of other Maine colleges threatened to undermine the monopolistic position held by Bowdoin. The eminent analyst of railroad finance, Henry Varnum Poor, departing from his fiftieth anniversary in sober mood, noted that there were fewer students in the college than in 1835, there was an air of apology about the campus, "an appearance of weakness on every side", and that the only thing that had grown was the campus trees. Prescriptions for the disease differed. Some wanted to simplify the college government by eliminating the board of overseers, the entrenchment of conservatism, "a lumbering coach fearfully and wonderfully made up". The Boston 

Post announced that what Bowdoin needed "is money and brains, the former with fat interest available, and the latter in the head of a big-headed, big-hearted, and well-informed president."

The difficulty of obtaining a successor to Chamberlain demonstrated the critical position of the old college. Although the committee entrusted with selection attempted to operate 27

quietly, the eminent men who had been approached or their friends soon inserted in the papers an announcement of the tender and its refusal. It was a distinction to be asked; it was a danger to accept. Eventually the Boards chose William DeWitt 

Hyde, a young Harvard graduate, and Egbert Smyth, his sponsor, pressed him to accept in a letter throbbing with moral earnestness and closing with a two word materialistic anticlimax: "Salary $3000." Hyde undoubtedly had advantages.

He was young, only twenty-six years of age, he had a young faculty, in 1891 the ranking member was only forty-six, and Chamberlain's administration, however misguided, had broken the cake of custom. The latter's insistence, for instance, on military drill prepared the way for physical education and his introduction of engineering and practical science had prepared the curriculum for changes. Although Bowdoin had chosen to ignore the earlier collegiate revolution set in train by 

Thomas Jefferson at the University of Virginia, a revolution that affected frontier institutions as remote from the Old Dominion as the University of Michigan, it was not to escape the transformation that Eliot had inaugurated in the seventies at Harvard. Hyde of Bowdoin was also Hyde of Harvard.

He may not have given allegiance to every article in his exemplar's creed, but within a decade he had enlarged the faculty, raised its salaries, established separate professorships in history and government and a new one in economics and sociology, given a new status to science and the modern languages, liberalized entrance requirements, and inaugurated, perforce, the elective system. For the old orthodoxy and evangelicism, he substituted "muscular Christianity". Nothing demonstrated the liberalization of the college better than the fact that in 1888 Hyde was able to advocate the election of Cleveland before the Young Men's Democratic Club of Brunswick. Although 28

he did not escape censure, he at least kept his job in a college whose visiting committee some thirty-five years before had condemned undergraduates for political activities: "As they engender variance and ill will and seriously interfere with the appropriate duties of the college, they should be studiously kept from academic shades, for the Muses can never be found amid the din of party strife."

The commencement procession that wound annually from the campus into the Congregational Church was a visible evidence of the close connection between college and church; it failed to reveal, however, the dominance still held over the town by Congregationalists in spite of the appearance and strength of rivals. The Catholics, far more numerous, have already been dealt with. Within the Protestant fold there were two Baptist churches, one of which in the eighties had built a Queen Anne edifice on Union Street, "a model of taste, convenience, and beauty;" the new church of the Universalists, erected after the destruction by fire of their older edifice, boasted a bell presented by no less a family than the Dennisons; the Episcopalians worshipped in a building designed by Richard Upjohn and were sung to by a vested boy choir; and the Unitarians met in a chapel "modern yet cozy, attractive, and free from debt", possessed a Bible presented by H. W. 

Longfellow, and assembled a congregation, in the words of the Portland Press, which "though not numerous" included "many persons residing in Brunswick and Topsham of high social standing and some of wealth." But the Congregationalists were unique. Their church was set on "the hill". Richard Upjohn, probably the most eminent church architect in the mid-nineteenth century, had designed it and the Congregationalists had paid him a modest fee for his services. The Episcopalian chapel, a mile away, was an act of missionary charity on his part. 29

Although purists had difficulty in admitting as true Gothic a building of battened wood construction, fake buttresses, and a dented roof instead of a clerestory, the Congregationalists had at least, since they had chosen the style, got a lot for their money. Now in the Golden Age they enlarged their physical plant by the construction of an adjacent chapel or parish house. 

The expansion was necessary, since the church possessed no rooms for committees or Sunday School, and “in case of a person fainting away, it would be necessary to take him outdoors." 

Fortunately the architects pronounced against the feasibility of raising the existing building and inserting a chapel beneath it; instead they butted a chapel of cottage Gothic against the main edifice. This addition was built virtually without debt and what is more, if the pastor is to be believed, "without an unchristian word".

In New England the eighties were a critical time for Congregationalism, for conservatives and liberals were battling for possession of Andover Theological Seminary and struggling over science and religion and the literal interpretation of creeds and the Bible. Whether such controversies divided the Brunswick parish we can only surmise. The sharp-tongued editor of the Brunswick Telegraph, commenting on the reshingling of the church, remarked of the carpenter, "If he can cover all the breaks in the creed of the denomination, he will receive the thanks of the best of the partisans of the old and new schools of theology." But whatever its internal difficulties, the church and the parish remained at the center of power. Mr. Fisher, the pastor in the eighties, thus revealed his congregation to itself: "Christians are on the whole, the well to do. Many of them are people of leisure. Many have a surplus after living well and supplying themselves with what they need and more. 

This is true of many of you. You have time to read and study 30

and talk art, and learn accomplishments, and indulge in modest pleasures. Most of you can supply all essential wants of yourselves and families. What is the method and policy of your living? You aim at ways wholesome for body and mind. You intend to be on pleasant relations with your neighbors. You visit back and forth. In some measure you propose to keep up with the times. You consult and conserve your social standing. 

Ladies have a calling list of those whom they consider somewhere about their level, and they do some hard work in paying and repaying and paying over again calls which maintain respectability for themselves and the men whom they represent. And they keep a visiting account, they pay their social debts. They invite those who have invited them. It is considered necessary to cultivate the acquaintance of the agreeable and the advantageous, the rich and people of standing in the world. This takes a great deal of time and strength, and is a tax upon the means of many." No historical record of the tremors set in motion by this stark analysis of social and economic position survives. But a mere scrutiny of the town's institutions supported the assertion that the "advantageous" were members of the parish. The Gentlemen's Club,-or sometimes simply "The Club"-organized in 1884 for social intercourse and discussion of topics of public interest had by the end of the following decade twenty-three members. One was the Catholic priest, one an earnest Universalist, one an unbeliever, and the rest were members of the Congregational parish. 

Of the ten town officers in a typical year, all the selectmen, the town clerk, the treasurer, the collector, the auditor, and two members of the superintending school committee were of the same persuasion. The church in the narrow sense of the word was no longer an established church; but its position in the town was certainly an "established" one. 31

The old Puritan austerities, however, were softening. The celebration of the Christian feast days, old and new, witnessed the change. Children's Sunday, conceived by the Methodists, spread in the eighties to the other churches. “Nothing”, said 

Tenney, "can be more attractive than the floral decorations, the singing of the merry-hearted birds in the churches the second Sunday in June." Christmas became a universal holiday. Every church had its Christmas tree with its candy and presents, and its Christmas decorations of wreaths and candles. "Doubtless a good many trees were set up in private houses”, wrote Tenney in 1883. Easter with its emphasis on joy and resurrection and the flowering of the earth usurped the place of Fast Day and Fast Day became simply a secular holiday. 

Although a faithful few straggled into the churches on Fast Day-they heard in 1883 "an interesting discourse, though possibly too long", most went driving, enjoyed picnics, and attended ball games. On the whole Tenney thought it a "good thing", but he reserved particular approval for Professor Lee of the College and Mr. Fish, principal of the High School, who went to Gurnet's bridge on Fast Day and dredged for specimens in deep water. The professor also "experimented with a water telescope 3 feet long with which he could see to a depth of ten feet. This was a most sensible way of passing the day, in the interests of science . . .' 

But the wider tolerance of the age did not embrace dangerous innovations in religion. Here hostility was a Brunswick tradition. President McKeen, Bowdoin's first president and one time Congregational pastor, had decades earlier fired a Parthian shot at the Baptist and Methodist itinerant preachers in Maine. They were "illiterate vagrants who understood neither what they say nor whereof they affirm." In the eighties a similar disdain was directed against the Salvation 32

Army whose invasion of Maine was regarded with the degree of apprehension reserved for an epidemic. Before the arrival of its practitioners strange tales were told of this "pestilent nuisance" and these "English dervishes". A "Hallelujah soldier" was reported to have risen from his seat in a railroad car and invited the passengers to give "three cheers for Jesus." Advance abuse did not keep them from Brunswick; a little band hired a hall and conducted a series of meetings. A warfare of letters and comment surged through the columns of the Telegraph. 

Their bass drum frightened the horses; their clapping of hands, singing, and parading misdemeaned religion. But a thousand attended a farewell meeting to the captain of the group and their ministry in Brunswick reached the "tough cases" hitherto outside the Christian pale.

A large share of the secular interests of the town naturally clustered about the churches. They gave organ recitals, occasionally unauthorized-after one evening service the new Congregational instrument was played "without permission or consultation of the Pastor, officers of the church or parish, or the musical committee or choir"; their autumnal fairs raised money and gave a market to the products of womanly skill and ingenuity; and church suppers fed crowds of two hundred to five hundred people with a menu so varied that attendants could choose their favorite dishes. Scalloped oysters and roast turkey were usually exhausted before the disgruntled late comers were fed. Brains were racked to give these affairs novelty as well as bounty. The season of 1885 ought to have been capped by the Universalists' "soap bubble levee,” but the young ladies of the Congregational Church surpassed it with an orange supper "orange in color, flavor, and orange golden in social intercourse". The tired Tenney remarked, "We have 33

had all sorts of entertainments and now the orange is to have its hour."

Although the chief organization remained the church, other groups enlisted lay loyalties and provided social relaxation. 

The fire companies, the Niagara and the Kennebec, were in reality men's clubs in which membership was given by vote of the members and their engine houses had rooms where members gathered for relaxation and business. Often their occasional rivalry led to unhappy inefficiencies in fire fighting, but reciprocal oyster suppers washed the bitter feelings away. 

Their frequent playing victories always aroused local pride. 

On return from such triumphs the band escorted the fire laddies from the station along a street lit by bonfires, and the editor, perhaps in minatory mood, commended them for their "orderly and quiet demeanour". Membership in a fraternal order, Odd Fellows or Masons, seemed much less vivid. 

Perhaps the latter organizations filled that human urge for secular religion which is as old as the mediaeval guilds and as new as Rotary and Kiwanis. But undoubtedly in the eighties the greatest satisfaction of this need was politics. The canonical belief was, of course, the Republican. Senior classes at 

Bowdoin expressed overwhelming Republican preferences; the

College on the eve of the presidential campaign of 1884 gave an LL.D. to James D. Blaine, eliciting from the caustic editor of the New York Sun the interrogation: "What has become of the

Professor of Morals in Bowdoin?" and state and national elections always rolled up unquestioned majorities for the Republican ticket. But in 1884 outsiders betrayed Brunswick. Blaine carried the town but the nation elected Cleveland. The joy of the downtrodden, the frustrated minority, knew no bounds.

As they heard the returns Tuesday night, the Democrats "yelled tremendously"; on Saturday when all was settled they 34

shook the town windows with a prolonged salute from the guns of the local battery; and then on the next Tuesday the official celebration brought to an end a week of serenades and noise. The first uproar was a torchlight parade designed to traverse every street in Brunswick and Topsham. It was led by forty men on horseback and wound its hilarious way accompanied by colored lights, rockets, roman candles, bonfires, and dynamite bombs whose explosion was "fearfully shattering to the nervous system". In the excitement the marchers omitted 

Potter and Cleaveland Streets, undoubtedly a portent to the righteous of the confusion to come for the next four years; and finally six hundred sat down to dinner in the town hall while on Federal Street there were private dinners "for gentlemen who personally enjoyed themselves." Quite evidently some of the patterns of propriety had strayed from the fold, if they had ever been in it. For the parade, Captain Mustard illuminated his house "in the old style" with fifteen candles to each window; other ship captains strung Chinese lanterns or ran up Old Glory or stretched signal flags from house to house; even on Federal Street the decorations for taste and enthusiasm bore off "the palm of the evening". But when the parade reached the lower edge of the town, it was the humble who celebrated. The occupants of the factory boarding house illuminated their windows and Oliver J. Trudeau and nine others, "unknown", lit up their houses or tenements along Mill Street. This was indeed a hint of the future. 

Naturally the Cambridge of Maine aspired to patronize the arts. If Brunswickians were not then writing books, they were at least eager to read them and the eighties saw the improvement of agencies for making them available. The young ladies reading clubs had books of their own; there were Sunday 

School libraries-Apples of Gold, A Ride through the Holy 35

Land, Busy Hands and Patient Hearts, Fred Roberts' Start in 

Life were sample titles from the Congregational collectionand there were two circulating libraries. All these were inadequate, the enlightened realized, and the "Bowdoin College Library cannot, for wise and judicious reasons, be opened to the public." Realizing the town itself must be relied upon for contributions, ministers, college professors and leading citizens in 1883 secured from the state incorporation as the Public 

Library and Reading Room Association. This organization then induced the town to lease it a room in the new town building at a merely nominal fee, and proceeded to solicit donations and sell memberships. Life memberships were fifty dollars, and other memberships were $2.00 and $1.00 a year. The response proved a gratifying one. Unwanted books were cleared from attics and bookshelves, Massachusetts donated over one hundred public documents on education and agriculture, and total subscriptions were over $1000. Both the 

Dennison and the Androscoggin companies gave $50; the 

Cabot gave none. Like other enterprises the Library was an excuse for benefit performances of one sort or another; the first by the Young Ladies was entitled "The Shakespeare 

Water Cure" which cleared $160, perhaps because the ushers were so "enticing" to use Tenney's phrase. A good share of the reading matter provided was pretty sobersided, but a list of the fiction purchased by the Book Committee headed by Professor Lee of Bowdoin was a credit to the taste of the era. 

When books grew tiring lectures and readings diverted and informed. The deluge included art lectures with colored stereoptican slides and ecstatic comment; the Bowdoin Literary 

Association course of lectures, some provided by divines, some by members of the faculty, listed Professor Young on "What's in a Name?", Professor Chapman on "King Arthur: the Hero 36

of English Epic", and Professor Carmichael on "Glacial Physics". The Bowdoin Y. M. C. A. lecture course was given largely by alumni; but the Public Library Course of Lectures called once again on the professors. Even the Bowdoin Football Association when it required funds could think of no better money-raising scheme than a lecture course of travel talks. Although Americans have always been gluttons for this form of instruction, Tenney admits at the end of the Public Library Course that only seventy or eighty people went to the lectures while four hundred would attend a free concert. 

Brunswick certainly had music. A good share of it naturally was imported and the variety was startling. At one extreme stood Madame Frye's family of vocalist and instrumental artistes. Madame Frye sang a tenor solo, "on the higher notes it was difficult to admit that the music was rendered by a female voice." At the other extreme was the English Opera 

Company giving Maritana or Don Caesar de Bazan. "English opera is easier to understand and appreciate than Italian opera," Tenney took the pains to inform his hearers, as the 

Miller & Dixie Opera Company gave three days of Patience,

Iolanthe, The Mascot and Pinafore. But if you wished to hear 

Theodore Thomas or the Boston Symphony Orchestra, you had to go to Portland. Local talent performed. The Catholic 

Church, with liberal raids upon its choir, gave musical entertainments; the College had quartets and glee clubs, and Professor Chapman's "fine bass voice". There were local instrumentalists galore and three brass bands, one of them juvenile. 

The true music lovers gathered either in the music class or later the Orpheus Club to try their voices under the direction of Hermann Kotzschmar, the German forty-eighter who did so much to bring an appreciation of music to northern New England. "More lady pupils than male pupils" elected to pay the 37

$2.fee for the twelve lessons and to sing selections from 

Haydn's Creation and from the rather exotic oratorios of 

Dudley Buck, a now forgotten American composer.

Finally there was painting. As in the case of music, much of the stimulus came from outside. Thus there were the College collections, a portion of which Professor Henry Johnson, back from Germany, was trying to hang in the unsatisfactory light of Memorial Hall. At recurrent intervals, furthermore, a new panel was executed upon the chapel walls. Thus Adam and Eve came to Bowdoin in a mural. Apparently prudery had abated, for no voice challenged the painting as improper; instead a coy controversy ran its way, with much quoting of scripture, as to whether the figures should be clothed with skins or fig leaves, until Tenney finally called a halt. "The person of refined and cultivated taste, however, does not examine the costume; but he looks at the faces, the figures, and we think, after one examination,... that he will come to the conclusion the picture in its strong outlines, in the warmth and naturalness of color, is one of extraordinary merit..." Meanwhile the ladies were hard at it. Every church fair had its painted articles. The art counter of the Congregational Church in 1883 had painted "sachet bags, one with birds in flight, marvellously well executed, blotters, a pair of owls, wise as 

Solomon, and a vase very richly colored." Someone else executed roses on ground glass; still others a "clock valance painted charmingly on velvet", or a painted apron. Kate 

Furbish was at the moment abroad studying art; four years later she induced twenty-five other ladies to form a life class, contribute $4.00 apiece and make sketches from casts, objects, and the model. To display their accomplishments they held a combined reception and exhibition in the Odd Fellows hall. 

They decorated their rooms with ferns and flowers, served ice 38

cream and cake, and along with their guests wore full evening costume. But that a feminine culture meant necessarily a lack of energy Kate Furbish refuted. No "sheltered woman", she rambled the woods of Maine, unescorted and without a pistol, building her own rafts to push into ponds to examine the water lilies, and painting in twelve years' time one thousand faithful portraits in water color of the flora of Maine, later bound into fourteen folio volumes.

It would be absurd to approach the culture of the town from a class basis. But quite obviously certain aspects of its life were peculiarly the province of the true, the wise and the good. Men of that order joined the Gentlemen's Club and contributed to its discussions; their daughters enrolled in the three leading clubs of the Mummers, the Crescents, and the Shakespeares; and as families they bought memberships to the public library that enabled them to take out books rather than read them in the library room and attended the more intellectual lectures and the organ recitals. The audience at one 

Congregational recital compensated for its smallness "by the character of the people who attended, not only in a social but also in an artistic point of view, the real lover of music being there." And their children were practically the only pupils in the high school. This institution, established in 1849 largely through the efforts of a Bowdoin College professor, was always doing "good work" in Tenney's phrase, but its graduating classes—once in the eighties only two graduated-were smaller than those of Freeport with a smaller population. Many children, particularly the boys, left the school system at the end of grammar school or after one year of high school. 

Members of the upper classes might go on railroad excursions to the White Mountains or Rangeley Lakes. They certainly did not patronize the Sunday excursions which attracted 39

"only the bad element of our population, men given to an inordinate use of liquor and to rough and rude sports."

Undoubtedly it was people of this type that watched with evident enjoyment the dog fight on Maine Street which eventually ended "when four or five men of honor, courage, and pity came forward and were successful in breaking up the fight. This occasioned a war of tongues with the two-legged animals, some strong blowing but no striking." Nor did the refined patronize and bet on the go-as-you-please races in the town hall or crowd into the roller skating rinks, particularly when there were polo games between town teams mounted on roller skates. The coarseness and roughness of such contests was as prominent as in present day hockey, but it aroused more condemnation. In Tenney's words the game was “as bad as horse-racing and almost as bad and demoralizing as church quarrels are coming to be." Even among the entertainments given in the new town hall the judicious had to make discreet choices. Good taste permitted them to see Margaret Mather in the fine old comedy, The Honeymoon-"our beautiful hall will doubtless be filled with a fashionable audience"-but Hi Henry's Premium Minstrels had an opulence that was certainly vulgar. It presented its twenty-five people "In a programme of choicest excellence... Among its many features are its costly and Elaborate First Part Circle, Dressed in Velvet, Plush, Silk, Satin, and Diamonds with an Orchestra of Soloists in Gorgeous Uniforms, followed by an Interlude of Brilliant

Specialties. The Entertainment terminating with an hour of genuine Opera, written expressly for them, entitled, "The 

Legend of Grey Beard's Rock'... and a Gorgeous Denouement of Glittering Splendor under prismatic colored fires." Fortunately in town there were only three subscribers to the Police 40

Gazette, the sort of journal which should not be tolerated in "a decent country village".

Those who knew better were always attempting to correct the manners and elevate the taste of those who didn't. But it is far from clear upon whom their blows actually fell. The 

Telegraph lectured girls against chewing spruce gum and putting their hands in the pockets of their reefers; but it was equally opposed to the bustle which the editor pointed out the Greeks had not attached to Venus. The discussion of table manners leads to further confusion. Tenney took a high stand against the use of the knife as a means of carrying food to the mouth-"one might as well use a shovel"; even the necessity of eating rapidly in a railroad restaurant justified no departure from decorum. At once the columns of the paper were filled with serious controversy on the subject. There were appeals to authority-the eminent Henry Ward Beecher always ate pie with a knife, there were discussions of peculiarly trying situations, the eating of pastry. "It would be an improvement to hash up the article with a knife, and carry it to the mouth with the fork," concluded a correspondent. Finally it was pointed out that technical improvements, in this case the four-tined fork, rendered obsolete the use of the knife. Probably table manners even among the best people required adjustment. 

The Ladies of the Methodist Church in the eighties issued The 

Ladies' Handbook and Household Assistant: A Manual of 

Religious and Table Etiquette. Its instructions concerning formal written invitations have an air of unreality but it descended to business on the subject of table manners. "Unfold your napkin and lay it in your lap; never use it in the form of a bib. Cut your food with a knife but convey it to your mouth with a fork... Drink from your cup, not from your saucer 

Never cut or break bread into soup or gravy... Never put 41

salt on the table cloth... Never pick the teeth at the table or in the presence of ladies after the meal." 

As the village came into its own, its physical appearance altered. In the outskirts they were dumping "tin cans, boots, hats, and whatnots" and cutting down trees along many a woodland drive but inside the village they were building fine new residences. Their irregular and sometimes archly disposed windows and decorative woodwork were enthusiastically admired "as a change from the everlasting style of wooden houses common all over New England, more especially in country villages." The conventional paint, white woodwork and green blinds, gave way to colorful combinations of orange, brick-red, and olive-green. Wealthy owners of the old houses began their modernization, installing steam heat and electric lights, altering the exterior with bay windows, so that insiders could see up and down the street, and replacing the fine interior woodwork and old dados with walnut trim, cherry doors, panels of whitewood, some with dark and mottled centers. Ceilings were frescoed in colors to match the wall paper borders. Even in death there was a new elegance. One of the local undertakers "has purchased a new hearse, built after the style used in the large cities, rich in its appointments and tasteful withal. This hearse is certainly an improvement over the one owned by the town and we have no doubt Mr. Toothaker will find a liberal patronage for it."

The streets took on a new urbanity. Brick walks and curbings were extended along Maine Street, granite street crossings were laid, electricity was substituted for the old oil and gas street lamps. The station grounds were graded and seeded; householders threw down their white fences when Tenney's campaign against pasturing loose cows on the streets finally bore fruit and began mowing their lawns instead of haying 42

them twice a year. The Village Improvement Society, with recurrent pleas for membership funds, inaugurated a campaign to beautify the Mall. On the lower Mall the pool, now a stinking nuisance, and the open drains were filled in and grassed; the young trees, large enough to cast a shade, were trimmed; and the whole was surrounded by a painted white fence. Later the upper Mall was laid off, planted and partially enclosed. And all the while, valiant efforts were made to solve the problem of the triangle on the hill. It was curbed at a cost of $200, but for years the stone surrounded a gaping, empty cistern. Finally even this was filled in and grassed. "It now waits the benevolence of some wealthy person interested in the good old town to plant upon it some ornamental work", concluded Tenney. 

And then as a final expression of civic pride the new town hall was built to replace the one, "a Yankee barn, built of brick, after a model of a Grecian temple," which had burned twenty years before. Campaign after campaign for the new building had frittered away upon the rocks of economy or better uses for the town money. Now there was no staying an aroused citizenry. A town meeting swept aside opposition and voted the necessary funds; houses on the town lot were whisked away and trundled about the streets to their new sites,-all with celerity; and as masons and carpenters went to work to embody in solid form the vision of architect Fassett of Portland, "The Town Hall Congress", observed Tenney, "assembled promptly every morning at seven o'clock on the sidewalk, and may remain in session all night, for aught we can say, as when we go home to tea, we leave the debaters hard at it, discussing various points of construction." At last in June, 1884 the building was done. From the beginning it was hoped it would be more than a mere structure to house the postoffice, the town offices, the jail, the municipal court, 43

and the Grand Army of the Republic. An elegant room with marble mantle piece was to contain the new public library. 

The large tastefully decorated auditorium was to refine the rough humors of the town meeting and the stage, with its six new and realistic sets of scenery, was equipped to give a proper setting to concerts and "to present in the best manner the ordinary dramas of the day". Thus the building in the words of the Hon. Charles Gilman was to be an "educator", an "ornament to the town," and "a superb expression of the wealth, progress, and public spirit of the citizens of Brunswick." 

Viewed from the exterior Tenney regretted that "the south front, exceedingly picturesque, is not as open to view as the Maine Street front." But his doubts disappeared when the interior was reached. Downstairs were the memorial tablets to the soldiers of the Civil War, “of mixed marble”, striking, yet "in perfect harmony". Upstairs one passed through the vestibule "painted in Pompeian style, with bands" into an auditorium, its balcony sweeping in graceful curves, and the whole lit by huge gas chandeliers and decorated with frescoed ceiling and walls “tinted in old gold, with a frieze on the top of the Persian order, of the 16th century, from designs taken from the Art Museum in London. The frieze on the bottom of the wall above the dado is of the Persian order, of the 6th century. The Proscenium in design and decoration, is of 

Romanesque with borders and spandrel ornaments of the 9th century, taken from the Library of Paris." Since all times and countries seem to have been gathered to pay homage to Brunswick, "the largest town in Maine," it seemed suitable that the opening of the hall should be a prolonged civic festival. That 

June week began with a dedication ceremony on Monday night, a round of speeches, heard, as well as acoustic properties would permit, by fourteen hundred people; the next day a 44

"charming" concert for the benefit of the public library;

Wednesday saw a concert given by a local band discoursing “music far above the average of ordinary bands"; and then two days of fair given by the Ladies' Relief Corps. In response to public opinion and $35.00 contributed by two Brunswickians, the ladies sold no chances in the new hall, it was far too sacred for that.

Looking back a few years later, after the sound of the selfcongratulatory speeches had died away, Charlotte Mellen

Packard, a shrewd spinster of unimpeachable social position reflected: "Once our fine building which roofs the postoffice and town library, besides the large hall and various private offices, was food for mighty wrangling; while the handsome 

High School building is yet the nightmare of the parsimonious. 

Providence gives some men the courage of their convictions, however unpopular, and to its honest, fighting, progressive citizens every town is beholden. Brunswick is proud of its solid citizens, now, as always, ready to lead the van of improvement. If a somewhat lengthy procession follows in the rear, the conservative habit of the rural mind is not always stupid; the tardy assent is often worth waiting for."
