Romance and Texas 

|EXAS is the giant of American states. It is a land of romance and super-achievements. The Tejas, the cavedwellers, the sun-worshipers and the cannibal tribes of the gulf coast are responsible for a strange and romantic history that fires the imagination. Like the fortunes of ancient 

Troy, King Arthur and his Knights of the Round Table, or the conditions of a civilization that permitted the whims of Cleopatra to change the fortunes of a nation, traditions, folklore, imagination fill in where there is no written history. 

Strange people, bizarre customs and impossible religion, their fortunes of war, the effect of blended races and traditions-if there were no "seven cities of Cibolo," no palaces mounted with sapphires and turquoises, certainly the blue haze of the Guadalupe, the liquid fire of the desert, and the great trees with long gray hairs (whiskers) were as real as they are today.

The staked plains, the great sweep of the open prairie, the boundless sea of grasslands—these seemed to cast a spell, a delicate fantasy. Wide as the sea, vast plains stretch out layers of velvet green, tossing waves of color that darken into shadows, and cloudlike fields, tinted, lit up in places, yet holding the mass effect of extent, an extent that cuts the skyline in a complete circle.

Overwhelming, too big for detail, most people only sought means for getting across this country quickly. Ox teams were slow. Cow ponies, caravans, the stage coach, the railway, the automobile, and now the airplane; but as we acquire speed, we are inclined to linger or we have more time to meditate. 

Layers of dust and sand cover up things. The history of a world, generations of plants and animals, races of human 1

-some beings before man learned to write or carve in stoneremains of his life, and the life that had gone before, is briefly sketched in rocks and fossils, in deposits of gas and oil, salt and sulphur, gypsum and lignite these are pages of geology that tell of a past if we could read them. 

Palo Duro Canyon and the gravel pits near Amarillo contain the richest deposits of prehistoric life that have not yet been carefully worked by scientists. The State Teachers College of Canyon has just succeeded in getting the work of excavating these ancient strata organized in a big way. With several leading universities and the United States Geological Survey pooling their efforts, we can expect to hear interesting things about this locality. 

An Iliad of adventure, Cabeza de Vaca and his followers were the first white men who arrived in this region. They were shipwrecked, stranded on the sandy shore, and found themselves captive slaves of the fierce Karankawa Indians. 

Most of the men were put to work bearing heavy burdens. 

They worked with the women; but there was no sentiment in those old squaws, who would strike, and even knock a white man down, just for fun.

Lively and vigorous are the tales of these cannibal Indians. 

One man was placed in a stockade and carefully fed. After a time, they brought a woman—a wife?

The setting for a romance. She was a captive from another tribe. They should have escaped and arrived at some happy valley.

With careful feeding, the pair got along well. But the old squaws came into the stockade every day, and they would "feel ribs." The woman was plump, but the old Spaniard had the flesh of an umbrella-a tough skin stretched across bare ribs. 2

One day a group of old squaws came with big dishes and wicked knives. No romance! The woman was prepared for the kettle, and the man was taken out of the stockade. 

Galveston is a glorious stretch of sand beside a ripping, tossing, surging sea. The surf's deep booming note, the scream of the seagull, a melody in the firm, invigorating salt breeze that puts color and romance into jaded nerves, an irresistible call that tingles latent harp-strings of a golden past. Where the world comes to swim, where pink oleanders make billows of colorful clouds that surge along the boulevards and into the quiet streets, where a modern resort with deep-sea fishing, water sports, dancing, roller-coasters and jazz music offers health and recreation-here was the pirate capital, the stronghold of freebooters and privateers, the rendezvous of Jean 

Lafitte and his brother Pierre.

Galveston Island was headquarters, but there are many other places that were baptized by the colorful legends of the heroic freebooters. It is certain that Lafitte held an auction at Baratana and 450 negroes were sold; that Old River and 

Lost River, in the San Jacinto River bottoms, were frequented by the pirates; but the "buried treasure" and the mystery of the "three trees" that have stimulated so many people to dig for the famous "strong box," like the stories of the "beautiful flaxen-haired women" who were held prisoners in the old stone house, probably sprang from some one's fertile imagination. "Remember the Alamo!" Travis, Bonham, Bowie, Davy

Crockett and a little band of Texans held the fort against Santa Anna and the best army of Mexico. This strange contest between 182 men and 4,000 lasted twelve days—until the Texans were out of powder. They went down fighting, one by one. "Thermopylae had her messenger of defeat; the Alamo had none." 3

But the heroes of the Alamo did not lose. Their valor roused a people; Texas became a free nation. Kings sent courtiers and ambassadors to the court of Texas. Then Texas chose to join the Union of free states. San Antonio is an up-to-date city with modern hotels and skyscrapers, but it is the halo of the past that gives it zest and holding power, a shrine, the old missions, old Spanish architecture. 

Brownsville in the Magic Valley has passed through remarkable stages. Asleep for years, when the railroad arrived they had trouble clearing the streets; goats and burros grazed at will; water carts were slow, and the crap games and cock fights would block traffic. But all has changed. Here is the green-wooded landscape of Resaca de la Palma and the region made famous by General Taylor's command, "A little more grape, Captain Bragg!"

Abilene, "the Athens of the Plains," recalls the old days of cattle barons, cowboys, Indians, outlaws, cattle thieves, Mexican bandits and Texas rangers; but it is a city with a clean face and clean feet. It is the home of three colleges, well-organized and endowed institutions, and it has one of the leading daily newspapers of the state. The editor makes a newspaper. Max Bentley of the Abilene Reporter is one of the big newspaper men of the South; for years he was managing editor of the Houston Chronicle, then he was connected with McClure's syndicate, and was a feature writer for Eastern magazines. 

Other great cities are: Houston, the metropolis, inland port, oil capital, and the historic shrine of Sam Houston and San Jacinto; Dallas, where the spindles hum, distributing point, business center of the black land belt; Fort Worth, packing center, the "Chicago of the Southwest"; Beaumont of "Spindletop" fame; El Paso, the jumping-off place—"mines, 4

mercury and Mexicans," irrigation, hills-the highlands and wonderlands of Texas are on the road to El Paso; but many smaller cities are in the picture: Cuero and the "turkey trot"; 

Raymondville, the "onion festival"; Beeville, honey; San Angelo, angora goats; and Corpus Christi, where we go to sleep, and swim, and fish, and watch the shore birds, and wonder if good people, when they die, will go to Corpus Christi. 

Old Nacogdoches and Huntsville, Childress and Quanah, Amarillo and Eagle Pass, landmarks of history and progress, the romantic background of a great state. 

Texas is a new country. The torch that was lighted by the pioneers is yet burning. An epic of democracy, bewildering contrasts, traditions of the past, actualities of the present, the black land belt-fertile black mud laid out in farms with fields and orchards and pastures and dairy cows wading in clover-the plains country, immense fields of wheat and barley, and the combine cutting and threshing the grainbut we may yet see a cowboy, with boots and spurs and a broad hat; he may ride into the sunset, silhouetted in the glowing flames of scarlet and gold, a study in art and colorblending and nature's harmonies that cast a spell over a primitive landscape.

If Robert E. Lee were living today, he could in truth be "listening to the footsteps of the coming millions." Modern cities, irrigation projects, oil fields, mines and factories, more cotton than any other state-the cotton area of Texas is large enough to cover several states. Here is the richest farm, the biggest orchard, the most productive oil well, and helium for the dirigible; one pecan orchard produced a crop that sold for $500,000. One cattle ranch contained a million acres; turkeys are marched to market, and there are acres of them, ten thou-

sand in a drove. The botanist's paradise, Texas has more kinds of plants than any other state. 

Men of vision founded Texas: Sam Houston, Mirabeau B.

Lamar, Stephen F. Austin; they believed in education and freedom. Today's dream becomes tomorrow's reality. The capitol building at Austin is the most impressive state capitol in the United States; it is built of Texas granite, and it cost 

3,000,000 acres of land. Houston dug a canal fifty miles long to bring ocean-going vessels to an inland port. Much dredging was required, but the ship channel, the turning basin and the wharf facilities are ample for the largest vessels. A "harbor of romance," a monument to enterprise. 

The length and breadth and might of the Lone Star State can be seen to the best advantage when traveling by air; the distances are great enough and the panoramas of plains and cities and tank farms and irrigation projects, pastures, grain fields and cotton patches are impressive. But it is worth while to linger by the surging fields of bluebonnets, hear El Troubadour, the Texas mockingbird, in his morning symphony, and catch the warm-hearted welcome of Southern playgrounds that have not yet been spoiled by too much tourist trade. 

There is a newness, a grandeur of great open vistas, liberty, life in the open, a native beauty, across vivid blues, reds and greens. It is something of a thrill of youth-gay, carefree, light-hearted.

When Cabeza de Vaca was shipwrecked on the coast of Texas, 1528, he found little time for explorations and mapping of land forms; he must be credited with originality, resourcefulness and unusual valor, for he survived to tell his story. His reports, combined with those of Coronado, 1540, gave Spain a claim to the territory, and she ruled it for three centuries.

Sense of ownership, the possibilities of a "Spanish empire in the west" for demagogues to rant about, and some appeal to the romantic and missionary spirit that somehow persisted in spite of a series of wars that demanded the young men and kept the country in debt, caused each successive ruler to make some plans concerning the "vast, empty empire" of the American continent. Little was accomplished. Contact with white men gave the native Indians some pointers on methods of warfare-it improved their technique in killing off one another-but when Cavalier de la Salle, a French navigator, happened into Matagorda Bay (1684) and laid claim to Texas in the name of France, all Spain was aroused. The colony and fort that La Salle set up was soon abandoned, but Spain began to establish missions. 

The mission at Nacogdoches was established in 1690 and the famous Alamo mission in 1718. That missions should be successful in this barren country, and with only the savage, shiftless and unreliable tribes of Indians as the object, must always stand out as an achievement worthy of admiration.

Nacogdoches became a center that soon attracted white settlers, and the Alamo drew large numbers of refugees of various kinds from the different provinces of Mexico, and some of the adventurers and "hunted men" of the United States. Other missions were established, and in spite of wild people, who were continually at war, the old Spanish missions became the landmarks of a century of progress known in history as the "mission period."

Foreigners were excluded from this territory until 1818, but the grant of Moses Austin for colonizing was obtained in 1820. After the death of Moses Austin, his son, Stephen F. 

Austin, took up the work, and he became the greatest of all the colonizers of Texas. Spain was having trouble with her

colonies, and the Mexican revolution which established the republic of Mexico in 1824 caused much friction with these isolated and loosely organized colonies. 

It was a period of pioneering, when the chief interest of the people of Europe and the United States centered around the settlement and the development of new lands, new countries.

While the colonies of Texas attracted all kinds of people, there were a few of the highest type, of Celtic or Anglo-Saxon origin, who recalled a long line of liberty-loving ancestors; they were resourceful; they made their wants known; they could not be badly governed by petty tyrants and little politicians.

A convention was held at Washington-on-the-Brazos on March 2, 1836, and they set up the independent republic of Texas. 

Four days later, "the Alamo!" Like a gigantic spark, the 

Alamo thrilled the civilized world. Colonel William B. Travis, with 183 Texans, withstood a siege and held the Alamo against the combined armies of Mexico. The fort was stormed. The 

Texans went down fighting, to the last man, but they never surrendered. 

Then came the "run-away scrape." The Mexican army, divided into sections, overran the country like it was a conquered province. Pillaging, burning cities, killing men and women, irresponsible groups of soldiers terrorized the country. 

People left their homes. It was a life-or-death struggle to get out of Texas. Old and young, women and children, with some ox carts and decrepit horses, but in many instances afoot and carrying burdens, they crowded the trails in the general direction of Nacogdoches. Heavy rains, streams out of banks, the

sick and the weak were abandoned, the dead were quickly buried; but the march continued. 

Wild rumors! The menace of Mexican soldiers! With the best horses, all of the men who were able to fight hastened to join the little army that Sam Houston was getting organized. 

And here were more disappointments. Trained soldiers were few, and there was no time for training. Every day brought reports of towns burned, of the new president of Texas fleeing to Galveston, and, more disheartening than all, that Sam 

Houston's army had retreated before a superior Mexican force until, "bottled in the bend of the bayou," they would be annihilated-"another Goliad." 

San Jacinto, April 21, 1836.

The war was over; Texas remained a republic for ten years; then, through the election of James K. Polk, Texas was admitted as a state. 

Texas has been under six flags: Spain, France, Mexico, the republic of Texas, the Confederate States, and the United States.

When the first election was held, Sam Houston became president of the republic of Texas. Problems of organization, Indian wars, cattle thieves, bandits—some of them raids by Mexican bandit chiefs-drouth, depression, tropical storms, mosquitoes and malaria; but the virile, thrifty, industrious and resourceful pioneers met conditions. 

The transformation of Texas represents four epochs or eras of development. The first includes cattle raising on the range, the supremacy of the "longhorn," "buffalo days," wild game hunting and the use of resources found on the surface; the second includes real farming, plantations, the development of the black land belt, corn fields, rice, the biggest cotton fields in the world; the third represents an attempt to make some

use of the mineral resources, "black gold," oil, gas, sulphur, lignite, quicksilver; and the fourth marks the diversity of pursuit, scientific farming, standard bred cattle, irrigation projects, citrus fruits, truck crops, manufacturing and city building.

Texas is undeniably big. In many ways it must seem unwieldy. The chamber of commerce idea has been worked out in a big way, and the results seem to justify the most extravagant claims. Virile business organizations like the East Texas 

Chamber of Commerce, the West Texas Chamber of Commerce and the South Texas Chamber of Commerce serve to get business leaders together and map out methods for solving the problems that are so perplexing when they concern isolated communities where there is a lack of sympathetic understanding. 

As an example, the South Texas Chamber of Commerce, under the leadership of F. W. Kirk, president, and Ray Leeman, executive secretary, took up the state college at Kingsville as a pet project. Mr. Kirk, the directors and the local committees gave much personal attention to the problems of the college. Mr. Leeman kept up an extensive correspondence, business leaders from other parts of the state were interested, and Kingsville State College became a pet project of the Regional Chamber of Commerce. As a result, the scope of the college was broadened, some changes were made, and Texas College of Arts and Industries became an all-state enterprise with the support of progressive men from distant parts of the state. Dr. J. O. Loftin is the new president. 10

The Buffalo Herd 

"The majestic and picturesque pageant of migrating buffaloes." 

O SINGLE movement of wild life in America can compare to a herd of migrating buffaloes. Massive N° and majestic, it swept the plains with a forceful and poetic allusion that persists in history. 

Slowly the two covered wagons with emigrants-men, women and children-jogged along the uncertain trail. Buffalo! A stream of brown was pouring through the defile between the hills. The buffaloes were coming through Buffalo Gap.

Pouring in a steady stream down the hill, bending towards a few weather-beaten cottonwoods that marked a narrow water-hole, the mass of yellowish brown suggested a strange kind of liquid rather than large, shaggy animals. The brown. stream extended more than two miles, but there was a distinctive breaking up and bunching together at the water-hole. 

The covered wagons continued to move forward until the horses became nervous. Massive, shaggy buffaloes crowded together, an unbroken layer of humps, a swinging and writhing series of immense brown bodies crowding one another forward, a steady flow forty feet wide and extending two miles to the cottonwoods, the buffaloes were using the pass-the people must wait.

A steady roll of heavy feet; the earth seems to tremble; a cloud of dust moves with the wind like heavy white smoke.

A strong smell of animal perspiration seems to roll back from the hill against the wind. Sometimes it is intense enough to provoke a cough, but usually it is but an exaggeration of the herd odor when cattle are driven along the highway. A com- 11

plete mass movement, it is a wonderful pageant of wild animal life.

No wonder that the horses pointed their ears forward and stamped their feet with uneasiness. There is an element of majesty in the irresistible forward surge. There is strength in numbers. Aside from military precision, this immense herd, this quivering landscape of living meat, assumed a stately grandeur, a nobility of oneness of purpose, that is akin to the hero-stuff of martyrs and reformers.

The old dog stood at attention as though he were pointing a bevy of prairie chickens, but the younger dogs kept a little back, looking and nervously shifting about. Then one of the hounds became bolder, and he began to investigate. First, he approached close enough to draw the attention of a large bull, who immediately stepped out of the ranks. 

With an air of annoyed arrogance, he bellowed his challenge. Lowering his head and snorting his defiance, he advanced directly towards the dog. But instead of giving ground, the dog began to bay. Loud and intensively, the dog barked, as was his custom when he called the other dogs and the men to the game that he had brought to bay. Old Bison hesitated, stopped, snorted, stamped his feet, switched his tail, and then he uttered a thundering roar that added trembles to the roar of hoofs.

Like a call to arms, this bellow caused many heads to raise above the mass of moving buffaloes, and no less than fifty strong bulls came out of the ranks. It made no difference with the stream of brown; others crowded into the vacant places, and the stream flowed on. 

Heads down, snorting and bellowing in short grunts, the great beasts advanced in a semi-circle. They advanced fast, those at the outer edges almost running and sidling as they 12

kept their heads towards the dog. The dog was almost surrounded before he seemed to notice their tactics, and he would have been closed in the circle had not the other four dogs grouped themselves with much barking and demonstration just at the little open space in the ring. 

Making the best of the opportunity, the dog turned and dashed through the opening. But he was pursued by a charging bull, a puffing engine of fury, that was almost on his heels before he got himself strung out to running in real hound fashion. For fifty yards the dog ran in a straight line and with only a narrow margin between him and certain death; then he turned to one side, and the great beast lost too much time in making the turn.

It was a tense moment for the people in the wagons. When the dog dashed out of the closing circle of furious brutes with a massive bull charging after him, his route was almost towards the wagons. In an instant, the circle dissolved into a wedge formation. Fifty heads were raised; there were snorts and bellows. Would they charge the wagons? The horses were frightened. One man had hold of a bridle, almost wrestling with a panicky horse. A man who happened to be holding a gun fired blank into the herd. 

But the great, shaggy bulls held their ground, snorting and bellowing; they kept their wedge formation until the old charger gave up the chase and returned, reluctantly and most angrily, his mouth open, slobbering and bellowing, and his flanks heaving with exertion and excitement. They watched him drop into the stream of flowing baffaloes, they slowly turned their heads, and the wedge began to dissolve. 

How one of these immense, unwieldy animals could inject himself into the crowded, compact stream of moving buffaloes cannot be explained, but the wedge dissolved and the stream 13

flowed on without a ripple; the brown stream of shaggy humps kept pouring through the gap and down the hill. The incident of the dog and the firing of a gun had no effect upon the stream of life.

The people waited an hour. It was 11 o'clock, almost dinner time. Just as well unhitch the horses; they can browse around a little, even if the grass is not so good, and everybody can eat and rest while they wait. While a stream of brown animals extended across the plain and out of sight, little groups and thin lines were spreading over the broad plain just beyond the water-hole.

Dinner over, the monotonous pouring along of great brown bodies being conducive to sleep, members of the party indulge in a nap. Midafternoon, and no change in the stream of buffaloes pouring through the gap. An immense herd has collected around the water-hole, and the broad plains are well dotted with groups and stragglers. Two thin streams lead into the distance, but numbers are dropping out of the line and scattering over the plains.

There is no cessation in the crowding, pouring stream of shaggy brown. The sun goes down, but the stillness of night seems only to emphasize the roll of tramping hoofs and the subdued mutterings of nervous animals crowding one another through the defile. Beyond the water-hole, the plain is a thick patch of black; the spaces have been filled in; the skyline is black-miles and miles of buffaloes. 

Near midnight, when the music of the howling coyotes has subsided into spasmodic barks and wails, there is a flutter in the rolling thunder that has kept the camp ground a-tremble. 

There are spaces between little groups that straggle through the gap.

The emigrants lost a day, but they witnessed a pageant 14

Raising buffaloes on game preserves and private pastures has been successful during the last few years. Canada has made the best progress; there are fine herds at Moose Jaw and at the wild animal paddock near Banff. Texas Bluebonnets 

HE EARTH is blued to match the sky. In pluming majesty, the blue torches spread a curtain of blue across the prairie. A sea of bluebonnets, an endless plain of bluebonnets, blue that predominates, forms the ground color, contrasting with the sky, blending with green and making turquoise hills, cobalt lakes.

We are guests of an earthly paradise. Jade blue, the blue of hope, of peace, of piety, of sincerity, of love of good works, like themes of ecstasy, it overflows in raptures of superlative delight. Passions of blossom, splendor of spread, they overflow into oceans of turquoise. They are irresistible; waves of bluebonnets flood our senses. Communion with bluebonnets is a sacrament; it purifies; it is good for the soul. In a field of bluebonnets, all of our sins are forgiven.

Blue fringes crowd the roadway. Demure, but buoyant and tossing, bluebonets lift above the green grass. While the bluebonnet is a descendant of a very old garden. flower, it has attained a remarkable development in a restricted area where the soil and climate just suit its peculiar demands. 

It flourishes best in the "bluebonnet bias," a belt of limestone outcroppings extending from Texarkana to El Paso. This "bias" is by no means regular, but the curves and breaks are not very abrupt; it is from 50 to 200 miles wide, and there are isolated acreas of bluebonnets, "lakes of bluebonnets," that are entirely out of the belt. Singularly, the great belt of blue- 21

bonnets does not extend into Mexico nor even into Arkansas. 

Brought here from the Mediterranean countries, possibly Europe or Africa, in shipments of grain and clover, it quickly escaped from the fields and took possession of the prairie lands of Texas. The old mission priests called it buffalo clover because the buffaloes fed upon it. For some reason, the Tejas 

Indians associated the great patches of bluebonnets with the spring migration of buffaloes; they were satisfied with the explanation that the immense herds simply came to eat the blue clover.

The bluebonnet might be classed as a clover; it is a legume and it belongs in the division of plants with the clovers. But it is usually mentioned as a lupine, since its botanical name is lupinus. It is quite probable that the bluebonnet can be traced to the "bonnet flower" of the Old World, the scabiosa of the old-fashioned gardens.

The bluebonnet improves the soil. Like the other forms of clover, it thrives on the poorest soil. It thrives where the prairie grass is sparce, where the only foothold is red, hard clay; it covers the bare spaces on the hillside with its mantle of beauty, and it enriches the soil so that other crops can grow. After a few years of bluebonnet pasture, the ground can be plowed with the full assurance that it will be rich enough to produce cotton or corn or whatever crop the climate of the locality might justify. It sends its roots deep down and fixes nitrogen in the soil. 

Like all other leguminous plants, these bluebonnets (lupines) carry little nodules or tubercles on their roots which fix or hold nitrogen taken from the air. These nodules are due to a kind of bacteria which begins to grow like a parasite, and it seems that the roots, in growing around them, or healing a scar, build up the material of the nodule. Since this nitrogen 22

is built in while this process is going on, "nitrogen-fixing bacteria" is an appropriate term. 

Since this process of building up the soil was not understood by the early settlers, and bluebonnet land was usually poor soil, they jumped to the conclusion that these lupines impoverished the soil. It required an extensive campaign of education and demonstration by scientific farmers to convince the skeptical natives that bluebonnets actually improved the soil instead of rendering it barren. 

A peculiar whim of the bluebonnets is their preference for the barren places, the red hillsides and the hardpan prairie lands. For a few years, they multiply rapidly, wax abundant and irresistible; then they fade out and give other flowers a chance. This is especially noticeable with Indian paint; blankets of Indian paint crowd into old fields of bluebonnets. 

Bluebonnet fields are sometimes taken by purple verbenas or creeping charleys. After a term of years, these whimsical blue fields may come again, taller and bluer than ever. 

There is a marked difference in the shade of the blue, in the amount and intensity of the red and the relative amount of the white and gray of the bluebonnets of a new field that is coming to its best and an old field that is waning. The deepest, darkest blue does not arrive for three or four years after the field has started, and it will reappear in its fullest richness only three or four years; then it will not be so blue; in a few more years, it will be a pale sky blue. Examined closely, each flower will have less deep blue, the red on the tips of the bonnets will seem more intense, and the white and gray will be much more prominent. 

There are yet a few people who insist that bluebonnet fields appear the same year after year; they do not recognize the rhythmic cycle which requires ten or twelve years to 23

complete. To such we can only say, study your bluebonnet fields carefully, make notes on the intensity of the blue, and compare these notes from year to year. 

While these blue flowers can be introduced into the Houston area, and they will grow well for a time, they tend to fade out in a few years, and that before they attain the richest cobalt-blue stage. They are easily raised from the seed, but it is hardly worth while to transplant them during the flowering season. 

Like peas or beans, the seeds of bluebonnets ripen within the pods. These peas are very rich in protein and they might find a place in human diet but for the fact that they have a bitter taste. They have been fed to animals, especially cows and hogs, with marked success; and "bluebonnet clover hay," which is cut about the time that the pods are beginning to form, is a good type of provender. 

After a dry winter, they will not do so well; only a few of them will appear, and they will be short, stunted and very pale blue; in some instances, there will be no traces of red. 

They appear in about the same condition when the soil is too rich. Like the good everywhere, they grow and expand best where they are needed most. They are doing a great service for the poor soils of Texas by storing nitrogen and making it available for other plants to use. For the benefit of the soil, they should not be harvested, and for the refreshing uplift, the esthetic appeal and the unbounded invitation of the great outdoors, they should be preserved and protected in their native environment.

Legends of Bluebonnets 

HE BLUEBONNET comes down through the ages with an aura of tradition and glory. This wealth of story and romance only proves that the roving Indians and the first white settlers were greatly stirred by the wonderful pageant of blue. Since they could not understand it, they wove it into their superstitions and religious beliefs. They did not, they could not, treat the bluebonnets lightly.

According to tradition, some Spanish priests made a pious pilgrimage to the Holy Land and from a hillside near the city of Jerusalem they plucked with their own hands some seedpods of the "blue blanket," and these were finally planted, with much ceremony, inside the walls of a Mexican mission. 

But the flowers flourished and spread and became wild, and, like the gospel, they spread to the four winds, and blankets of blue were spread all over the land. 

A great battle was being fought. The noise and tumult of men and horses, the shrieks and cries of rage and triumph, the wails and groans of the wounded and the dying became so terrifying that men went wild; yet reinforcements arrived whenever one side began to waver; victory was undecided; more warriors came, and it seemed that all the fighting men would be killed. The fury of battle became so intense that wounded warriors fought harder; mortally wounded, they would continue to fight. As they continued to fight in the happy hunting grounds, they knocked chunks of the sky from the vaulted dome of blue, and these fell to earth in shattered bits, took root and grew as bluebonnets.

Several of the early accounts refer to these blue flowers as "blue blankets." Probably this is due to the romantic tales of "Old Chief Blue Blanket" and his two sons, "Big Chief 26

Chief became leader of his tribe, he led a party of warriors in a raid among distant tribes. They brought back much plunder, tents, blankets and weapons, some ponies and dogs, and several squaws that were heavy and slow and dark and sullen, but one of these squaws was slim and quick and light and lively.

Old Chief was already supplied with squaws-probably several of them-but the "Blue Song Bird" took his fancy. She was light as the wind and she could laugh to music. She would sing soft bird songs that would make Old Chief sleepy, and even when she beat the cymbals or the deerskin drum, her stroke was so light that it sounded like a bird calling.

Since she sang so well and beat the drum so lightly, it was not necessary for her to work like the other squaws. Old Chief ordered them to wait on her, and he made dire threats, casting down his ax and spear to portend death to all if anything happened to his favorite. When Old Chief was in camp, she was at his side, crooning or singing or chattering. She did not even wait on him, but she would order his pipe to be brought or his blanket to be placed. And the squaws who were heavy and slow and dark and sullen always obeyed her orders.

When Old Chief was away from camp, Blue Song Bird would busy herself making headdresses, and the ones that she made were more brilliant than any that the tribe had ever known. But she did not make red ones. Red was a favorite color of the tribe, but no difference-she made blue headdresses, and Old Chief wore blue. Red was the color of blood, of war, but blue was the sky, the bluebird, a song. Blue became the emblem of the tribe, and under the blue the tribe prospered. 27

Old Chief and Blue Song Bird lived to be very old. They did not make war, and other tribes did not make war on them. Since they thought much of the blue sky, the songbird and crooning songs, their hearts became light; they did not think of war; they wanted everybody to live and be happy. 

The Great Spirit was pleased with his people. He sent blessings of rain and sunshine, fruits and nuts, fat birds and furcovered buffaloes; and when his people responded so kindly, even the Great Spirit showed his pleasure by spreading the emblem of his favorite tribe over the hills and prairies-the bluebonnets.

The son was even greater than the father; Big Chief Blue 

Blanket was wise and peaceful; the people worked and played together; they did not order one another around; and they would all join in singing the "bluebird songs" and the praises of Big Chief Blue Blanket. He lived long, and his departure was grieved; but the Great Spirit made the bluebonnets bluer than ever.

It was a sad day for the tribe when "Little Blue Blanket" became chief. He was selfish and cruel, irresponsible and despotic. One of his whims was a hatred for good-looking young women; his squaws had to be wrinkled and ugly and strong to do much work; and he did like to smash the heads of young squaws. It was necessary to keep the young women out of his sight. His people were very tolerant, and they did not kill him until he had committed so many outrageous crimes that he was known as "Bad Medicine Blue Blanket" to the neighboring tribes. Then they took him out and laid his head on a flat stone. Even after "Bad Medicine" had disgraced the tribe, the Great Spirit permitted the blue blankets to spread over the prairies.

HE MOCKINGBIRD is irrestible. We are strangely thrilled by the almost human mimicry and the surprising scope of this wonderful impersonator. The multitudinous melody throbs with entrancing harmony, the soft music of contentment blends with the inspiring strains of devotion, and life's burdens are lifted. The old garments of care and regret are laid aside and we are conscious of the bright sun, the cool air and the cheerful outlook.

When the magic touch of spring's fairy fingers has transformed the forests and woodlands and the fantastic whorls of colored foam roll into clouds among the green, the full and almost continuous symphony of the matchless mockingbird swells into a wild extravaganza that requires some interpretation. Quality of tone, timbre, the extent of overtones and the faultless mimicry of some other bird's song all tend to intrigue the fancy so that cold science must see the bird with a halo, part real and part imaginary. 

Like the elusive charm that lurks about the jade-green pools and the pictured silver-bells, in full bloom, set in a crowded swampland, the music of the mockingbird has a charm of setting. Most people are satisfied to accept the gifted singer as a part of the environment and to enjoy the music as a blessing of peace and joy and cheerfulness. When locked up 33

in a cage or sometimes when trying to imitate, at random, a song that has been carried in the memory for some time, there is some variation from the the original. It would be too much to expect that the heart-throbs of a voory or a yellow-throat could be reproduced by a mockingbird. When the imitator has attained a mechanical perfection that leaves no doubt as to what bird is being imitated, that seems to be highly complimentary. 

Four types of bird songs have been recognized by bird students who have carefully studied their particular branch of ornithology. First, there is the simple, short song, repeated several times at short intervals, the typical sparrow and warbler song. Second, the alternated song in which two or more short songs are given at short intervals and in something like regular alternation. 

For this second type, the towhee, the redstart and the meadowlark have been mentioned as examples, but none of these birds use this type exclusively. In some instances, three or more songs are given in alternation. 

The third type consists of a long-continued song composed of short phrases following one another at short intervals. This is the song of the robin, the catbird, the vireo and the pewee. 

Some of the later writers, including Dr. A. A. Saunders, have gone so far as to include the mockingbird in this list. There can be no objection-the mockingbird belongs here-but it might be placed in each of the other classes. 

The fourth type is the most extensive. The long-continued singing consists of comparatively long phrases, with correspondingly long pauses alternating with them. Probably the hermit thrush is the best example of this type. But—did you ever listen to hermit thrush when he trilled his long selection, took a long rest and then went through the same series of 34

THE MOCKINGBIRD 

tones again without a variation, and after his long rest repeated his song, this over and over again, until you went to look for the musician-and a mockingbird flew out of the thicket? 

Why will a mockingbird sing the song of some other bird over and over again? Is he "learning the song?" It would hardly seem so for the technique is perfect. With several other people, the writer listened to a complete robin song repeated several times by a mockingbird, and that without any other notes. It just seemed that the mocker was making a careful trial at playing the part of a robin. 

The flight song, the hovering song and the "dropping song," the songs that are given by the versatile songster while in the air, either while flying, hovering above a tree as though ready to alight, or that ecstatic night-song which is so frequently given at night when the bird is in the act of dropping or falling to the ground-all of these seem to be the result of a certain musical excitement in which the singer must use the entire body in the expression of the song. A musician that has completely surrendered to the melody, at the supreme musical height, this falling in song must represent a stage of singing in which the world is forgotten. "Crazy with song," the bird will sometimes continue to sing from the ground after the descent. On moonlight nights, at the edge of the woods, and especially in the open country along the bay, Seabrook and Kemah, the flight songs of the mockingbird become an enchanting mystery. In most instances, it is not possible to see the actions of the bird, but the spirit of the melody attains the sublime.

As though dreams take wings, the wonders rise and fall with the heart-throbs of the bird-song. Like the flame of blossoming trees, the comfort-breathing of wild flowers- 35

violets, cone flowers, yellow daisies-these songs of youth and love, of adoration and ecstasy, cast a spell of rapture. From a spirit brimming with human sympathy, this throbbing melody of perfect peace comes through the night as a dream, a prayer, a wayside gladness. In the enchantment of silvery moonlight and the stillness of a summer night, the ring of these merry bells and the flutes that play in the dark float down like angels calling through the star-mist from the fields of paradise. 

The charge that the mockingbird is sometimes mechanical cannot stand against the night song. This rapturous voice comes to sooth insomnia, to drive away abiding cares that restrain sleep, to allay misery, and to lead the way to that happy sea of dreamland. Then it is the matchless troubadour throws its soul upon the night; it is the music of earth, the music of spheres. The song is for the unfortunate, for the wearied, the infirm, the aged, for youth, for freemen, for the beauty of earth, for the blessings of health, for loves that have brightened the way, and for the loves that are waiting in the distance.

According to some bird students who have made special investigations, the singing vocabulary of the mockingbird is enormous. More than five hundred songs and chirps and calls are uttered by this marvelous vocalist. A bird that can imitate a crow, a bluejay or a warbler with equal facility has attained a high place as an imitator; but the mocker can do much better than this; it can imitate blackbirds and yellowthroats, ducks and snipes, woodpeckers and orioles.

The wooing of the mockingbird accounts for much excitement among the early spring birds. Clattering through the tree-tops or over the roof of a building and singing in the loudest tones is a public announcement. When the mockingbird goes a-courting, he "tells the world." Most people are familiar with some of the capers and ceremonies of a mockingbird courtship, but the complete description cannot be given.

The most familiar dance begins with both birds facing each other with wings drooping and heads and tails erect.

After swinging their heads as though they would strike one another with their beaks, they begin stepping together and, after a few steps, they begin hopping together sideways, only making a few inches at a time, but always keeping the same distance apart. When this chase has taken them five or six feet, they wheel and retrace the same route. Other birds know better than to interfere, but bluejays, English sparrows and several other kinds of birds will join the spectators in scolding and helping to keep up the excitement.

In the spiral "mounting" and "dropping" songs, the male begins near the ground and flits from branch to branch in something of a spiral around the tree, singing as he goes, until he arrives at the topmost branch, where he will deliver a short but penetrating solo. Then he will begin ascending from branch to branch, but now with a mellow, soft and gurgling series of blending notes. This climbing and falling series ends in the "love-call" of the mockingbird which has been described so frequently by poets and nature writers. The mounting song is given by the male, the female usually remaining silent on a nearby tree. .

The father mockingbird is an exemplary husband and head of the family; he is vigilant in protecting his home from intruders, and he is industrious in helping to care for the nestlings. He works untiringly in gathering food, not only for the young, but also for his mate. He is so busy during the nesting season that he seldom finds time to sing in the daytime, but any time during the night, and especially on bright moonlight nights, he will pour out sonnets and sonatas pulsing carols and quivering trills. He is the moonlight symphony of the Southern woods. Sometimes the female will answer with a one-syllabled "twit" or "peet," just to let him know that she hears and approves his song, but she seldom sings from her nest.

Sometimes during the day when she leaves the nest, she will indulge in a medley of sketchy songs. But there is an element of haste and nervousness about her song that suggests her cares. The song of a brooding mockingbird is quite unlike that of the male; as Dr. Lestrero states it, "the song is burdened with care."

Among the very first birds to begin nest-building, the mockingbirds work fast, complete the nest in two or three days; then they may indulge in a noisy revel of music for a few days before the first egg is placed in the nest. The work of building the nest deserves special attention; it is a partnership affair from the time the first stick is lodged. Each stick seem to have been selected to fit, for very little time is required to put it in place. The sticks are laid down in layers, one end being secured below two or tree other sticks, and the other end raised over one and under another. This is an approach to some of the most primitive forms of basket-weav- 39

ing. When the framework or outer walls are in place, an inner wall of lighter materials is worked in with surprising regularity. In sharp contrast to the outer wall of coarser twigs, mesquite, prickly ash or crooked brush, the inner wall consists of Spanish moss, strips of bark, coarse feathers and even strings or bits of cloth. The lining is of finer materials, soft feathers, horse hair, thistledown or tufts of cotton, pressed down and smoothed to a surprising regularity. 

When the first egg is in the nest, father mockingbird begins a regular patrol of the surroundings. No policeman ever walked a beat more faithfully than this vigilant master of the preserve, circling high and low, paying special attention to open spaces, leaning trees and especially such places from which a cat might approach without being noticed. But a cat cannot get at the nest if this guard sees him first. Like a flash, the bird will strike from above with his sharp beak and utter a sharp scold. His mate from the nest is at hand almost like an echo of the scold; then there is an unequal fight, two against one, and the two continue to strike from different directions and to remain out of reach; and if the cat wants to get away with both of his eyes, he must make a quick dash to the ground. No cat is a match for a pair of mockingbirds when it is in a tree; and even when it gets to the ground, they will soon drive it to the shelter of brush or a fence. 

The pair will kill a snake that attempts to rob their nest.

Let us consider the salient facts of this epoch-marking battle which gave the English-speaking white race dominion over a million square miles of territory including the gold mines and orange groves of California, and the much-heralded oil wells and yellow-pine forests of Texas.

When the Texans rebelled against the government of Mexico, the population of Texas was about 30,000, but since this number includes Mexicans and negro slaves, as well as negro freedmen, it is probable that the number of white settlers from the United States was less than 10,000, while the population of Mexico was about 7,000,000. 

On March 2, 1836, Sam Houston's forty-third birthday, the Declaration of Texas Independence was adopted by a convention that was in session at Washington, a town on the Brazos River about eighty miles northwest of Harrisburg; and two days later, March 4, Sam Houston was appointed major general and commander-in-chief of the Texas army. At that time the Texas army numbered about 800 men: 182 under 

Colonel Travis at the Alamo, 450 under Colonel Fannin at Goliad, while the remainder were at Gonzales awaiting the direction of the commanding general. They were without 47

uniforms or "uniform guns"; it was a motley looking army, but they were hardy frontiersmen, ready to meet any emer gency. 

On the same day that Sam Houston was appointed commander-in-chief of the Texas army, Colonel Travis' little band of 183 officers and men in the Alamo prepared to meet the final assault. Is was on this day that Colonel Travis called his men together and informed them that the besieging army had been reinforced, probably to 5,000; that there could be no relief, and that it was only a question of a few hours when the Alamo would be carried by assault. After asking his men to forgive him in case they felt that he had done wrong in holding out the hopes of relief even after it seemed evident that it would be impossible for his comrades in arms to get through the ever-increasing lines of the enemy, Colonel Travis took his sword and scratched a line on the ground. "All who wish to leave, stand in their places. Every man who will die with me, come across the line! Who will be first?" As he spoke the words, every man but two jumped across the line. 

James Bowie, the famous inventor of the "bowie knife," was too ill to walk. "Boys," said he, "move my cot over the line."

The other man, alone and uncheered, climbed the wall and departed.

Two days later March 6, the Alamo was stormed, and the brave defenders, by their valiant sacrifice, sounded a chord of chivalry which vibrated around the world. The heroic death of these intrepid patriots rendered more service to the cause of Texas Independence than could have been accomplished by an army. The war-cry became, "Remember the Alamo."

The sending of this woman to Sam Houston was regarded as a brilliant stroke of diplomacy by Santa Anna, who thought that the offer of "peace and general amnesty to those who would lay down their arms and submit to the government" could not be rejected under such circumstances. But Sam Houston was a man of courage.

With a copy of the Declaration of Independence of Texas, he sent the reply: "The Texans are not conquered." With about three hundred men, he left Gonzales on March 13, crossed the Colorado River and began the disheartening retreat to the Brazos. Couriers were sent through the country to tell of the fall of the Alamo, to warn the people to leave with all speed, and to send all possible reinforcements to join General Houston's little band. Thoroughly aroused, the people turned their hogs and cattle out, took a few valuables and fled the country; there were not enough carts and wagons for the aged and the sick; women and children walked; if any one became weak or was unable to keep up with her party, she would simply lay down beside the road and hope to fall in with another party. Those who died of exertion and exposure were quickly buried at the next camping ground; the horrors of the "run-away scrape" form one of the darkest pages of early history.

General Houston marched from San Felipe up the Brazos to Groce's plantation, a point about fifty miles north of where the city of Houston now stands; there he crossed the river and began his march to Harrisburg. Flushed with success, the 49

Santa Anna, with the main division of the army, was supposed to be going north of Harrisburg, but hoping to capture the president and other state officials of the newly formed government, he had changed his plans and marched on Harrisburg.

General Houston encountered many difficulties in getting an army together. Men would come and go at will; supplies were obtained with difficulty, and there was much dissatisfaction because the engagement was deferred. By the time he reached Harrisburg, the outlook was a little more encouraging; besides some recruits from the eastern settlements, two small cannons, known as the "Twin Sisters," were received, and with them some experienced artillerymen. These cannons were gifts from the people of Cincinnati, Ohio, to the new republic, and as there was no war between the United States and Mexico at that time, the guns were billed as "two pieces of hollow-ware and contents." While this spot, which was at that time covered with moss-draped trees, has always been referred to as "the site of Sam Houston's camp," the inference is rather inclusive, for there was not a single tent in his army; there was very little camp equipment of any kind, and the horses were simply turned out to graze. 

With an army of only 783 men, General Houston seems to have chosen a very good position to withstand an attack of a large force, since his men could be deployed along in the edge of the woods while the attacking army would be obliged to advance from the open prairie. Santa Anna had with him about 1,300 men, and a few hours before the battle, he was joined by General Cos with 500 men, and these were the best troops of the Mexican army. While the Mexican army had never been defeated, there is every reason to believe that Santa 50

According to an account given by a member of his staff, Santa Anna became excited when one of his officers informed him that Houston's army was close at hand. At the time this report was brought in, the general and his staff were at New Washington, but the main body of the army was strung out in a narrow lane that extended several miles below the town. 

This lane was through dense pine woods, and it was so narrow that mounted men had to go in double file, while pack-mules had to go in single file. After hearing the report, Santa Anna did not wait to give orders, but mounted his horse and started for this lane, and, whether angered by the slow progress of his troops in getting out of the woods, or in actual alarm because of the proximity of the Texan army, he displayed much excitement. At any rate, his troops were glad indeed to reach the open prairie.

Knowing what the decision would be, he sent Deaf Smith to cut the bridge across Vince's Bayou. 

Both armies had come to this place over that bridge, and when it was destroyed, there was no way for the Texan army to escape in case of defeat. In other words, this was to be another 

Alamo; these undaunted Texans, while facing an army that outnumbered them two to one, decided that they would defeat that army or die on the field. 

A little after four o'clock in the afternoon, the motleydressed, variously armed and individualistically drilled band that made up the Texan army advanced to the attack. With their only drum and fife playing the tune, "Will You Come to My Bower?" and their line only one deep, they advanced on the run.

With all of their variations in uniform and equipment, and ideas of military science, they were alike in one 52

Yelling their defiance, they advanced across the open to attack an army that outnumbered them two to one and that was posted behind a complete line of breast works. Heroes, these; worthy to have their memory perpetuated by the flower-service of a grateful people. The impetuous rush was not checked by the artillery fire and the volleys of musketry from the Mexican lines. With cries of "Remember the Alamo!" and "Remember Goliad!" they climbed over the breast works. 

Santa Anna had decided that he would not make an attack that day, and it seems that he did not seriously consider the probability of the small Texan army attacking his fortified camp. The nervousness that was occasioned by the unexpected news of the presence of Sam Houston's army had been dispelled by the arrival of General Cos with 500 men. These reinforcements were received with the roll of drums and most hearty shouts. But it seems that these men had been rushed by force marches and had not slept the night before; accordingly 

Santa Anna ordered them to stack their arms, remove their accoutrements (the Mexican soldiers were fairly loaded down with trappings of questionable utility) and to quietly go to sleep in a nearby grove.

At the time the bugler signaled that the Texan army was advancing, Santa Anna and his staff were asleep in their tents, and the army, with the exception of a strong guard, was at ease. Many of the men were asleep; others were eating; some were repairing their clothing; while some were scattered through the woods in search of boughs to make them shelter.

Almost as soon as the infantry had clamored over the breastworks, the Texan cavalry, who were accustomed to rough riding, began to get their horses over by rolling the top pieces down on either side so that the light Texas ponies could climb and jump across.

The two cannons, "the Twin Sisters," were advanced from either side so as to pour in a crossfire of grapeshot, while the Mexican cannons were captured in their camp defense positions before they had time to fire more than two or three rounds. Meeting little resistence, the Texans dashed, lightening-like, through the camp, and the little bands of Mexicans who offered resistence were soon convinced that they must fly for their lives. Many of them threw up their hands with the cry, "Me no Alamo" or "Me no Goliad," but in the first flush of victory, it was difficult for General Houston and his officers to restrain their men from killing Mexicans when it was unnecessary. They threw away their hats and coats and some of them kicked off their shoes that they might run the faster.

Some of them escaped by swimming across Buffalo Bayou, and a few succeeded in getting across the river, but the number of prisoners taken amounted to 730.

This strange conflict which was fought without regard to military tactics, in which the valor of a small army made it more than equal to an army twice as large, and in which the number of prisoners marched into camp was almost equal to the entire force of the victors, was the event that stamped an ideal of valor and headlong courage, a spirit of self-sacrifice and devotion to the cause of freedom that proved to the world once again that oppression can only exist where it will be tolerated; and this strange conflict decided that Texas should 54

be free and that the English-speaking people should rule a large part of the continent. 

The battle lasted only eighteen minutes, but the real extent of the victory was not known until the next day. The 

Texan army lost eight killed and seventeen wounded, while the Mexican's loss was 630 killed, 208 wounded and 730 prisoners.

A small group of grave-stones marks the graves of the Texans who were killed in the battle. The site of the large campfire around which the Mexican prisoners spent the night after the battle is also on the bayou side of the shell road. 

Colonel Delgado, aide to General Santa Anna, describes with much feeling how he and a large company were marching across the prairie-barefooted, for it seems that he had lost his shoes in the muddy bayou where his horse mired down as he was attempting to reach the "island of trees"-and he complains bitterly of the treatment that he received. He also tells of seeing the Texans build a large fire, "made up of huge piles of wood, even whole trees being used," and he goes on to say: "I and several of my companions were silly enough to believe that we were about to be burnt alive, in retaliation for those who had been burnt in the Alamo. We should have considered it an act of mercy to be shot first. Oh! the bitter and cruel moment! However, we felt considerably relieved when they placed us around the fire to warm ourselves, and to dry our wet clothes."

While the positions of the various divisions of General Houston's army are carefully marked out, they are important mostly as a background for getting an idea as to how these units started, for the real battle was fought within Santa Anna's camp, just after it had been stormed, and around the 55

Several officers and Almonte, Santa Anna's secretary, made a covenant that they would fight or surrender but that they would not retreat. So many of the men and their horses mired that the road almost became a bridge of dead men and horses, and it was behind this mass of struggling men and horses that the Mexicans made their last stand.

General Houston led the charge against this "island of great trees," but his horse, pierced with seven bullets, fell dead. It seems that the Texans did not know until this time that their general had been wounded, but as Houston's wounded leg touched the ground, he fell. Then it was that he gave over his command to General Rusk and he was placed upon another horse and taken to the rear. The Spell of Indian Summer 

HE AIR is charged with fable and romance. Reverie is invited. The spirit is soothed in the contemplation of the landscape. There are flashes of brilliant wings; the scene is dotted with color, but a veil of silver creates a mood. "It is dreamy Indian summer."

A shimmering veil enwraps the landscape. A film of blue haze softens the outlines of distant objects. Myriads of silken spider webs float in the air and glisten from the tall grass. The birds that trail and circle about utter slow, careless calls. The scold of the bluejay has lost its sharpness, and the mechanical squeak of the purple grackle trails off into a musical ending.

There is a continual hubbub of frail and interblended sounds, but these are softened and subdued until they float like echoes from a distant symphony. Sharp tones have been modulated. A rhythmic harmony rolls with the breeze and settles with the shade. The sun shines with a diffused softness. Butterflies flash and scintillate across the fields, making zigzags and festoons of color, but in every case they seem to be moving with a conscious deliberation. Dragonflies, mosquito-hawks with metallic wings, skim and glide around as though they were mechanical contrivances set on wires. 

Grasshoppers lift themselves with a jump and a flutter, only to balance in the air for deliberate glide with little wingmovement into a bunch of thick grass. 

A ballooning spider, one of the "little people" of the open spaces, too tiny to "go places and do things" under its own power of locomotion, spins a web for a balloon basket, flings tendrils of silk in the air, leaps into space and floats away, 65

content with any destination. Luck, chance, the spirit of venture are blended into the mood that makes everything unreal and yet a dreamlike reality. The faith of the tiny balloonist may call out admiration, but it kindles no sympathy. 

Although it is early in the afternoon and the sun is well above the treetops, the restless katydids saw their fiddles. 

This seems to stimulate the harvest flies, and they begin to grate their tamborines. In sharp contrast to the buzzing and droning of the wasps and bumblebees, a tree-frog pipes in shrill bagpipe notes, and the snowy tree-crickets chirp their high tenor. But all of this floats and rests on deliberate cadences. It has lost its dash and spirit in the padded junglesilence; a dreamy restfulness pervades the hazy air.

The sheep have sought the edge of the timber. Some of them crowd to the shade; others nibble at the sparse vegetation that remains in this frequented locality; but a few of them have stretched themselves out in the sunlight as though they would absorb the sun's rays. It is a picture of inconsequent loafing and dreamy restfulness.

It is the halcyon period of autumn; there is a gypsy-blood tingle in the air. This tingle is charged with fable and romance. We want to leave the path and strike out across the field. There is an urge to ramble among the inkweeds and elderberry bushes. Summer's fullness and contentment seem to call for a stock-taking, a check-up of hidden resources. 

A Virginia creeper is swinging among the branches of a wild apple, and a silvery nimbus hangs about a cluster of scarlet bells. There is a mystic lure in the deep color of these scarlet funnels that dangle so gracefully among serrated leaflets and immense green pods; but the great droning, goldbanded wasp is not tempted to explore their depths. In accord with the spirit of the day, this nectar-loving insect must find 66

sweets that can be had with little effort. Bearing flowers and fruit, this trailing vine of the Virginia creeper shows the fullness of summer and the torch of autumn. 

A faint blueness fills the spaces between the trees, but it is not smoke. It is Indian summer.

The halcyon calm of the harvest moon is fixed in the landscape. There is romance and mystery in the waning splendor. A shimmering veil of gossamer, like a fairy net, enwraps the trees and the hummocks of Cherokee roses, and spreads across the pasture lands. It has run together, repaired itself, and there is no scar. 

There are no cow-tracks in the gossamer veil of Indian summer.

When the breeze is not too strong, there may be cables of flying spiders, or a ballooning spider may be afloat in a silken basket. A careful glance upward is likely to reveal a set of thin silvery, sinuous filaments drifting through the air. While these silky threads are bent in various directions, and taking 67

the general direction of the wind, they converge in a basketlike contrivance that holds a tiny spider.

A story in some of the very old books describes marvelous feats of an Indian conjurer. He would throw a rope into the air and then climb up it. Strange, fanciful, absurd, yet a young spider does something that comes very near to that feat. Seeking the highest point of a weed or a grass stem, the spider begins to spin threads.

Standing on tiptoe, with its head to the breeze, the spider spins these fine threads of silk and lets the free ends float on the breeze. After it has spun out fifteen or twenty feet of thread, there is a perceptible pull, but the tiny insect sets its claws firmly and spins another thread. When about six such threads have been sent out and their pull has occasioned something of a strain, the eight claws are relaxed simultaneously and the spider jumps into the air.

The trailing, light threads spread out so far and come in contact with so much of the air which is in motion, that the mass of web not only remains afloat, but it has sufficient buoyancy to carry the little spider. Launched in the air, this tiny balloonist has some advantages over the unfortunate Andree who attempted to float over the north pole in a balloon some thirty-five years ago.

When the little spider vaults upward, its body is turned back downward, and the ray of floating threads is broken loose from the spinnerets and grasped by the feet. A little mat or net of delicate threads is constructed, and while the feet cling to the net another silken filament is spun and allowed to float behind. This forms a sort of control rudder; it might be compared to the tail of a kite. But the spiderling can swing the balancing-tail or rudder around and can draw it in or reel it out. As the basket nears the earth, the spider throws out a sinken rope, which becomes attached to some object—a landing rope, a wharf line.

This glimpse of the mystical, fairylike manifestations of life and force, this playing and dallying with great truths and natural laws, this inconsequent rambling with mind and body, furnishes an opportunity for the subconscious self to be recognized. When the outlines are softened and distinctive features fade out, materialism seems to fade also and we are conscious of the unlimited and unbroken flow of force. It may be science; it is science-aeronautics, the law of gravitation, modern engineering-but in the great universe, among planets and suns and stars and nebulæ, a human being, even the wisest philosopher or scientist, cannot rate so much higher than the tiny spider in her silken balloon. 69

Revery has a place in the mental makeup of a normal human being. Silent meditation permits the mind to rearrange materials and to make deductions without prejudice. 

The "spell of Indian summer" invites revery. It must be useful, or we would not feel it. Man is happier when he learns to work with natural forces. Experience proves this. 

But in the dust and din of a busy world, we can hardly withdraw to the "mountain tops"; we can hardly throw off the yoke of cares. From the standpoint of mere efficiency, a man will do more work in a six-day week than he will in a sevenday week, and his year's work with a vacation will be more productive than his year's work without a vacation. Is it possible that this "Indian summer," this "Sabbath of the year," may have a deeper significance than we have been inclined to give it? 70

The Texas Roadrunner 

"Feathered comedian, in crazy-quilt color pattern, Caricature of a bird."

F peculiarities were quills, the Texas roadrunner would be I' a queer porcupine. A bird that is not like other birds, a bird-curiosity that defies all birdlike attributes, an outlandish caricature of bird-life in appearance and in everyday habits, this monstrosity in feathers streaks and volplanes, capers and monkeyshines along the edge of the chaparral thickets, along the edge of credulity. 

Seeing is believing. It requires some personal experience with a roadrunner to form a background of friendly credulity that can accept the strange and unreasonable facts. His crazy-quilt color pattern of blue and gray and white set off by an unseemly topknot bristling with drollery, and this displayed from one point of a tri-square, seems to define a comedian in the character of a buffoon.

In spite of his apparent awkwardness and lack of adaptations for running, this droll bird can throw his tail forward to a Spencerian angle, point his neck straight ahead and give an exhibition run that will keep him well ahead of a good pony. And these "pony races" seem to be made only as a matter of amusement, for he is an adept in gliding into a thicket whenever it happens to suit his convenience. In addition to this, he can take to wing whenever he wishes. 

These wonderful runs can only be accounted for on the ground of playfulness.

There is an iridescence, a sheen, that imparts a princely dignity to his coat, but even this can hardly be mistaken for anything but a mere accident in putting an extra touch on dressing a clown. Probably it does signify real vitality and a superior bird intelligence, for the roadrunner is both efficient and wise. He carries no surplus flesh, and, like an umbrella, 90

he is little more than draped framework; but his power of endurance is remarkable. His muscles and tendons are strong and tough; he is a good machine. 

A roadrunner can kill a rattlesnake-not only a little ground rattler or an immature specimen, but a hardy diamondback wearing six or eight rattles. Some people will not believe it, but the testimony is overwhelming. The writer never witnessed such a combat, but he has listened to the recital of the minute details by trustworthy observers. During the summer of 1913, I happened to be hunting butterflies in the vicinity of the United States army camp near Laredo, Texas, when my attention was attracted by a crowd of soldiers and Mexican women and children. Threading my way among clusters of cactus and bunches of thorny brush, I arrived at the scene of the excitement. 

They were gathered about a dead rattlesnake and a roadrunner was watching from a distance. The snake had five rattles and a button. It was a well-proportioned, good-sized snake, and the tail was writhing about, "waiting for the sun to do down." The people watched the fight from a distance for a while, but they had gradually crowded closer until the two combatants were almost within a circle. 

The bird was so intent on the business of fighting that it did not seem to notice the gathering crowd of people until the snake was so completely overcome that it could not lift its head from the ground. After taking two or three sharp thrusts, the wary bird lifted its head as though it would look around, but, as though surprised by the presence of the crowd, it ran away.

The bird continued to lurk around, usually in sight, until I left the place.

Mr. William Witte of San Antonio furnished the data for this description. The large rattlesnake was in his coil ready 91

to strike, with his head held well above when the "piasano," roadrunner, began making passes towards him. At first the bird circled around at a safe distance. The snake made his rattles buzz, almost like playing a tune, and sometimes he would push out his forked tongue. After much pretense, darting forward and falling back, the bird began hopping and dancing about, often threatening to strike. This seemed to anger the snake, and he began to bow his neck and to threaten. 

The bird continued teasing until the snake began to strike. 

After the snake had made several strikes at the bird, all of them being very wide, the bird changed its tactics. When the snake made a long strike, almost his entire length, and fell on the ground, the bird gave him a hard stroke just behind the head. After this was repeated several times, the snake became more cautious and did not jump so far. As the snake began to show caution, the bird increased his blustering attack. With a clattering noise, made by rattling or the snapping together of his stout mandibles, the bird rushed forward many times without making a strike. Then, from a distance, he would rush forward with his wings half spread and his tail erect, and with wings and tail flexed, ready to spread, he would come and deliver a lightninglike blow, just one fierce thrust, and retreat. It seemed that the sharppointed bill found the same spot each time. The bird was employing "system." With surprising agility, the bird's attack was that of an expert fencer. 

As the snake becomes less alert, the bird makes regular dashes and delivers a stroke each time. When a spot of blood appears on the scaly neck, every stroke seems to be directed to that point. The sharp bill is smeared with blood. As the snake weakens, the bird drives his heavy beak with greater 92

force. It seems that he is striking for the brain or the spinal cord. When the snake can no longer hold up his head, the fierce bird deliberately picks an eye out. After a pause, he knocks the other eye out. With a little more digging in the gaping wound, just back of the head, the fight is over. And the bird came through without a scratch.

With most birds there is a marked difference in the shades and color-markings of the sexes. This is usually noticeable in the depth and extent of the sheen, especially on the slim. feathers of the neck and the short feathers just in front of the tail quills, but among roadrunners there is no difference in the sheen; male and female are marked alike. 

It takes a good ornithologist to tell a male roadrunner from a female in the field. A slight difference in the voice is a real distingnishing feature, but in most instances the lonesome bird will not utter the "er-r-t" or "ar-r-r-t." 

Throughout its range, this bird has acquired many nicknames, most of which suggest a characteristic. Typical of these are "chaparral cock," "swift hawk," "paisano" (Spanish, el piasano, fellow countryman), "war-bird," "lizard bird," "running cuckoo," "ground cuckoo," "snake bird," "snake killer," "choor-ray-ah" (Spanish, la churrea, in imitation of its peculiar cry), "matador," "medicine bird," "streak bird,” "ground eagle," and "el correo del carmino" (Spanish, applied because the bird seems to enjoy running in the road just ahead of a horseman). In scientific literature, the roadrunner is known as Geococcyx californianus (Lesson), and in the 

American Ornithologist's Union's check list of American birds it is number 385. 

As one of the nicknames suggests, the "chaparral cock" inhabits the barren plains that are covered in part with low, thorny brush and cactus thickets, especially in the Rio Grande 93

Valley of Texas, the lower part of California and some parts of Arizona and New Mexico. As a bird of the arid plains, it has attracted much attention in Texas and in California. 

There is but a slight variation in the local forms. During the last few years, the California roadrunners have enjoyed fairly good protection from hunters and careless sportsmen, while in Texas the condition has been reversed. Our great "lizard bird" and snake-killing clown has been hunted diligently by people who were seeking trophies to mount. 

The roadrunner is not a table bird. The meat is dry, tough, muscular, like that of a hawk. The bird is valuable to the farmer, and it deserves protection in its natural range. 

Since it rids the country of snakes and lizards and insects, it should be welcome everywhere. It captures grasshoppers and crickets on wing, it catches field mice, and it will make a meal of centipedes or scorpions. It will also eat snails and the fruit of certain kinds of cacti. 

No bird can be more entertaining around a camp. When treated kindly, one of these jokers will come to the camp regularly and perform the same stunt in the same way every day, and, like a trained actor, it will retire when it has gone through with its stunt. At a camp on Flour Bluff, a few miles from Corpus Christi, the roadrunner would appear every morning a little after daylight. When the dogs would bark, he would stop, then turn and walk around in a semi-circle, but gradually getting closer to the camp, and finally he would make a big run for the table.

With all the false dignity of a small town wag, the bird would mount the table, whirl around a few times, walk to the opposite end, lift his wings as though he intended to fly, and finally close the scene by jumping off of the table and run- 94

ning away so fast that even the dogs would not be able to see "just where the streak has disappeared."

At one camp, where a tall tree lay as it fell when uprooted by a wind storm, a playful roadrunner would come every day about the middle of the forenoon and amuse himself by running the full length of the log. Sometimes he would walk carefully around to the top again and repeat his run. 

The real surprise of this exercise was his studied method and his rigid regularity.

One instance is quoted of a party that was encamped in a little grove that was surrounded by a low scattered chapparal. 

They had four dogs, but one of them was a mere pup. This pup was almost as big as the grown dogs, but he was quite undeveloped mentally. One day this pup came running into the camp as though something terrible was after him. Investigation showed that a roadrunner had chased him. The next day the same thing happened again, and for five succeeding days the timid pup was chased into camp. The bird seemed to acquire boldness with practice until he would come almost to the tent; but for some reason, he never attempted to molest any of the grown dogs. If one of the dogs made a move as though he would interfere, some of the men would call him off and the bird would follow the pup with a great show of fierceness; but somehow, he never quite overtook the pup.

The punctuality of a roadrunner is proverbial; it has been said that a roadrunner will pass a certain place every day at nearly the same hour and that he will engage in the same kind of a prank every time that he comes to a particular place. If the particular place happens to be a stump, he may hop upon it, whirl himself around and go his way. While he may pass many stumps without showing the least interest, 95

when he comes to this stump, he must go through this particular gesture. This peculiarity may also be shown in crossing a deep dry gully at a particular place; here he may volplane across, but in other places, where the conditions seem to be the same, he may walk down to the bed and up the hill on the other side. Sometimes he will mount a stump or a post and amuse himself by puffing out his feathers. 

The erratic joker of the chaparral wastes is also an erratic eater. Almost as ravenous as a peccary, he will eat about everything. He will eat some things that could hardly be considered fit fare for a bird, "everything that grows" and "everything that runs or crawls." He swallows mice, centipedes, caterpillars, spiders, millipeds, butterflies, snakes, bumblebees, and even horned toads. In the Rio Grande Valley, where the great tarantulas abound, there is a red wasp that is even greater than the tarantula. This wasp is known as the tarantula-hawk, because he kills the tarantula and drags its limp body to his nest. This tarantula-hawk is quite a formidible insect, large and strong and fully armed with a poisonous sting; but this wasp is only a bite for a roadrunner; and this bite is usually taken rather carelessly while the bird is on the lookout for something else-possibly a live tarantula or a ground rattlesnake.

The roadrunner does not migrate. He prefers to stay on his range and shift for his food. He can hardly be considered a cheerful companion. A pair is seldom seen together. They spend most of the year much like two hermits having adjoining holdings, and they seldom encroach on one another's territory. It is believed that, when once mated they are mated for life, and that the pairs are inclined to respect the territorial rights of other pairs.

Nest-building, with the roadrunners, is more a matter of 96

instinct than intelligent originality. They usually select a location in tangled brush four or five feet from the ground. 

The nest is well hidden among the thick branches of a lowgrowing tree or bush. While nests have been found in elms, rock cedar, highland liveoak, hackberry and black-fruited persimmons, the favorite location is a rambling mesquite; and if that can be found just above a cluster of prickly-pear cactus, the place is ideal. Sometimes they will nest in the pricklypear, and, in rare instances, the nest is built on the ground. 

While the nest is rather a coarse, flat platform, it is compactly put together. Sticks and bark and short pieces of grass are carefully worked in so that it becomes quite serviceable. 

The lining consists of weeds, roots, cedar bark, moss, Indian tobacco stems, chicken feathers or cast-off snake skins, but this part of the work is not so well done. Occasionally you may find a nest that is very carefully lined, but sometimes the nest is unlined.

With her reputation for regularity in performing fool tricks, madam roadrunner is shockingly irregular as to the number of eggs she lays for a setting, the intervals at which she lays them and the time at which she begins to sit. The set may be any number from two to eight eggs-eleven has been reported. In some instances the eggs are all laid on successive days, or one egg may be laid several days before another, two or three may be laid on successive days, then another interval of several days may be had before the last eggs of the set are placed in the nest. She may begin to sit as soon as the first egg is in the nest; some observers assert that she always begins to sit before the set is complete; then we have the peculiar observation that in some instances she rests a day or two after the last egg of the clutch has been laid before beginning to sit. The eggs are about the size of an 97

ordinary hen's eggs, the scientific measurements being 1.50 by 1.20; they are short, ovate, plain white and without gloss.

Sometimes they are covered with a chalky white coat of dust; in rare instances, buff-white or dirty white eggs have been found.

Several men were employed on Mr. Garcia's ranch. They lived in tenement houses strung around the ranch buildings. 

In foraging for special items of food, one of the men happened to find the nest of a roadrunner. He brought five eggs to the house, but the first one that they broke contained a half-developed chicken. TRANGE are the adaptations of the "toad-lizard." This flat, dry, horny and spiny toad-like lizard, entirely harmless, but suggesting some kind of a monster of the dragon type, has attained a high place in the folk lore and literature of every race of people who have lived in the arid regions of Texas, New Mexico, Arizona and California.

The horned toad is one of the quaintest of living creatures. 

It is a true lizard, although it has a flat body covered with spiny scales and a circlet of horns about the head. It is not a toad. Toads have dry skins, in contrast to that of the frogs, which are moist and slimy; but no toad has scales or spines. 

Of the seventeen kinds of horned lizards, thirteen of them can be found in the plains region and the Pacific slope of the 

United States; the other four are Mexican forms. The Texas horned lizard (Phrynosoma cornutum) is the most abundant and the most widely distributed horned lizard; probably this species includes more individual members than all of the other sixteen counted together, and their range extends over six times as much territory as any of the others. From California to Louisiana, Nebraska to the central part of Mexico, the 

Texas horned lizard is the prevailing form. 

The "horned toad that put Texas in the story books” shows many strange adaptations for life in barren plains, where flesh-eating animals are hungry and sharp-eyed birds are continually on the lookout for food. The pebbly and blotched color pattern of his back blends into the landscape; then he has a habit of covering himself with sand so as to al- 102

low only his head to protrude above, and that has the appearance of dirt, broken sticks or the roots of dead grass rather than the head of a lizard.

The horned lizard is colored like the soil; wherever he is found, his color accords with the soil so well that he is almost invisible until he moves. Sometimes a moving lizard can almost drop out of sight by simply stopping on the sand. 

When they live on white sand, horny lizards are very light in color, but if the soil is black they are correspondingly dark, and those found in the mountain districts where bits of variously colored rocks make up the surface of the soil, exhibit some wonderful markings of red and blue and yellow, and these put on in irregular patches and blotches that just accord with their surroundings.

A change in the surroundings will bring about a change in the color of the lizard. Professor Swarth tells a good story of one of these color changes. Two collectors were among red rocks in the mountains of Arizona when they found specimens of horned lizards that were almost completely red, "dark red and lighter red, but all red." They thought that they had found a new species, and they hurriedly wrote to the university and to the United States Biological Survey, describing their find and promising to bring in plenty of specimens. Telegrams were exchanged, and in high spirits they started two boxes of "red horned lizards" by express. 

In one of the boxes they included four specimens of the light colored form from the sand hills. A week later, when they arrived at the museum, there were no "red lizards," and it was very difficult to tell the difference between the lizards that had been collected on the sand hill and the famous "red horned lizards" of the red rocks.

If one of these horned lizards is placed in a box with a 103

few inches of black soil in the bottom, the lizard will become darker colored. When placed in a box with light-colored material or white paper, one of these lizards will soon have rows of grizzled scales, and sometimes a scale or a little group of scales will become white; sometimes the heavy scales around the nose become pale or grizzly. 

When a horned lizard burrows into the ground, he uses his head instead of his forelegs. By twisting and screwing the horny head, the lizard forces the sand to one side, and his body can be pressed so as to follow wherever the head can get through. When roughly attacked, a horned lizard may "play 'possum," in which condition he may be rolled and tumbled around without the slightest show of life. 

The "sacred toad" of Mexico is any kind of a horned lizard. The so-called habit of "shedding tears of blood" is one of the most surprising traits that can be found among these undecipherable marvels of animal life. They do not "spit blood" or "weep blood," but, under certain circumstances, a horned lizard may shoot a fine jet of blood from beneath the upper eyelid. This phenomena is unusual, and with some individuals it seems to be impossible, or the exact stimulus cannot be found. Until a few years ago, there were herpetologists who scoffed at the mention of "jets of blood" from the eyes of the horned lizard, but facts are persistent stones in the pavement of the highway of knowledge; it just remains for doubters to investigate. Since it is necessary to try several or many specimens in order to get the reaction, some present-day scientists think that the trait is fading out of the race. 

It is possible to put a horned lizard to sleep by rubbing it gently on the top of the head and between the eyes. When it is rubbed this way, it will turn its head down and "go to 104

sleep" so well that it may remain in a sort of stupor for five minutes, even though it is moved about. This "petting the toad to sleep" was practiced by the Indians of the Southwest before white men came to America. 

The horned lizard lives on insects, beetles, bugs and flies, but mostly ants. And here is another strange adaptation: the lizard simply swallows live ants-and they agree with him. 

If a human being took a mouthful of live ants, his difficulties would be something worse than indigestion. Why is it that the ants do not bite or sting, gnaw their way out?

Instances have been quoted where dogs that swallowed live ants died from the irritation—and a dog can digest bones. 

Most lizards are oviparous; they lay eggs which are hatched by the heat of the sun or by heat produced by decomposing vegetable matter. But the Texas horned lizard is viviparous; the young are brought forth alive; and these young are pocket editions of adults, except that they do not have spines. They are frequently encountered when they are but a few days old, tiny little "baby-toads," running about like little flat beetles.

How can they endure so much heat? In sand that is baking hot, how can they keep from baking? In the fall of the year when insects become scarce, they fall back on that marvelous "butting themselves in" into the ground, and after they have "dug themselves in" with their horns, they go to sleep and they sleep several months. They do not go down deep; in some parts of their range they are not below the frost line; yet, after freezing and thawing through the winter, they wake up in the spring ready for an active summer. 

Under ordinary conditions, a horned lizard is timid, retiring and inoffensive, but sometimes an individual will make a rather strange and ridiculous show of self-defense. An 105

occasional specimen will resent any annoyance by assuming an attitude; it will arch its back, bend its head downward, and jump straight up. In some instances this clown-capering is accompanied by a sort of hiss or sneeze.

The whole performance is more like "throwing a fit” than a bluff.

This inoffensive. insect-eating lizard deserves good treatment. Certainly, a harmless, ant-destroying lizard deserves a better fate than to be crudely mounted between two pieces of bread and sold for a few cents at a curio store. The Texas "horny toad sandwich" is a crude joke that should be suppressed.

The Trade Rat HE TRADE RAT is the practical joker of the Ameri can desert. He is a droll prankster. While he is something of a comedian, he does not answer curtain calls.

The curious blending of a remarkable intelligence with what seems to be an instinctive premonition invests this peculiar animal with a reputation for occult ability, the gift of foreseeing events, and also certain powers of reading the intentions of other animals, and even the intentions of man. 

People who have camped in the plains regions of California, Texas, Arizona and New Mexico relate wonderful stories of how the trade rat will carry off spoons and matches, pipes and cartridges, trinkets and any kind of plunder that is left laying around the camp. But in most instances, this purloining thief leaves something in return; he does not steal; he simply trades. He seems to have a peculiar trick of picking up anything that happens to suit his fancy and carrying it around until he finds something that suits him better; then he simply drops what he is carrying and picks up the new object.

While the "pack rats" do collect much material of various kinds in their nests, it is safe to say that only a small part of the things that they pick up and carry ever reach their nests. 

Like a man getting a walking stick who takes the first one that he finds, carries it until he finds one that suits him better and continues to exchange all along the road, this whimsical rat will make dozens of changes and finally arrive at his nest with something of much less importance than the first thing picked up.

A story is told of a man who waked up in camp to find that his glasses were gone, but there was a ten-dollar gold- 107

piece in their place; another man lost his pocket knife, but he was compensated with a red poker chip; on another occasion all of the matches were gone out of a man's vest pocket, but in their place were cucumber seeds; the beans were all removed from a can, and the can was nearly full of pebbles; the prunes were removed from a paper sack, and the sack was partially filled with acorns; one night all of the spoons were removed from the box that served as a table, and nice white pebbles were left; all of the cartridges on one side of a belt were removed one night, and there was a miscellaneous display of sticks and cactus leaves scattered about the sleeper; all of the crackers were removed from a box, and the box was almost full of chips of wood. These are but samples of the many accusations that are piled up against the trade rat. 

Professor Edmund C. Jaeger of Pasadena, California, has given much study to this wonderful enigma of instinct, wit and industry. He says that the trade rats will not live in habitations that are infested by our common house rats; they are cleanly in their habits; they have interesting manners; and they make most engaging pets. Like many other phantoms of life that so frequently elude our gaze when we are almost staring at them, these great rats blend into the landscape so completely that it requires some skill in order to see them. 

Unless you are actually looking for them, you may cross the desert without seeing a trade rat. After seeing a streak as a rat went through a thin bunch of weeds, it took the writer several minutes to locate a large trade rat that was sitting humped up on the bare ground; and even then he did not seem to be much like an animal; his chin was flat on the ground; his ears were drooped down, and the color of his body exactly corresponded to the sand. But after the shape of the body in this pose has been firmly fixed in the mind, it 108

is comparatively easy to see the rats, especially because the eyes are quite large; as it were, you look for the "spots on the stone."

A story is told of some prospectors who were traveling along the bed of a dry river when, without apparent cause, a trade rat leaped from a pack on one of the burros and scampered up the side of the canyon. Men who are accustomed to living outdoors and meeting the conditions of life on equal terms are sure to make inquiries about anything that seems to be out of the ordinary. While they were discussing the probable reason for the rat's abrupt departure, one of them said, "Isn't it a little darker? Maybe there is a storm coming." The other man climbed up the side of the canyon, and, sure enough, there was a dark cloud. Before they succeeded in getting the heavily burdened animals to the level ground the water was rushing along the bed of the stream. 

It seems that the senses of the rat were a little more acute than those of the men. There is little doubt that a trembling of the earth, a little darkening of the sun or some other physical phenomenon gave notice to the keen senses of the rat that there was a storm approaching. At any rate, it seems safe to account for such a fore-knowledge as the rat exhibited or a purely scientific basis. To a person with a scientific turn of mind, the fact that the rat became aware of the approaching storm before the men or the pack-animals, suggests. keen sense-not the occult. While the bed of a dry stream is a much-used path in these rough, dry. regions, the rats well know that they must seek the high ground when it rains.

There is no reason why these animals should not be as desirable for food as squirrels. They do not eat refuse like the tame rats; they are scrupulously clean in their habits, and their food consists of plant stocks and seeds of various kinds. 

The trade rat was the original American "hobo." And like the modern type of "hobo," he seems to be simply seeking "adventure." It seems almost incredible that a rat will get into a mans' pack and allow himself to be carried from day to day across the desert, especially when food is scarce and the pack must be overhauled at least once a day, and the man is unfriendly. A story is told of two prospectors who had been walking for several weeks when a pack rat ran away from the camp site as the packs were thrown down. Since the question was raised as to whether the rat had been carried all day, they watched next day when the things were being tied up. That night the rat again ran away and into a thicket. For seven successive evenings, and in spite of every precaution, this efficient rat would be seen leaving just as they were beginning to unpack for the night. Finally they succeeded in killing the rat. It is an established fact that our common house rats often cross the desert with the large caravans, but traveling with two people is more difficult. 

The stories of these rats traveling with wagon trains are even more wonderful. One writer asserts that the trade rats will never allow themselves to be carried out of the real desert country. As the wagons emerge from the sandy regions, the rats drop off and begin the long trek back to the home nest.

After a long wagon train had passed well into the land of thrifty vegetation, some horseback riders who were following a few hours behind met a string of no less than seventeen pack rats that were making their way back along the trail. 

By the people who tell these stories, it is claimed that the only reason that the rats are not more frequently seen as they journey back home is because they travel by night, and even then they are extremely coy and wonderfully adept at keeping out of sight.

One of the explanations that is offered for such strange conduct is that man is a mystery to them and they are by nature very inquisitive. From this it would follow that curiosity causes them to endure many hardships and to subject themselves to unnecessary dangers. But when a town springs up near a nest, even though the nest may be in a well-pro- 111

When fuel is scarce, it is the custom of campers to go to one of the large nests of the pack rats. With the conglomerate mass of stones and sticks and bones, there is usually some dry wood. Like an ancestral castle, some of these larger nests must be quite old. While the young are frequently going out and building new nests, some of them remain with the old homestead. Just how old are some of the larger pack rats' nests? A century? Two centuries? Or are they all of comparative recent origin? We lack data. 

However, it seems to be fairly well established that some of the oldest ones are at least one hundred years old. From the researches of Dr. Mearns, it would seem that the extinct cliff dwellers used the bones of these rats for certain utensils, scrapers and picks; and from the fact that he found charred bones among the ruins of the cave dwellers, he inferred that these people used pack rats for food. 

Several years ago the practice of "prospecting in rat stacks" was somewhat familiar to the adventurers who were braving the rigors of the desert and risking their lives in attempts to find big fortunes, or in simply trying to find out what could be found. Adventure, curiosity! Is there a similarity between men and trade rats?

On one occasion, some prospectors were digging through a big nest that had been admirably placed under a ledge of rocks. There had been much material collected in this ledge; possibly more than a ton. After they got well down into the mass, one of them found a Spanish gold coin. On the third day they came upon the old camp site on a little eminence next to the rocks; here was a human skull and several large human bones, a gun barrel, an old rusty revolver, the remains of a saddle-tree with a brass horn and some pieces of metal that they could not identify. Not a scrap of cloth or leather remained. Not far down the hill there was the skull and some large bones of a donkey. 

While these men had found about $800 worth of coins, they found no real clue to the identity of the victim or of the cause of the tragedy. Pack rats do not write history. 

Because of the destruction by fire and the ravages of certain wild animals that are not afraid of a few cactus thorns when they are digging for prey, it is only the nests that are well protected in the rocky ledges of the more inaccessible canyons that remain for many years. While cayotes and desert foxes are fond of trade rats, they will not attempt to dig among the thorny mass that makes up a rat nest. 

The story of the "trade rat strike" reads like a dime novel. 

While the real mine has not been located to a certainty, there are mines in the immediate vicinity. While some prospectors were scrambling up the side of a canyon, they noticed some shining bits of rock among the usual materials of a rat's nest. 

Investigations proved that it was gold ore of a high grade; evidently this ore had been taken from the ledge by some miner. A search of the nearby hills revealed two skeletons. 

Bits of clothing and camping equipment attested the fact that they were miners, probably prospectors like the men who found them. From the evidence, it seemed that these unfor- 113

tunate men had discovered paying ore, but, like so many baubles for which so many of us are yet striving, the gold was useless to them; they could not take even the few nuggets with them. And here again the trade rats failed to record history; their nest was destroyed; even the ledge was blasted away, but nothing was found that gave a clue as to the identity of the skeletons, or as to the location of the vein from which the ore was obtained. 

Probably the great horned owl is as successful in capturing pack rats as any other enemy of this extremely vigilant animal. This owl hunts at night; he is a still-hunter and he seldom makes a failure when he makes a swoop at a rat; in other words, he does not train his victims by letting them escape several times.

Some of the big rattlesnakes will go into the nests after the rats, but since the wily rats have more than one exit, the crafty snake is by no means sure of his prey when he goes into a nest. Frequently the big snake will hunt all around the nest and finally leave while the cunning rats are watching him from points of a rocky ledge. In the open country, the rats make a series of runways with several avenues of escape. 

The tribes of Indians who use the trade rats as food are extremely destructive in their methods of hunting. In many instances, they simply set fire to the nest and watch for the rats to try to escape. But while they are reasonably sure of driving the rats from the location, they are by no means sure of getting the rats; a rat may remain in a shallow burrow just under the burning nest; and in some instances rats will choose to stay and be burned rather than run the risk of being killed. The Hopi Indians are especially fond of one kind of pack rats which they name "kee-wha-cal-a."

The pack rat (Muridæ neotoma) belongs to the group of 114

animals that are known as rodents. They include mice, squirrels, beavers and groundhogs. They are not destructive animals; even when they travel as unwanted guests, in some man's pack where food is comparatively scarce for the people, whatever toll they take is seldom missed. It seems that they never try to get away with much food; this traveling about is a sort of whim, and it yet requires some explanation. They are even more inquisitive than squirrels. In their lonesome homes in the desert they seldom see people, yet they seek some form of association with people whenever they can. 

In a sense a pack rat is an interrogation point. What is his object in life? Endowed with more intelligence than most any one of the other animals with which he is associated, why does he continue to do queer and foolish things?

The modern-classic Rio Grande begins in Colorado and meanders through New Mexico; then it assumes the coy and overbearing attitude of an international flirt as it swings right and left, sometimes even moving the boundary line between the United States and Mexico. The family-tree of grapefruit, the ancestry of this modern marvel of horticulture, can be traced back several centuries to the shaddock, a fifteen-pound ornamental fruit that was used for decorating the banquet table. This large, coarsegrained fruit was found in Oceania at a very early date. It 123

was among the curios brought back from the East Indies by traders. Some of the largest of these "tree melons" were found in Ceylon.

The shaddock was introduced into the West Indies, probably by the Spaniards. Some of the enterprising priests who went with the "soldiers of fortune" to all parts of the world might have brought this tree with other plants to try out in Cuba or Porto Rico. These old priests had a predilection for planting strange and curious plants. Even today, the gardens of the oldest missions offer surprises and marvels of plant life. 

The zeal of these old men in bringing plants and seeds from remote places, sometimes half way around the world, was followed with untiring care and a surprising knowledge of horticulture. 

The name "pomelo," which was first applied to grapefruit in the West Indies, has not been satisfactorily traced to its source. By some writers, it is thought to be an abbreviation from the Dutch word "pompolmoes." "Pomelo" is little more than a combination of "pomum melo" (melon-apple), an appropriate name, but "grapefruit” is more intimately expressive.

Grapefruit is not an accident. It is not a "sport," a “mutant" or a "spontaneous variation"; it is the culmination of centuries of the most careful husbandry. A subtropical fruit has attained its present perfection largely by adaptations to soil and climate of different localities. After reaching its highest point in one place, it is introduced into a new locality, where it thrives with a new elegance. Change of locality, careful husbandry and budding have combined to produce the marvel.

It is impossible to tell how many graves there were—and several men might have been buried in one grave. Some vigorous, green trees add to the seclusiveness and the charm of the location.

Salt cedars, which are mentioned in the very earliest accounts of the old town, and which have been an outstanding feature, a delicate fringe of the cobalt bay, have finally disappeared from the water front. It requires careful search to find even remnants of the marvelous, trailing, vine-like trees that thrilled the tourists from Chicago and New York City in the old days before the storm destroyed most of them and a water front improvement program that accounted for the last tree.

A marker of Texas granite has been placed in the well kept yard of David Hirsch school. This marker bears the inscription, “General Zachary Taylor and his army encamped here, 1845."

The exact plan of the camp cannot be reconstructed at this time. There was a look-out station on top of the hill, and this was supported by no less than three hidden intelligence posts conveniently located in the low chaparral which extended for miles with a monotonous sameness. 

Since the worst Indian trouble of those times consisted of raids by small bands that would move quickly and fade out in the presence of real opposition, it was found most efficient to send out small groups of soldiers, and these would be supported on all sides by numerous native scouts, jokingly referred to as "the hounds of the army." The soldiers who were sent out on these little raids referred to them as "hunting 157

parties," and they would write home in a lighter vein, sometimes giving colorful descriptions of the sport of "hunting Indians."

Conservation 

Just as it is our duty to preserve the priceless works of art, literature and science accumulated through the ages, we are under obligation to save for future generations a fitting sample of every type of natural area, complete in its indigenous species of plant and animal life. This country has lived and grown so fast that the buffalo, the prong horned antelope, the wild turkey and the prairie chicken are gone, and even now sweet briar, ferns and sassafras are disappearing from the landscape. 

Flowers by the roadside should be left for everybody to enjoy. Even where flowers are plentiful, people picking flowers should go a few paces back from the highway.

As conservation chairman of the Federated Women's Clubs, Mrs. Herbert Roberts is carrying the message of conservation into the public schools. 

Conferring with teachers, meeting with club groups and study organizations, addressing parent-teacher association meetings and making general assembly talks, Mrs. Roberts is really getting across with the idea that roadside wild flowers can be enjoyed without being picked and that Christmas greenery should not be gathered where it belongs in the landscape. 158

Historical Trees 

REES are the glory of the plant world. Plant life is nature's workshop and storehouse, and trees furnish the machinery for converting energy and making it available for the use of man and the lower animals. Life on the earth, as we know it, could not exist without trees. 

Like a bevy of carefree children, a liveoak adds joy and overflowing life to the city streets. Great strength, a wonderful power of outgrowing an injury and a mass of low-hanging, friendly, evergreen boughs combine to make this tree the treasure of Southern cities. It is always Springtime in a city of liveoaks, set among time-worn walls and scenes of renown, their outstretched arms waving a welcome or raised in a benediction, they appear as symbols of life itself. 

The liveoaks of Florida have been heralded in literature, but even more effective has been the flowery descriptions given in the guidebooks for winter tourists; the liveoaks of 

California, especially the Valley oaks and the Le Conte oaks of Berkeley, have been formally introduced to the world; it yet remains for Texas to speak of the most symmetrical and venerable oaks that cling as a halo about the famous old missions that have continued as beacons through the strife of years. 

The Davy Crockett tree stands in the City of Crockett.

It is a broad-sweeping liveoak which is regarded by the citizens of the community as something of a living shrine that keeps alive and green the memory of that illustrious hero whose tragic death made him an ideal of staunch patriotism. 

On his journey from Tennessee to San Antonio, just a few weeks before his death in the massacre of the Alamo, Davy 

Crockett made camp and slept beneath the protecting 159

branches of this tree. The tree is well cared for by its owner, S. M. Minzingo; it is insured for $500. 

The "Election Oak" of Bosque county stands in a pasture on the farm of Tom Pool, it is a tree of large size and remarkable beauty; under its ample shade, the first county election was held, October 4, 1854. For nearly one hundred years, meetings, picnics, and community gatherings of various kinds have been held under this great tree that seems to be large enough to shelter everybody in the county. 

The "Rio Frio Oak," in the valley of the Rio Frio, attains the magnificent dimensions of twenty-six and one-half feet in circumference, and a spread of 120 feet. Competent authorities have estimated the age of this venerable giant to be something more than 700 years. Under its shade the first school house in that part of the State was built in 1866. Since this school house was intended to serve the double purpose of school and church and also as a fort in which the people might assemble in case of an attack by Indians, it was built of cypress logs with double walls, the space between the walls being filled with adobe, this made the building safe against the best guns of that time. The shade of this tree has been the recognized community center since the first settlers began to hold church and social affairs. 

The "Temple" liveoak at Brazoria received its title from the fact that the first Masonic Lodge of Texas was organized under its protecting shade, "the star of the Lone Star State," the "arboreal temple of the Blue Lodge" protects the secrets of the lodge as well as it protects all secrets entrusted to its keeping. But it is free and open with its hospitality, offering shade and comfort and good cheer to all who may come. The age of this green "temple" is given as 400 years.

Any discussion of the historical trees of Texas must include something about "The Old Treaty Oak" at Austin. 

This beautiful tree is happily located on the West bank of the Colorado River, the situation and the view of the river adds to the beauty of the tree itself but experts have scored it and their decision is that it is very near an absolutely perfect specimen of its class. In the Hall of Fame of Forestry, at 

Washington, D. C., a picture of the tree hangs among the famous and the most beautiful trees of the United States. 

According to the estimate of scientists, this tree is six hundred years old. The Indians called it "the father of the forest." Long before white men came to Texas, this "Father of Woods" had been the meeting place for the tribes in war and in peace. The Tejas and the Comanches held their powwows, their councils, their war dances, their feasts, and their religious ceremonies under the ample shade of this forest giant that shades more than a quarter of an acre of ground. The superstitious Indians endowed it with supernatural and uncanny powers, the Tejas maidens thought that a sort of tea made from the tender green leaves, if drunk with both eyes looking at a full moon, would keep their lovers true, and, when the tribe was at war, if such a potion could be taken without anyone else knowing about it, the lover would be sure to return safely from the battle.

Stephen F. Austin met the Indian chiefs under this tree, and after "smoking the pipe of peace," they signed the first treaty granting the white men the right to occupy territory in Texas. A short distance beyond the "Treaty Tree," a "dead line" was established; all that was East of the line belonged to the white men, and all that was West of the line belonged to the Indians-and the Indians could not come beyond the line. 162

I'll take a walk-I said-beyond the hills

Where cottonwoods and willows grow; 

And as, I pass I'll see the colorful flowers all aglow.

The Terra Blanca, where willows o'er hang, is running clear, I'll sit 'neath the shade and plait of willow a charm 

To hang-for luck-upon its bough drooping near; 

Or Irene' chose to do for her knight The Prince of India-put about my bodice-fitting tight, Imitating the Indian Warrior, his lucky wampum 

Before the fight. 

My thoughts will turn to my Black Knight of recent dreams; 

I see him yet shielding and guarding me-it seems.

O Guardian soldier of my need, an army you faced- 

Gloriously, bravely, victoriously—I watched

Lines of enemy-warriors-fierce-browed-so surely erased. (When raging fever so recently burned my brow; 

Between me and dangerous black lines-even now Picture I that gallant man and armored charger, Facing the enemy-outnumbered a larger Brain, I thought, then possessed by all. 

O Beloved! I see you yet-as though at my call 

You placed yourself between them and helpless me.)

These dreams recur as I daily walk by the laughing brook- 

You wonder, I know, "Did Angelique' forget her book?"

The bluebirds today are swiftly on the wing- 

Like distant bell-chimes I hear them merrily sing, Meadow-larks greet me in a trilling chorus everywhere 

With their ever musical song so clear. 179

I reach a spot I love to haunt where water flows, A sloping meadow where green grass grows; 

Behind the shrubs, growing thickly near, I hear a chewink calling "dear, dear!" 

White-breasted song sparrows softly sing: "Good cheer!" 

Listening-charmed-through the twilight's calm

I hear a thrush repeating-religiously-his psalm: 

I remember how many days you made sweet with music's balm! 

O voice that is for me so superfine- 

To watch you win greater fame is a wish of mine!

Such thoughts I have by a cooling, clear-flowing stream, Where I've watched-intently-the rays of light softly gleam, Where I may wade, through woodland shade

And with the poets of yesteryear idly dream. 

I'll lay my head on Nature's fluttering heart-so free And recall, again, those first silver notes that became a part of me. 

Intertwining faint as a whisper from a heavenly shrine, Lulling to quietness my soul as a fountain in the sunshine;

Then stirring by the south-wind's soft breezes from inaction A girl of gold-brown tresses, stooping to catch her reflection- 

Beheld the book, "So Red the Rose," lying 'mong grasses there,

Unopened—beside her-reflected, too, nestled like a rose fair. 

Thus my ardent is quieted as I watch the brook's gentle flow O'er moss-bedded pebbles-through the meadow where zephyrs blow, And butterflies are happily dancing, like golden rays, about

O'er summer's flowers that grow for them-without a doubt. 

Fain would I, too, like the butterflies, stay in the meadow hushes,

To tenderly caress-with the breeze-the tall slender rushes; 180

Or sit on yon heavenly cloud so gold-fleeced and warm. 

I muse: "Should I ever become a dryad and change form, I'd choose to be tranformed into a soft young sapling birch,

To grow into a hunter's shade and in storms to sway and lurch.

Surviving it all for centuries ere the sap ceased to flow, In autumn the red and gold leaves fall, to flutter to and fro, And winter would come at last to watch an aged tree die:

That would flash up my spirit-bright and free In a flaming fiery-chariot so fleet-and lighting all the sky-

Luck and Alaska

By RALPH A. SELLE 

Alaska is the tourists' paradise. The last of the great open spaces, there are hills to climb, mountain ranges to explore, or you can sail over blue fiords between green-feathered hills and look into the skyline of snowcaps and hanging glaciers. There is romance history to read in totem poles and thrilling adventures of the "gold rush," all set in a green land where the northern lights spread over midnight skies.

The sketches brought together in this volume are compilations and adaptations from much material that has been published from time to time during the last ten years in the Houston Chronicle.

They are the impressions of a visitor who has made six trips to this, the newest and oldest, the grandest and most overwhelming, the strangest and the most whimsical region that is now reached with modern conveniences.
