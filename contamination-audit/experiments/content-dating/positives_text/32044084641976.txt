GETTING MORE OUT OF LIFE 

IFE IS not the same to all of us, even if every one had an equal number of years to live. To some it is filled with handicaps from the start; to others it is a dull, mediocre routine. There are the idlers and "mooners" for whom it is a dream. Only a few regard life as a gift to be enjoyed.

Thomas Huxley, the great biologist, felt like saying at the end of every day "thank you" to some Power he scarcely believed in, for he was an agnostic. But most of us have something to complain of; some feel that nature has cheated or wronged them; and some allow themselves to fall into such depression that they imagine themselves tired of living. And yet there is truth in that oft-repeated saying, "Life is what you make it."

We sometimes think of life in terms of years, but how many live beyond their three score and ten without accomplishing anything more than merely existing!

There have been others, again, who in a short life have traversed centuries; who have produced masterpieces which are and will be the delight of millions for centuries to come; who have packed into a span of 30 years or so the experiences of ages. We lament the shortlived Joyce Kilmer, Edgar Allan Poe, Percy Bysshe Shelley, John

Keats, Franz Schubert, and others whose life work has been cut short, but they may have had the advantage over those of us who are muddling through somehow, and have nothing to show for our myriads of cares, sorrows, vexations, disappointments, and disillusionments.

Different people have different notions of what life is and what it should mean. To a man like Carlyle, achievement through work was everything. The majority of men would prefer to "take it easy." 

The average Frenchman views the bustling American with an air of amusement. The American looks on the Latin races in general as living for pleasure only. These two eternal viewpoints have been vividly described by Tennyson in "The Lotos-Eaters." But it is not necessary to take a definite stand. Moderation, as in everything else, should act as a safety valve to keep us from exaggerating an impulse to overwork, or to take life too easy. 

One of the questions which will come up at the outset is this: Since we differ so much in the number and kind of our qualities, is it not almost impossible for every one to have an equal chance? For while

there are persons who have been what we may call well born, the vast majority consider themselves devoid of possessions or ability or personality. One is reminded of the passage in the Scriptures which reads, "For he that hath, to him shall be given," a statement which at first thought seems tinged with injustice. When we consider, however, that we can manage to have what we have not been given in the first place, the apparent unfairness is reduced. 

Of course, you will immediately want to know how you can manage to get what you have not been given. There are three channelscharacter, intelligence, and personal charm-through which you may work to get out of life what you have not been given. Which of these three channels is the most important is a matter of opinion; but I am probably playing safe when I say that character is not the least important of the three.

GETTING MORE OUT OF LIFE THROUGH CHARACTER 

OST people who have a wide experience of life know that the qualities of courage, faith and optimism play an enormous part in building up a happy and successful life. 

The man who most fires our imagination and arouses our admiration is the hero who is unmindful of hardships and consequences. 

Courage is a quality to be displayed not only on the battlefield but also in the bivouac of life. It has been truly said that "the coward dies a hundred deaths; the brave man dies but one." 

Although there are men and women born with mettle and grit, the vast majority of us must, as the phrase aptly goes, summon our courage. We all have a modicum of it, and we must take a determined, not stubborn, stand when occasion requires it. The difference between the timid and the dauntless is often no more than a difference of will, of tensing the muscles, of gritting the teeth and, more than anything else, of doing the thing in accord with a cherished principle in the face of threats or even violence. 

Courage and Optimism 

One aspect of courage is faith-to believe that the good and the true will triumph. The cool, constructive observer will not allow himself to become either an incorrigible optimist or a down-in-themouth pessimist. But of the two extremes, optimism is preferable because it leads to action, cheers others as well as ourselves, and thus makes life worth living. If a situation is hopelessly bad, there is no sense in carrying on unless we believe that there will be a change for the better. All our efforts will be labored; our efficiency will be reduced; we shall have a feeling and expression of helplessness. 

In every vocation the moderate optimist has the advantage over the pessimist. To a certain extent a physician's "bedside attitude" which has been commented on so much, is merely a question of temperament. A doctor who has a "never-say-die" attitude will bestir himself, and in the end may cure what has been considered a "hopeless case." Imagine the outcome if a physician glanced at his patient, recalled a similar case which proved fatal, and did not even go through the motions of cheering the sick man.

But the people who help to steer the world are the ones who "hope for the best and prepare for the worst." We can not do more; and in order to get the most out of life, we must first of all be convinced that there is something that each one of us, no matter how humble, can contribute to existence. Self-Confidence

Confidence in yourself is closely related to courage and optimism. 

The victorious attitude, provided it is not carried to the point of obnoxious vanity, is a strong-armed ally; self-distrust and self-consciousness are enemies. Nevertheless, sufferers from self-consciousness must be greatly sympathized with. They usually depreciate their

own worth or else exaggerate the good qualities of others. The great have not been immune to this handicap; and many an ambitious person has missed his opportunities because he could not bring himself to meet the right person or say what he would like to say. The restraining hand of his complex, if a metaphor of this sort may be used, is always keeping him from taking a step forward. 

The self-conscious person should keep his mind fixed on his goal and not concern himself about what people will think or say about him. After forcing himself to take the initial step, he is often relieved to find that there is no hardship involved. The foreman turns out to be human when such a person makes an application for a job in person. The physician receives him with a smile. Even the lawyer who is feared puts him at ease the moment he enters the office.

Prophets, agitators of all kinds, reformers, politicians and promoters, in short, all those who are intensely interested in the measures they are proposing are never troubled by self-consciousness. If you have no faith in yourself, surely you can not expect others to believe in you. Act as if you had no doubt about your success and others will have confidence in you. Adopt a positive rather than a negative attitude. When you are trying to put your ideas or suggestions across, say to yourself: "Those people to whom I am talking must accept my suggestion"-not: "I wonder how they are taking my talk."

It was this positive attitude which carried one author through his first talk before the microphone, although he did not have a single word written down. Perhaps hundreds of thousands were listening in, but that idea did not occupy his mind. His message was uppermost. Like him, take a decided stand, speak as if what you say is important (whether it is or not), instead of mumbling it, and, above all, work up faith in your message or work. 

Now let us examine the relationship between self-confidence and success. As we walk into a hotel lobby, we notice that some people are active, radiant with enthusiasm, alive with interest; others are slouching; they walk about aimlessly or lean against a wall or table. 

There is an air of inertia about them. If they are women, they typify the "wallflower" who shrinks at the thought of speaking out. 

Variations in Self-Confidence

The difference between the two types is often nothing but a difference in self-confidence. Those who believe in their ability, who feel there is something to be done immediately energize their motor mechanism and are on the way to accomplishment. But the people who lack faith in themselves, no matter how many worth while ideas may occur to them, are beaten from the very start. Their ideas stick as if in a turnstile, with nothing to push them forward. They lack initiative. There can be no question of enterprise because they meet every opportunity with the attitude of "O, what's the good? 

I couldn't possibly do that!"

The moving force behind every great invention, every outstanding enterprise, every shining feat, was the belief that the thing could be accomplished. When General Goethals took in hand the business of finishing the Panama Canal after the work had been disorganized

because of well-nigh insuperable difficulties, he knew that once the forces could be unified, the Canal could be realized. 

Similarly, when Charles Lindbergh conceived the idea of spanning the Atlantic in a solo flight, he did not allow the offensive epithet "Flying Fool," with which his associates dubbed him, to stand in his way. FIELD-MARSHALL FOCH 

"When one knows what one wills, everything becomes easy," said the Commander-in-Chief of the Allies in the World War. Even a person of ordinary capacity, he declared, if single-minded in purpose will reach his goal. Foch lived his creed: sustained effort; neither success nor failure an occasion for pause that he believed in his powers; the others were not so sure of theirs. 

In the World War, it took a man like General Foch to subdue the German Army, which was thought even by some of the Allied Forces to be invincible. Foch had before him a maxim which influenced all

his acts: "The enemy could be beaten." He was confident of this, first and last, and he was certain, too, that as Commander-in-Chief of all the Allied Forces he must prove the truth of the maxim. 

Whether you are a salesman or a physician, a politician or a clergyman, a business girl or a housewife, self-confidence is a spur to action and therefore to success; for without action there can be no success. Self-confidence of the right sort is not only likely to lead to accomplishment but the attitude which goes with self-confidence is bound to make a better impression on others. 

Let us, for example, suppose an employer assigns a difficult task to an employee, who receives the instructions cheerfully and understandingly and indicates that he is equal to the task. His superior will feel pleased at the prospect of having the work done successfully, and will be well disposed because the assignment has not brought any unpleasant reactions. What would his attitude be if the employee knitted his brow, offered objections and finally blurted out, "I can't do anything like that. It's beyond me"? The relationship between the two would inevitably become strained; the employer would doubtless lose confidence in the employee and offer the opportunity to some one else. 

Advantages of Self-Confidence

Now I do not say that self-confidence always spells success. We have all seen people whose self-assurance was so far ahead of their capacity that they started things they should never have tackled and came to grief because of their too extensive operations. They are the ones who should remember the proverb: "Little boats must keep near the shore." These people are not to be blamed for their selfconfidence, but for their lack of judgment or insufficient experience. 

Their self-confidence is still a great advantage to them; in fact, they are better off in general than those who have greater ability but no belief in themselves. A motor car with a good engine and plenty of gasoline, is less useful if it can not be started, than one which is ill-equipped but which can be made to run. 

There is another advantage in a reservoir of self-confidence; that is the feeling of elation which goes with it, the buoyancy which is experienced every time you conceive a plan that you can carry out as contrasted with the feeling of frustration that seizes a person whose self-confidence is low. 10

The most encouraging fact in this connection is that, of all the qualities necessary for success, self-confidence is the easiest to build up. After you have mastered the essentials of whatever it is you are attempting to do, ask yourself why you can not do it as well as the other fellow, and perhaps a little better.

How to Build Self-Confidence 

Perhaps you would like to open a tea room. Naturally, you must have some capital and know something about this type of business, otherwise you would have chosen some other type of enterprise. 

Now, answer the question: "What cause is there for supposing that

I will not succeed as well as the many thousands who are conducting profitable tea rooms all over the country?"

Or, you decide to run for political office. The probability is that you enjoy and have an aptitude for holding office. "Ah," you say, "but what chance have I against Mrs. So-and-So?" You begin listing all her assets-she has many friends, she is influential, and so forth. But you, too, can make friends; and you may find that you have made fewer enemies than she, that you have other qualities which stand you in good stead. And as you survey all the factors, your self-confidence grows, and, like yeast, it leavens your plans and activates your purpose. Sometimes, in occasions of emergency, a stimulant, if it is only a cup of strong coffee, may do the trick of raising your spirits.

Picture to yourself the person who, urged to undertake a task, keeps whining all the time, “I can't," or "I don't feel strong enough,” or "That's not the job for me." No wonder so many who come in contact with such a person refer to him as "a pain in the neck." He not only displays a poor spirit, but tends to rob you of your own selfconfidence. Such a person becomes known as a "wet blanket," "joy killer," "calamity howler," and his society is not sought after. 

If Columbus had lacked self-confidence, America would not have been discovered in 1492. If Henry Ford had not kept alive his faith in himself while tinkering with what others would call "funny contraptions," the automobile industry today would have been a "horse of a different color." Your self-assurance need not be of the boundless variety, but it should be large enough to begin a given enterprise, and then, as you begin to see light, even if it comes in only through crevices, confidence is bound to grow until you achieve your goal.

Persistence and Patience

One of the greatest mistakes that ordinary people make is to believe that success comes all at once; that a noted author who may have become "famous over night" actually started to write only a few months before; that business men just get a lucky break and climb the ladder of fortune; that scientists, painters, inventors are simply born gifted and something in their brain clicks, and that is all there is to it. 

Little do they realize that the saying, "A tree is not felled with one blow," applies just as much to the genius as to the backwoodsman. In fact, it is the genius himself who discounts his wonderful endowment and ascribes his achievement to his persistent plodding. 

Genius has been defined as "an infinite capacity for taking pains"; and against the view that genius is "inspiration," it has been contended that it is "inspiration plus perspiration."

We need only look into the lives of notables to discover that persistence is one of their chief characteristics. It was not their specialized brain or their gifted mind that separated them from others so much as their perseverance, and sometimes doggedness, in the face of trying misfortunes, even of the loss of years of labor through an accident. 12

It is not necessary to leave our own generation to cite examples. 

Fannie Hurst is rated as one of America's most successful authors.

Few people know, however, that before she sold her first story she had collected a vast number of rejection slips. Many beginners would be discouraged. "Surely," they would argue, "all those editors can't be wrong." Not so a person who is determined in her self-confidence. Kathleen Norris's experiences are similar, and only recently has it been divulged that Montgomery's Anne of Green Gables, which has had a sale of more than 500,000 copies, was rejected by six publishers before some one saw the possibilities in the manuscript. 

How many potential writers are there among us who are too sensitive, too vain, and so feel disinclined to take their pen in hand again after having had their stories or poems returned! But those who persist in their endeavors frequently rise high enough to sell the story which tells about their one-time rejection slips. 

Not only writers, but singers, dancers, and actors can give us a similar tale of persistence in their work. The greatest tenor of the century, Enrico Caruso, was only a stable boy when his voice attracted attention. It was not only necessary to cultivate his voice, but before he could appear on the opera stage he had to learn the complicated technique of singing, to master the languages of the operas, to study the art of acting, and last but not least, to memorize the parts of the operas. What a prodigious performance for a stable boy! Yet he climbed to the heights of fame.

Mastering Our Emotions 

If we want to get more out of life, we must guard ourselves against becoming a prey to our emotions. Emotion in small doses is good for us. Without fear, anger, love, and even a certain amount of sadness, not to mention joy, our world would hardly be worth living in. Emotions are a boon and a bane to mankind. They leave an indelible impress on the face; and we have but to recall disagreeable faces to confirm this fact. A scowling face repels because it indicates a past of contention and harshness and a future likely to abound in the same. The human face is a mirror reflecting hate, jealousy, scorn, suspicion, and grief, and such reflections are disturbing.

But a person with an amiable, cheerful nature and a winning smile is likely to keep his head in an emergency. His personality attracts 13

because he puts us at ease; we feel that he will not irritate others by making sarcastic remarks. In other words, an amiable disposition soothes the emotions; and the more emotional we are, the more we appreciate the company of agreeable, well-balanced people. 

Indeed, emotional people, as we all know, have a habit of infecting others with their emotion. Nothing is more contagious than fear and more than once a panic has been started by the ill-grounded fright of a single person. Likewise, a surly, sulky person may sour a whole company. Who has not seen faces drop at the approach of such a person? A genial, humorous person, on the other hand, will sometimes start people smiling or laughing the moment he appears.

It is difficult to say whether emotional or unemotional people get more out of life. The unemotional do not get sufficiently excited about anything to live with great zest. Their advantage is that they do not wear themselves out, that they can think clearly and still feel fresh when their emotional competitors have drained their energy. 

Effects of Anger 

Let us take instances from every-day life. To show anger when thwarted or crossed is a human quality, particularly when we are certain our position is the right one. We raise our voice, assume a threatening air, say things for which, perhaps, we are sorry afterwards. Does our anger impress any one? Hardly ever, unless the person at whom it is directed is afraid about his job. No wonder the 

French have a saying: "He is angry, then he must be wrong.' 99 It is curious that anger is displayed less by successful people than the unsuccessful, although the former could better afford it. John D. 

Rockefeller is said never to have allowed himself to become perturbed. If his interlocutor became enraged, he would merely smile. 

A smile of this sort is a weapon more effective than shouting or pounding one's fist. It requires self-control, but once that is gained, a beaming attitude serves as a tonic. If a President of the United 

States as, for example, Grover Cleveland, could listen to insults and abuses without becoming excited, why can not the average man or woman temper feeling with reason and indignation with sympathy? 

If we realize what anger, when allowed to become a frequent or habitual emotion, can do to our digestive organs, our nervous system, our facial expression, we shall go a long way toward conquering it.

Exercising Self-Control 

Anger has never convinced anybody, has never made any converts, has never done anything constructive. It leads to strife and destruction; and it may be concluded with certainty that we can get far more out of life when exercising self-control than when succumbing to rage. Indignation or resentment may have its use when a social wrong has been done; but when the personal note is sounded, beware of the smoking ego which is at the bottom of it all, for it is liable to consume itself in senseless words and objectionable acts, if not in actual violence ending in tragedy. 

In a dispute, a good method of controlling your emotions is to imagine that you are representing some one other than yourself. Your attitude then becomes more moderate and the words allowed to pass 15

your lips sound a good deal milder both to the interested person and outsiders. In this respect, we may learn much from diplomacy. 

Diplomats, even when their countries are on the verge of war, couch their words in such language as to keep their real feelings in the background, holding them in leash to a surprising degree. 

Fear and Worry

Another great handicap that besets most of us is the emotion of fear with its offshoot, worry. Quite aside from the scars with which fear seams the face and the hunted look that characterizes the chronically frightened person, energy is sapped and all action is inhibited or stalled. Our greatest fears are often of imaginary things. 

I know families where a telegram is received with trembling hands. 

This means that a prospective visitor may not announce his coming except by letter. Again, if a member of the family wishes to telephone that he will be delayed, he can not make such a call late at night, or the rest of the family will suppose that the call is to announce that something terrible had happened. Think of what these benighted families endure. They call it "devotion," but it is simply emotion.

Every spell of worry takes something out of life. We have quite a few proverbs relating to unnecessary worries, such as "Don't cross your bridges until you come to them," "Don't trouble trouble until trouble troubles you." But these proverbs fail to make an impression when a person's particular complex is touched off. Indeed, there are many thousands who work themselves into a state of frenzy about something which, if it did happen, could not be any worse than the worrying frame of mind that sometimes leads to a nervous breakdown. It has been often repeated that we worry usually about events that will not happen, or, if they do, worrying can not help. 

Anxiety or Mental Suspense

Probably the greatest destroyer of mental equilibrium and of physical well-being is anxiety or mental suspense. Some would welcome the worst and thus get adjusted to its conditions, rather than keep turning over in their mind for the thousandth time the possibility of such-and-such a misfortune. Anxiety is worry, but in a more acute form. 16

The more responsible and serious-minded a person is, the greater will be his anxieties. Also, the more entanglements people have, the more anxieties will result from them. One bit of advice which may be pondered is this: If the game is not worth the candle, remove the entanglements which cause anxiety. It must be remembered, however, that excitement of some sort is indispensable to a certain type of person; and he is willing to endure anxiety, worry, and suspense for the sake of his quest. One may look on the tendency to worry about a mishap as a form of self-torture or penance. The problem is how that disposition may be modified so that a person may become inured to a situation and say, "Come what may, I will be a sport and face the music." Once this stand is taken, much of the anxiety vanishes; and this determined attitude gives one the courage to withstand future onsets of anxiety. 

To Get, One Must Give

One of the commonest mistakes is to suppose that in order to get the most out of life, we must make a grand scramble and walk over everybody. Examples are cited of financiers, "robber barons," and politicians who grabbed everything and enjoyed themselves. 

The truth is that we do not know how much they enjoyed themselves. Some of them were desolate figures. But it must be borne in mind, too, that "getters" and "go-getters" are also givers on a large scale. There is scarcely a magnate of note who has not endowed some institution or contributed to a worthy cause. 

To be of some service to others, whether to comfort them in their trouble or to help them in an emergency, is not only a matter of giving but also of getting. The happiest people have been those who were able to be of assistance to others for they receive the fruits of their blessing while alive. Aristotle, probably the greatest mind of antiquity, made happiness or well-being the foundation of all ethics. 

That was the "highest good" to him as to many other philosophers. 

And what did he decide was the greatest virtue? Not piety, or honesty, or temperance. Just magnanimity—in a simpler word, bigheartedness.

I have never seen a big-hearted person unhappy. Though he has been disillusioned, though he has been the victim of his own generosity, he still gets more out of life than the crabbed miser. 

The Biblical injunction "Cast thy bread upon the waters" still 17

holds good, perhaps more so in an age which has become too practical to insure security. The shrewd, self-seeking person develops a mien of rapaciousness which somewhat resembles that of a hawk, for a hard face is often the reward of egoism. People may smile at him because of his influence or wealth, but let the first sign of adversity appear, and he is shunned, if not scorned,

An epitaph on a tombstone in England eloquently describes this state of mind. It reads: 

What we gave, we have; 

What we spent, we had; 

What we left, we lost.

At first sight, this seems like a riddle; in reality it is a profound truth. The couple lying beneath the tombstone that bears this inscription certainly had money to spend. What they gave is still theirs in terms of a memorial or prestige, and, according to the belief of many people, in terms of due reward in the hereafter. What they left, however, if it does not go to some good cause, is actually a loss to them.

GETTING MORE OUT OF LIFE THROUGH INTELLIGENCE 

N intelligent person makes allowance for his own personal bias. He is able to separate the essential from the inessential, to go direct to the bottom of an issue and remove the dross or chaff. 

In our voyage through life, we are constantly beset by reefs, icebergs, storms, fogs, as well as by will-o'-the-wisps. In order to steer our ship safely through turbulent waters we must use a compass and consult charts. Unfortunate the pilot-still more his passengers-who is not equipped with adequate instruments or who does not use them. 

To be intelligent is to be alert, to ask yourself questions, to seek devices for self-improvement. Intelligence is supposed to be constant. 

In other words, you are born intelligent or not, but it is true, nevertheless, that you can do more with whatever amount of intelligence you happen to have.

Adaptability to People 

Most American psychologists define intelligence in terms of adaptability. Paradoxical as it sounds, the strong are weak in refusing to make concessions, particularly when the concessions do not involve an important principle. Popular lore has made much of this quality, and such phrases as "stoops to conquer" or "oaks fall when reeds brave the storm" tend to bring out the desirability of yielding instead of being stiff-necked.

In some respects the lower animals are better off than man for through instinct they adjust themselves to circumstances. Man often misuses reason to resist conditions and to antagonize people by insisting on a point, no matter how trivial it might be. 

I do not mean that one should be a "yes" man, but when we can easily agree with our interlocutor, we might as well do so and do so graciously. Some will draw out a reluctant "y-e-e-s,” puckering and twisting their lips all the while as if it were most difficult for them to make the concession. In reality they do not like to admit that some one has said anything which they hadn't thought of first -a reflection of an inferiority complex.

The experienced salesman will naturally fall in line with the topic of conversation of the prospective buyer, will be inclined to take a favorable attitude toward the opinions of his principal, and will 19

think twice before crossing a customer. A girl who is adaptable will win a wide circle of friends and gain popularity. On the other hand, a girl who is disposed to disagree with her companions will soon discover that acquaintanceship will never ripen into anything more. 

Young men who are greeted with "I don't believe it" or "That's as clear as mud," or some similar remark are not anxious to cultivate the friendship of the utterers of such remarks. Their reaction is that a person who is uncompromising to begin with will not only continue in this attitude, but will grow worse with age. 

Leaders in society, in politics, in business have always been quick to make compromises when necessary. Samuel Gompers, the immigrant who built up the powerful army of workers called the American Federation of Labor, ruled more by virtue of the kid glove than of the iron hand. Benjamin Franklin learned early in life that he could not put forth his views in an aggressive way, and that, as in the fable of the sun and the wind, he could gain his point by indirection rather than by emphatic assertion. Perhaps more than any of his contemporaries, Andrew Carnegie knew how to catch flies with honey. Even as a youngster he was able to exploit some of his schoolmates by praising their efforts in collecting things for him. 

The very man who gave us the one-price system, the most inflexible principle of merchandising-John Wanamaker-was very pliable in dealing with his employees. He showed a friendly spirit to all; and in this regard he was not alone among merchant princes.

Adaptability to Conditions

Adaptability to circumstances is of a somewhat different brand; for there are those who can well adapt themselves to people without being able to adjust themselves to conditions. They pine for what they can not get; they chafe about the weather, the political situation, and almost everything they happen to think of. They become known as cranks and kickers. Eventually they even become dissatisfied with themselves for a conflict arises in them every time an issue comes up. 

They take refuge in day dreams, develop eccentricities, and become neurotic. Wherever they go and whatever activity they take part in, there is likely to be a shadow of maladjustment. If they could only learn to submit to situations which can not be remedied, their whole outlook would change; and they would enjoy many experiences which in their state of mind seem ordeals. 

The secret of adaptability lies in making the best of a situation. 

You feel bored with some one's company-very well; but you will not become less bored if you keep thinking how bored you are. In 21

deed, the trouble is only magnified, so the best step is to show a sympathetic and friendly attitude to the bore, and in the long run you may find that you have gained not only peace of mind but, perhaps, some valuable information.

How Intelligence Can Be Used 

An intelligent person will not despise criticism, but will take it even from an enemy. The main thing is to learn from experience, to analyze our mistakes, and to say, "This shall not happen again." If you can not do this, you are not intelligent. Perhaps the commonest instances of failure to learn by experience are those countless cases in which people will not, or can not, live within their means, no matter how often they have already been brought to disaster and humiliation by their extravagance. No doubt vanity and self-indulgence play their part in the love of ease and display of such people; but the fact remains that by permitting recurrences of financial disaster they have proved themselves lacking in intelligence.

There are various kinds of intelligence. A man may be a social boor, and yet in his business relationships play a shrewd game, outwitting, say, a famous scientist. There are learned men who can not grasp a simple mechanical principle in machinery and are helpless with their cars. Again there is the fine social hostess who is muddled when a political, economic, or philosophical issue is discussed. 

The highest degree of general intelligence is not to be found in the genius, for he has a highly specialized type of mind and possesses originality enough to lead mankind in some special direction. Men of that capacity are scarce in every generation, but we expect general intelligence in the average man and woman. 

Study the lives of those who have made good. At first they did not seem any different from the general run of people. Multimillionaires, captains of industry, executives of national fame, such as Andrew 

Carnegie, Henry Ford, John D. Rockefeller, Sr., started out as office boys, clerks, apprentices, or like Benjamin Franklin, as a printer's devil. But it was not long before they were promoted and advanced by leaps and bounds for there is always room for one more near the top.

They made it their business to suggest something new, something profitable. They were never content with routine, with the usual. To them an answer like "We have always done it that way" was not 22

satisfying. Intelligence of this practical variety calls for imagination, by which we can project ourselves into the future and inject ourselves into different situations, understanding the needs of others, their likes and dislikes.

Enemies of Intelligence 

What acts as a stumbling block to intelligence is personal feeling, vanity, emotion. The ancient fable of the crow and the fox need not be repeated here. If we can attain that state of mind which renders us dispassionate, impersonal, and objective when some persons would draw a hard line between themselves and others, considering first and last whose "ox is gored," we have already established our title to intelligence.

Let us ask ourselves why it is that so many otherwise intelligent persons make fools of themselves when they are putting forth something on their own behalf, and we shall perforce come to the conclusion that they have not succeeded in separating the beloved ego from the vital issue. I have in mind a speaker who once addressed an audience which was too conservative to applaud heartily the witty remarks in his lecture. He was evidently annoyed and reproached his listeners for their apathy.

People become jealous, suspicious, quarrelsome, because they themselves are involved. If the question were to touch some one not related to them, they could see things straight; in other words, come to a just and reasonable understanding. 

It requires a sense of proportion and perspective to place ourselves in relation to others "to see ourselves as others see us." We may be highly intelligent, even intellectual, and miss out on this point. 

What we lack in this case is a sense of humor, to which we shall later refer.

Intellectual Pursuits 

No matter how comfortable we are or how many our accomplishments, unless we possess a certain amount of education and a cultural background, we are living on a low plane. Until we have some method of interpreting what we see, hear, or read, we are more or less blind; for the same experiences do not mean the same thing to everybody. 23

No wonder that the title of one of the most widely read books in the English language is "Pleasures of Life" by Sir John Lubbock, later Lord Avebury, and the pleasures he refers to are poetry, memory, art, and so forth. It is not always the college man or woman who has the most enlightened viewpoint or the best understanding of a good book or play. We can all taste of the higher pleasures of life.

To be cultivated is to enjoy that which is enjoyed by the select men and women in every age and in every country. They belong to a minority, but it is a minority of quality. When you read Shakespeare, Goethe, Balzac, Anatole France or Tolstoy you are certain that they will not be outmoded; that their writing is universal and ageless; that when you understand them you have earned a decoration, an intellectual order of merit, and have peered into a vault that only the élite are privileged to see. Besides, you are building up an independent judgment instead of looking on dumbly and letting other people impose their opinions on you.

Americans read a great deal. Millions of magazines are sold every month, but for every magazine of quality that is bought, perhaps a hundred so-called thrillers are purchased. There is nothing in them which makes us reflect, nothing to round out our personality, nothing to make us feel that we are human. On the contrary, they are calculated to stir our baser or more infantile emotions, to excite us without a purpose.

Selecting Books 

It is the same with books. The books which are best sellers are not always the most enduring; and after all, it is the most lasting which we ought to seek for only then do we know what has value. 

Time is the judge. There are book lists containing the titles of the most useful volumes or those which every cultivated person should read. In the matter of new books it is not so easy to tell. We can not always go by what reviewers think. Sometimes they are prejudiced; sometimes they are mistaken.

In selecting our books, let us keep the following rules in mind, and it will not be long before we shall be able to judge discriminately for ourselves. 

Beware of books that have a too-promising title like "How to 

Grow Rich," "How to Live 150 Years." 24

Don't accept every conclusion of your author. Examine his logic and his fairness to opponents. Don't accept the opinion of only one reviewer. Remember, too, that best sellers are not always the best books.

A book should be well written but, above all, it should be a faithful guide. Don't read third-hand presentations. Popularizers are good only if they are masters of their subject. You are safer with a dull book written by an expert than with a clever piece of claptrap. 

The slogan, "Before you invest, investigate," holds true of books as well as of stocks and bonds, for in reading a poor book you are wasting time, sometimes money too, but above all, acquiring misinformation.

Artistic Pursuits 

There are people who derive their greatest pleasure from the contemplation of artistic productions. The common folk can not understand how a painting by Raphael or Rembrandt can make their appreciators happy, but it is nevertheless true that when we are troubled, there is nothing that makes us gain our serenity so much as looking at worth-while paintings or listening to good music. The very essence of art is to lift us above the ordinary, to see or hear the unusual-what some would call the Divine. 

I can say emphatically that whoever has not taken an interest in art has not lived. He may be quite satisfied with himself and laugh at the cultivated, but John Stuart Mill, the English philosopher, has said, "I'd rather be Socrates dissatisfied than a pig satisfied," and this dictum has become the slogan of the intellectural aristocracy. 

We have all heard the expression, "He is losing himself in art." 

Those who lose themselves in that fashion are really finding themselves. Indeed, I am ready to accept the seemingly contradictory statement that only those who lose themselves in art have found life. 

What a world is revealed in every phase of art; architecture, sculpture, painting, dancing, acting, drama, and music!

Imagine what the world must be like to a cat or a dog. The ignorant and uncultivated are not much better off. They are cut off from the great monuments of the mind, the mighty and tender reverberations of the heart. Not only can they not produce, but they can not even appreciate. They, too, can say that pictures appeal to them, but what kind of pictures? Music has a fascination for them, but what kind of music? 25

Lately the radio has offered us the best in music-symphonic concerts and opera of a superb order. Thousands still ignore these opportunities, and instead tune in on a cheap imitation of good music. These listeners can just as well learn to like the great compositions, but they do not give themselves time. They fancy that unless the music can be understood immediately, it can not be pleasing. The truth is that after several repetitions, symphonies and concertos, the operas and oratorios will begin to have an effect on even the average listener; in fact, the full meaning of the music will be understood only after half a dozen hearings.

It is the same with movies. It is better to select our sound films with care than to decide that since we haven't much to do "we might as well take in a movie." One good performance is worth ten poor ones; and it is for us to gain the key of discernment. 

We must learn to live with concentration in order to get more out of our time. At first, it will be rather trying to change our routine of life, but once we have formed the habit, we shall never want to go back to lower types of amusement. 

A Sense of Humor 

If we take stock of our likes and dislikes in personal contacts, we shall probably find that those we like best have a sense of humor. 

Our acquaintances and friends may be artists, authors, professors, and scientists but if they are dull, their company will not be relished except for a short time.

That there is such a thing as a sense of humor, no one will deny. 

The difficulty comes when we try to define it. Some people associate a sense of humor with laughter, measuring it apparently by the extent of a guffaw. Children see humor only in the gyrations and contortions of a clown. Adults with juvenile minds laugh boisterously at the crudest jokes. Some salesmen's yarns are well known as a type of humor that is appreciated by a "Babbitt" only. Is the sense of humor, then, anything which will arouse the risibilities of the largest number of people?

There is undoubtedly such a view, perhaps in keeping with the philosophical doctrine in ethics that the highest good is the greatest happiness of the greatest number. But a sense of humor is not to be gauged by the intensity or frequency of laughter. In fact, from observation we might come to the conclusion that he who laughs 26

loudest and oftenest has a sense of humor not unlike that of the laughing hyena or braying ass.

A sense of humor is that quality which enables us to perceive a possible incongruity in situations, even when we ourselves are involved. Intelligence is a factor of this quality but only one factor; objectivity—that is, a completely dispassionate, disinterested, and impersonal outlook-is another important factor. A person who is subjective-that is, self-centered, personal in outlook, always viewing things from his angle alone-can not be said to possess a keen sense of humor.

The immortal author of "Huckleberry Finn" and "Innocents

Abroad" had a keen sense of the ludicrous-at home and abroad. Objective, impersonal, decidedly American in flavor, his is our native humor at its best. Born Samuel Clemens, he chose Mark Twain, a term used on his beloved Mississippi, for his pen name

Detachment is as necessary here as in art. The content is different and the approach is different, but there is a resemblance between the artistic and the humorous in that the personal element must be first eliminated before the object can be enjoyed. If a man can not laugh at himself, and occasionally, see the ridiculous in his own actions, he is unfortunate. 

Elasticity of Mind

Elasticity of mind is another important ingredient of a sense of humor. The too-concentrated person, the zealous reformer or uplifter misses that quality, and on that account often becomes a bore. 27

A Mental resiliency is the very warp and woof of a sense of humor. person whose ideas are narrow or run in grooves, even though he be a genius, can not hope to attain that quality which is called brilliancy or which is better expressed by the French word esprit. What this mental resiliency, or springiness, amounts to is this: The mind must be set so as to perceive resemblances, differences, and possible comparison; that is, we must have a ready flow of mental associations that would appeal as associations to other people also. 

We must realize that the world as an object of humor is full of possibilities that are waiting for some one to bring them out. The person lacking a sense of humor always says, "I can't see anything funny about that," particularly if the reference is to one of his own foibles or some cause in which he is personally interested. As a matter of fact, there is nothing in existence which has not its humorous side.

Comedy is often so intertwined with tragedy that one feels like weeping and laughing all at once. One of the finest witticisms ever uttered is the epigram attributed to Horace Walpole that the "world is a comedy to those who think and a tragedy to those who feel.” 

Since the most intelligent people both think and feel, they can not fail to appreciate the comedy and to experience the tragedy. 

Shrewdness Is Self-Protection 

Of all the types of intelligence, shrewdness is the least admired or appreciated, for a shrewd person is always looking for his personal advantage and does not care about the social benefit of an issue or transaction. Shrewdness, like every other phase of intelligence, is inborn, and the man who lacks that quality, much as he may be respected and loved by his fellow beings, will simply be out of luck when he is surrounded by those who have it and make the most of it. 

To them he is not an honest, fair-minded, sympathetic soul; he is just a "sucker"; one on whom the shady gentry, whose motto is "Never give a sucker an even break," thrives.

It is tragic that the ones who should be treated most considerately on account of their noble qualities are so often harassed and exploited. The lot of the Good Samaritan is not always enviable. It is heartrending to read in the newspapers every now and then of the man who has befriended somebody on the road only to be beaten and robbed by the one he has helped. 28

We are living in a world where it behooves us to protect ourselves if we want to get more out of life. Sometimes we must dodge the glib salesman who tries to sell us oil on the moon or gold in Utopia; at other times, it is our duty to keep from falling a prey to the schemes of relatives, yes, even to the selfish wishes of loved ones. 

How many daughters have been prevented from marriage in order to minister to an invalid mother who could have shifted for herself. 

Wife and daughter often take husband and father to be a cross between a beast of burden and Santa Claus, little caring about the gigantic burdens he is carrying on his shoulders, and of how much they are depriving him. 

Even if shrewdness is an inborn characteristic, there is no doubt we can ward off impositions on our good nature. We may be taken in once or even twice. If we allow ourselves to be deceived again, we are fools, and if a fourth time, we are worthy of the name of "sucker." In that capacity we can not get much out of life. Parasites will live off us; and we shall lose not only the respect of our associates but our own self-respect. 

Frequently, relatives suppose that they are specially privileged in their unreasonable demands, since if their kin did not lavish his bounty on them, he would be taken advantage of by strangers; and they further argue that he should be happy to make his dear ones happy. The probability is that he would be still happier if allowed to enjoy some things for himself and not by proxy. 

Indiscriminate Kindness

An indiscriminately kind person is often unfair, not only to himself but also to others for he gratifies the aggressive at the expense of the reserved and considerate. It would be well for such a person to look into the motives of his petitioners and ask himself whether their demands are justified. A person who believes in the doctrine of "live and let live" will readily do one a good turn, but he must be on his guard against a mass effort on the part of "chiselers," bounders and "besters" to make him an easy mark, even laughing at his stupidity. A defensive shrewdness is something becoming to the intelligent person, and a safety device in our complicated civilization. 29

If it were impossible ever to change one's personality, the situation of many people would indeed be unpleasant and all advice would be useless. I do not mean that we can give ourselves a new personality merely by wishing it; neither can we make the change at will. Importance of the Voice

If any one's face offends our artistic sensibilities, we can keep the situation in hand by averting our eyes from him as much as possible. 

But if any one's voice annoys us in conversation we can not put our fingers in our ears to shut it out. We can get accustomed to homely faces and can even admire certain types of plainness, but it is quite impossible to get accustomed to, or to admire, a voice that resembles something between a crow's call and a foghorn.

The range of qualities and defects in the human voice is wide. 

Think first of the musical tones of a cultivated woman's voice; then compare it with the nasal twang, the metallic tone of hundreds of other women. These contrasts will give you an imaginary picture of a wide difference in personalities. 

Faults That Can Be Corrected

Voice defects can not always be cured, but in a large percentage of cases, improvement is possible. There is no need to speak in tones so loud and piercing that the conversation penetrates the whole room. A nasal tone can be corrected by a better placement of the voice, by keeping the lips rounded and the throat opened, and by bringing the words forward when speaking instead of keeping them in the back of the mouth.

Other speech defects that should be guarded against are the monotonous voice, and too-fast or too-slow speech. Mental stimulation is a help towards correcting the dull, uninteresting sound of the 33

monotonous voice; while fast and slow speech can be modified by paying strict attention to the voice combined with a determination to correct these speech defects. The chief difficulty about improving our voices is that we don't hear ourselves as others hear us; but if, when we hear some particularly pleasing speech, we mentally compare it with our own, we may do something in this direction. But we must not go to the point of deliberately imitating another person's manner of speech, for if we do, the remedy is likely to prove worse than the disease.

Many types of speech impediments will yield to treatment. Stammering has an emotional basis, and, expertly treated, can now be corrected to a large degree. In all cases, treatment (when necessary), a real desire for improvement, and persistence in attempting it, will work wonders. A beautiful voice may be, and usually is, a gift of nature; but the development and management of this gift are a matter of will and intelligence.

Above all, tact calls for the ability to imagine oneself in another's place, a dash of resourcefulness, and a pinch of self-control.

Tact is not, as many suppose, an inexplicable natural gift. It is rather the result of imagination, sympathy, observation, and quickwittedness. The young have to be trained to it; the old have usually learned it in the hard school of experience.

MAKING THE MOST OF OUR OPPORTUNITIES 

S O MUCH has been said about recognizing opportunities that to dwell on the subject becomes tedious. But since there is so much talk about "getting good breaks," it must be said that opportunities are not exactly the same as the "breaks" of popular parlance; that wherever we are, opportunities must in a sense be made; and that the stories about good breaks we hear so much of are often little more than fables.

Although we are living in a world that is not always encouraging, there are still opportunities for every one who is blessed with foresight and will. In fact, so far as the possibilities of self-improvement go, there are many more opportunities today than there ever were. 

With radio, forums, extension classes, libraries, rapid transit, and accelerated communication, we can accomplish ten times as much as the ambitious youth of a century ago who had to read his solitary book by candlelight and trudge miles to go to work or to school. 

Horace's slogan Carpe Diem (seize the day) of nearly 2,000 years ago, while intended to apply to the enjoyments of life, is really even more applicable to the opportunities of today that many of us neither see nor hear. 

In his famous poem "Drop Your Bucket Where You Are," Sam

Walter Foss wrote of the men who were sailing on the fresh waters of the Amazon River, and almost dying of thirst. To those of us who drift into a similar plight, blind to the advantages within our reach, there is a lesson in the stirring lines: 

Seek not for fresher founts afar 

Just drop your bucket where you are! 

Opportunities Disguised As Snags

Most opportunities-and perhaps this is not so well known-come in the form of responsibilities, snags, and difficulties, and we are apt not to recognize them as blessings in disguise. We prefer to follow the philosophy of Omar Khayyam and live joyously from day to day. The opportunity of doing something worth while slips by; and in addition to losing it, we lose also the hold on ourselves; for "nothing succeeds like success," and by that token, nothing fails like failure. It is not true, however, that opportunity knocks but 38

once. It knocks very often. The most unfortunate thing about not grasping an opportunity is the fact that every time we miss it, we become what might be called in medicine, “opportunity-tolerant." In other words, we become blind or deaf to the knocks and the calls of Good Dame Fortune, and so must be prepared to listen to other cues, the hard knocks of life.

On the other hand, the rank opportunist who is always looking to see how the cat jumps must of necessity become unprincipled and lose the esteem of his friends.

Our guide should be intelligence tempered with conscience. Only then will we benefit by the opportunities of life without either disregarding them heedlessly or creating them at the cost of self-respect. 

And when we have done all we could in steering this middle course, we shall, as we grow more mellow, be able to say with Wordsworth: 

Though nothing can bring back the hour 

Of splendor in the grass, of glory in the flower; 

We will grieve not, rather find 

Strength in what remains behind;

In the primal sympathy 

Which, having been, must ever be; 

In the soothing thoughts that spring 

Out of human suffering;

In the faith that looks through death, In years that bring the philosophic mind.
