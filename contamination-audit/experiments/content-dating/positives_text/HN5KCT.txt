Cliff Palace nder the arching roof of a tremendous cave stands a silent, empty city.

For almost seven centuries it has stood there looking out across the canyon toward the setting sun. 

Proudly, almost haughtily, it has resisted the heavy tread of those slow centuries. Like a giant with a shawl of everlasting stone pulled closely about its shoulders it has stood with unbowed head, an eternal monument to the intelligence and industry of its builders.

After Cliff Palace was deserted by the Cliff late in the thirteenth century it stood, Dwellunmo- lested by man, for many hundreds of years. The owls and pack rats took it over and enjoyed its security, but from all evidences it was many centuries before men again entered the cave.

The Cliff Dwellers themselves may have intended to return when conditions became normal again but they never came back. There is no evidence that farming Indians ever lived in the Mesa Verde after its desertion by the ancient people. Other Indians came but they were hunters and they seem to have shunned the silent cave cities.

A couple of centuries after Cliff Palace was deserted an important event took place, an event that was to have a strange effect on it at a later date. 

America was rediscovered!

Fifteen thousand years after the Indians discovered the continent from the west, white men entered it from the east. A new people blundered into the western hemisphere that had so long belonged to the Indians. to The newcomers were a greedy lot and they began stretch acquisitive fingers in all directions.

The first American settlers entered the Mesa Verde region about 1870. Miners, farmers, trappers, cattlemen, even bandits, came pouring into the Mancos Valley and found it to their liking. None of them had ever heard of or would have been interested in the ruins. To them the past was dead and forgotten; they were looking ahead. They were interested only in taming the wilderness and in keeping their scalps firmly attached to their heads.

At that time the entire region was terrorized by the Ute Indians. Naturally a war-like group they were goaded into a frenzy by the loss of their hunting grounds. They made life miserable for the whites. Adventurous miners and trappers were slain; farming settlements lived in constant fear of the merciless warriors. The situation became acute and soldiers were finally sent in to hold the Utes in check.

One of these natural strongholds was the Mesa Verde. Its warm lower canyons had long been the winter home of bands of Utes and they were familiar with every nook and cranny in it. The deep narrow canyons and high mesas offered sanctuary to the oppressed Indians. 

The settlers in the Mancos Valley respected this wilderness stronghold and it remained a place of mystery to them. From the time of Professor Newberry's climb to the summit in 1859, we have no definite record of white men entering the Mesa Verde until 1874.

Today Two-Story House still clings to the face of its cliff. It has changed little since Jackson saw it three-score years ago. Not a dozen men have entered it since that fatal day when the Cliff Dwellers left it behind. 

Long ago the people of Two-Story House were neighbors of the people of Cliff Palace, the great cliff dwelling toward which we are moving. To them it must have been a metropolis, a great city, the largest they ever knew. It took only an hour for them to trot up the canyon to the larger community. Often the men of the little village must have slung their prized possessions over their shoulders and set out for Cliff Palace on trading and gambling expeditions. It was "big town" to them.

When Jackson was at Two-Story House he was very near Cliff Palace but he did not see the larger ruin.

Cliff Palace soon felt the effects of the new order for in the spring of 1909 a long-deferred house cleaning began. Dr. Jesse Walter Fewkes, of the Smithsonian Institution, moved into the cave and began a systematic excavation of the ruin. The fallen stones and adobe, the rat nests and the litter of the centuries were carefully removed. Once again the houses, kivas, passageways and courtyards were clean and neat. 

A small amount of repair work was done to prevent further deterioration. Cracks were filled with adobe mud, weak walls were braced and foundations were strengthened. 

No effort was made to rebuild the fallen parts and to the casual eye Cliff Palace is as it was when the cowboys found it except that the litter has been cleared away. 

As Cliff Palace emerged from its mantle of dust and debris the unusual size of the cave and its ruin became more evident. THE BASKET MAKER PERIOD 

The Basket Makers were the earliest farmers in the Mesa Verde region. Their culture was comparatively simple and was featured by excellent weaving, expecially of baskets and bags. Because of this trait they were called "Basket Makers 11 by the early explorers and the name has stuck.

At the beginning of this period the Basket Makers had an extremely simple culture for they had just turned from hunting to farming. At first they probably farmed only incidentally and regarded it as a sideline. Its possibilities became more and more apparent with each harvest and soon the conditions were reversed. Farming became the main stay and hunting the sideline.

After full realization of the advantages of agriculture came to the people they never returned to a hunting life. Their main dependence was upon their harvests and hunting became secondary in importance.

The lack of the bow and arrow forced the Basket Makers to rely upon an odd weapon, the atlatl, that was as difficult to use as it is to pronounce. 

It was used in all parts of the world before the bow took its place.

The atlatl was a spear thrower that served as a mechanical lengthener for the arm. It was a slender flattened stick about two feet in length. At one end was the grip, equipped with two leather loops for the fingers. At the opposite end, on the upper face, was a short spur or hook, against which the dart or spear rested. 

The dart, either a reed or a slender shaft of wood, was four or five feet long. One end was tipped with a stone point. The other end was feathered and this basal end was cup-shaped to fit against the spur on the atlatl. In reality the dart was merely a very long arrow.

When the hunter was ready to use the atlatl he held it lengthwise in the palm of his hand extending between the first two fingers. These fingers passed through the finger loops, with the other fingers and the thumb curled over the top of the atlatl to give a firm grip. The dart was hooked against the small spur at the rear end of the atlatl and was balanced by the first two fingers of the throwing hand. The arm was drawn back of the shoulder with the atlatl extending out behind horizontally. From this position the arm was thrown sharply forward in a long sweeping arc and the dart was projected from the end of the atlatl. this way the length of the arm was doubled and great force was imparted to the missile. Basket Maker men, while on a trading expedition, may have seen women making vessels out of clay. Realizing the superiority of these vessels that could be placed directly on the fire the men mentioned the matter to their wives upon their return home. Possessed of only the idea the women set about trying to make pottery. 

They were practically forced to invent pottery all over again for they knew nothing about the actual process. They had merely heard that fine utensils could be made out of clay. Their first attempts were extremely clumsy and resulted in absolute failure.

They made the first ones from pure clay and as fast as it dried the vessels cracked and fell into pieces.

After a time the women realized they must add something to the clay, a tempering material. Straw and juniper bark were tried and with a certain amount of success. 

The vessels had less tendency to crack but when they were placed on the fire these materials burned out and the result was more in the nature of a sieve than a pot.

The patient women continued to experiment and at last they learned that some kind of sand or grit was necessary for temper. In several places in the Mesa 

Verde volcanic blowouts provided the potters with an abundance of volcanic grit. This was ground fine and mixed with the pottery clay. Vessels made of this material did not crack upon drying. Many of the first pieces of pottery were merely sun dried but soon the women learned that if they were subjected to intense

CLIFF PALACE 

heat they were much stronger and were perfectly waterproof. Each vessel was carefully baked, or fired, and true pottery was the result.

The effect of pottery on the food habits of the people was profound. With vessels that could be placed directly on the fire whole new lines of food were available. Soups, stews, porridges and greens became commonplace. Many new plants were utilized and innumerable combinations of meats and plants were discovered.

Pottery was of inestimable value to the Indians for water storage. Baskets were not well adapted for this purpose because of their tendency to disintegrate within a comparatively short time because of the moisture. Pottery lasted indefinitely and each woman could make an unlimited number of large water jars. On rainy days every jar in the village was filled with water and the terrors of drouth were lessened. Many of the jars were slightly porous and enough evaporation took place through the walls to keep the water sweet and cool.

Thus it is easily seen that the perfection of pottery was one of the major steps in the progress of the Indians. The diet was improved, the drudgery of cooking was lessened, the range of foods was widened, storage facilities were increased and the entire domestic economy of the Basket Makers took a decided turn for the better.

At first the pottery was crude but each succeeding generation showed improvement in the new craft. 

The proper percentage of temper and clay was discovered, designs were introduced and improved upon, and vessel shapes became more graceful and efficient. As time passed the fingers of the potters became more and more deft.

The adoption of a permanent house was also of great importance. Life in the open caves was never too comfortable. Heavy rains, deep snows and the bitter temperatures of winter held certain terrors for the Basket Makers who were without actual dwellings. No great population could ever grow out of such

conditions. A permanent, secure house was necessary to stabilize the culture.

The first dwellings were in the nature of pithouses, partially above and partially below ground. 

The underground portion consisted of a shallow pit, two or three feet deep and from ten to fifteen feet in diameter. Sometimes the dirt walls were plastered with mud, or if the walls had a tendency to cave, they were lined with stone slabs. In the floor, forming a large four square, holes were dug and a crotched post slightly higher than a man's head was set upright in each. A long slender log was stretched from the top of each post to the next, forming a square framework, the main support of the roof. Slender poles were slanted from the edge of the pit up to this framework at twelve or fifteen inch intervals, entirely around the room.

Other poles were placed across the flat top portion and the skeleton of the roof was completed. 

To this framework was lashed a solid covering of reeds, brush, bark or coarse grass and the entire roof was covered over with a layer of earth several inches thick. A small opening was left in the center of the flat portion of the roof. This served as a smoke hole and it was often equipped with a ladder and used as an entrance. Some of the pit houses may have had doors in the side walls but these side walls are so badly destroyed that above-ground side entrances have not been detected.

Many of the houses were equipped with odd crawl entrances. In a house of this type a low tunnel was dug under the south wall of the room and extended for a distance of several feet. At the end the tunnel was enlarged into a room, usually very small but sometimes almost as large as the main roof itself. It is believed that this small room was roofed over except for a hatchway in the center that served as a door. In reality a house of this type consisted of the large main room and a small antechamber, the two being connected by a tunnel. The person entering the house lowered himself into the antechamber, dropped to his hands and knees and crawled through the passageway to the main room.

The change is made when certain new trends become apparent. Indications are that enemy groups began to harass the peaceful farmers. Nomadic Indians entered the Pueblo region and the small scattered villages fell easy prey to their raids. The border regions suffered first and Soon the inhabitants were forced to abandon them. In those border regions small villages have been found that give unmistakable evidence of having been destroyed by a ruthless enemy. 

Life in small villages was no longer healthy and the population began to concentrate in regions that were especially favorable because of abundant supplies of water, good farming land or excellent natural defensive locations. Large villages developed as the people from the small villages gathered for mutual protection. 

Great communal buildings grew up; terraced villages rising as high as four stories. These compact structures with their thick stone walls served as forts and gave the Pueblos a chance to defend their homes and families. 

Sometimes the villages were not large but us ually in that case a number were built close together and the same protection was afforded. Natural defensive locations such as high points, mesa tops and caves became especially desirable. Other Spanish explorers followed Coronado, missions were established in many of the pueblos, Spanish colonies were founded and for almost three centuries the Spaniards were in close contact with the Indians. Later the American explorers entered the Southwest and they too came in contact with the Indians. 

The chronicles of these explorers, both Spanish and American, contain many passages concerning the life and customs of the Pueblo people. Many of these early records have been translated and compiled and from them we gain knowledge of Pueblo life during the last four centuries. It is true that not all of the observations were accurate. Many were spiced with prejudice and deliberate fallacy but still they have been of value.

Ethnologists have done a vast amount of work that supplements the labors of the archeologist and the historian. The ethnologist is a scientist who makes an actual detailed study of a group of Indians. 

Every cultural detail is recorded and there have been ethnologists who knew more about the Indians whom they studied than the Indians knew about themselves. 

Some of the ethnologists have lived in the pueblos for long periods of time. In some cases they have been accepted by the Indians and have even been taken into the priesthood. The women of Cliff Palace make the beautiful black-on-white pottery that is typical of all the Cliff Dwellers of the Mesa Verde. They are proud of the graceful shapes and exact designs and each woman strives to excell her neighbors. All of the women use the same methods and there is a surprising sameness about their products. Each one varies her designs and no two pieces are exactly alike but all are of a standardized type. Each piece proclaims its Mesa Verde origin.

The women are very proud of their pottery and seldom swerve from the conventional type. Sometimes when the men go to distant regions on trading trips they bring home a few pieces of foreign pottery. Their wives compare this pottery with their own and are always satisfied. They feel that their wares excell all others and continue to make the same types that their mothers and grandmothers made.

Pottery making is a long detailed process that requires much skill and it is only after many years of practice that women are able to make pieces of the finest quality. Each step must be carefully and thoroughly executed or the final result will not make a woman's husband proud when he compares her pottery with that of the other women.

Two ingredients are needed for the actual construction; pottery clay and a tempering material. The clay occurs in a shale layer at the foot of the upper cliff of the canyon wall. There are many deposits, large and small, and each woman has a favorite place from which she obtains her clay. A mile up the canyon from Cliff Palace, at the head of the right-hand fork, is an excellent deposit that is favored by many of the women.

If a water jar, or a bowl is being made the work is only half done for it must be smoothed and decorated. At last the vessel is smooth and shapely and the potter is satisfied. She places it in the sun to dry and begins to coil another. 

After a number of vessels have thoroughly dried in the sun the next step begins. From the mesa top, where it occurs just under the red top soil, the potter has brought a quantity of white clay. A small amount of this is ground up and mixed with water until a white, soupy liquid results. This is the "slip" and it is painted over the entire surface of the vessels giving them a chalky white covering. After the slip has dried each pot is carefully polished with a smooth pebble.

Short, brisk strokes are used and the entire surface is polished until it shines. This polishing is a tedious but important step for the smoothness, luster and hardness of the finished vessel depend upon it.. 

At last the pots are ready for the decorations and this is the part the potter likes best of all. It is her opportunity to demonstrate her creative ability.

Among the Cliff Dwellers the designs are always black, a color that is made from a local plant. The shoots of the common Bee Weed are boiled until a thick brown liquid results. Pottery designs are painted with this liquid. 

Out of thin air the woman snatches a design.

She has a fierce pride in her ability to create these designs for she knows that later her finished pots will have to bear comparison with those of her neighbors. No tracings or trial pictures are made.. She merely selects one of the sun-dried vessels, notes its size and shape and develops in her mind a design that will fit it. Following this mental picture she paints the vessel with the brown liquid. The brush is a small piece of yucca leaf, one end of which has been chewed to loosen the fibers. Her free-hand strokes are swift and sure and soon the vessel bears an accurate, carefullybalanced geometric design. At this stage it is drab looking for the brown lines are not attractive. 

At last, after many hours of tiresome work, the potter has a number of pots ready for firing. This is the crucial step and the excellence of the pottery depends upon its success. The pots are carried down to one of the lower terraces at the front of the cave and stacked in a shallow pit that has been scooped out. 

Over them the potter piles the fuel; wood, bark and cakes of rotting humus from under the trees. When it is ignited it burns and smoulders for hours, subjecting the pots to an intense heat.

When she is satisfied that the pottery is wellfired she rakes it out of the fire, polishes it with a piece of cloth or buckskin and her work is finished. 

The brown paint has been changed by the heat to a deep black that stands out in striking contrast against the light gray background. From the simple ingredients; clay, ground potsherds and Bee Weed, has come this beautiful, enduring pottery. It is the highest artistic expression of the Cliff Dwellers.

Stones for the house are not quarried. The men simply go out along the canyon slopes and pick up small rocks that are of the proper size, though of irregular shapes. As soon as a large pile is accumulated the men begin to dress the stones. A few well-directed blows from a heavy maul or hammer give a stone the general shape desired, while a thorough dressing with small pecking and rubbing stones gives it the final finish. 

Half an hour of work results in a well-shaped building block. The sandstone is soft and the men are skillful, so in spite of the simple tools the building stones are turned out surprisingly fast. 

Mortar for the walls is prepared by the women.

All of the houses in Cliff Palace are much like this one. They are small, simple rooms that serve principally as sleeping quarters and for the storage of possessions. Most of the activities take place in the open courts and on the roofs of the terraced structures So there is no need for large houses. They would be difficult to warm in winter and would reduce the number of families that could live in the cave.

When the boy has finished his weaving and the girl's house is ready the wedding takes place and the boy moves his personal property; his clothing, weapons and tools from his mother's house to the girl's house. 

They are now husband and wife. Although he is sixteen and she only fourteen they have entered the serious phase of life and must bear their share of the community responsibility.

The first few weeks of married life are difficult for the young husband. He is living in the midst of his wife's relatives who are watching him critically. If he fails to fit in life will be miserable for him and he may come home some evening to find that his wife has thrown all of his possessions out of the house, indicating that he is no longer welcome. Few marriages end that way, for the young husbands, being still pliable, are able to fit themselves into the groove their in-laws cut for them. In some cases the house may not even be built until the young husband has lived with his wife's relatives for a time and has been fully approved by them.

The individual is not important to the Cliff Dwellers. The social unit is the family and a group of closely united families means a strong, self-sufficient family group or clan. The interests of each person are important only as they affect the welfare of the clan group. True courtship, personal privacy and individual ownership of property are practically unknown. The interest of the individual is welded into the far more important interest of the family group or clan. As the clan prospers so does each individual prosper and class distinction does not exist.

While the men are preparing the fields the medicine men are watching the weather very carefully. It is their duty to set the planting date. There are countless signs that they must take into consideration. 

They watch the sun in its movement along the horizon and they note the position of the new moon. The return of certain birds from the south is observed and the appearance and growth of spring plants is watched carefully. The clouds and the sky are observed constantly and the wind is tested many times a day. All of these things have a meaning. Countless generations of medicine men have developed a "weather sense" and barring occasional mistakes they are quite accurate in their predictions.

In addition to setting the planting date the priests must also perform certain ceremonies over the seeds that are to be planted. Spring is not an important ceremonial season for the men are too busy to spare time for the long, elaborate ceremonies. Certain rites must be performed, however; offerings mus t be made to certain gods so they will smile upon the people. It is a simple form of "crop insurance." 

The fertility rites are especially important for unless the gods of fertility and reproduction are pleased the seeds will rot in the ground without sprouting. 

Around the precious seeds the priests and their helpers perform the ancient rites. Year after year the ritual is the same; countless generations of priests have developed this elaborate formula that is followed in the finest detail. There is endless chanting, parts of the legends are dramatized, and there are offerings of prayersticks, corn pollen, cornmeal and perfect ears of corn. If the ceremonies are performed properly the germ gods will be pleased and the seeds will sprout and grow well.

As the end of spring draws near everything is in readiness. The germination ceremonies are over and the fields are ready for the planting. The eager farmers await the nod of the priests that will send them scurrying to the fields to plunge the seeds into the earth.

In any normal year in the Mesa Verde there are several days of rain about the middle of May. The Cliff 

Dwellers like to plant their crops just before the rains come in order that they may get the greatest benefit from the moisture. If the planting is done too early, however, there is danger of late frosts so the medicine men are in a dangerous position. If they sanction the planting too soon frosts may nip the tender young shoots, while if they hold off too long the rains may come. Not only will the planting be delayed many days but much of the benefit of the moisture will be lost. 

Carefully the priests watch the signs. The birds, the plants, the sun, the moon; everything goes through the regular progression of spring. Then comes a day when there is a slight haze in the sky and the air feels cool and damp. That is the signal. The moisture is in the sir; only an east wind is needed to bring it down. The planting must be completed before the east wind comes. All of the men and boys, even some of the women help with the planting. The moist red earth is easy to dig and with the abundance of help the work proceeds rapidly. From sunrise to sunset they work, always keeping an apprehensive eye on the weather. 

The first few hills in each field are planted ritually. They are laid out in a certain manner and brief prayers are chanted. After this ceremonial beginning the rest of the hills are planted helter-skelter. There are no rows, simply individual hills planted from five to eight feet apart. with no effort toward orderliness. The only care exercised in the location of the hills is that they are not in the same spot as those of last year. Enough stubs from last year's crop have been left in place to indicate where not to plant. 

By changing the location of the hills each season and by wide planting the men save the soil, for it may be years before two hills are planted in the same spot.

Every effort is made to conserve the water supply for as the warm weather continues the springs begin to dwindle and the pools shrink. There is no repairing and building of houses; water cannot be spared for the mortar. Pottery is seldom made at this time for that too, requires water. By using it only for the human needs the supply can be drawn out for months if necessary.

There is no cemetery; burial may be made anywhere. Quite often the grave is dug in the trash pile in front of the cave for the ashes and trash make easy digging. In other cases the grave is dug along the talus slope or even up on the mesa top. Sometimes

advantage is taken of a crevice in the rocks or of a small cave under a large boulder. After the grave is prepared the tightly-wrapped body is placed in it along with the personal possessions of the deceased. A bowl of food and a mug of water are also placed in the grave. All of this is a part of the regular ritual of burial. The Cliff Dwellers feel that the spirit of the dead pers on is going to another world very much like this one and in that world it will need the things that have been provided; provided; food, food, water, jewelry, clothing, tools and weapons. The spirit of the person will take the spirits of these objects and journey off to this happy afterworld. the With the conclusion of the burial ceremony grave is filled with earth and rocks and all evidence of it is wiped out. The men who have performed the ceremony return to their homes and purify themselves so there will be no bad effects from this contact with the dead.

Occasionally death comes when a severe storm is raging outside the cave. Rather than face the storm and attempt to dig through the deep snow and into the frozen earth the men bury the body in the trash room in the back of the cave.

The cave roof is too low for houses to be built in that space so it is used as a trash room and turkey roost. When a body is buried there it is surrounded by perfectly dry materials such as ashes, dust, corn tassels, feathers and turkey droppings. The chill of winter prevents immediate decay and the body begins to dry out. The moisture in the flesh is gradually drawn out by the dry materials that surround it and the body slowly dries and hardens. 

After all of the moisture is gone nothing is left but the bones and the hard, dried tissues. In that condition it will last indefinitely, a dry, hard, prune-like body. In only two respects has it changed; it has lost its spark of life and its moisture. If it is kept perfectly dry this mummy-like body will remain unchanged for countless centuries.

In January the cold becomes more intense. Scores of fires burn brightly in the great cave as the people attempt to drive out the cold. Some nights the temperature falls very close to zero. High overhead hangs a brilliant white moon and the snowy canyon is almost as light as day. From the cliff near the great ceremonial building comes the wail of a coyote; from the mesa top comes the mournful hoot of an owl. The cave is quiet except for the low chanting in some of the kivas and the snoring of old men. Sometimes a baby whimpers or a sick person groans. Now and then a muffled scream echoes through the cave as an aged sufferer cries out from the agony of arthritis or an abscessed tooth. 

There is little travel during the coldest periods. Even the men forego their hunting and visiting and everyone stays close to the sheltering cave with the single idea of keeping warm and well. The city is quieter now; there is none of the boisterous gaiety that was so pronounced during the other seasons. 

The men spend most of their time in the kivas while the women and children gather around the fires in the courtyards. The turkeys that roost in the back of the cave at night come out in the daytime and wander about the courts and roof-tops, fighting with the dogs for scraps of food that are thrown to them. During the periods when there is deep snow in the canyon the turkeys are fed small amounts of the precious corn but in late winter they grow thin and bedraggled. When they are not roaming about the city searching for food they sit in quiet rows on the housetops, feathers fluffed against the cold. The turkeys are highly prized by the 

Cliff Dwellers. Their feathers are of great importance in making ceremonial equipment and large quantities are needed for the feather blankets.

The years of catastrophe did not come often. 

It was only occasionally that the crops failed and when they did the people were prepared for it. A thousand years of farming in the Mesa Verde had taught them that every few years they must expect a dry year without a harvest. Often they were able to predict such a season in advance. If the heavy snows of December and January failed to come the men began to worry. If February and 

March failed to provide the essential winter moisture the farmers became quite sure that the harvests would be poor.

Months in advance they began to prepare for the lean seasons that faced them.

Cliff Palace Today 

Cliff Palace broods oday in its great protective cave, a silent deserted city. The heavy hand of time has brought certain changes but for the most part it is much as we saw it in the year 1266 A. D. Roof's have disappeared and a few of the houses are gone. 

Some of the walls have cracked or partially fallen and ladders no longer lead up over the terraced house tops.

In spite of these changes, however, much of the ancient city is just as we saw it. Many of the walls are exactly as they were the day they were built by the Cliff Dwellers.

Since Mesa Verde was made into a National Park in 1906, every effort has been made to arrest the destructive forces that threatened Cliff Palace. 

It was first cleaned out, then necessary repairs were made. 

Leaning walls were braced, cracks were again filled with adobe and weak foundations were strengthened. In most cases these repairs were of a minor nature and had little effect on the appearance of the structure. Today it looks much as it did on that memorable day in 

1888 when Richard Wetherill and Charles Mason first saw it.
