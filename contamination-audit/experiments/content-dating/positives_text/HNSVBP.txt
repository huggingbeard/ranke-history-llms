FOREWORD The first printing of BRING YOUR BROTHER WITH You was oversold at least we received orders which could not be filled, and we therefore feel justified in making a second printing. The author (at present, rector of Trinity Church, Florence, Alabama) states in a recent letter, that he has nothing to add to his narrative, as little more concerning work in the Philippines is now known than when this article was written in 1942. 

This monograph is as witty as it is wise, and as moving as it is witty. It is an adequate compendium of the racial, military, religious, and political history of the Philippine Islands, and may well serve as a handbook, to those who wish to acquaint themselves with those topics. 

The pith of the book is in the main title: The author makes clear his con viction that the Four Freedoms Freedom of Speech, Freedom of Worship, Freedom from Want and Freedom from Fear, must, or at any rate should, be shared with all - yes, all - of the races in the Islands, by those who may be 

fortunate enough to acquire them earlier.

It is a challenge to the fair play of the great nations; Whatsoever desirable things you seek, BRING YOUR BROTHER WITH YOU.

"BRING YOUR BROTHER WITH YOU" THE EPISCOPAL MISSIONS IN THE PHILIPPINES 

by 

THE REV. EDWARD G. MULLEN SECRETARY, PHILIPPINE EPISCOPAL CHURCH CHAPLAIN OF ST. LUKE'S HOSPITAL IN MANILA 

This is a report of the work of the Episcopal Church in a part of the world that is very much on our minds at the present time. From your town or city have gone out many young men in the Armed Forces of our country. Many of them were in the Philippines · some are still there and before this war is won many thousands more will find themselves in the Philippines and in the other islands of that important part of the world. 

We have not always had the Southwest Pacific area foremost in our minds. 

Until December 7, 1941, many people in the United States gave little thought to that part of the world. Many did remember the Spanish-American War, and in almost every town there were to be found a few veterans of that short campaign; some also remembered the valiant body of school-teachers who went to the Philippines by the hundreds to bring the light of modern education to the masses of the Filipinos. But, by and large, most Americans gave little thought to our most remote island possessions.

Not that the Philippines did not affect our lives, or we the lives of Filipinos because there was a constant exchange of goods that made the lives of both nations more comfortable. Once we, in this country, began to miss those commodities, we began to inquire why we were no longer getting them, and our thoughts were once more directed to the East Indies. 

-― As soon as the ships stopped running between Manilla and San Francisco, we began to think and talk about walking more and riding less. There is not any more rubber, and apparently there is not going to be any more rubber for civilian uses at least until the war is won. In the Philippines we have the Goodyear Rubber Plantations, and the American Rubber Company, and from the East Indies of which the Philippines are a part comes ninety-eight per cent of all the rubber in the world. All of it is in the hands of the enemy at the present time. Thousands of tons of sugar come to us from the Philippines every year enough to sweeten millions of cups of coffee and we are going to have to get 

---

along without that sugar until after the war. And so it is with many other products that have come to our factories and to our kitchens: hemp to make our rope; coconut fats to make our soap, our margarine, and our explosives; hard wood to furnish our ships with decks and panels; not to mention the other products that we can get along without-tobacco, embroidery, and gold. These products have come to us to the value of millions of dollars every year, and we have been very glad to have a source of these supplies in the Philippines. We were just beginning to realize the valuable deposits of manganese and chrome ore, of iron and gold, and other mineral products, that the Philippines contain. 

AMERICAN INFLUENCE 

In turn, the Philippines took millions of dollars worth of our manufactured products. From Connecticut each year thousands of clocks and locks and other manufactured articles poured into Manilla and were distributed throughout the Commonwealth. From other areas went other goods, cotton shirts and moving pictures and gasoline and chewing gum, to the value of millions of pesos. In recent years the Commonwealth of the Philippines has become the fifth best customer the United States has in the world. We were very glad of that trade, and we are looking forward to the day when we may once again send our products to our friends on the other side of the earth. 

These business enterprises have been of great importance in making the entire Orient the place it is today. We make a mistake, I believe, when we try to limit the term "missionary" to one sent out by the Church. There are all kinds of "missionaries" in the Orient. The recent graduating class at West Point had its attention called to the fact that its members were "armed missionaries", representing the nation, and being sent to the ends of the earth for a particular purpose. 

Business firms send missionaries, too. In fact, there is a saying in the Orient that almost every foreigner you meet works either for the British and American Tobacco Company, and he sells smoke; or he works for the Standard Oil Company, and he sells heat; or he is a Christian missionary, and he deals in light. 

As long as we send the SMOKE and the HEAT, we want to continue to send the LIGHT as well. We want to try our best to help these brothers of ours to adjust to the new way of life that we have introduced among them, and that will make their lives increasingly complicated after the war. 

Missionaries are not so much interested in changing the lives of people their lives are being changed fast enough already - but our task is to help them to adjust to changing conditions. To enable their souls to catch up with their bodies, is the task of the Christian missionary today; because this change in living has come suddenly and has completely revolutionized life for millions of Malays. We say they have had to make the step from carabao to Clipper overnight. The carabao is the water-buffalo, the beast of burden of the Orient. If you keep prodding him, he moves along at the rate of about two miles an hour.

For thousands of years the carabao has set the pace of the Orient. Suddenly overhead there appear the Clipper ships, and the war planes, flying now at more than seven hundred miles an hour. That is quite a change, from two miles an hour to seven hundred miles an hour, especially if you have to make the change all in one hour! Speed has come to stay, and many of the people living in remoter parts of the Philippines are breathless trying to keep up with the onrushing civilization. 

Sometimes this presents some queer pictures to our way of thinking. In 

Baguio, the capital of the Mountain Province where some of the richest gold mines in the world are found, the original inhabitants are Igorots. They are the "head-hunters" of the travel books, and they wear very little in the way of clothing only a loin cloth or a little skirt. Yet civilization has reached these people, and I have seen these Igorots, dressed or undressed as I have described them, coming up out of the gold mines on pay-day, calling taxi-cabs, and driving to the movies to see "Gone With the Wind." They can tell you all about Scarlett O'Hara in Baguio! They think that life in America is lived as 

Clark Gable lives it in the movies. But many people in this country think that life in the South Sea Islands is lived as Miss Dorothy Lamour lives it in the movies, so it is about fifty-fifty! moon 

It is so easy for us to imagine what life is like in the tropics, and with the help of Hollywood and a few novels, each one builds his own picture of the tropic scene. We imagine the sandy beaches and the fringe of coconut trees, with the always a full moon for some strange reason! shining down. At one's back is a banana plant, and all one has to do is to reach up and pick a banana and eat it, and go back to sleep! Life is all either siesta or fiesta! But such is not the case. There are too many wants created by modern business, too much sickness, too much hard work in planting rice, to make life as pretty as that. 

The products of modern civilization have influenced the lives of people in the remotest areas. I was talking with one of our American generals who has explored parts of the Philippines that are still marked "unexplored" on maps. 

He told me that one day he want into a valley on an out-of-the-way island, and, so primitive were the conditions there, that he felt sure he was the first civilized man to set foot in that valley. He approached a native hut, pushed aside a woven screen, and looked in. Greatly to his surprise he found a woman there sewing on a Singer Sewing Machine made in Bridgeport, Connecticut! The "missionary" from Bridgeport had been there before him.

OUR CIVILIZATION NOT THE FIRST 

The Philippines have been influenced by many types of civilization. Thousands of years ago the great Hindu Empire reached out its fingers and took in parts of the Philippines. Many things Indian remain to influence the lives of the Filipino people. Centuries later China controlled the Islands, and to this day

Chinese ways of cooking rice and Chinese ways of dress are found throughout the Philippines. Wherever China has gone, the people have been taught to dress in the Chinese style, the man wearing a skirt and the woman wearing trousers. 

Many Filipinos dress in that way today. That new style that is coming into this country is not a new style at all, but an old Chinese custom! 

For almost four centuries Spain ruled the Filipinos, and Spain brought the Christian religion, and schools and orphanages and hospitals to the Philippines. 

The oldest university under the American flag is not Harvard, but the University of Santo Thomas in Manila, founded many years before the University in Cambridge. When men were chasing Indians in Cambridge, or being chased by the 

Indians, Filipinos in Manila were taking doctors' degrees at Santo Thomas. 

But, under the Spanish system, only a few had a chance for education. Most of the people were kept in ignorance and serfdom, with little chance to change their living conditions.

The great contribution of America in the forty-four years that have passed since the Battle of Manila Bay has been general education, with the resulting freedom in religious thinking and the improvement in sanitary conditions and health. Forty years ago in Manila, of every hundred babies born, eighty were dead at the end of the first year. Last year, in Manila, of every thousand babies born, only fifty-six were dead at the end of the first year; an excellent record for a city of almost a million people in the tropics. The old moat that surrounded the city wall, and was full of dirty water, breeding mosquitos, has been filled in and is now the Municipal Golf Course. One may play nine holes of golf now, around the city wall! Schools have been opened everywhere and from north to south, over the whole range of the Commonwealth, one may travel using only the English language.

Many other nations, with colonies in that part of the world, have criticized America's policy, or lack of policy. They have warned that if we let the native people learn our language, they would read our books and get ideas of independence and labor unions, and all kinds of trouble would result. But the Americans went on teaching their "little brown brothers" as President Taft called to read; and the results of a year at war have now proved that America was right. them 

For years Japan has approached one country after another in that part of the world with her plan for a Co-prosperity Sphere in Asia. As is well known, many of the countries approached were taken in by Japan's plea that Asia be kept for the Asiatics. One after another of the colonies of other nations was taken by 

Japan almost without a fight. In fact, the native peoples of some areas joined with the Japanese against their white rulers. In the Philippines, the Filipino people put up a resistance against the enemy that the whole world will talk about for years to come. There is a reason for the fact that the Commonwealth of the 

Philippines stands alone as one of the United Nations among all the Malay peoples. There is a reason that the Filipinos who joined whole-heartedly with

the Americans were willing to suffer and to sacrifice to keep the Four Freedoms alive in their land. There were many reasons, in fact. 

One of the reasons is the fact that for more than forty years the school system has spread education far and wide over the Philippines. Almost half of all the children in the land, of school age, are now attending school. All of the instruction is, and has been for more than a generation, in the English language. 

American books and magazines and newspapers are widely read. Because of the high standard of living, compared to that of their neighbors in the Orient, many Filipinos have radios and they listen to the world news. They knew what Japan had promised and what Japan had done. They knew that Japan meant "Asia for the Japanese." They had studied in history what Japan had done to the native people of Korea and Formosa, and they knew what had been done in Nanking and in other parts of occupied China. And they wanted none of that in their own home land. The much-criticized general education bore fruit at last. 

Another reason for the overt response of the Filipinos when the call for help came was the fact that America had promised the Filipino people their independence in 1946. From the earliest days independence had been promised. Other nations with colonies in that part of the world had made no definite promises of independence, and few were the native men who were willing to die simply to prevent a change of masters. When the history of this war comes to be written, it will be found that the loyal response of the Filipinos was the one bright feature during the dark days of Japan's spreading victories. When the time comes to retake the Philippines, the inhabitants will be found willing to cooperate in driving out the enemy and in building a better world in Asia. 

THE IMPORTANCE OF THE PHILIPPINES 

Before the attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, most American people had little knowledge of the Islands of the Pacific. Many thought of all the islands as being too far away from our shores to bother about, but when Japanese bombs fell on Honolulu, only 2000 miles from our mainland, and when our own shores were shelled, minds were turned to the West, and atlases were opened and geography became a much-needed subject. 

On that memorable Sunday I was preaching at Burlington, Vermont. When the news came over the radio of the catastrophe at Pearl Harbor, some students at the University of Vermont asked me how far Manila was from Pearl Harbor. 

They were surprised to know that Burlington is nearer to Pearl Harbor than Manila is! So great are the distances across the Pacific that those who have been taught to think of oceans in terms of the Atlantic Ocean have to revise their ideas.

In ordinary times it takes almost a month to go from our own West Coast to the Philippines. In war time, with slower moving convoys, it takes much longer. We cannot count on our supply ships making more than three round trips a year to the Southwestern Pacific area. In the last World War our transports

made two round trips a month to France. Three round trips a year is a very different matter. And that means that the war may last longer than we hope it will last.

The Philippines, as most people now know, are not a tiny group of islands in mid-Pacific, but a large archipelago with 7100 islands, large and small. The 

Commonwealth spreads almost 1200 miles in length down the southeastern shore of Asia. And on these islands live seventeen million people; no small number when we think that in all of North and South America only our own country, Brazil and Mexico are larger in population. We have already seen the importance of the Commonwealth as a source of agricultural and mineral products. 

WAR WITH JAPAN EXPECTED 

Because of the location of the Philippines, only a few miles from the expanding Japanese Empire in 1940 — and only six hundred miles from China it was seen by thinking people that sooner or later Japan would attack the Philippines. 

The surprise was not that Manila should have been attacked, but that Honolulu was attacked first. Many people thought that Japan would wait until 1946, when the Philippines had obtained complete independence from the United States, before attacking. It was hoped in the Philippines that by that time an army would be trained to defend the Commonwealth, or at least to make it too costly to capture. But Japan did not wait. The Philippines were right in the way, and they contained just the mineral and agricultural resources that Japan lacked. 

For years Japan has been taking a good deal of the Philippines' products; hemp and sugar, and iron and other minerals.

Some men, in every year since 1900, have spoken of the danger of attack by Japan, and some preparations had been made by business firms and individuals in storing food on remote islands where a stand could be made when trouble broke. Some men predicted that Japan would capture the Philippines in a few days, or weeks, and not all were sure that the Filipinos would prove as loyal to the cause of the United Nations as they turned out to be. 

When the Philippines were attacked, on December 8, 1941, one hundred thousand men of the Philippine Army joined with the American forces and fought, not for weeks but for five months, holding back the hundreds of thousands of Japanese who had planned to sweep across the Philippines and southward into the Dutch East Indies and Australia before the United Nations had united. 

Finally the troops of the Filamerican armies were forced to surrender on Bataan and later on Corregidor, but smaller groups continue to fight in various places in the Philippines, and will continue to oppose the enemy as long as he occupies one foot of Philippine territory.

THE PLACE OF THE CHURCH 

The Christian religion was taken to the Philippines by the early Spanish explorers and for almost four hundred years the only recognized religion in the 10

Philippines was that of the Roman Catholic Church. One finds no ancient Buddhist or Hindu ruins in the Philippines because all evidences possible of other religions were destroyed. No Protestant mission work was allowed in the Philippines until after the taking of the Islands by the American armed forces in 1898. 

As has been mentioned, the early Spanish priests and friars and nuns did much for the Filipinos and gave them all they had in the way of schools and hospitals. In later years the Church joined forces with the State and oppressed the Filipinos so that a revolution against Church and State was about to succeed when the American Army and Navy arrived during the Spanish-American War. 

The majority of the Filipino people are still Roman Catholics and that Church is making a great effort now to win the allegiance of the college people by sending the finest priests to educational centers. 

During the last decade of the nineteenth century, so strong was the feeling against the Roman Catholic Church, that several Filipino priests broke away from the Church of Rome and founded the Independent Filipino Church molded along the lines of other national Catholic Churches. One of the priests was made the "archbishop", the Mass was translated into the language of the people, the priests married, but the intensely nationalistic spirit remained. Filipino heroes were canonized and every attempt to secure complete and immediate independence for the Philippines was heartily endorsed by the Aglipayan Church so named because Gregorio Aglipay was the founder and the first "archbishop." That

Church today has about two million members scattered over the Commonwealth. 

After the American occupation of the Philippines, all religious groups were allowed freedom of activity and many of the Protestant Churches in the United States sent missionaries to Manila. The largest work today is being done by the 

United Evangelical Church of the Philippines, a union of the American Board (Congregational) work with that of the United Brethren in Christ, and the Presbyterian. This union has fifty thousand of the quarter of a million non- 

Roman Christians in the Philippines. The Methodist Church has almost as large a communicant strength, and almost all of the larger denominations have work, large or small, in different parts of the Commonwealth. 

The geography of the Philippines has helped Church unity forward, and the thousands of islands have been divided among the larger Christian groups. Territory is assigned to denominations where schools and hospitals and Churches may be organized and run without the fear of competition from other Christian bodies. 

Most of the missionaries have respected these boundary lines, and there has not been the duplication of effort and the waste of men and money that would result if no attempt at division were made. Many of the denominations have gone into outlying districts where the Roman Catholic Church was very weak or where there had been no mission work for many years. Some have ventured among the pagan peoples where no Christian missionary had gone before. There is no need for proselytizing for there are thousands of people in the Philippines who have not yet been taught the Christian way of life. 11

THE WORK OF THE EPISCOPAL CHURCH 

We are glad to know that even in this time of war the work of the Episcopal Church in the Philippines is going on. We have not heard directly from our 

Bishops or other missionaries recently, but we do hear from those who have come from Manila on exchange ships, and what they report makes our hearts glad. Our missionaries were released from concentration early last year and are free to carry on their work in most areas. Our Cathedral in Manila was open and services were being held daily and Sunday as usual. St. Luke's Hospital in 

Manila was doing work of heroic porportions in caring for the wounded as well as the usual ill patients, and our physicians and nurses, together with the chaplains, were working to relieve pain and suffering. Our schools have all been closed, but we know that the teachers, too, are finding much to do in a land so torn by war. Teachers in the early years of this century did much to bring about an understanding following a war, and we know that our mission teachers will do all they can now.

The work of our Church began in very trying times; in the first days of the 

American occupation. Some of the Army chaplains who were with the forces in 

1898 were priests of the Episcopal Church, and they held services whenever they could for the civilian population as well as for the military. Some members of the Brotherhood of St. Andrew started a chapter of that society in Manila. A dungeon in the old wall surrounding the city of Manila is the site of the first public celebration of the Holy Communion according to the Book of Common Prayer. 

In 1901 Bishop Charles Henry Brent was sent out by the Church in America to be the first Bishop of the Philippine Islands. He saw that our Church had an important part to play in the winning of the Filipino people if the Church did not try to spread thinly over seven thousand islands, but would confine her activities to the areas that needed her most. Accordingly a plan of action was drawn up and certain areas of work, not being under the jurisdiction of any other Christian group, were taken up by the Episcopal Church. 

First of all, the Church began her work naturally enough with the Americans and Europeans who were coming into the Philippines in large numbers in the opening years of this century. There was a British colony in most of the larger cities of the Islands, and many of those people from Great Britain and the colonies were members of the Church of England, and joined in the work of our branch of the Anglican communion. A mission was started in Manila which soon grew to be an independent parish, with its own vestry and rector. In Baguio and in 

Zamboanga there are many European Church members. The second Bishop of the Philippines, the Rt. Rev. Gouverneur Frank Mosher, was so greatly interested in carrying the ministrations of the Church to those Americans and Europeans who live on isolated plantations, that he appointed a Canon Missioner whose duty it is to visit those who cannot attend Church. Every year the Canon baptizes, marries and buries those who are prevented by distance from the attentions of the parish clergy. He conducts a Church School by mail, and keeps in 12

touch with hundreds of British and American Churchmen throughout the Commonwealth.

AMONG THE CHINESE 

Work was extended to include the thousands of Chinese people in Manila at an early date. Most of the Chinese residents of the Philippines come either from Amoy or from Canton. They are business men for the most part, and a large share of the retail business of the Commonwealth, from the department stores of Manila to the tienda in the smallest villages, is operated by these Chinese people. 

Nothing much had been done for them in a religious way, and, because some of them had had contact with the Anglican mission work in China, our Church was able to start missions in Manila. Bishop Brent secured the services of missionaries from China and later Chinese priests were called to carry on the Church's work among their own people. In Manila we have an independent Chinese parish, St. Stephen's, and a mission to the Cantonese Chinese, St. Peter's. St. Stephen's parish operates a school for Chinese girls where several hundred students are trained in all the requirements of the Philippine School System as well as all the requirements of the Chinese National School System. 

In recent years work among the Chinese people in Zamboanga has been started, and a flourishing school for the Chinese youth of that city was in operation when the war began. Every attempt is made to make this work self-supporting, and the Chinese people are willing to pay for the education of their children. 

Many of the leaders of the people in China were trained in mission schools in the Philippines and elsewhere, and many of the leading Chinese residents of Manila and Zamboanga are on our school boards and are anxious to have Christian training given to the rising generation.

AMONG THE IGOROTS 

Close to half a million Igorots, and allied peoples, live in the Mountain Province of the Philippines. They are a sturdy people, descendants of an earlier race that once lived over the whole archipelago. By necessity or by choice they finally settled in the uplands of the islands of Luzon, and there they developed a civilization that has some remarkable features. The rice terraces cut out of the sides of mile-high mountains which use the rain water in an elaborate system of dikes and canals, are one of the wonders of the Oriental world. For centuries the Igorot people have wrested a living from the mountain sides and from the rushing mountain streams. They have dug gold from the mines and have made themselves into skilled basket and cloth weavers. Wood carving is another specialty.

These people, like all peoples, have some customs that seem peculiar to those who were brought up to practice other customs. The Igorots, like the American 

Indians in times past, are fond of eating dog flesh. Dogs are not so much pets as 13

articles of diet among them. Some people dislike this feature of the Igorot diet, but the Igorots do not like some of our common foods either. Many of them, in common with other Orientals, do not see how we can possibly eat cheese. They think that anyone who will eat cheese will do anything. We do not like the dogs, and they do not like the cheese, and that makes us even! 

Another custom that has received much publicity from travellers is the headhunting activity of the Igorot. Head-hunting sounds much more dangerous than it really is! It is not your head or mine not just any head that is wanted, but the head of some enemy of the village or tribe. It all starts quite naturally, so these people think, when an outsider comes in and commits some crime against the code of the Igorot village. He may steal some rice or dried fish; he may kidnap some woman or child; he is a public enemy. In a land where, until recently, there was no organized law-enforcement, the men took the law into their own hands and organized a posse and went after the criminal. If they could not find him, sometimes they captured a relative or fellow-tribesman of the criminal and killed him. Then they cut off his head and took it back to the village and displayed it prominently, both as a token of victory and as a warning to other men to behave decently, for no man, no matter how primitive he may be, wants to lose his head. 

The American Indians who have many similarities to the Igorots when they killed an enemy, often took his scalp as a souvenir; the Igorots go one better and take the whole head, but when the man is dead it does not much matter what is taken! And, our own ancestors, not so many years ago, did exactly the same thing when they exhibited the heads of criminals from the walls of jails, or when they put up on a pike-staff on the town common the head of an enemy of the people. It is a custom which we are trying to discourage, and which we do not practice any more ourselves, but if we try to understand that these Igorot people are, in many ways, like our ancestors of a few centuries ago, we can help them more. Head-hunting is not practiced now, except in remote cases (and in time of war with the Japanese!), and the American teachers and officials have tried to substitute tugs-of-war and base-ball games to test the prowess of the young man and to show which town had the bravest people. There has not been any indication of cannibalism ever found in the Philippines. 

These people have a religion, too. It was remarked by Plutarch, long ago, that you may travel around the world and you may find cities without walls, cities without forts, cities without theatres, but a city without a Temple you will never find in this world. The religion of these Igorot people is one called 

Animism, spirit-worship. Evil spirits are everywhere, these people believe, living in stones and brooks and especially in the belete tree. These evil spirits are seeking to do one harm, and life becomes one round of fear of disturbing a spirit and having bad luck as a result. The anitos will get you if you don't watch out!

They will make you sick, even kill you, if you do not know how to deal with them properly.

When a person is ill, the Igorots believe that in some way an evil spirit has 14

gotten into his body and the way to treat the illness is to drive the evil spirit out. 

One way to do this is to gather around the sick man, ignoring his headache or fever, and make all the noise possible. In doing this, the Igorots are acting like primitive people in every land, who think that by beating drums or gongs, by firing fire-crackers or other noise-makers, they will scare the demon out of the patient's body. Outside some hospitals one may find signs warning the passer-by not to beat the agong · a brass gong nor to fire fire-crackers, in an effort to make the patients more confortable. 

Very often this crude noise-making does not have the desired effect, but we all know that there are some patients who will recover no matter what treatment is given them! Those who are still ill find themselves the victims of further lack of attention. Nothing is done directly to comfort the patient, but an attempt is made to entice the evil spirit out of him by the smell of food! Sometimes, in a more refined way perhaps, more advanced civilizations make use of the same technique; we all know that more people will attend any meeting at which there are to be "refreshments." The Igorots know that, too, and they believe that spirits work the same way as human beings. The offering of a hen or a dog, a pig or even a carabao, is made to try to gain the good will of the anito, and the flesh is roasted over an open fire, the people believing that the odor of the roasting meat is so good that the spirit cannot help but come out and get some. Some of the meat is given to the spirit, but most of it is eaten by the people themselvesthey are practical people and they are not going to waste any food! Rice wine and other alcoholic beverages are drunk and the party often turns into an occasion, something like a wake. It generally ends up as a wake, as usually the patient dies. 

Into this part of the Philippines our Church has sent an army of mission workers. Very little was done by the Spanish priests in the Mountain Province, and our Church had an open field among these half-million people. Today in 

Baguio and Bontoc, in Sagada and Besao and in Balbalasang, our Church has centers of work, with a school and hospital or dispensary in each place. Many outstations are maintained by each of these centers, and the clergy go to smaller barrios or villages to take the ministrations of the Church to the people who live in them. The United Thank Offering of the women of the Church has supported many women missionaries in the mountain province for many years. When we contribute to the Blue Boxes of thanksgiving we can think of ourselves as helping the army of nurses, deaconesses, teachers and evangelistic workers who have brought about great changes in the Mountain Province and who have given the young men and women there a chance to lead more abundant lives. 

The young women, high school graduates, come to Manila and enter St. 

Luke's Hospital School of Nursing. These Igorot girls have many things to get used to when they come to Manila, but they soon adjust themselves to the heat and the noise of the city, and in their three-year course of study in the School of Nursing they take the same examinations, both written and practical, that are given by any first-class School of Nursing in this country. They are not backward 15

people, but heretofore they have not had much of a chance to show what they could do. The Mission is uniting its efforts, by churches and schools and hospitals, to bring to the Igorot people the blessings of the Christian life. 

Today, these Igorot communicants are carrying on the life of the Church. 

As far as we know the Japanese have not penetrated into the remoter hills, and there, as elsewhere, the missionaries are being helped now by gifts of food from those who have little to spare.

Bishop Mosher was greatly interested in doing everything possible to develop a native ministry in the Philippines. Promising young men were trained by the clergy at the mission stations to be catechists. These catechists accompanied the priests on their visits to the outstations; interpreted the sermon, and stayed on to teach the people and to prepare them for baptism and confirmation. Bishop 

Mosher had the great pleasure of seeing three young Igorot men so well trained that he was able to ordain them to the ministry of the Church. They are the beginning of a large number of native clergymen on whom will fall the task of carrying the Church's blessings to all their fellow-men and women until all have seen the light of Christ.

The Sisters of St. Mary, together with the Sisters of St. Anne, are doing a noble work at Sagada where they assist in the school work, train the girls in lace making, and run a kindergarten. A native Sisterhood has been started, with girls from different parts of the Philippines now professed. 

The very rich gold deposits, as well as mines for other minerals, brought about many changes in the Mountain Province, and our Church was hoping to meet the need of those who have migrated from their homes to be near the mines by some Church Army workers. The war has temporarily interrupted this work, but when the war is won there will be an increased need for mission workers among those miners and tradespeople of the new mining towns. We are planning to go back to that area and carry on the work so bravely started, to show those we have fought so valiantly for the cause of the Four Freedoms that we mean that people of every race and tongue may enjoy them. 

AMONG THE MOROS 

In the southern part of the Philippines, almost a thousand miles from where the Igorots live, there live the Moros, half a million strong. They are Malays who were converted to the Mohammedan religion in the thirteenth century by Arabic missionaries. These Moros are the largest body of Mohammedans under the American flag, and they were the last to come under the American control. 

They, too, have proved their loyalty to the United States by fighting bravely against the common enemy, and they have sworn on the Koran not to rest until every Japanese soldier is driven out of their territory. 

The Moros are a sea-faring people. Many of them are born on small boats, spend most of their lives sailing and fishing, and when death takes them they 16

rest in island graves with small wooden ships as markers. Some of them claim to be so used to the motion of the ship that when they come on land they get landsick! Our work among these people began with a plan for a hospital-ship that was to minister to the Moros on isolated islands and take those who needed more attention to the Mission Hospital at Zamboanga. 

Our missionary work among them today makes use of their knowledge of boats and sailing. We use common illustrations like this one to help them understand the Christian way of life: Every Moro knows that it is not the ship in the water that causes trouble, but the water in the ship causes the ship to sink. 

Every Christian ought to know that it is not the Christian in the world, but the world in the Christian, that causes trouble. Just as the Moro must bail out his vinta by hard work, keeping constantly at it, lest his ship be sunk, so the Christian must keep at the task of driving the world out of himself, all the while living in the world.

Moros being Moslems, have some habits and customs that are not like ours. 

Moros will not eat pork, and will not drink any intoxicating liquor. The men will not wear a hat with a brim for they must be able to touch their foreheads to the ground when they pray, and keep their hats on at the same time. In the 

American Army in the Philippines, special hats were designed for the troops who were Moslems.

The most important fact in the life of the Moro is his religion. The Koran, his holy book, guides him in daily living. It gives him regulations for all the duties of life, and it gives him a philosophy by which he can find reason in the Universe. Among the Moros, who are not altogether orthodox in their Mohammedanism, the philosophy of Islam boils down to one or two points. One is, Allah created the world for men and not for women. Women are not considered as possessing souls, at least as fully-developed souls as men possess. Women need not attend the mosque, and need not pray, at least with the regularity that men must pray. Religion is for men only, and the Moro is reminded of that belief daily for the opening words of his morning prayer are these: "Oh God, I thank thee that thou hast not made me a woman.' 99 Constant inter-tribal warfare, and the hazardous occupations of diving for pearls and gathering sponges in shark-infested waters, have reduced the number of men below the number of Moro women. That fact, too, has led the Moro men to look with little respect on women, and the first task of the Christian Church has been to try to give the women a place of respect in the community. Islam came with an apparent solution to the problems created by the larger number of women, and polygamy was permitted. Each man was allowed to have four legal wives, and an unlimited number of honorary wives. Divorce was made very easy; all a man had to do was to say to his wife, in the presence of witnesses, "I divorce you." Then the woman had to leave, and take her children with her. She had no recourse, and could do little but join some other man's family, or go to her own parents or relatives. 17

Our Church started work in Zamboanga in the early years of this century by opening a Moro Settlement House where Moro girls could live. A few orphan girls were the first to attend. A school was started, and soon Moro parents were asking that their daughters be admitted to the school. The Sulu Press was operated by the Mission and portions of the Bible and manuals of instruction were printed in the dialects of the people. More and more girls came to the Settlement House, and many graduates of the school went to Manila to be trained as teachers or nurses and to work among their own people. After some years boys were admitted to the school, and the experiment of co-educational instruction was tried for the first time among Mohammedan people. It proved to be quite successful, and up to the time when the Japanese captured Zamboanga the school was crowded with pupils. 

One of the facts which we hope we have learned from a study of history is this: no nation is ever able to rise above the level of the women of that nation. 

Ancient nations with many "great men" went down in decay just as quickly as more primitive nations, when the women could no longer respect themselves and when the men no longer respected the women. When the Moro women have a part to play in the life of the community, when they can make a contribution to their people as nurses or teachers or social workers, they will win the respect of the men. Most of these young women can not marry, as, if we maintain a Christian standard, there are no men to marry and there will be even fewer men when this war is won. But they can, with training, do something useful with their lives, especially in helping their younger brothers and sisters to enjoy pleasant, useful lives. Not all the students of the school, or even the graduate teachers or nurses, are Christians, but all are leading more Christian lives, and another generation will show results in more baptized Church members. 

In Zamboanga, and in Jolo and Cotabato, our Church is at work among these people, on the fringe of the Moslem world, and the opportunity is almost unlimited for future work. Most of these half-million Moros are still without the blessings of Christian schools and hospitals, but all of them are now our allies in this war for freedom.

AMONG THE TREE-DWELLERS 

At Upi, in the Province of Cotabato on Mindanao Island, in the Southern part of the Philippines live the Tiruray tribe. They are the world-famous treedwellers, and in remote places still build their houses in trees, using hemp-rope ladders to reach their homes. Our Church was able to begin work among these interesting people because an American ex-soldier, who had married a Tiruray woman many years before, asked one of our missionaries to visit Upi and see if Church work would be possible there. This visit was made and the missionary became so much interested in the Tiruray people that he learned their language, and for more than twenty years has lived among them. 18

Mindanao island is the frontier of the Philippines. Government settlement projects attract many from the over-crowded cities of the other islands to the rich farms of Mindanao. Upi itself became a center for homesteaders and the town is now made up of people from many provinces. Because our Church was the first to be established in Upi it is the largest Church there today, and the patronal feast day, October fourth since the Church is dedicated to St. Francis of 

Assisi has become the holiday of the township. 

The missionary and the ex-soldier saw that what these people needed was scientific farming so that they would not have to depend on catching and eating monkeys for food. Hundreds of miles of rolling pasture land could be used to feed herds of cattle. Pineapples and bananas, rice and other grains, could be raised in amounts large enough to feed all of the Filipino people. Our mission work has tied in very closely with the Government Agricultural High School at Upi, and daily services are held at the Mission for the students, as they pass the Church on the way between their homes and the school. Many of the Tiruray young women have trained as nurses and teachers and some are now at work among their own people. Some of the young men are in the Theological School at Sagada, training for the ministry of the Church to the Tiruray and the other peoples who have come to Upi to build their homes. There is the closest cooperation between the school authorities and the Church, and a great opportunity presents itself to the missionary-minded Christian to help these people to enjoy the Four 

Freedoms. 

Just a few miles from Upi there are many other barrios (small villages) and there our Church has outstations with chapels and small schools. Some of our clergy live in those villages and help the people to live better lives spiritually as well as economically.

THE WORK IN MANILA 

Perhaps we should have considered the work in Manila in detail first, but we do not think that Manila is typical of the Philippines, and by presenting a picture of the Church on the front line, we can see the work in the capital city in its true relation to the work in the provinces. The work for Americans and Europeans naturally centers in Manila where most of the Americans and Europeans live. For the same reason the work for the Chinese centers there also. And our largest mission hospital in the Philippines is located in the poorer district of that city.

A critic once said of a certain sentimental author that "he certainly did rattle the cans when he delivered the milk of human kindness." We have no desire in the mission field to "rattle the cans", and many of the finest examples of Christian missionary heroism never see the light of print. Many of those stories center around St. Luke's Church in Manila. 

At the beginning of this century our Church opened a Mission in Trozo, one of the poorer districts of Manila. There was no Church of any denomina- 19

tion in the vicinity, and no hospital anywhere near. A small dispensary was opened at St. Luke's Church and a nurse did what she could to give treatment to the hundreds who came daily to the clinic. From such a small beginning has grown the present St. Luke's Hospital, with its compound occupied with buildings, and its services ranging "all through life.” 

We have never forgotten at St. Luke's that we are a Church Hospital. The 

Church comes first, and, in fact, the Church building is the only permanent structure on the compound. Ths hospital is there only because the Church was there first. The Church built the Hospital and all the services of the School of Nursing are held in the Church. Religious education classes are held for the nurses, and all the hospital employees are encouraged to attend similar classes held for their benefit. Graduate nurses have their own discussion groups, and an opportunity is given to all the people on the compound to learn more about the 

Christian life.

The first Kindergarten in the Philippines was opened at St. Luke's in 1901, and for more than forty years children have been trained there in the way they should go. Daily the nurses examine the pupils, and milk is given every morning to about forty youngsters. Children of the pupils of thirty years ago are now attending the Kindergarten.

For many years the Church operated an orphanage called the House of the Holy Child. Hundreds of girls, of half-American and half-Filipino parentage were brought up in that House, and many of them return to St. Luke's Church today for the Sacraments for themselves and their families. 

Work among the students who come to Manila to attend college is carried on through the Brent Association, an organization of young Filipino men and women from all provinces who are members of the Church. This society provides an opportunity for these young people to keep in touch with their Church as well as for keeping in touch with each other. 

In 1907 a School of Nursing was opened in connection with the growing St. Luke's Hospital. More than four hundred young women have been graduated from the School since that date. Most of them are still engaged in the nursing profession, and many of them were with the Filamerican troops on Bataan and Corregidor and took care of the wounded soldiers from hundreds of American cities and towns. Many of us have contributed to missions for years, and have known, in a vague way, that some of that money went to assist in the training of native nurses; but it all seemed very far away. Now that these very nurses, trained in a School that we helped to build and support, are taking care of our sons and brothers and husbands, it seems much closer home. Before the war is won, hundreds of men who now are safe at home will owe their lives to the skill of these Filipina nurses. St. Luke's Hospital is doubly proud now of the way the nurses were trained there, and we hope, after the war, to train even larger groups of young women to cope with the health problems of a post-war world. Under- 20 20

nourishment and epidemic will leave their mark on the Filipino people and nurses will be all the more in demand after the war. 

SUMMARY 

Thus, in chapel and in Cathedral, in mission outstation and in parish church, in small dispensary or in a large hospital, in a tiny barrio one-room school or in one attended by hundreds of students, the work of the Church goes on. For forty-four years the Church has done her part in bringing the light of Christian living to thousands of Filipinos. In time of peace and in time of war, the march onward of the Christian soldier never ceases. For Him the isles shall wait and on 

His arm do they trust.

Sometimes we are tempted to think that Christianity is one of our own creations; something that we manufactured and that we are free to export or not as we see fit. A little thinking makes us remember that we were not born 

Christians, we were made Christians at our Baptism. Someone else had a hand in it, and many others are responsible for the fact that we were born in a Christian environment. Our ancestors, a thousand years ago, were not Christians. They were as wild as many a primitive tribe today, and they made short work of many missionaries who tried to work among them. Some people gave up hope of ever making civilized Christians out of them, but more missionaries went to them and finally even your ancestors and mine were converted and became Christians. 

We owe something to those valiant souls who braved the terrors of the dark forests and the far-off islands to bring to our ancestors the blessings of Christianity. The way that we can pay that debt is to help other people of our time to see the Master, too.

A recently-converted Christian once said, "You missionaries sometimes talk as though you brought Christ to our country. In fact, it was Christ that brought you to our land." That is true. From the days of the cry from Macedonia, "come over and help us", to the present, the truth of the fact that God has not left Himself without witness in any land or among any people is plain to all. 

These people were prepared by the Holy Spirit, acting through their own great teachers, for the coming of the knowledge of the One true God. 

The newly-made Christians are willing to support the Church work and do pay what they can in labor and in money to help the religious, educational and medical work of the mission. It is not at all unusual to find gifts of chickens or rice left at the door of the Church, or even to find eggs placed on the offering plates at the time of service. As a thanksgiving for our blessings we ought to give more from the bottom of our hearts and less from the tops of our purses to help this good work spread.

Of course, the need is great at home. We cannot export that which we do not have, and unless we have a sound parish church and diocese of our own we cannot send out missionaries with the true spirit of wanting to help their fellow 21

men. We cannot do good, unless we are good, and we cannot be good unless we ourselves are converted. But we must not let the fact that there remains much to be done at home discourage us from missionary work abroad. Let an example from the history of the early Christian Church show us how the problem was met shortly after our Lord ascended.

The struggling Christian mission at Antioch came to be respected throughout the Christian world because it put into practice the saying, "it is more blessed to give than to receive." The need was great in Antioch; the people of the city were not all Christians, and the Christians were not perfect, but the name Christian was first applied there because, perhaps, the people seemed more Christlike. 

They did not think of their own need first, but were willing to separate Barnabas and Saul for the work of the mission field. Barnabas and Saul were needed at home, but they were sent out with the blessing of the Christian community, and were even supported by gifts of money, to show that Christ's spirit had touched the people at Antioch and had produced results. They were willing to share, and we can do no better than to do likewise. 

When we are through sending our sons as armed missionaries to various parts of the world, we hope that we may be able to send them as missionaries of the Prince of Peace. Our sons are now experiencing the brotherhood of the 

United Nations; they are finding that all men are of one blood. They are practicing brotherhood with men of all the races on earth, and they know that it is this brotherhood that will mean peace on earth because brothers are men of good will.

Brotherhood is nothing new, of course; it is nothing exclusively Christian. "Within the four seas all men are brothers" is a saying common to the Chinese people, and they make up a quarter of the world's population. In the very first book of the Bible we have a story that brings home to us the necessity for brotherhood. It is the story of Joseph; one of the best known stories in the world. 

Joseph was a slave in Egypt, sold because of the jealousy of his brothers. 

And being a slave in Egypt in those days was about as low as one could go in the social scale. However, by a variety of circumstances, Joseph rose to become a very important man in Egypt a man we all would like to have known had we been living then.

Joseph was the man in charge of the rationing of food. He was the one to whom people applied for rations of food, and his own brothers came one day to get some food. Joseph recognized his brothers, but they did not recognize him. 

He saw that not all the brothers had come; one, his favorite, Benjamin, was not there with the others, and Joseph particularly wanted to see Benjamin. He gave his brothers food, but also gave them words of warning as they left his presence. They, and he, knew that the brothers must come again to get more food, and as they left Joseph his words rang in their ears, "Ye shall not see my face except ye bring your brother with you." Bringing the brother with them was the price of admission into the palace of the King; bringing the brother with them was the price of life itself! 22

a world of people vainly willing to pay the price

In that we may see a parable of our own world struggling for peace and prosperity and plenty, but not of going after the brother and bringing him with them. We seem to hear these very words sounding in our ears today. God seems to say to you and to me, to every Christian person, "Ye shall not see my face, ye shall not have the Kingdom come that you pray for every day, ye shall not have a just and a durable and a lasting peace ye shall not have any of these except ye bring your brother with you."
