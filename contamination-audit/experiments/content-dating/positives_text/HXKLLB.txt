THE ARTIST AT HIS FURNACE

XEIXEIXEIXEIXEIXEIXEIXEIXEIXEI The Master Potter of La Chappelle-aux-Pots ÉLIX AUGUSTE DELAHERCHE, hermit and philosopher, fashions pots and vases and vessels of pure outline in a little hut which crowns the pensive hillside of La Chapelle-aux-Pots, a tiny village in the red sand district of Armentières. Two hours away is Paris, but Paris is noisy and the men and women who disport themselves there are eaten up with passions and appetites that render them odious to this twentieth century Diogenes. As a result, the great potter and ceramist has seldom visited the center of the universe; and the shops where Fame is manufactured and Reputation made up know him not. 

From time to time interviewers have sought him out in his hermitage, as Alexander the Great is said to have sought out Diogenes in his cask, and, like Diogenes, he has told them to begone as they were shutting out his view of the sun.

And yet despite this reticence, this shunning of publicity, Fèlix-Auguste Delaherche has been a name to conjure with wherever connoisseurs of pottery, amateurs of kiln-made jewels, or lovers of art congregate. To possess one of Delaherche's Grés Flambés is to proclaim oneself one of the cognoscenti of modern ceramic art. There is nothing being turned out of the kilns anywhere that can compare in contour or in color with the alchemies that have come out of this modern Prometheus' oven. 

A short time ago Delaherche was prevailed upon by the Director of the Beauvais National Manufacture of Tapestries, to exhibit a dozen of his creations at the Exposition organized in Beauvais as an annual event and held at the Manufacture, at the City Hall, at the Beauvais Cathedral and at the Museum.

[4] At this Exposition one of Delaherche's vases was bought for exhibition here as a museum piece for the Palace of the Legion of Honor in San Francisco by the donors of the buildings, Mr. and Mrs. Adolph Spreckels. 

The exhibition is a unique conception, which consists in securing, from the Government on the one hand and from private owners on the other, tapestries and works of art hitherto guarded from public view. In one of these exhibitions there were assembled thirty Beauvais tapestries, sixteen priceless sixteenth century tapestries, emanating from Rheims Cathedral and portraying the history of the Virgin and the History of St. Rémi, and 

139 canvases from the brush of François Desportes, the famous animal painter of Louis XIV. This, together with some modern Gobelins tapestries and more than a hundred pieces of porcelain, loaned by the Sèvres factory, might well be calculated to throw into the shade the twelve comparatively small pieces exhibited by Delaherche in one of the rooms of the Beauvais Manufacture. As a matter of fact, a comparison may not be drawn between a tapestry and a piece of pottery, but the success attending this display of Delaherche's flamboyant vases and cups was such that the retiring potter was forced out of self-sought obscurity and loudly acclaimed as the greatest worker in clay and the greatest colorist through the transmutation of metals that ceramic art has produced since Bernard Palissy. 

The French Government which had officially recognized his talent many years ago by awarding him the Legion of Honor, promoted him to the rank of Officer of the Legion and the Paris newspapers sent their representatives to beard the veteran Maître-Céramiste in his flower-environed dungeon and force from him some expression of his art and aims. 

The newspaper men found a surly giant, a vigorous Ancient Mariner, garbed in overalls and potter's apron with a beautiful white beard and a florid complexion, dwarfing three small brick shacks one story high and connected like two small freight cars hitched behind a no less diminutive locomotive. The locomotive is the kiln, the "four" of the master which he lights once a year only and out of which come the earthen jewels that his mind's eye has conceived before the incubation has made them real. 

Delaherche admits that in the early days of his playing with fire, several results he obtained were due to chance. Later, when some of his calculations were wrong and the action of the fire on his metallic alloys sent back on a glaze or color that was not the one he started to get, he destroys the piece.

[5] "A quoi bon?" he says. "My pleasure is in making the fire do as I want. If

I want a certain purple tint it does not satisfy me to have it come out blue or green."

By long experiment he has gained knowledge that permits him to give to his enamel the iridescence that his eye has conceived. He knows the secret of pouring molten gold over his earthen vessels or of robing them with the varied hues of the peacock's feathers, or the delicate glow of the sunset; or he can stud them with sapphires and rubies that liquefy under the action of the heat and weep large sparkling tears down predetermined grooves to the deliberately chosen "renflement" or depression in the vase or goblet which his hands have turned.

Behind his atelier is a mound of broken débris which represents the tricks played upon him by too much heat or too little pigment, or the wrong proportion of two or more metals entering into the formula for indigo or sang-de-boeuf red or the four hundred different shades that this master of masters pours at will over the clay he fashions. 

In his early works, Delaherche found pleasure in decorating his pottery with ornaments composed from his study and observation of vegetal and animal life. Among the pieces exhibited by him as long ago as 1889 were a number of vases into the decoration of which he has introduced peacock feathers, flowers of every description, foliage and fruit. One of the most famous of his vases is engarlanded with maple leaves and is now the property of the Arts Décoratifs Museum; another is fashioned after a cluster of lilacs; another is decorated with clematis; another is inspired from horsechestnut leaves; another owes its adornment to holly leaves. 

Each of the pieces thus embellished both as to color and outline by resort to flowers or foliage or other extraneous ornament is deserving of the highest praise and stamps its author as a sculptor of great talent. In his later works however, Delaherche has abandoned ornament almost altogether. "It only hides the form," he says, and as his eye can see and his hand fashion vases and bowls and goblets that are intrinsically beautiful by virtue of form and outline only, no quarrel may be found with him by reason of it. 

Extreme sobriety and simplicity of style characterize his work as they characterize his life. He has the independence of Cyrano, and the famous tirade "Non Merci" might have been written for him. To a millionaire porcelain manufacturer who had sought to secure him as designer and super-

intendent of his plant by an offer of a hundred thousand francs, he pityingly replied: "Alas, Monsieur, you have the good fortune of being rich, and the misfortune of being nothing else." Delaherche does not understand how any one can work-do good work-except for the joy of working. 

Since 1904, he fashions only unique pieces, cups, vases, jars, bottles, dishes, pots of which one example only exists. These he works over lovingly, first over the naked shape which is always faultless in the purity of its lines, and again over its coloring which he evolves from strange chemical mixtures and metallic oxides. The clay rotates in front of him on its little circular platter and the hands, big burly massive hands, such as a giant like him must of necessity possess, go over it caressingly again and again. It assumes form, it is born under his eyes, under the pressure of his thumb. It is perfect and yet its creator is not satisfied but puts it back on the "tour" for a further fashioning, a finishing touch, another affectionate pat.

[7] Brieux in his play "Les Américans Chez Nous" describes this very characteristic trait of the French to take joy in the work of their hands. He puts in presence an American manufacturer who is all for "efficiency” and "quantity production" and the supremacy of machinery over "handwork," and a French workman who has tried to bend himself to the exigencies of automatic machinery but has not been able to do so. 

After railing at the cruelty of the inexorable machine which must be fed regularly, unending hour after hour with the same motion, the same speed, the laborer tells the American: "You see, Mr. Smith, every nation has its distinctive character. In France the laborer likes to know what he is doing; he wants to understand why he must do this or that detail of the job. 

He has pleasure in doing well what might easily be done badly if less care or skill were employed. He does not relish to be set at turning out things that cannot be wrong, that mechanically come out uniformly of one form and shape and degree of completeness. When the French workman has finished a thing, he likes to look at it, to pet it as it were. He says to himself, 'Yes, it's finished, I see that, it could go into the hands of the consumer just as it is, but I'll just give it another stroke or two with my file here, and another here, just for luck.' We don't do it to please the foreman, Mr. Smith, we do it to please ourselves, for our own satisfaction, for our self-respect." 

Félix Auguste Delaherche has this same respect for his work, this same conscientiousness but developed a hundred fold as it naturally would be in the soul of an artist as compared with the soul of a factory worker. His pile of broken pottery grows faster than his collection of signed and finished pieces. A pot that the fire has altered as to shape, even though it be splashed with the most beautiful enamel that the heart of the potter might desire, even though it might bring a high price if put into circulation, is unhesitatingly destroyed. Not only must the form be faultless, but the iridescent icing, the enamel, must also be of just the desired hue and brilliancy. Unless the two be as the master has willed, out it goes into the scrap heap. 

Félix Auguste Delaherche will not countenance the mention of his name in the same breath with that of Luca della Robbia. The modest and unpretentious Master Potter of Armentières is happier in the thought that squirrels and sparrows are not afraid of him than in having a parallel drawn between himself and the famous Florentine. Without being a mystic like the sixteenth century sculptor and ceramist, Delaherche is spiritual in his work. There is

[ ] no trace of materialism in anything that he does. He holds that the beauty of man-made things consist in the proportion of their parts and the harmony of their colors. "It is in something beyond the visible that we must seek for the essence of supreme beauty," he contends.

In a familiar causerie with a friend, he emitted the truism that "whenever profound truth is to be expressed you must have recourse to figure. 

When you can say all that you mean directly, be assured that it is perfectly trivial." When Delaherche wants to express his soul in clay and sapphire colored enamel, the finished product is never trivial. Always there is more to it than is seen by the corporeal eye. Besides the form, besides the color, there is a sentiment, an emotion, a thrill. "The eyes," says this poet-philosopher, "provide one instrument for seeing, but there are others. The most beautiful visions are those beheld in dreams. Close your eyes and the whole sky may open to you. Open them and you can only take in a few objects within a restricted area. But the things that your soul can perceive or your heart feel are immeasurable and limitless."

He gets an idea for a bowl, or a cup, from mental pictures evoked by the reading of poetry. Lamartine, Hugo, de Banville, Sully, Prudhomme, Verlaine, Rostand have all inspired him into translating their rhymed thoughts into some moulded form against which butterflies unfurl their painted wings or faience flowers of fantastic shape blossom in artistic disorder. It permits the happy conceit that one might send the Master Potter one's favorite poem and ask him to fashion a loving cup or flower-holder therefrom. Fancy a cup fashioned after this formula: "Blue were her eyes as the fairy-flax 

Her cheeks like the dawn of day, And her bosom white as the hawthorn buds 

That ope in the month of May."

For those who like dates and statistics it might be mentioned that Delaherche has been moulding pottery for fifty years; but his work shows no faltering and his hands are as full of vigor as his heart is full of courage and sunshine.

He works leisurely, as the mood strikes him, as the inspiration of the hour dictates, and accepts no commissions that carry with them a specifi-

[9] cation as to color, design, or any detail whatever. He will not set a price on any of his work in advance of its execution. His atelier and cottage are lost among trees and climbing vines and the birds come in at his window and sit around as he thumbs the clay of Armentières into fairy shapes. He is in tune with nature and children and dogs scamper after him. He is that rare thing under the sun, a good man and a happy one. "Fame," he answered a friend gently, "why run after it? If your work is good isn't that enough? Why must a man derive anything more out of his work than happiness. Why must he have joy and glory besides?" The friend replied to the octogenarian: "When a man has the heart of a boy, he cannot be expected to value Fame." 

And Delaherche has the heart of a boy.
