THE AUTHOR 

Miss Pauline Cammack, a missionary to the Indians in New Mexico for nearly ten years, is a native of Texas, having been born and reared in Waco. 

While a student in Baylor University, she volunteered for medical missions in China, and trained to this end during her last two years of undergraduate work. When it became impossible to continue her medical training, she took postgraduate work to prepare as a teacher. Then followed six years of teaching in the public schools of her state.

After completing work for the Master of Arts degree from Baylor in the summer of 1929, Miss Cammack accepted a teaching position in Montezuma College in New Mexico. Later when this school had to close, she taught for two years at the Normal (now Highlands) University in Las Vegas, which position she resigned to teach again in Montezuma when it was reopened. 

Of her present work she writes: "The Lord led me in a very definite way to come to the work among the Indians of New Mexico in December of 1934."

Tony Jojola, whose story is told in the first person on these pages, has been known to the author for a decade. He has worked with her on this book from the beginning, supplying information, checking facts and offering suggestions, so that he, “a first American,” is virtually a co-author.

CHAPTER I WHERE I LIVE 

"Tony, look how pretty the sunset is!" My sister Lupita was watching the sun sinking beyond our village of Isleta. "Yes," I replied, "and look how pretty the red chilies are on the white walls of the homes." 

I had just been thinking how glad I am that 

I am an American; in fact, one of the First 

Americans, because my ancestors were on the reception committee when the first white man landed on this continent. 

I am an Indian. I am glad that I am an Indian.

Not for anything would I want to trade places with anyone else.

I am a Pueblo Indian. My people have always been a peace-loving people at heart. In olden times they had to fight, but only when their homes were in danger from invaders. Today, my people are some of the most patriotic and loyal Americans to be found anywhere. I am proud of my people. "I wonder what our pueblo looked like many years ago before the Spaniards came," Lupita was saying. 13

"Where were you last night," I asked her, "when 

Ta-Ta and Teh-Eh were talking about that?" "Maybe I was looking after little Miriam. What did they say?"

ISLETA, THE HISTORIC 

Ta-Ta (father) and Teh-Eh (grandfather) had said that "Isleta" is a Spanish word, meaning "Little Island."

Most of the houses used to be in the northwest part of the present village, west of where the church is now. I think some of those old walls still standing there must be parts of those houses built several centuries ago. There was no plaza [open square] in the middle of the village. Our people who live here have for a long time been called Tiwas.

Our records are not very complete, but some of the old people say that a long time before the 

Spanish came, our ancestors used to live up on that high mesa northwest of here for protection from their enemies.

For some reason they moved over to the present location of our village. They had to have the water from the Rio Grande to irrigate their crops, like we do now. Teh-Eh said they used to grow corn, beans, squash and pumpkins. 1

I was interested in what Ta-Ta said about tools. "In the first place," he said, "they didn't have steel spades like we have today. They used to make their own spades by cutting off a tree and shaping up their shovels."

He said they did not have plows like we have today. They used to break the sod with wooden plows, and when the steel plow first came in, they did not seem to like it. 

It was funny what they said about wagons. 

Father said, "They didn't have wagons like ours, but they had two-wheel carts they made out of their own material. They made their wheels and shaped them some way out of wood. My grandfather used to tell me they would go over to Rio

Puerco and get wood from big cottonwood trees for these wheels. My grandfather was the first one to have a two-wheel wagon. He had to hitch an ox to it."

They used to have mills to grind their corn. 

Those mills were run by water. I wonder how the Indians at that time learned to make those things turn with water from the ditches. They had a few ditches around the village. Where the ditches were high enough for the water drop they put those grinders.

Our people have always been lovers of horses. 

According to Teh-Eh, the only use they had for horses then was to ride to chase rabbits, antelopes,

buffaloes, and to run races. They always raised horses for this purpose, instead of working them as we do now.

Today we hitch our horses to plows, and plow our fields. We hitch them to wagons and go to the mountains after wood. Some of the people go from here in their wagons to Albuquerque to trade. "I'm glad we have a pick-up truck and don't even have to use horses hitched to wagons, when we want to go to Albuquerque," Lupita said as she went into the house.

ISLETA, THE BEAUTIFUL 

About thirteen miles north of Isleta is Albuquerque, the largest city in New Mexico. Both of these places are located along the Rio Grande River.

Isleta is one of the four largest Indian pueblos. 

Over twelve hundred Indians live here.

Because Isleta is on the highway not far from 

Albuquerque, many tourists come to see where my people live. As these visitors come out the highway from the city, they see our pretty farms scattered along the road for a mile or two before they reach our pueblo.

way, as they turn off the highway, they soon come to the plaza of our village.

Hardly do these visitors stop their cars to see things that are strange to them, before Isleta boys and girls come running from all directions to sell their pottery and beaded moccasins as souvenirs.

At one shop on the plaza the visitors watch an 

Indian making silver jewelry and marvel at the numerous tools he uses. At the opposite corner of the plaza they go into a curio shop and watch an Isleta woman making pottery. It looks easy, and the only way to find out that it is not is to try it.

Many of the visitors take time to go through the largest building on the plaza, the Catholic church. The priest who lives there delights to take them on this tour, where they see many old pictures of Catholic saints, as he tells them something of the history of this church. This is about what he tells the tourists:

The famous Spanish invader Coronado and his expedition visited the pueblo region of what is now 

New Mexico in 1540. After he left, Franciscan friars built an important Catholic mission here with church, convent, and quarters for the local priest, by 1629. I say the friars built it. What

I mean to say is that the Indians built it, with stupendous labor, under the direction of the friars. 

This first Catholic church in my village was called

San Antonio de Isleta. During the Pueblo Revolution of 1680 this church was burned after the retreat of the Spaniards to El Paso. When the mission was rebuilt in 1709 by Fray Juan de la Pena, it was given the name of San Agustin de Isleta. It has adobe walls four feet thick, or nearly twice as thick as my arm is long. Each year now the Isleta Indians clean and repair the church, and have a colorful fiesta for St. Augustin on August 28. 

I once heard my father say that a number of years ago the men did all the repair work on the church, replastering the walls and everything. In 

1920, when my father was sheriff, the governor of our pueblo called a meeting of the men to plan this work. At this meeting it was decided that the women should do all this repairing because it was their kind of work. The men had let the church get in such bad condition that they did not know what to do about it. The women agreed. They have fixed the church ever since. 

Now all the men have to do is to prepare the mud. 

Women continue to do most of this repair work, especially on the inside. Some of the men who have learned how to plaster help work on the outside.

The priest also tells the tourists a legend something like this. When Coronado and his expedition returned to Mexico in 1542, Padre Juan de Padilla

and his brother stayed in this country as missionaries. While Juan de Padilla was trying to convert the Indians at Quivira he lost his life, a martyr to the cause. In some way his body was brought to Isleta and buried near the altar of this church. 

Each year this coffin with the remains of Juan de Padilla pushes itself up to the surface of the ground. Pieces of his clothes, the priest says, have worked remarkable cures. 

As I wondered about this legend one time when 

I heard it, I asked my father about it. My father is very careful about explaining things to me. He answered my question by saying that the Spanish people had a custom of bringing the body of a dead person to the nearest Catholic church to be buried. That was why they brought this Juan de

Padilla here to Isleta and buried him in the church. 66 "A long time ago," my father went on to say, "my grandfather was the lieutenant governor. He was notified by the priest that this coffin had come up to the level of the floor. He went over there to see. They had the coffin up on the surface all right. They said it had pushed itself up, but my grandfather didn't believe it. The coffin was rotten, and there was a body that had been put in there fresh. The clothes were not rotten, only in one little spot. "My grandfather didn't believe all they told the people. The priest used to tell them this body

came up every year, but it wasn't so. They put the coffin in the grave again. Some of the people believed what the priest told them, that Padilla came back after some clothing and some books. 

So they put some of these in when they reburied him."

After hearing the priest's stories and legends, and paying a contribution or fee to the church, the tourists get back into their cars and drive around our pueblo. We think it is a beautiful village, but it seems strange to people who are not accustomed to it. They find only a few small trees scattered about, here and there. Through our narrow, crooked, unpaved streets they drive, and look with interest and curiosity at our kivas, our clean adobe homes, and our oval-shaped outdoor ovens.

I should have mentioned only one kiva, as the one near the big irrigation ditch is the only one that most of the visitors see. 

One day as I was going home from school, a carload of tourists stopped near this kiva. Now a kiva is a sacred place to an Indian. It is a round house made of adobe. Most of it is down under the ground, and the only entrance is through a ladder leading down from the roof. Two of the women visitors in that car wanted to see what was in there, and they started to investigate. They meant no harm or discourtesy, but some of the

Indians living near there were watching and ran outside quickly and called to the women, "You can't go in there." The visitors withdrew with apologies, for they did not know that this is one of the places where the Isleta people have their secret worship ceremonies. They found out in this embarrassing way that this was one interesting place where outsiders are not allowed to go.

Visitors seem to find the outside of our homes interesting to see, and they take many pictures of them.

All pueblo homes have flat roofs. The thick walls are made out of adobe bricks. Sometimes our people just cut the bricks out of a swampy place, and spread them out in the sun to dry. 

Sometimes they mix adobe dirt with straw and water, which they mold into bricks in little boxes about 4x8x16 inches. When they are thoroughly dry they are almost as hard and serviceable as an ordinary brick.

Our homes are plastered inside and outside with white clay. We think they are very pretty, and the clay protects the adobe bricks from the rain. 

My pueblo is the only one where white clay is used for plastering the homes.

Our houses have two or more rooms, and as a rule the windows have small panes of glass. 

Some of these homes are built in a long row,

one after another, so close together that they seem like one long house.

No Indian family would think of being without its outdoor oven. Sometimes there are two ovens, a small one for family use, and a large one in which to bake for fiesta time. Sometimes there is a small play oven for the children. 

One time I watched my mother build one of these ovens. She made the square part at the bottom out of adobe. Then using some lava rock, she built the dome-shaped oven on this adobe foundation, and held it all together with adobe mud, old cans, and smaller rocks. Using some more adobe mud, she plastered it over smooth outside and inside. She left an opening in the front for a door, and a hole in the top of the dome to let the smoke through. Finally she plastered the floor and all over the outside again with white clay. Later when she used the oven, she closed the opening left for a door with a piece of wet gunny sack held over the door with a piece of tin propped with a board.

Now and then we invite some of the tourists to see inside our homes. Here they see that some of our furniture is like their own, but they also see that there are home-made beds and woolen mattresses alongside store-bought ones. There are heavy Navajo rugs on our white clay floors and

on our beds, too. Along with the Navajo handwoven blankets, we have some manufactured ones that the white man made. We have store-bought chairs, tables, radios, and sewing machines, too.

In some of our homes there are now electric lights. It is hard now to understand that some of the older people objected to these being brought into our pueblo. In our kitchens there are usually good wood-burning ranges, with oil stoves in some cases for summer use.

The inside walls of most of our pueblo homes are decorated with many pictures of Catholic saints. Frequently these are mounted in tin frames. 

Also there are crucifixes and different kinds of images of saints. In many of the homes there are racks hanging from one of the vigas, or crossbeams. On these racks hang additional Navajo rugs, store-bought shawls, and ceremonial mantas. 

Outside the main part of our pueblo the Government has some buildings. Until just a few years ago there was only one day school. Now we have two. The old building is used for the first few grades; the new building for the other grades through the ninth. The teachers are employed by the Government, and live in quarters provided at the schools. Also the Government field nurse for our pueblo lives on the school grounds at the old school.

ISLETA, MY HOME 

In our pueblo when a couple gets married their people give them some land. The woman gets two or three acres from her parents, and the man gets the same from his parents. Also they ask the governor for about two acres of land from community property. The governor gives them this land, but if they do not cultivate it within a reasonable period of time he takes it away from them. They can buy more land, too. That is to say, one Indian can buy land from another Indian, but no white man can buy land in an Indian village or on an Indian reservation.

When I was a small boy our family had a home inside the pueblo. There were only two rooms in this house for my parents and five children. It was located on a narrow, winding street, over on the south edge of our village. A few years ago they sold this place to another Indian, and moved out to our summer home on one of our farms. 

When we first moved out of the pueblo to the farm to live all the year around, our house had only one room and a porch. Gradually we have built on to the house, until now it has four rooms. 

Near our house are some giant cottonwood and tall poplar trees growing along the irrigation ditches. We are very proud of them. There is a large orchard of peach, pear, plum, apple, and

cherry trees. A big patch of alfalfa furnishes some of the feed for our stock. We have horses, a cow, and some pigs. My mother also raises chickens and turkeys. 

All summer, when I am home from boarding school, I help my father with his farm work. Our land is not all at one place. On our other farms we grow hygira and more alfalfa; also, corn, chili, and several other vegetables.

When I come in from the fields in the evening, I find my mother still working. I think she works all of the time.

Sometimes I find her making up a big tub-full of amole suds. To do this, she shakes roots of soap-weed or yucca in a tub of water until a thick, heavy lather is formed. In these fine suds she washes our Navajo rugs and our woolen garments. 

She also prepares soap in this way for washing our hair. 

One evening, when Na-Na was getting a tub of suds ready, my little sister, Miriam, who had not yet learned to talk much, went after every member of the family one by one, and led each to the suds for a head-washing! A white lady was visiting Lupita that evening, but Miriam did not slight her. She took her by the hand and led her to the tub of amole suds, too! 

I am glad when I come in from the fields on my mother's baking day, and find her taking

out those good loaves of bread and those fruit pies from our outdoor oven; better still, when 

I find her making fry bread. I think Na-Na is the best cook in Isleta anyway. I can hardly wait until she gets supper finished, and I rush to get cleaned up to eat.

It is a happy time for me when supper is ready, and we can all sit down to a meal of chili, pinto beans and meat stew or fried potatoes cooked with eggs, with plenty of my mother's good bread. 

Many times we have tortillas instead of ovenbaked bread. Then for dessert we have some of her fruit pies, or cookies, or canned fruit which she has put up from our orchard. Not every boy has a mother who can cook like my mother. 

When our grandfather comes out to our house many times he tells us stories after supper, and we never fail to look forward to these evenings. 

He is eighty-eight years old, and remembers many interesting stories to tell. I wonder if I shall ever know as many wonderful tales as he does. 

Our people love beautiful things. I remember a story Teh-Eh told us about what some men did one time to get some pretty things they wanted. 

Here is what he told us.

In the olden days, three men from Isleta went out to the Hopi villages in Arizona to trade their burros, bluing and linen for mantas. When these three Isleta men were returning home, after travel-

ing two days, they stopped to spend the night, and prepared supper. Just as they were ready to eat, along came a young Navajo Indian on a fine pinto horse. On the horse were a new saddle and bridle, and also a beautiful Navajo saddle blanket.

The Tiwa Indians and the Navajos were great enemies, but when these three Pueblo men saw what beautiful property this Navajo Indian owned, they craftily invited him to eat with them. The surprised Navajo accepted their invitation. As they were eating, the three Tiwas planned how they could get this bridle, saddle and blanket.

Soon one of the Pueblo men struck the Navajo on the head and killed him. Then they took him up to some high cliffs, stood him up at the edge of an opening in the walls, and turned him loose. 

They removed the saddle, bridle and rug from the horse, slapped him on the back and let him go. Then they came home, happy with their booty, and with no remorse over the way it had been acquired. 

Teh-Eh's grandfather was a rich man. He had many sheep. One time when Teh-Eh was a boy he went with some sheepherders to the Navajo country. They took his grandfather's herd of sheep to Alamo, forty or fifty miles away. They were there during lambing season, that is the season when the little lambs are born.

There was a Pueblo Indian in the group who

grew up with the Navajos, because he had been captured by them when he was small. He knew the Navajo customs. He knew that when the 

Navajos bury a dead person, they always put their turquoise beads and their richest clothing on the body to bury it.

This captive man told the herdsmen to go and get the body of a Navajo man who had died recently. They did this, but when they located it they did not find anything of value on it. Somewhat disappointed, they put the body back again. 

That night something strange happened to the people at the camp. The sheep became frightened and rushed into camp. They ran over the men and all the camp equipment. The herdsmen thought the dead Navajo had appeared and terrified the sheep. "This happened pretty near every night," said

Teh-Eh, "until this captive man, who told us he had been dragged by his feet by the dead Navajo, ran off from the herd." 

Here is another story grandfather told one time.

Long ago these people used to go out and herd sheep, and they used to travel a distance. Three of these herders had one burro on which they had packed their food. On their way, they spent several nights. One night they decided to practice

Indian medicine man way. One of them sat down and sang, and the other two got up and wanted

to doctor the burro, because he was tired, to give him strength. While they were practicing, one of them went out to chase the bad spirit. Another of them went up in a tree to ride a dry branch.

While he was riding that branch, it broke off, and he fell. He went back to the campfire, and told his partner to go out there and cure that burro. The partner went back of the burro, and the burro kicked him, leaving him half dead.

Instead of curing the burro, both doctors had been injured. It was surely a tough contest with the bad spirit there.

Teh-Eh never runs out of stories. Many times we wish that he would leave his home in the village where he stays all alone and come to live with us. 

ISLETA AT PLAYTIME 

Before we moved out of our village to our present ranch home we used to have many playmates. After school in the afternoons and after supper in the evenings we would play games. 

Sometimes we would just watch others as they played.

In the early spring, usually in March, at the time the men are cleaning out the irrigation ditches the game of shinny is often played. The boys play shinny after school in the afternoons, and

after the men come in from their ditch work and have supper sometimes they play it in the evenings. 

Shinny is played with a ball and crooked sticks, curved at the bottom like an "L" or "J." Sometimes the ball is made out of an old stocking stuffed with rags and then wound with twine. 

Once on the way home from school we stopped to watch a game of shinny. The boys divided into two teams, one from the Black Eyes Clan, and the other from the Red Eyes Clan. They began playing at the center of the plaza. One boy from each team put his stick on the side of the ball, and both together tossed the ball up in the air. Like in the game of soccer, each team tried to get the ball with their curved sticks and take it to their side.

The Red Eyes took the ball south and kept on going. They got it close to their goal, and made their goal at a place between the silversmith shop and one of the houses near there. 

Then they started from the center again, and kept on playing. Every time the Black Eyes failed to put their ball on their goal in the Catholic church yard. They were always beaten. 

It was getting late. The sun was going down, and all of us started home. The Red Eyes knew some of the girls were Black Eyes, and thumped the Black Eyes because they were beaten. It is a custom for the members of the winning team

to thump the foreheads of the clan members of the losing team.

That night after supper they made an Indian dance. The Black Eyes had to take charge because they were the losers. Every night during March they had long dances. When I mention a dance, I mean an Indian dance, not a white man's dance. 

They are not alike at all. 

Another game we play is the game of the Three 

Sticks. Boys play this game during the winter months. It may be played with any number of players, but we usually have five. 

Around a circle about twenty inches in diameter are arranged thirty small stones in groups of ten. 

The ten stones are placed one after another along the circle rather close together. The spaces between the groups of ten stones are called "rivers." 

In the center of the circle is a larger flat stone about the size of a small plate, on which three sticks are to be dropped by each player in his turn. 99 These sticks are prepared by splitting down the middle a stick as large around as a broom handle. 

Each one is two and a half inches long, flat on one side and round on the other. The flat side is referred to as the "face" of the stick.

The players have to begin at one of the rivers and go all the way around the thirty rocks back to the starting point. The first player to reach this river wins the game. Each player's progress

around the circle is according to the number of points he scores in turn as he drops the three sticks. These sticks are held firmly in the hand a few inches above the large rock and allowed to drop on the rock perpendicularly.

Scores are counted according to the way the sticks fall. If all three sticks fall face down, the player advances ten rocks and is given another turn. All three sticks falling face up count five; two face up and one down count three; one face up and two down count two.

Each player's progress is indicated by his marker, a short stick about the size of a small pencil, laid between the rocks. 

If one player comes to the same rock as another, the first loses his score and has to start over again. 

This game is sometimes played on the mound.

This mound is an interesting place to me. It is near my grandfather's house and not far from the plaza. Higher than a man's head and about one hundred and fifty feet around at the bottom, it is made up of ashes, cans, broken pottery and dirt. My father said there used to be about three of these mounds, but now this is the only one left. 

On this mound the boys and young men assemble during some ceremonial or feast day. At night, especially, they go there to pass their time and entertain themselves. Here they smoke cigarettes

CHAPTER II MY SCHOOL DAYS 

My schooling is different from that of most Pueblo Indians, because mine has all been in 

Government schools. Most of my friends, in addition to their Government school training, have been taught in the ways of the Indian religion, but because my parents are Christians I have not had to have this kind of training.

PRE-SCHOOL DAYS 

Before I started to school and learned to read, I remember that I used to hear many stories and loved them. Grandfather never seemed to run out of our kind of fairy stories to tell us. Most of them were about animals. I remember one story especially that I used to ask Teh-Eh to tell me over and over again. This was my favorite story as he told it to me then. I still like it and some day hope to tell it to my own grandchildren. 

One day a Serpent was creeping along on the ground when a big rock fell on him, and he was trapped right there. Soon a Little Red Fox came 35

by and saw what had happened, and he felt sorry for the Serpent. The Little Red Fox got very busy and helped the Serpent, and soon he had him released from the rock.

Now when the Serpent was set free he wanted to show his appreciation by eating the Little Red 

Fox. The Little Red Fox said, "No, that's not fair, when I did so much for you, and now you want to eat me up. We had better go over to ask that Ox and see if this is right!"

So they went to the Ox. The Ox was old and had already been turned out by his master, and he felt very badly about it. The Little Red Fox asked the Ox to decide between them.

The Ox said when he was young he was stout enough to do anything for his master but when he came to be old his master thought he was worthless. So he was just turned out on the plains. 

That was the reward his master gave him. "You also did good to the Serpent," said the Ox to the 

Little Red Fox, "so now the Serpent is entitled to eat you up.' 99 

But the Little Red Fox was not satisfied with what the Ox decided, so he said, "Let's ask that old horse and see what he has to say." The Serpent agreed and they appealed to the Horse, but the 

Horse gave the same reply as the Ox, for he, too, had been turned out by his master. 

Still the Little Red Fox was not satisfied. So

they decided to appeal to an old Coyote. When they came to the Coyote they presented the matter and began to hold court. The Coyote wanted to see nothing but the truth. So he told the Serpent to lie down the way he had been lying with the big rock on his back to show the proof. 

Then the Coyote asked the Serpent, “Is that the way you were lying?" and the Serpent said, "Yes." "Now you just stay there because you are not grateful," the Coyote said. The Little Red Fox and the Coyote left the Serpent there, and went away rejoicing as two good friends. 

On their way, they caught a Little Rabbit and sat down to bake it, but the Coyote planned to eat the Rabbit all by himself. While the Little 

Red Fox was doing the baking he told the Coyote to go up on the high hill near there and see whether there was a big rain coming. While he was gone Little Red Fox ate all the Rabbit and ran away as fast as he could.

When the Coyote returned and found Little Red 

Fox had gone away and left him nothing but bones, he said, "Whenever I find him I will surely make a Rabbit out of him." So he began to chase the 

Little Red Fox. He found him leaning against a cliff. 

The Little Red Fox said to the Coyote, "This cliff is going to fall over, and I'm holding it. If you will help me, we will both hold it up, because

if it falls everybody is going to be killed." So the Coyote forgot that he was going to make a

Rabbit out of the Little Red Fox. 

"All right," he said, "I'll help you."

While they were holding the rock the Little Red 

Fox said, "I'll go and see if this cliff is going to fall over soon." He just slipped away. When the 

Coyote got tired of holding the cliff he decided the Fox had played a trick on him again.

So he ran and chased after him until he found the Little Red Fox at a Bee-hive. "Now I'm going to kill you," said the Coyote, "because you have been lying to me.' 99 "Just wait," said the Little Red Fox, "I'm the teacher here. I have a class, and these are my students in here. If you help me, we are going to have a rich lunch." "All right, then, I'll help you," said the Coyote. "While I rest, you put this class to work. Just take this stick and poke it in the hole and you will hear the class sing, or they will read." 

Then the Coyote poked the stick in the hole and the whole swarm of Bees just beat him and chased the Coyote away. 

The Little Red Fox went home.

In Indian stories, other animals frequently play jokes on the Coyote, as he is considered to be very stupid.

THE GOVERNMENT DAY SCHOOL 

I could not always sit and listen to my grandfather tell stories. All too soon the time came for me to attend school. Yet I was anxious in a way to begin school, because Ignacio and Lupita were already going to the Isleta day school.

When I first started to school we were living in the village, and I did not have far to walk from home to school. 

My first year we had play school. Most of the boys and girls did not know how to speak any English, but we knew that we must learn to speak it. In playing games, we would have a little conversation, and gradually we picked up the language like that.

Learning to speak English seemed so strange to me that I thought I would never learn it. But all of us finally learned how to talk in this "foreign" language.

In the second grade we began to learn how to read. Then we began to study arithmetic. Later we studied history and geography. All of these subjects gave us practice in thinking in our new language, for all of the lessons were in English. 

It seems hard on little children, but they learn quickly.

Besides our classroom studies, we were also

taught in grade school how to do things, and we liked that, too.

In the fifth grade we had a carpenter's shop and learned to use tools. First we built a house for the chickens. Soon we could make picture frames with carved designs on them. Later I made a tray out of pine, and I thought it was beautiful. 

Beginning in the fifth grade the girls had arts and crafts, too. They learned to weave and studied home economics.

Since the Indian men of my village are all farmers, and their sons needed to learn, the boys had a little garden at school. We grew onions, peas, lettuce, tomatoes, cabbage, corn, and radishes.

Sometimes it was not much fun at school, because in the classrooms and on the playgrounds our school mates used to tease us, especially my sisters, for attending Sunday School.

In Lupita's class one day they were reading a story where the Queen of Sheba was mentioned. 

The teacher was a Christian, and had her Bible with her. She called on one of the children in the class to find a verse in the Bible that said something about the Queen of Sheba, and gave the reference in the Old Testament. One by one they took turns, but none was able to find the verse. 

When Lupita's turn came last, one of the boys said, "Well, she's a Protestant; she'll find it." "That sounds interesting," said another boy who

listened so attentively when Lupita found the passage and read it. "Why don't you come to Sunday School? You'll learn," Lupita told him. Then some of the boys started making fun of him. 

They teased Lupita, too. “We'll make you give up," they told her, but that just showed that they did not know my sister. 99 "They used to call me all kinds of names,"

Lupita told me. "Hallelujah was one of their favorites. They said all the Protestants were going to hell, and that I would go to hell, too, if I went to that Sunday School. Finally, when they found they couldn't make me give up, they quit teasing me and calling me names. I didn't get mad about it, and after a while they made friends with me."

Our day school at Isleta goes through the ninth grade. After I finished the eighth grade, I decided that I wanted to go to the Government Indian boarding school at Santa Fe. 

My big brother Ignacio had chosen to attend the Santa Fe Indian School instead of going to Albuquerque Indian School, where most of the students from Isleta continue their school work. 

He wanted to be entirely away from his own tribe, as he knew he could learn the English language better where there was no one with whom he could speak Tiwa outside of school

hours. Ignacio went to school in Santa Fe several years, and then transferred to a public school a few miles from our home, before he volunteered to serve in the Army. 

Lupita followed Ignacio. She went to Santa Fe

Indian School, too, and had been attending there three years when I decided to transfer to that school. The next year after I went to Santa Fe, Evangelista, our sister just younger than I, transferred to the Indian School there too, even though our parents had hoped she would want to go to Albuquerque Indian School, where she would get to come home often.

Some people think it strange that Indian boys and girls go away to boarding school while they are so young, but we do not think it is strange at all. It is just the way we do it. 

MY SCHOOL DAYS IN SANTA FE 

Having heard Ignacio and Lupita talk so much about their school, I was curious to know what kind of a place it was.

Therefore when I went there about the first of September, I was interested to see a large, beautiful campus with well-kept lawn, over which were scattered a variety of trees, including giant cottonwoods, maples and Chinese elms, and colorful

flower beds of cosmos, marigolds and gladiolas. 

Most of the buildings on the campus are of brownplastered adobe or brick in Spanish-Pueblo style. 

Everyone who sees it thinks it is a very beautiful campus and that Indians are fortunate to have such a nice school.

Indeed we are proud of our campus at the Santa Fe Indian School. Like most of the other students who come to this school, I guess I was a little homesick right at first, but I soon came to love it.

Our Federal Government opened this school for Indians a little over fifty years ago, we are told. 

They used to offer just the lower grades, but gradually during about the last fifteen years they have done away with these lower grades and added the upper grades. Now they have grades seven through twelve, and frequently offer some postgraduate work.

A few years ago students used to come from many tribes in many states and even Alaska to attend our school. In more recent years, they have come mainly from our own state and Arizona. 

Our Government provides free tuition for all of the students, and we work for our room and board at various types of institutional jobs, such as kitchen and dining room work and cleaning buildings and campus. These work assignments do not last more than two hours a day, and some

are less, according to the job. My job has been to clean part of the boys' building each morning. 

The subjects we study are very much the same subjects that are offered by our public schools. 

The only language offered is English, which is required for us but of course does not meet foreign langauge requirement for college entrance for the few students who elect to go to college. 

Algebra and geometry are not offered as separate courses, but some of the fundamentals are included in a course of general mathematics. No science laboratory courses are included. 

There are these two further exceptions. Because vocational studies are featured in our school, we do not cover as much ground in our academic subjects as do the same studies in public school. 

Also many of our academic studies are definitely related to our vocational studies. 

For instance, our general science course which we study in the ninth grade stresses agriculture. 

Tenth grade arithmetic gives special applications related to our various arts and crafts. 

Our school gives fine training in arts and crafts and various trades. Before graduation each student is required to master at least four. 

On our registration day at the beginning of each school year we are given an interesting list of vocational subjects from which to choose. These subjects are grouped in six main divisions: Voca-

tional Shop Work, Drawing and Painting, Native 

Crafts, Silversmithing, Tanning and Leathercraft, and Woodwork.

Vocational shop work includes a rather large variety of studies, such as carpentry, shoe repairing, general mechanics, auto mechanics, plumbing, farming, house painting, and home economics. 

During my first year at Santa Fe I studied electricity and agriculture. Later in the school year 

I learned how to operate the big boilers at the power house. In our course in agriculture I learned how to butcher and cut meat.

I am sure that our Government realizes that most Indians are talented in art, because of the wide variety of studies offered in art and in crafts based on Indian art. A separate building on our campus is provided for art alone, called the Art Studio, and another building near it, the Crafts Building, has numbers of rooms where the various crafts are taught. Visitors frequently take time for interesting trips through both of these buildings to observe our distinctive Indian art in all of its various forms.

At the Art Studio, students learn to do water color and mural paintings.

In the Crafts Building, the course in native crafts includes the preparation of wool involving carding, spinning, washing, and dyeing; various types of weaving including hand weaving; Indian

embroidery, and the making of Indian dolls. 

The leather craft shop in the same building gives instruction in tanning hides and skins, and in making such articles of leather with Indian designs as billfolds, belts, book covers, albums, and key rings.

In the woodwork shop students learn to do beautiful cabinet and furniture making and wood carving. Here again Indian designs are used to good advantage on small articles such as book ends and picture frames, as well as on the furniture and cabinet work. 

The silversmith shop proved to be of much interest to Lupita. During the school year of 

1942-'43, because many of the silversmiths were serving in the armed forces, the school began teaching girls to learn the silversmith trade for the first time. I asked Lupita what there was about it that interested her so, and by the time she was through telling me I was interested, too. 

I never had wondered who started silversmithing, or when, but Lupita said in 1850 a man named Cassiba came up from Old Mexico and taught some of the Navajos out on their reservation how to work silver. The first Indian Pueblo that was taught was Zuni. 

Here are some more things Lupita said. "The first thing we learned was how to name all the tools. There are more than one hundred and

fifty tools a silversmith uses. After we learned all the tools we started with copper to make copper bracelets. If they came out good, and stamped good, then we could make a plain silver bracelet with designs on it. "After that, we learned the seven parts to a ring the stone, the bezel, the silver plate, the shank, the prong, the rain-drops or silver shots, the twisted wire. After we learned the parts to a ring, then we made a ring. And we had to estimate the price of it, too. The silver was 75 cents per ounce. We weighed it in grams and ounces. A ring is $2.50 an ounce. The stone was figured by the weight and the kind of turquoise or stone it was. The labor cost for the bracelet is $1 per ounce.'

Lupita is handy with her hands, too, and has learned to do some beautiful embroidery work.

The first year she was at Santa Fe she made some dresser scarfs with Indian designs on them. Then she made some pillow tops and some Pueblo Indian dolls. I do not know much about materials and such things, but Lupita said the material she used for the pillow tops and dresser scarfs was called monk's cloth. The girls had to make up the designs themselves, and they used wool yarn for the embroidering. Some of these designs are thunder birds, Indian crowns and Kachina dancers.

Because of such interesting studies offered, it

is plain to see that our school days in such a place are not the least dull. One of the main difficulties is that we have to work extra hard if we are working for college entrance.

Some of the students might become bored with just school work, though, interesting as it is. And so each year our school provides a varied program of recreation and sports. We really do have some wonderful athletes. 

For those who wish to play tennis, excellent courts are provided, and tournaments are held in their season. 

Other seasonal sports, such as football, basketball, baseball, indoor baseball, soccer, and track, furnish much exercise as well as enjoyment for us. Usually both interclass and interscholastic contests are arranged.

In each dormitory, of which there are three, including two for girls and one for boys, radios and pianos are provided, besides table games such as Chinese checkers and pick-up sticks. 

In the large school gymnasium there is a scheduled time each week for picture shows and roller skating.

Also in the gymnasium are frequently held school dances. These are of the ballroom variety of the white man and not the ceremonial dances of the Indians. Every student is required to learn how to dance and to attend these school dances.

I am sorry we have this rule. It is especially hard for those of us who are trying to live the Christian life, because we do not want to learn things that will harm our Christian growth and influence.

RELIGIOUS TRAINING AT SCHOOL 

A Government regulation permits Indian boarding school students under eighteen years of age upon entering school to sign for religious instruction of their parents' choice or preference. From then on only upon written permission from the parents may a transfer be made from that religious group to another. When a student reaches the age of eighteen he may then have the privilege of making his own choice without written permission from his parents.

Among those attending our group for religious instruction are not only Baptists but Protestants of several denominations and some who are not yet Christians.

The services which may be attended by the students of our group are Sunday School and worship at the First Baptist Church down town, leadership class on Sunday evening, and Thursday night religious instruction. 

Not many of the boys attend our Sunday morning services. Some do not go because of work

duties and room inspection. Most of them, how. ever, do not seem to be interested in attending even if they were not prevented otherwise. Very few of these boys are Christians. They are my opportunity, and sometimes it frightens me to think about it. 

On Thursday nights we have the best attendance.

Here we like to sing. We have preaching sometimes, and Bible study. Some of the boys and girls who have never had a Bible in their homes become interested in studying God's Word in our classes. We do both individual and class Bible study at this instruction hour. Frequently the students ask questions about the Scripture, and many times they ask for Bibles to have for their own use. 

Because many students of our group have not had the advantage of any Bible training before they come to study with us, our Bible study classes on Thursday nights are planned to meet their needs as well as the needs of those of us who have had previous Bible study. 

To meet these needs, our classes use graded

Bible study work-books. Even the Old Testament studies present Jesus in every lesson. To answer questions and complete statements in these workbooks, each one of us has to really "search the 

Scriptures" to see what the Bible says. I enjoy studying God's Word in this way, and I believe the other students do too.

We are glad when our Christian students become interested in leading their friends to know Christ as their Saviour. This is not easy to do, but sometimes it is done on our campus. 

Lupita told me about her experiences in trying to interest two of her roommates. "Because of the school rule," Lupita said, “Margaret could not come with me to our instruction, since she had not signed up for it, but she would listen with me to Gospel messages over the radio. 

She would read every story paper I would bring and also my Bible. I knew she was becoming interested, but soon she had to return to her home before she became a Christian. She took with her my book-mark with the Twenty-third Psalm on it, and also a Gospel of John. "After Margaret left," continued Lupita, "Mary was my roommate. She, too, became interested in reading my story papers and tracts. She learned many of the songs we sing. Her favorite was 'Blessed Assurance." "

Lupita feels that both of these roommates will become Christians some day. 

One of Lupita's Christian friends, Hellon, who has already graduated from our school and gone into nurses' training, also did personal work with many of her roommates and friends. Hellon led several of these to accept Jesus as their personal Saviour. I think some of the experiences she had

are wonderful, and I asked her to tell some of them. She wrote about them in a letter, and I shall just copy part of her letter so you can read it exactly as she wrote it. She does not brag and never would have written this unless she had been asked to do so. "When I was a freshman in high school," she wrote, "I roomed with a Pima girl from Arizona and a Navajo girl from New Mexico. We were close friends, but we looked on life differently. 

I prayed for them and encouraged them to go to church and at night we kneeled together and prayed. We often read passages from the Bible and in that way we were made more conscious of the things we did. Neither of these girls made a definite decision for Christ while they were at school, but I pray for them, hoping that those seeds planted so long ago will flourish and grow, and that the girls may learn to love God as we do. 

I want them to accept Christ as their personal Saviour." 

Another year Hellon's roommate was a Hopi girl from Arizona. Kathryn became interested in the Bible, and often the two girls would read passages of Scripture together before going to bed. 

Many times she would ask questions about our religion. "She wouldn't come right out and say she believed," Hellon wrote, "but many times I would

walk into our room and find her kneeling beside her bed praying. I don't remember a night passing during our stay together that she missed praying, or a meal she would eat without praying first. 

I know at heart Kathryn is a Christian, and with a little more help she will build her faith and show publicly she is one of God's children. I am praying for her."

At our school the boys and girls get to know each other, and sometimes they have a little time to talk. Hellon was the kind of girl who used many opportunities to win boys, as well as girls, to Christ while they were on the campus. 

One year she went with a Laguna boy named 

Joseph, and she helped him to find Christ. About the time of his graduation he was converted and then baptized a few weeks later. "He is in the 

Army Air Corps now," Hellon said. “Every time he writes, he says he is so happy that he found Christ and that he wants to help others to know Jesus as he knows Him. Joe changed from a life of pleasure to a life of one of the most conscientious Christians I know. He feels his love for

God deeply, and he wants to help others to find the love and joy that only a Christian can know."

Some of the boys and girls who are genuinely converted want to be baptized and become members of a Baptist church. When they make this decision, they are baptized by the pastor of the

First Baptist Church at Santa Fe, and become members of that church, if they can secure the written permission from their parents in case they are under eighteen years of age.

We now have Christian boys and girls in several of the pueblos, and Indian boys in the armed forces have served in all parts of the world. They often write back saying how happy they are that they found Christ while they were in school, frequently adding that they want to help others to know Jesus also.

Of course everyone is interested in the boys who serve in the armed forces, and there is not an Indian alive who is not proud of the record made by Indians. I heard of one who was turned down by the draft board for some physical reason. 

He was so disappointed that he went back to the board six different times, and every time he went he had a different name! He was so anxious to go that finally the board let him in. That is the kind of boys we Indians are! And we make good loyal soldiers, too.

One thing that concerns some of us who are Christians is that so few Indians know anything about being saved. I guess most of them never even heard of such a thing. It frightens me when

I think of many of those who serve our country being in such danger, and not being able to pray because nobody ever taught them anything about

Jesus. I have known how to pray nearly all my life, and I have known about Jesus. It seems strange and sad that so many Indian boys have never heard of the important things I have known most of my life.

In our religious education classes we talked about the boys from our group who were in the armed service. We liked to talk about them, and read letters from them, and wonder about the ones who did not write. The fact that we studied the Bible together, just a small group of us, made them seem pretty important to us. 

We love to sing. Before these boys left us, while the chaperones were taking the girls back to their dormitories following our religious education classes at night, we boys liked to gather around the piano and sing more songs than we could find time to sing during our one hour of instruction. Sometimes a tenor would strike a wrong note, and then we would all tease him and laugh together about our mistakes. We would stay there as long as anyone would play the piano for us and listen to our singing and chatter.

Some of our boys in Alaska, in Africa, in the 

Solomon Islands, loved to sing with us when they were here. We wonder if they ever get a chance over there to sing the hymns we knew they loved. 

And we wonder if they have found friends who loved those same hymns; friends like we were at

school in Santa Fe, when we were all together there.

We would try to keep in touch with these boys. 

Although some of them did not write to us, many of them did. At our meetings we prayed for them. 

We would write to them and send them the devotional magazine, Open Windows, and a little booklet just for those in service, called On Duty for 

God and Country.

Many of these boys wrote back to tell how these booklets helped them to learn more about God and live closer to Him. One of our boys serving with the Marines in the South Pacific area wrote saying that others in his tent were reading and enjoying these booklets as much as he was. 

Sometimes I recall the stories my grandfather tells, and then I look at our school in Santa Fe and compare things as they are today to the way they were when he was a boy. Then Indians hated and killed each other, not because of something they had done exactly, but because they were of different tribes.

Now Indians from many tribes live at our school in Santa Fe. They share rooms and even clothes. 

They eat and work and play together, seldom even recalling that their grandfathers once hated each other, maybe even killed each other.

Since there is little of this former hatred and fear in our school, it is a wonderful place for boys

and girls to find Christ. They come from many villages and reservations where they have never seen or heard a missionary, nor heard that a person is saved by faith in Jesus.

At school these can hear the message of salvation if they come to our group for religious instruction. Those not in our group can hear the 

Gospel, too, because we who are Christians have many opportunities to talk to our school mates about Jesus. These are opportunities that our missionaries can not have, since their work is limited to students of our own group by a Government regulation.

If we are faithful as Christian students in witnessing to these others we can win many of them to Christ. After they leave school they may not have another chance to learn about Him.

CHAPTER III HOW MY PEOPLE WORSHIP 

My people are very religious. For centuries they have been worshipping in the Indian way, passed down from the older to the younger people in each generation. In their worship they have tried to please many gods.

About four centuries ago the white people from Spain came up through Mexico, bringing their priests and their religion with them to my people. 

At first the priests tried to get my people to do away with their pagan ceremonials, but in this they did not succeed. My people kept up their ceremonials and became Catholics at the same time. Now most of them attempt to worship in both ways.

After I was born some missionaries came to bring the Bible to my people.

BEFORE THE WHITE MAN CAME 

Long before people of other races came to live in this country we Indians had our kivas, or places of worship. In these places my people carry on the secret practices of their Indian religion. Here, too, the Indian dances are prepared. 58

When a study is made of the religions of other nations of the world, most of their beliefs can be found out, but not so with the religion of my people. We read in books many ridiculous things about Indians' religious beliefs, but our people just go right on in the practice of their religion, as they have done for centuries, unaffected by what outsiders think or write about the Indians' worship. 

However, some facts about my people's religion are known by outsiders.

As I have said before, my people worship many gods. Important in their religion is the worship of the sun. Early in the spring in my village races are held. Men and boys run swiftly to give the sun strength to reach its zenith. With our people it is a serious and important occasion, and one they would not dare to fail to observe.

Once a highly educated man in our village was talking to my father. "I believe in that sun," he said, pointing to the sun. "I can get a priest, or bishop, or minister by the coat tail and turn him around, but I can't get hold of that sun and turn him, and make him go. the other way."

He worshipped the sun, the strength of it, and the power. He did not want to worship anything he could control. Religion is involved in almost every phase of our lives, even to the naming and dividing of the clans.

There are several different clans in our village. 

Membership in one of them originates first through a person's mother. For example, my mother belonged to the Blue Corn Clan through her mother and grandmother. Therefore I belonged first in the Blue Corn Clan. Besides the 

Black Eyes and the Red Eyes, which are social clans, there are clans named for the particular color of corn which is sacred to that group in their worship of the sun. A different direction is represented by each in this order: East, white corn; 

North, black corn; West, yellow corn; South, blue corn.

In certain ceremonies some of the older men fast for several days, saying they are giving their lives for the people whom they call kim-oo-weh-een, "my children" or "my sons." 99 

Our grandfather is head of the Red Eyes Clan. 

Not long ago my younger brother and sister, Ernest and Andrea, were going to spend the night with grandfather. They are scared of the gourd rattles, drums and images he uses in the Indian religion, for we do not use them in our home. "How can Teh-Eh stand it by himself with all of those things?" asked Ernest. "It's too scarey and spooky in there."

Spooky or not, those objects are precious to his heart and essential to his worship.

According to their Indian way, our people be-

lieve that corn protects and keeps them safe. It is not unusual for a mother to put an ear of corn of her clan color near her baby's cradle, believing that it will keep the baby from harm. 

Our people, especially the men, are lovers of corn meal which has been ground by a woman on a metate.

Every man who is a believer in the Indian way carries a little leather bag filled with corn meal. 

This they use to feed their gods and to throw offerings of the corn meal to the rising sun. 

One day my mother was grinding corn for the baby chickens with the meat chopper. This ground corn was on the table, when a man, who was one of the believers in the Indian way happened to come in to our kitchen. "I am lucky to come just at this certain time when they have the meal ground,” he said.

So he pulled out his leather bag and filled it with this meal. He did not know that it had been ground by a machine-and to feed the chickens. 

Besides corn meal, another offering is prayer sticks. These are small sticks with some feathers of a dead bird placed in a slit in one end. Sometimes the people take these prayer sticks out to their fields and stick them up in the ground. They imagine the wind blowing them will wave a blessing over the fields.

A list of the gods worshipped by our people

would be long. Not only do they worship the sun but also the earth and the moon. Fog they call "father." Besides corn, other sacred objects would include yellow roses; stones, such as arrowheads and meteorites; and animals such as the bear, lion, tiger, coyote, owl, eagle, yellow bird, blue bird, and bluejay.

Lupita likes to collect rocks. Once she showed 

Teh-Eh a piece of meteorite she had found. Eagerly seizing the stone, he held it up before him admiring it. "A rain god," he said, and much to Lupita's distress he began praying to it. 

He wanted it very much for his own, and many times since then he has tried to get it from 

Too-Too. Once he even offered to buy it from her. He wanted it to use in his ceremonials in connection with sand-paintings. 

Once we were storing grain over at grandfather's place. A bat happened to fly into the room. In great excitement Teh-Eh exclaimed, "Oh! kill that bat. That is a witch! It will do harm to the people." 

According to the Indian belief, a witch may be an animal or a person, and there is naturally much fear of witches. If someone believes in a different way from the rest of the people, that one is thought to be a witch, which makes a very unhappy situation for the one who dares have an

opinion of his own. 

My father recalls that before the first World

War the Indian population was decreasing because of the Indian way of doctoring the sick people. 

Especially in the pueblo of Sandia, where the people speak our same language, the population was fast dying out because of their belief in witches.

Because of these practices many times people would lose their sick children. The medicine man would put fear into the people, telling them that bad spirits or witches had been harming their children. Now our people are finding out through our Government nurses who have been sent to help us, that many times a child sick from overeating will be saved by a little teaspoon of castor oil. Since the Government nurses have come, our population is now gaining in every community. 

People of different nations have many thoughts and fears about death. I only know what my people think.

When a person dies they believe that his spirit stays here a certain number of days until the clan head comes and holds a ceremonial. Where this ceremonial takes place, the members of the clan sing and pray. Then the spirit leaves the house, and is directed to a certain place which he has earned according to his deeds, good or evil.

CATHOLICISM ENTERS WITH THE CONQUEROR 

"We are not Catholic by faith; they compel us." In this way, my father's grandfather used to explain to him how the Spanish Catholic people came and explored this country by force rather than by faith. He even remembered during his life time how the grown Indians, when they were baptized by the priest, used to go off and wash their heads with ashes or mud or sand because they did not want that belief.

My mother tells an interesting story about the time when the conquistadores came to our pueblo. "My father used to tell us," Na-Na said, "that it is not an obligation to be baptized, because they came and forced the people. They just treated the people ugly. Our people were scared; they didn't know anything about baptism. They just had Indian names at that time. They didn't have 

Spanish names like we have now."

It is funny. In our language, the word for "baptism" means "soaky head" or "wet head."

This is the story mother told us that came to her through her father, from his father, and so on, back to that early day when the Catholics first came back after our people drove them out.

After he let her go, she ran weeping and calling to her sister. She got sand and put it on her head because it had been soaked. "I don't know what kind of people they are," she said as she told her sister to watch, "because they have already done this to me."

They both decided to put them in the little storehouse for grain in the walls of the house. The little boys understood, because many times they had been told a witch would come after them.

After they hid their children, the younger sister decided she would continue to grind corn at the metate. There were no locks on their doors, but she thought she was strong enough to protect herself.

Soon the Spaniards came after her and told her they were going to take her to be baptized. She paid them no attention. Many times they told her. 

But she still said nothing.

Then they tried to take her out, one pulling on each side of her. She fought with her belt from her chonga. She was a strong woman and did not want to give up, but the men were stronger. So finally they took her and baptized her too. They did not find the children.

The Spaniards kept on going everywhere throughout the village and surrounding territory to look for the people. One day they went to the irrigation ditch where many Indian men were working with wooden spades. At this time the rebuilding of the Catholic church was only about half completed. The soldiers pointed toward the building to tell the Indians that it was their church. 

Then they gathered them together and drove the Indian men into the unfinished building and baptized everyone of them.

From that time the people were stirred up and antagonistic. Many of the families left the village and went out to their fields to live. They did not understand what the Catholic baptism meant and thought they were going to be hurt by the pouring of that water on their heads. After a while when things got quiet and nothing else happened to

alarm them, the people came back to their homes in the village.

After the church building was finished, two bells, a large and a small one, were hung in the towers. When the Spaniards thought they were through baptizing our people they used to ring the bells every Sunday morning. First they rang the big bell twice; then they rang the small bell once. The people did not go to church at first but stayed at home and watched. They would ask each other, "What for they ringing that bell?”

A few of our people learned to talk in Spanish. 

Finally the priest explained to these what that baptism meant.

Some of our people were wealthy, because they had many sheep and cattle they could sell and get some money.

The first time the priest talked to them after the church was finished he told them to worship St. 

Augustine and bring some offering to him. The ones who had money were the first ones to become Catholics. So when our people saw the rich people going to church, and the poor just watching, they decided it must be wise for them all to go.

At first they were told that they were not going to be charged for their weddings, baptisms and funerals. Today this is not the practice of the priests. Now they charge our people for masses for their dead, no matter how long since their

1 loved ones died. They charge them too for their baptisms, and weddings. And St. Augustine is still waiting for more offerings. It seems as though he will never have enough.

I was interested to learn about how our people first became Catholics, through this long story my mother told, because today the Catholic church is one of the main centers of the life of our pueblo. 

Since I have been going away to school I have found that this is true about most of the other pueblos in our state too. 

In the Catholic church center many of the activities for most of our people. All the way through his life an Indian from this majority group will go to the Catholic church for many different occasions. As a baby only a few days old, he will be taken by his godfather or godmother, as the case may be, to this church to be baptized and given a Spanish name of some saint. Also here his birth will be recorded. Then as he grows up he will go with his parents to mass on Sunday mornings and on the special feast days honoring many saints, including the patron saint, St. Augustine. When he becomes old enough to learn his catechism he will make his First Holy Communion and go to confession. When he is grown and wants to get married his wedding will be here in the church; and when he dies his body will be taken out through the doors of this church to its resting

place in the Catholic graveyard. 

Ta-Ta told how his grandfather described one of the contests they used to have to celebrate one of the saints' days. Two men would carry from the Catholic church flags representing San Pablo and San Juan. Each man would also carry with him a strap or switch or a real black jack. Leaving the pueblo on their horses they would go north and make a circle for a mile or two, going in opposite directions around the circle. When they met they would whip each other. One time he saw two flag carriers fight in this way. One of the men used the handle of his black jack to hit the other bearer on his head and knocked him off his horse.

The custom is different now. On certain saints' days, such as San Juan's Day or San Pedro's Day, the contest the people have as a part of their celebration is a chicken fight. Today only boys about fifteen years of age take part. They still have the contest on horseback. When they go out from the church they pick out some men by the name of John, Paul, or Peter, and expect some offering from them. For the contest they dig a hole in the ground and put a chicken in, just leaving the head out. Then the boys ride by on their horses and try to get the chicken. The one who gets it is then fought by the others who try to obtain possession.

In this fight, various ones are hit in the face with the chicken, even some of the bystanders. This is supposed to represent Paul's persecution of the people. When one of the riders is thrown from his horse the people think it represents Paul being thrown from his horse when he was blinded by the light. This is a bloody contest. 

On San Juan's Day, June 24, our people like to irrigate their fields because they think the water is holy on that day. Also they like to go early in the morning to swim in the river for the same reason. "When I was about twelve years old," my my father said, "four or five of us waited in the Catholic church all the night before San Juan's Day. When the first cock crew, we rang the bell, awakened the sleeping village, and went to swim in the holy water." 99 Sometimes when a person gets sick some of his people will make some sort of a sacrifice for him. 

Our uncle's wife had a brother who was sick. She promised St. Augustine that she would walk with the next procession in his honor if her brother would get well. Her brother got somewhat better, and she thought she had to fulfill that promise. 

That year they took the image of St. Augustine out in a procession for about seven miles, and true to her promise she marched in the procession, walking barefooted all of the way.

Her older sister also had made a promise. She promised St. Augustine that she would go all the way from her brother's place to the Catholic church on her knees if her brother could be restored. She, too, was barefooted, and as she went to keep her promise she skinned her knees and toes.

These two sisters do not know whose prayers will be answered. The younger sister is proud that she did not suffer much in walking those seven miles barefooted. She thinks that maybe her prayers are closer to God than her sister's, because her sister's knees and toes were hurt. But their brother is not well yet. He is still suffering. 

And they still wonder.

INDIAN WAY AND CATHOLICISM WALK TOgether 

Since the early priests could not succeed in making our people give up the practices of their Indian religion, through the years, these two religions have been going hand in hand.

After meeting the demands of the Catholic church, the Indians carry out the obligations in their own ancient way of worship without further interference from the priests.

The practice of these two religions together is shown in many ways. Even in every day affairs it is evident. Our people have this idea about thanking God for their food. After they have eaten sufficient food to kill their hunger they will say,

"Thanks, thanks my God." If they fail to have enough, then they just do not give thanks. 

Both of my grandfathers are good examples of

Indians worshipping according to two religions. 

My mother's father was a medicine man and at the same time was a true Catholic. Many times 

I have already mentioned my other grandfather who is head of his clan and also strong in his 

Catholic way.

In naming an Indian baby his mother first gives him an Indian name in the ancient way through her clan. Then the baby is given another Indian name by the baby's uncle through his clan. In the Indian way this uncle is called "aunt." If for any reason the baby's "aunt" should refuse to name him, then his parents get an Indian name for him through his "aunt's" clan head. If the baby does not have an "aunt" then he gets his second Indian name through his father's clan.

Later on an Indian who is chosen to be a godfather gives a boy a third Indian name, upon his initiation into the Red Eyes or Black Eyes Clan.

In a similar way a girl is later given a third Indian name by a godmother.

Indian names are selected from the most beautiful things created, such as sun, moon, stars, rainbow, animals, evergreen trees, flowers. When a color is used, white is preferred and is used to show the most beautiful or best things. Black is

never used. Examples of Indian names are Good

Road, Bright Road and Rainbow. The most beau tiful things like flowers are used for girls names. 

My mother's name is Flower. Both of my grandfathers were given the Indian name of Kim B’ahthoo, meaning White Lion. That is my full Indian name also, although it is sometimes shortened to 

Kimo, meaning Lion. My family calls me Noo-oo.

Next, in the Catholic way, the baby is given a Spanish name by a person selected, then the priest baptizes him by spilling water on his head in the church. As my father says, here they are taught "that God is landing in this house day and night from heaven." 

My Spanish name given me at the time my godfather baptized me in the Catholic way is Juan 

Antonio Jojola. From this comes my name of Tony, by which my friends call me.

In some of the religious ceremonies there are both the Catholic and the Indian parts. 

Our Indian leaders, governor, lieutenant-governor, sheriff, war-chief, and other council members, are elected every twelve months. The gov ernor works in the Catholic religion. One of his official duties is to celebrate for the patron saint, which is moved from the Catholic church to his home to honor the home and to be worshipped.

At some time of his choice, the governor issues an order for the community to go to church to

make a procession. They pray to the saint, asking rain for the crops.

Three men are appointed to lead, one with a crucifix, one with "holy" water, and one with a bell. A small boy goes beside the third man and rings the bell. One or two men follow along with guns. Four women and four men are ordered to carry the image of St. Augustine, their patron saint, on a sort of stretcher with handles. Men, women, and children come next in the procession, following the patron saint. Down the highway and around the fields they go, finally bringing the saint back to the governor's house. 

Then they serve soda pop and cookies and, if this procession is about the middle of June, some of the early fruits, such as cherries and watermelons.

At the Indian dance that takes place in connection with this ceremony on or near the plaza, the men and women dancers are dressed according to their ceremonial ways. During the dance the 

Indian officers throw things up into the air, and the ones who are singing catch them. Some of the things they throw are dishes, boxes of cookies, crackers, chickens, sacks of flour, money and cloth for dresses and men's shirts. Relatives of one partner in the Indian dance give things to the other partner.

In the Indian part of the ceremonial, the prayer

is something like this in the Tiwa language: "First we are wet head [meaning in the Catholic way]; next, in our poor Indian way. Really and truly righteous fathers (gods) sitting all around the circle they are on, who are giving us the life and carrying on our life with their great power, we have met together, and are seeing each other dressed up. You should appreciate and see the appreciation, that you are living with health and good feeling. 99 

Then they just wish, wish, wish. They say, "I wish more and more you will be successful in what you desire to have or own; all your family and your animals out to the smallest game, and the rain, and the crop. Our father gods, while we are in this position, give us the permission to talk to the people."

When it rains, they think the rain comes with the saint.

The main celebration for St. Augustine is the 

Fiesta on August 28, St. Augustine's Day. Ever since this saint was made patron saint of our pueblo the people have been celebrating each year on this date in his honor.

On the plaza in front of the church they put up for this occasion a ferris wheel, a merry-goround; curio, soda pop, and ice cream stands; gambling booths and all sorts of carnival concessions. The custom is for others living outside of

Isleta to lease space for their booths or various concessions.

This is the varied assortment spread out before the image of St. Augustine, which after a mass, has been carried in a procession for the occasion to a special booth arranged for this purpose. Each year in the past until 1943 a part of this celebration has also been a colorful Indian dance in front of St. Augustine's image.

On this day our people imagine that they are free to drink and take part in all sorts of gambling. 

This is their Catholic teaching, and they consider it their obligation to perform in this way every year at this time.

In fact our people learned well through much of this practice how to put on such a celebration. 

Those who drink and gamble decided a number of years ago that this one celebration for St. Augustine's Day was not enough; and so these Indians themselves started another similar fiesta at Ranchitos, a community not far from our pueblo. 

For this second celebration they also used the idea of leasing their land to outsiders for various booths and concessions.

An Indian woman from Isleta, who was one of the strongest in the Catholic faith, went to Ranchitos to put up her stand for this occasion. She had already become rich by selling wine on the feast day celebrations held on the plaza. One of my

grandfathers told her she should pay for her stand.

But she did not want to pay to lease space for her saloon at Ranchitos. This led to a big argument. 

Since that time the people at Ranchitos have not again held their feast day.

I believe that God in His own way will destroy works of unrighteousness, sooner or later. God has told us that "evil doers shall be cut off." The home of this woman who became rich selling wine is now in ruins.

Many times our people get all mixed up in their two religions.

Ta-Ta told a story about his uncle who had some strange ideas concerning his health. When he was old he became ill. Someone told him that little puppies were very good for rheumatism. 

When night came he got a little puppy and put it under the covers. Then he went to sleep and forgot about his dog. 

During the long night the little dog got the disadvantage of staying under the cover and tried to get out. When the puppy started to bite my uncle on the back he got awfully scared.

He wanted to try different ways of getting well. 

One day his good wife was baking bread in the big outdoor oven. "Tell me when you get through with your bread,” he asked, "so I can get in there. 99 When he was growing blind he heard a wrong

reference about the Bible. He asked a Government employee, who said he had a Bible, to search for the place where it told about a blind man being cured by a fish gall-bladder. The Government man told him, “I have a Bible, but I haven't read it yet. 99 

An Indian medicine man tried to cure him with the fish gall-bladder and dropped some in his eyes. 

Instead of curing him, he got worse.

After he was already blind he used to dance for St. Augustine. He did not know the way of the true Physician. "Both of the religions are in evidence even when a person dies," my father tells us. 

First of all the clan chief or head man is called.

He will come, providing the person during his life has been a good attender and obedient to the call of the clan chief. 

When the head man comes "to practice for the soul," he sings an Indian song, and lays a corn meal pathway for the soul to be led out to the presence of the Trues (or "fathers," or gods).

In his prayer he mentions gods in two ways: first, gods in the "baptize" or Catholic way; next, gods in our poor Indian way according to the clan. "After all they deliver the body to the Mother Earth that she will raise him or her like a stalk of corn or wheat," Ta-Ta said. "So at the burial place they deliver the dead body to the Earth,

the same as it was raised to live by the Earth, as they believe now the Mother Earth is to be fed by this dead body."

On the Catholic side, if the person was not a good attender and follower he can have no hope of being saved. If the dead person's family is poor and has no way of paying the priest the amount he asks for the funeral, there will be no Catholic service and the priest will not regard them at all. As they are taught this service is necessary to their salvation, naturally they leave no stone unturned to get the required sum of money. If the person was a faithful Catholic and the family can pay the burial fee, the priest will do almost anything for them.

When there is a Catholic service, which is usually the case, it is held at the church. From there the body is carried, the entire funeral procession walking, to the cemetery up near the new school house.

At the grave a close relative of the dead person makes a talk. He thanks the people that come for their sorrowing and sadness. The dead person is put away to feed Mother Earth, as he was raised to live. He is expected to be in the right place as god or saint, seated facing one of the directions, and might have a right to talk to their gods, petitioning the highest for a certain group. Or his people may talk to him and petition for what

they wish to be done. They expect everyone of their people to be there.

If a person has a long sickness before he dies it means that he is punished. All expect to be punished before they die. They think they go to heaven to be saints and can ask anything through their name to the gods.

All the close relatives of the dead person go to his home and sleep four nights. Early in the morning of the fourth day before anyone else gets up, the relatives all go at day break to the river to wash their faces and hands. Then they go back to the dead person's home, where the clan priest comes. They pray with corn meal in the clan where they belong, asking forgiveness for the bad things done on earth by the dead person. Then the people are dismissed to go back home until night. 

All the women go home and prepare their best bread and other best food for a feast that night.

In the afternoon whoever wishes goes to cut up food with the left hand for the dead person to be fed that night by the clan priest.

CHAPTER IV HOW I BECAME A CHRISTIAN 

I think that God must have planned several generations ago for me to be a Christian. Some of the influences on my parents in their childhood make me think so. Since my conversion is so definitely linked with that of my parents, I must first tell about theirs in order to tell mine. 

MY FATHER BECOMES A CHRISTIAN 

One evening Ta-Ta told us about his boyhood days. "My grandfather," he said, "was the one that raised us, especially me. We been lack of mother since we were very small of age. We didn't really recognize our mother. For my part, I don't know her activities in raising the children because I was very small when she passed away. My father didn't have much to worry about these children, because he was also depending on his mother since he lost his wife."

While Ta-Ta was with his grandparents he remembers that they knew a very little about the 

Gospel. When his grandfather was old and blind 83

he quoted the Ten Commandments from memory, and also he knew the Beatitudes. "I remember a lot of their influence among their neighbors, the Spanish and Pueblos," he told us. "My grandfather had two brothers. They all showed much love toward each other and helped each other. We had a wonderful training by those grandparents to stay together with all the family and work together." 

Ta-Ta recalls one time when his grandfather and the two brothers were telling stories of their younger days when they went on the buffalo hunt and had to fight the Navajos.

In talking about the Navajos, one of the brothers said, "If we catch them we have to kill them, because if they catch us they kill us." "My oldest grandfather," Ta-Ta said, "was timid and never did brag. He was gentle and had a little schooling in his younghood." 

Ta-Ta asked whether his oldest grandfather ever killed a Navajo. "No," he replied, "I never did kill a Navajo because it is prohibited to kill.” 

Ta-Ta's grandfather must have done much thinking for himself. Some of his beliefs were very different from the teachings our people have in their two religions.

Once he saw a man peddling some images which

were packed on a burro. When he heard the man calling out, "Who wants to buy saints?" the grandfather said, "Better have the worst burro than the best image." He did not believe in those saints.

Neither did he believe in Indian peyote or magicians (medicine men). Ta-Ta remembers hearing him remark to himself one day, "It is not hard to learn those magic things. A person with common sense can learn those magician ways of fooling the people.' 99 

When he went on the buffalo hunt, the man who went along to work for him in the way of looking after his ox or horses or burro was a medicine man. "He knew those magic things," said grandfather, "but he didn't know how to hobble a burro.' 99 "I was not captured by the beliefs in medicine man's ways,” Ta-Ta told us. "Since my grandfather did not believe in them, he never did teach us in the way the other people do. Today I see lots of people compelling their children to go to the medicine man's ceremonies, but he never did advise us to go over there." 

When my father grew to be a man and had a family to support he started to raise some sheep. 

When a person is out looking after sheep he has much time to think. Out there with the sheep he prayed, even though nobody had ever taught him how.

"Lot of long days, and lot of long nights taking care of sheep alone, in the hot sun or in the dark nights, I always pray to God and asked Him to give me what will be worth while or to be successful with the sheep, or if He has another way for me to live and help me out," Ta-Ta said.

God did help him out. He sent someone to show my father God's way to live.

One evening he came back from the mountains with a load of wood. On the way home he stopped at our uncle's place at Chical, a community a few miles out from Isleta. A couple by the name of 

Ramsey were school teachers at the Chical school. 

Mrs. Ramsey brought a visitor of theirs over to my uncle's place, and they were there when my father came in.

The visitor introduced himself to my father saying, "I'm a new man." He was a big, heavy-set fellow with gray hair and beard. "What do you mean," my father asked, "that you are a new man when you are old?" "I'm the new missionary," he said. "Baptist missionary." “Oh, we have a lot of religions around here," my father replied. "Baptist," he thought. He had a little light on that because he had heard at school about John the Baptist who was beheaded. My father had

attended St. Catherine's Indian School at Santa

Fe, a Catholic school for Indians, when he was a boy. "I haven't got any more time now,” my father told him, because he was tired, but he asked the visitor to come to see him at our home. 

The new missionary was Rev. C. W. Burnett, who was sent by Southern Baptists, we later learned, to bring the Gospel to our people. 

It took Brother Burnett about two weeks to find my father again. After that first visit he kept on coming to our home. "Sometimes," my father continued, "he found me plowing, and he didn't want me to stop work. 

He would take hold of the lines, and we would plow a few rows while he talked to me about the Gospel. After a while, I would tell him, 'Let's sit down here under the shade and talk.' 999 It was a long time before my father got things straight, for the Bible was different from the way he had been taught. Sometimes he would get discouraged.

In the meanwhile mother thought that this missionary was using up too much time from my father's work. She had just heard about a Navajo who had come many miles all the way from Alamo to Isleta and Albuquerque bringing a sick boy with him to find a priest to baptize his boy in the Catholic way as he had been taught when he

was attending a Government Indian school. This 

Navajo father could not find a priest in either place who was willing to baptize his boy because these Indians at Alamo lived outside of their parishes. Finally in the next village beyond Albuquerque he found a priest willing to baptize the sick boy.

This story that my mother heard gave her an idea. “Why don't I tell Seferino (my father) to send this missionary out to those Navajos? They don't have a priest or missionary or any one to help them." So she told my father about the sick 

Navajo boy, and asked him to send this missionary out to those Navajos. 

One time when my father was discouraged he wondered whether he was the only one on whom Brother Burnett was working. The missionary had said his work was for the poor and tired and hungry. My father remembered what mother had told him about those Navajos. 

Then he asked the missionary, "Mr. Burnett, why don't you go to those people at Alamo? They are poor and needy, and they need you." 

At the Indian agency office in Albuquerque the missionary found out how to go out to the Navajo country at Alamo. He began going there to tell those poor Indians about Jesus, but still he kept coming to Isleta and to our home.

He opened up a Sunday School in the day time

and my father was the only one to attend, walking in from the ranch. 

One time my father and his brother were up in the mountains looking after their sheep. My uncle sent word to my father that his rams had been gone about fourteen days and could not be found. When my father went over there my uncle stopped hunting. "The wind was blowing," my father said, “nothing but a blue sky up the top. Thick forest. I was in the dark. "I been thinking, I'm going to try God. I been realize Brother Burnett been telling me the help for the nerves, patience. He said the Bible has a reference of salvation and asking God for the blessing. So I said I was going to test God. I wanted to get the meaning God meant for me. 

I need Him. I'm going to try God. Nobody around to hear me. I'm going to try Brother 

Burnett's remedy. I kneel down on my two knees. "I had all the directions about me and didn't know which way to go. So I said 'Father, I'm lost. Whichever way you tell me, I'll go.' "I got up and turned south. I found a deer trail and followed it. It went around the rim rock. I stood up and the wind was blowing awful strong, just swaying me. I looked down and saw something that looked like wool. I followed down under the rim rock. It was very steep. There was that

wool and those tracks. There I found my sheep." 

This is how the Good Shepherd found my father.

He went on to say, "Since that time I took faith. That's how I found my salvation. I thanked 

God right there. I was thankful to God that He helped me. And I prayed that God would help me get the sheep back up that steep place where I came down. He did. They were just so gentle and I drove them back the same way I came."

When my father had this experience it had been about two years since the missionary first came. 

One April day in 1933, about two more years after this, Brother Burnett took us over to Albuquerque to see some people baptized at the First Baptist Church there. My father had already learned what was expected after faith, but he had no intention of being baptized when he went to the service that afternoon. "When I saw all those people going on in there my heart was itching," he said later.

After the baptizing was over, Brother Burnett asked, “Is there anyone else, who has never taken a stand for Christ, who wants to do so before we adjourn?" 

Then my father went forward. "I am accepting 

Christ and joining in with Him," he said. "When do you want to be baptized?" the missionary asked him. "Right away," my father replied.

The people stayed, and he was baptized right then. 

MY MOTHER ALSO PUTS GOD TO THE TEST 

Like Ta-Ta's grandfather, Na-Na's father must have done some thinking for himself, too. He had some convictions also that were not according to the teachings of his two religions. 

Although he was a medicine man, he did not invite his children to come to the ceremonials. 

They would just have to wait for him on the outside. He wanted them to leave him alone in this kind of practice, and not to become interested in it. 

While he was still a boy he suffered from rheumatism. He said then that if he got well he would become a medicine man. He did not get well, but he became a medicine man anyway because he thought at that time it would be something nice for him to do.

When he was about fifteen he was an altar boy at the Catholic church and lived with the priest. 

Every time he would get hold of the Bible the priest would not let him read it. "Come on," the priest would say. "Let's go rabbit-hunting." And off they would go. When they came to the river he would carry the priest across on his back. "Somehow when he got away from the priest,"

1 mother tells us, "he got hold of the Catholic New Testament and read it. He got mad at the priest, because he found out the priest wasn't teaching him what the Bible said. Because my father was a true Catholic, he didn't like for the priest to pass the collection plate; he knew the priest would use it for himself rather than to teach the people what the Bible said. My father said that salvation was free."

This grandfather of ours was wealthy in land and goats. He would help people with meat from the goats and lend out milk goats to the poor. 

He was a kind man.

Each year in October the people of my village gather crops of corn and chili for the priest. One year grandfather gave him a barrel of corn. That same year, on the first day of November in the morning, grandfather died. Before he passed away one of his brothers asked him if he wanted the priest to come so that he could confess his sins. "No," grandfather replied. "God already knows what I have done in the past, and He knows whether I am going to die or not. So I'm just going to leave it up to the Lord."

He left it up to his children to call the priest if they wished. He thought if they did the barrel of corn would be enough to pay for his funeral. 

They called the priest after he died, but the barrel of corn was not enough. They still had

to pay him six dollars to come to the house and go to the cemetery.

After her father died my mother seems to have been much confused in all of her religions. Besides her father having been a medicine man, she belonged to the Blue Corn Clan, as I mentioned before. Then, too, she belonged to the White Corn 

Clan, to the Black Eye Clan, and was also a Catholic. "Why do I have all those religions when I have to pay for my salvation?" she asked herself. "After my father died," she said, “I never took interest in going over to the ceremonies of these religions. After he died no one came to tell me about God, not the priest, nor the heads of the clans, to tell me about my salvation. I knew there was a God, but I didn't go to church, although I still held on to those images." 

Once mother lost two rings. She thought she had left them on the table and that some one might have taken them. She just searched everywhere for them. After she could not find them she was very worried.

One day she and her sister-in-law were talking about the lost rings. 

Mother said, "I am going to take two candles to St. Augustine if I find my rings."

Her sister-in-law laughed at her and asked,

"Why don't you make a promise to go to church?" "Oh, that's too much!" mother replied. "Why?" she asked. "Because I don't want to kneel down," mother answered.

After awhile the time came when they were going to celebrate for St. Augustine in the pueblo. The next day they were going to have a mass. While my mother was scrubbing the floor in our home her sister-in-law came running in. "What do you know about tomorrow, sister?" she asked. “Why don't you go to mass and take two candles for St. Augustine to see if you can find your rings?" "Well, I promise. When they bring St. Augustine to the governor's place I'll take these two candles and worship him, if I find my rings before sunset this afternoon. I'll see how powerful St. 

Augustine is." 

I was only about five years old then. Before sunset I found the rings and ran to take them to 

Na-Na. She was still scrubbing the floor, and my aunt was still there. "There you are," she told mother. "Take those candles you owe now, and worship St. Augustine." 99 "I was just talking," mother said. "I won't go." 

Finally I asked my aunt, "Why should mother have to go and worship that 'long whiskers'? It's nothing but wood. I'm the one that found the rings.

So I'm the one who's going to get the nickel from mother, I guess. She said many times that whoever finds her rings is going to get her nickel, didn't you, Na-Na?" "Yes," mother agreed. So mother gave me a nickel. It cost her just a nickel to get her rings. "When we don't know the truth about God," mother said one time, "we just have such funny ideas. I was ignorant that way. The old folks teach us to believe like that-the way it is not so. 

So today there are many people yet ignorant the way I was." 

Our oldest brother and sister both died when they were babies. Both were baptized in the Catholic way, and both were named in the Indian way by the clans. The people were taught that the little babies should be baptized so their sins would be forgiven. 

Mother did not believe that. "Why would a little child be sinful, when it didn't know anything?" she asked herself.

She used to feel sad and lonely after her babies died. "I didn't know how to talk to God to ask Him not to let me have any more children, if they were born to die," she said.

Her cousins and aunts used to tell her that if she would light candles for the saints her children would live. She used to have a big image of St. 

Anthony. The people told her to light a candle to

this saint to keep them from getting sick and dying, or to ask him not to let her have any more children if they were going to get sick and die. All of this made her even more confused and she did not know what to do. It was harder, too, because none of her friends understood how she felt. 

After awhile our brother Ignacio was born. He was also baptized and named in the Indian clans. 

When he was ten months old he became very sick. 

Mother thought he was going to die also as the first two had done, and she was very frightened. She talked to God. "I don't have anything to give," she prayed. "All I am good for is to ask. Please let Ignacio live. Also let me find a way to talk to God, a way that is satisfactory to talk to God." "I asked Him that," she said. "I believe He has heard me. God has answered my prayer.'

When God sent the first missionary to our home, there were already three children, Ignacio, Lupita and I. "Brother Burnett told me how I could have salvation," mother said, "and have my prayers answered. He told me if I asked through Jesus' name my prayers would be answered.” 

Mother went on to describe how she felt. "I didn't believe in it somehow. My head was too hard. I recall what I did before I was a believer in Christ. Twice I asked God a thing what I needed

through Jesus' name, as Brother Burnett told me, just to find out if God would answer my prayer in 99 that way."

On one of these occasions mother was alone with us. Ta-Ta was up in the mountains with the sheep. 

She heard of someone who was going up to the mountains, and she wanted to send along some food for Ta-Ta. She wondered what she could send him, as she had no money in hand and no one to send to the store in the village. She thought of sending some bread, but she had no yeast and no money to buy yeast. "That night," mother said, "I prayed to God. 

I asked Him just for a nickel to buy yeast to bake bread. I asked through Jesus for a nickel. If God really loves Jesus, if Jesus was His real Son, I was going to believe."

Early the next morning, about six o'clock, a white woman came to our ranch. Mother went out to see who it was. "Damacia," the white woman called to mother, "I'm in a hurry. I want fifty cents worth of apples." 

She paid for the apples with a dollar bill. Mother told her, "I have no change, and I am alone with the children.' 99 "Do you need the money now?" the lady asked. "Sure. And I don't have anyone to send to the store for what I need. I want yeast." 99

Then the woman went home, but she did not promise to bring the yeast right away. On the way back she met an old man, our neighbor, and picked him up. "She buy yeast and sent it to me," mother said. "That was the first time that I believe that God hears through Jesus. And yet I was afraid, and once more prayed to God to forgive me my sins, because I asked Him just like making fun of Him. 

I shouldn't have done that, I thought afterwards. 

Later on I did that same thing again."

The second time was when mother lost her golden tooth. She was wondering how to find it again because it cost her twelve dollars. Na-Na was just worrying. Again her people gave her advice to burn some candles to some idol, especially St. 

Anthony as he was powerful, they told her.

Sometimes she used to believe, and sometimes not. The missionary told her again that Jesus is true, that He is the only one, and that if we pray through Him we can get what we want. 

Finally mother said one day, "Let's see. I'm going to ask this Jesus if it is true that I'm going to get what I want through Him. Then every time when I thought of my golden tooth, I used to pray 

Jesus and ask for it." 

It took four years. One day I found the golden tooth near the oven where mother bakes and ran in to show it to Na-Na. Again she was scared be-

cause she had said she was going to find out if it was true what the missionary said about Jesus. 

That day her sister-in-law was there. Mother told her, "Really and truly, it is true what the Bible says. Believe me or not, it is four years already since I lost this tooth." "Then what do you owe Jesus, and what are you going to do from now on?" she was asked. "I am going to try to believe on Him truly, that 

He is the Son of God," mother replied. And so she did. 

Before the second testing was finished my father was baptized. That same day he was baptized mother nearly took a stand for Christ. She said, "Mrs. Burnett was asking me if I didn't want to trust Jesus, too, but I answered, 'No'. I was afraid.

I was thinking if these people are just here a short time, if they fool me and are not here long, and if I leave my Catholic religion, then where will I go to worship? All the people will hate us and they won't come to see me. And in my Indian religion they will put us off and won't want us any more. 99 Mother used to worry about this a lot. She told my father, "You are just looking for troubles and troubles."

After some time mother got sick. She began to think, "I might pass away sometime, and I haven't as yet taken any stand in trusting Jesus. I love Him and trust Him only but here I am always asking

and begging things from Him. It would be better if I would take a stand for Him and be baptized." 

God had met every test. He convinced my mother that Jesus is truly His son. She did take a stand for Him and followed Him in baptism about two years after my father was baptized. 

JESUS WON THIS INDIAN BOY 

After our parents became Christians our home became a Christian home. At meal time we had prayer before we ate. We began to have Bible reading, and my father talked to us about God's Word. Here in our home we also began to put into practice some of the Bible verses and songs we learned at Sunday School. 

One day my mother told Ernest and Evangelista, my little brother and sister, to go to the orchard and pick up some apples that had fallen to the ground. 

Instead of doing what she told them to do, they climbed up in the wagon and began to pluck apples from the tree, the best they could find. My mother called to them and told them again to get only the apples from the ground, but they pretended not to hear her. Finally she started toward them, and as she walked along she stooped and picked up a small stick. They saw that and quickly climbed out of the wagon. As my mother approached with the stick in her hand Ernest put his arm around Evan-

gelista's neck and they both sang, "Everybody ought to love Jesus." They did not get the whipping! 

During the time that Brother Burnett was working at Isleta he started night services at the Isleta day school. The people got stirred up. One night when the missionary was showing pictures the pueblo governor came and told him that he could not have services at the school any more.. 

When Brother Burnett told my father about this, Ta-Ta told him, "You come to my place and we will furnish a room for you." He said this even though he had not yet become a Christian. 

My parents had two rooms in a building just off the plaza which they used for storing grain and hay. They moved these to one end of the long front room and made a place for Brother Burnett to hold meetings. Later they cleared out the whole room for use of the mission work. 

After services were begun at our place the governor again came to see Brother Burnett and told him to stop. My father said, "If they want to put us to stop, let them come and see me." But they did not interfere further at that time.

This place of ours is still being used for our 

Sunday School. Brother Burnett is no longer our missionary because God called him home after he had worked with our people about four years. His daughter, Miss Ethyl, who had been helping out in

the work, moved away after she married. The 

Home Mission Board sent us Rev. C. W. Stumph as our missionary in 1933, and about a year later also sent Miss Pauline Cammack another helper.

Most of our family have attended Sunday School regularly when we have been at home. 

One by one we have followed in the footsteps of our parents. First Lupita became a Christian when she was about nine years old and was baptized the same year that mother took a stand for Christ. 

Later our older brother Ignacio confessed Christ as his Saviour and was baptized while he was attending Santa Fe Indian School. We are glad he found Christ before he entered the armed forces. 

About two years later Evangelista, my sister just younger than I, trusted Jesus at our Isleta mission and also was baptized. 

When I was about twelve my Sunday School teacher was Mr. Cloma Huffman, who was then a student in the State University at Albuquerque.

Now he is a Home Board missionary to the Spanish people, but at that time he was a volunteer worker at our mission. 

One Sunday afternoon in our Sunday School class we were studying John 3:16. The whole period we spent in finding out the meaning of this one verse. I remember that Mr. Huffman used just a few words of the verse at a time until he had explained all of it.

I came to realize something about God's great love and saw myself as a sinner. I understood then that Jesus came not only to save my parents, my sisters and my brother, but to save me also. Right there in class my teacher invited any who would to accept Jesus. I did. Jesus won my heart.

CHAPTER V MY HOPES FOR MY PEOPLE 

Some of the experts who study my people say that no matter what or who comes or goes, the Indian does not change his religion. They say the Indian may follow other religions and join other churches, but that he still clings to his ancient beliefs and practices. They also say that the Indian keeps his religion to himself and makes no effort to win others to his belief. 

This is just about true in many cases. But these experts must have overlooked the Indian who has really been saved by the blood of Christ, and who knows it.

CHRIST ALONE CHANGES THE INDIAN 

I know that since we have learned to love Jesus everything is different at our house. 

One day the people in our pueblo were planning to have a chicken fight for San Juan. We were the only ones in the pueblo who had chickens. 

Some of the people came over to our home to get a nice fat chicken from us. They thought my 104

mother would give it to them because the Catholics just give their chickens away to use in the fight if they have them. "No, I won't give any chickens away, and I won't sell any to be used in this way to make them suffer," she told them. "The chicken fight doesn't do anyone any good."

Mother had already become a Christian. 

A few days later when San Pedro's Day came along, and the people were planning another chicken fight, they did not even come to try to get a chicken from my mother. They knew it would be of no use.

Sometime later when we moved to the ranch some people came to buy our grapes. They wanted to make wine. My father would not sell them grapes for that purpose, and after that he got rid of all his grapevines except one or two for our own use.

We used to have images and pictures of saints in our home, but now we have done away with those. "Thank the Lord I'm a Christian, and I don't believe on those things now," my mother said. 

Not long after my mother became a Christian she even began to dress in a different way. She no longer wears the Indian dress of our people. 

One time in June during our Vacation Bible 

School my grandfather was going to fast for four

days with some other men at his house. Two girls from his clan had been called to carry water from the river and take care of the ones who were going to fast. He wanted my mother to stay at his house to look after the house and the girls. But my father did not want mother to miss Vacation 

Bible School. He just told grandfather kindly, yet plainly, why he did not want my mother to come to help like this. Since we did not believe that way it was no use for us to have anything to do with his religion. "Before the Gospel came to us," my father says, "we thought we were Christians by being Catholics. 

Later when we studied the Bible we found we were not." "Why did you become Baptist?" some of our people asked my father. "You have been Catholic and around the Indian ceremonials." "Only believe; that's the way," my father tells them. "I'm standing on that Bible. That's a foundation nobody can throw down."

When my mother was growing up she did not learn to read. Because her mother was not living she had to stay at home to look after her father, and did not get to attend school. After becoming a Christian once she told the missionary, "I'm going to learn to read because I want to read

God's Word for myself." Even at that time she had already begun to learn from the Beginners

Bible Story cards. 

My father describes how he was before he became a Christian. "I was just like a blind man trying to find his way around. I was just in the dark." Another time he said, “I was blind and dumb and just disgusted to see a newspaper or book. After I became a Christian I became interested in reading and talking." "When the first missionary came, Brother C. W.

Burnett, the first time he gathers the people in our building," my father recalls, "I remember how ashamed feeling I had at that time when he called me to interpret for him and tell the people. 

The missionary didn't have any other language but the English, and he select me to interpret. So 

I realize how hard it is to come to be a Christian. 

I didn't stand on my feet, but was kneeling down to hide from the people to do the interpreting for the missionary. But today I wish everybody would see me interpreting or explaining the Gospel of Christ.' 991 All of these changes have taken place in our family and many others, too, because Christ came into our hearts. We cannot help our grandfather in his religion, but we want more than almost anything we know about for him to have an understanding of the true way. Because we have the love of Christ in our hearts, we want grandfather also to know Jesus, who is "the way and the truth

and the life." 

One day my mother and Lupita were cleaning grandfather's house. Above the door he had a little saint in the form of a wooden crucifix which he called Jesus. In cleaning the walls mother accidentally knocked it down. Grandfather picked it up, went outside on the porch, and started looking at it. The head came off and one arm and a leg. "Oh," he said, "it has a heart, too!" In the side it had a little red heart made of cotton. 

Lupita tried to take out the little red heart, but he would not let her. "Is it alive?" she asked. "Yes," he replied. "Maybe it is because it has a heart." 

Lupita remembered that our state secretary of

Woman's Missionary Union, Miss Eva Inlow, wanted a little saint like that to use in her work. 

Lupe asked him for it, but he did not give it to her. 

Again she asked him, "Do you pray to that little saint?" "Yes," came his answer. "Does the little saint answer your prayers, Teh-Eh?" 

He paused for a while. Then laughing he said, "I don't know."

Lupita went on to tell him that Jesus is not dead any more, that He is alive now, and that He is never going to die again. She told him the

Bible says that we are not to believe in any idols or images of any kind. "Jesus is the one who answers our prayers, Teh-Eh," she said. "May I have the little image?" "Why do you want it? You just got through telling me that a Christian is not to believe in idols." 

Laughingly she replied, "I just want it to think of you." "I'll give it to you if you are going to believe in it and pray to it." He knew that she was not going to worship the little idol. Not knowing how to get it for Miss Inlow, Lupita playfully told him that she was going to pray to it. But grandfather knew that she was not going to believe the way he did. He still has the saint at his home, and it is still broken. 

Later Lupita asked Teh-Eh why the little idol fell off the wall. "If it were powerful, it would not have fallen," she told him. "Because your mother knocked it down,” he responded. "Then how could that little saint protect you when it couldn't protect himself?" she asked.

Grandfather did not reply. He just started humming to himself. 

It is taking a long time to convince grandfather of the true way, but we believe that little by little he is coming out into the light. Many times we

talk to Teh-Eh about Jesus and tell him what the 

Bible says.

One of the stories we have told him over and over again was about how Jesus cleansed the temple. Teh-Eh must have been doing a lot of thinking about how Jesus overthrew the money changers' tables and told those who sold doves not to make His Father's house a house of merchandise. 

Also Teh-Eh must have been thinking much about the kind of practice that is carried on each year by our people in their celebrations on their feast days buying and selling, gambling and drinking, all to celebrate St. Augustine's Day, along with a real Indian dance, according to their custom. 

To this kind of a celebration my grandfather objected in the year of 1943. Because he realized that this is just an evil practice, he did not want the Indian dance drums used in this way. As head of the Red Eyes Clan, he wanted "to prevent the foolish way of worshipping any greatness, and refused to allow the Indian drums to be used. 

Therefore they did not have the real Indian dance, and a custom of long standing was broken. 99 We realize that God wants those unrighteous practices which are carried on during each feast day celebration to be wiped off the face of the earth. Surely my grandfather is gradually coming out into the light to realize the true way also.

During these years since mother has become a Christian she has done her part in trying to lead 

Teh-Eh to know the truth about God. And not only with Teh-Eh has she worked, but also with many of our relatives and other people in our pueblo and surrounding communities. 

She never seems to grow weary of telling our people about God and His love. Her face shines as she talks about Jesus and His power to save. 

I know that some of these are going to come to know Jesus, because I have heard her earnest prayers for them, including many who have opposed us in our Christian way.

Grandfather has not opposed us, but sometimes there has been opposition from others because we no longer believe as they do. Many times the people think that we are witches because we do not believe in their ways, and sometimes they wish they could make trouble for us. 

My father's own brother is still angry with him because he left our people's religions. My father was not brought before a judge or anything like that but some of the people did try to prevent him from having the mission. They used to shoot through the windows of our mission with air guns. "I create a lot of enemies for the cause of 

Christ," my father said. "One day the Government investigator told me to stay away from the interior of the village with the mission work, that

I am obstructing their feeling for their customaries (Indian customs). So he wish for me to stay out of the heart of the village." "That's not the only place I'm going," my father remarked to the investigator. "I'm going into every heart of the Indians." "If you are a man of the law," my father continued, "am I doing wrong?" "No," replied the investigator.

Then my father asked him about the constitution of the United States. "This law man told me that the whole United States is back of me. So I am that big to carry the Gospel."

It is interesting to hear my father explain how he came to "carry the Gospel." Let me give his story in his own words. "I believe that the Lord has meant so much to me that He didn't want me to stay out there any longer with those sheep. After the Gospel has been impressed really in my heart, I thought I really sell those sheep and come home, and if that is His plan also to be one of His messengers. "I used to tell God to let me go through, if

He has some other selection for me. When I came to be a Christian I just thought I was going to be just like any other ordinary Christian people. "I never had any thought or intention of preaching the Gospel to the people. But the more pages that I turn in the Bible, I have found that the

true believer or follower of God has his obligation to pass it on from one to another and not to keep it all for himself. "The Bible make me reach out to Jesus. He put the Holy Spirit in my heart, and it make me itching, itching, itching to preach the Gospel." 

My father really does like to preach the Gospel. 

A part of his time he has to spend making a living for his family, but he is happiest when he is telling someone about Jesus. 

He sings an Indian song he made up, "It's 

Lonesome Without Knowing Jesus." "N'a'h-'tuh h-cum eh-may n'a'h kah-cha wayan Jesus ah-ee tee."

A person entering the new life realizes what he has been missing without Jesus. With all of his heart my father wants others to come to know his Saviour.

In his work with our people he frequently meets indifference and thick walls of prejudice. He knows though that God is able to deal with every hindrance to the spreading of the Gospel. And so he never tires of planting its seeds, even though many times he is not well-received by our people. 

A young woman came to our home with Christian friends. My father knew that she had been given a Bible sometime before this. To open a conversation with her about the Bible and salva-

tion he asked her, "How far have you gone with your Bible or understanding about God's Word?" "I do not know what became of it. I have lost it," she replied. 

My father felt sorry to hear her say this, and then told her that her soul was lost if she did not care. "The Bible is to know how far we are and how close we are and which way to come to God," he said.

Once an Indian woman with whom my parents have been working became angry with my father. "I want you to have this in mind," she told him, "that I will try to close those Catholic church doors so they won't let you in there alive or dead." 

Quietly he replied, "You can shut all of those doors, every one of them, but you are not going to shut the door of heaven. I'm sure you can go and tell all the people about me, but you can't do anything up in heaven where my share is." 

About a week after that her family had a terrible accident. Her husband was kicked by a horse and died. Then in about three weeks my father was again in this home. He read the fourteenth chapter of John and prayed with the family. They appreciated his visit this time and told him to come again. 

A cousin of my father's scoffed at him for going to our mission services. "Why are you still

going there when not many come? Aren't you ashamed to act as a missionary or priest? Nobody comes there to attend your services, nobody but your wife and children, and yet you don't give up.

The people talk so much about you acting as a priest or missionary. They make fun of you, and yet you don't give up.' 99 

My father prayed for his cousin. The next day he went to work on him, and finally his cousin began to talk friendly. My father gave him a 

Testament and asked him to read it. 

A few days later the cousin was in a store preaching to two men there. One of them was our real enemy, my father's brother-in-law, who has been fighting our work since the first missionary came, and has not been friendly to us. 

Since then he has let my father talk with him. 

They spent a whole day talking. When my father left his brother-in-law told him, "I would like for you to come to my home anytime and talk with me.' 99 

My father is deeply concerned that others besides those in our own pueblo may come to know our Saviour too.

In the summer of 1941 he and another Indian preacher who lives in Oklahoma, Brother V. F. 

Zunigha, went prospecting to Mesita, one of nine villages of the Laguna Pueblo. My father was encouraged to believe that the people there were

"hungering after righteousness," because as he said, "five men and women acted deciding for Jesus as their personal Saviour. So this one fact shows us the Lord is moving to the people that need Him."

Later, after he had visited and worked among the people of this village for a while, he said, "Every time I go it seems like they are coming closer and waking up from sleep." 

Most of the time he would be well received there at Mesita as he went from home to home to talk with the people about Jesus. Occasionally he found some opposition which he described in this way, "Just a little once in a while the devil bark but he lay down."

Mr. Paul Shattuck, the father of some of the children who attend our mission Sunday School at Isleta, is from Paguate, another Laguna village. 

One day he received word from his home village that his aged father had passed away. My father took Mr. Shattuck to Paguate for the funeral, but no minister came to conduct the services. Finally someone mentioned that my father was a preacher, and then they called on him to preach. 

After the funeral was over some of the relatives asked my father to come back and preach to the people again.

Since then he has gone back many times. During the summer of 1943 he held a Vacation Bible

School there, and my sister Evangelista helped by teaching the class of beginners and primaries. 

Evangelista does enjoy doing Christian work too, and is always willing and glad to help out whenever she can.

CHRIST HELPS THE INDIAN THROUGH THE WHITE MAN Immediately following the Vacation Bible School which my father held at Paguate he took three girls from there together with Evangelista and Lupita, to Inlow Youth Camp, our state Womans' Missionary Union Camp for young people. This camp is held each summer in the Manzano Mountains east of Isleta, and our state paper, The 

Baptist New Mexican, tells of hundreds of white young people from all over the state who go there at that time.

Because of helping with farm work I did not get to attend myself, but from what my sisters tell me it really must be a wonderful place for any young person to go to learn more about Jesus. 

I was glad to learn that besides the five girls my father took to camp from Laguna and Isleta, over twenty other Indian young people were there, most of them from the northern pueblos. 

Evangelista said, "Some of these attended Christian services for the first time in their lives while

they were at camp. A fifteen-year-old Indian girl had been very unhappy in the religion she had been compelled to follow. While attending camp, this girl found great joy in trusting Jesus as her own Saviour." 99 "Some of the other Indian young people became Christians too, while they were up there, Lupita almost interrupted in her haste to tell us the good news after she and Evangelista returned from camp. "The Laguna governor's daughter was one of them. Maybe she thought she was already a 

Christian, but there at camp she found that Jesus. was very real to her, and she realized that she never had definitely trusted Him as her Saviour before."

After hearing about those happy experiences there at the Youth Camp, I began to think how wonderful it is that the love of Jesus in peoples' hearts causes them to want to help each other. 

I am grateful to God for Christian white friends through whom Christ is helping my people. I am glad that they make us feel welcome in their meetings, such as Inlow Youth Camp, the state conventions, associational meetings, and churches like the First Baptist Church at Santa Fe which the students from the Indian school attend.

In these meetings we who are of different races can not only worship God and have Christian fellowship together, but also my people can get

some idea about how to carry on our Christian work among our own people, which we need so much to learn. 

I thank God, too, for volunteer Christian workers from among our white friends, such as Mr. Cloma 

Huffman was when he led me to accept Jesus, who give freely of their time to help in mission 

Sunday Schools, Vacation Bible Schools, and work among students like ours at Santa Fe Indian

I wish I knew how to thank God enough for all of these white friends through whom Christ is helping my people and for the many friends not only here in our state but throughout the Southland who have sent white missionaries to us through the Home Mission Board, beginning with 

Brother C. W. Burnett, to tell us about Jesus in the first place.

MY HOPES FOR MY PEOPLE 

I do not know much yet about how plans are made for carrying on mission work among my people, but it seems to me that we will have to depend on our white friends for a long time yet to send us white missionaries, if all of my people are going to have a chance to know Christ. 

Also I think that if my people are going to

have this chance, more Indian missionaries will be needed. The only Indians who are working as missionaries among our Indian people in our state are my parents and Rev. and Mrs. George Wilson who work in Albuquerque. I hope that more Indians will hear God's call to "carry the Gospel" to our people.

I have already told of members of my family who are already doing mission work. Maybe they have learned much about how to do it by actual practice in mission Sunday Schools and Vacation 

Bible Schools. 

In these days we hear much about the importance of training. My brother went to a training camp because the Army wanted him to be a good soldier. My Government school teachers frequently have to go away for more training during the summer time. I wonder if God's work is important enough for Indian Christians to be trained to do Christian work among my people. I believe that it is, and I hope that we may be able to find out the best way in which our Christian people may get this kind of training.

The more I think about this work of giving all of my people a chance to know Christ, the more I realize that it is a big job. But our God is big, too. 

My father was talking our people not long ago. "I think our Indian about this work among 

This is what he said: people are very much

unsatisfied with their old ways. So the harvest is plenteous, but the labourers are very few. I know the Lord is with us always. These people have a long way to think for themselves. Some go away forever waiting to decide for themselves.

But God so loved the world. We have a love to share with many. It is not hopeless; only the old snake (the devil) has his long time now. He is sore because the light is coming in through some cracks." 

I hope that these "cracks" through which the light of the Gospel is coming into the hearts of some of our people will be made bigger and mutliplied, not only by more missionaries, but also by every Indian Christian.

It is always interesting to me how the Gospel is passed on from one to another and how God uses plans that we would never think about. 

I shall never forget hearing my parents tell about how they tried to get rid of the missionary, before they became Christians, by sending him to the Navajos out at Alamo. And just to think! 

In May, 1943, one of their daughters, my sister 

Lupita, went out there to teach in two Vacation

Bible Schools. 

Several years ago during one of the first Vacation Bible Schools at Alamo, two young Navajos who had already dropped out of school decided

they would continue their education by going back to Santa Fe Indian School. Both were willing to enroll in the eighth grade even though they were over age for their grade. Both won high honors from the school while they were there. 

One of these boys, Frank Apachito, was already a Christian when he went back to Santa Fe Indian School because Brother Burnett had won him to Christ after my parents thought they were sending the missionary away for good.

The other boy was his foster brother, Wilson 

Guerrero, who through Frank's influence and that of our Christian work on our campus accepted Christ and was baptized while he was in Santa Fe 

Indian School. 

It makes us happy now to think of some of Wilson's experiences as a Christian. Both as student and later as a soldier, he was active in leading others to know his Saviour, for whom he has a deep love.

Once he wrote back while he was in training camp, telling how he felt when he would go on long hikes with his full field pack. He described it in this way: "I'll be out in front. We talk and sing, but most of the time I look far ahead and think and wonder to myself. Most of all I think about how

Christ had died for us. When I begin to feel the pain on my back from the pack I'm carrying, I

think about Christ carrying the cross on His back and the pain caused by the cross. "When my feet start to give me pain, I often think about the pain He received when the spikes were driven through His feet. And when my hand begins to hurt from carrying my rifle, I think of the nail that was driven through His hand. And when everything I'm carrying begins to feel heavy on me, I often think of the burden which was resting on Him when He was up on the cross. "When our 'hitch' is all over we take our packs off. Oh, what a relief! That means the rising from the death, burying the burden."

Sometime later he wrote to us from Iran where he was located on active duty for our country. 

His message reached us about the time we were beginning our studies again in a new school year. "With all of the studies," he told us, "the Bible is ahead, leading into a better life, into the true life of God. I am now here in the country where 

Christ once walked, studying more and more, seeing more and more the truth about Him, also praying more every day.'

Then Wilson went on to describe a scene which reminded him of the Twenty-third Psalm: 

"When I was on guard the other day I sat there watching the night overtake the day. All night long

I watch. The mountain and the hill, the sky, the stars, even heaven seems to draw near to protect

the ones that are sleeping. "Hours after hours pass. I sat there. Soon I saw out of the darkest night day break across the sky. Brighter and brighter it gets. The stars wink once more and fade away. The heaven, the sky, the stars, the mountain, the hill move back again. 

The shadow of the mountains still darkens the valley, and what did I see. Across the plain heading for the dark valley a man on a donkey. "And soon out of the valley the shepherd leading his sheep. Right below me there's a little stream running, and up and down, straight to the stream it's green with grass. Three shepherds come leading their sheep to this green spot. Soon they went up on a small hill and sat there playing their music. "That was the scene that I watched when I was on guard. I hope this letter finds you and the students working up to this green spot which is called heaven, taking the Lord as your Shepherd. 'Lead on, Lead on.""

While Wilson was still in training camp, he wrote to tell us that he was continuing his Christian work. He mentioned that there were more than ten boys in his hut. Every morning, every afternoon, and every evening he would read his New

Testament, and these boys in his hut would see him. After a while three of them began to read. their Bibles once each day.

"I pray for them every night," he wrote, “that's one way I'm working. Remember, I have not given up my church. The Bible is still beside me, and my body is still the temple of God.”

Wilson has helped his close friend, Tony, to come to know Christ. Tony, whose home is in one of the northern pueblos, tried to learn the truth about God while he was attending our religious instruction at school. But just like my parents were before they became Christians, Tony was much confused, and just could not seem to get things straight. He left school without finding Christ.

These two friends went to training camp together, and later served our country together on overseas duty. 

From Iran Tony wrote to us: "I have been reading the Bible right along as I have time in doing so. It is hard to understand, but with the help of Jesus I am beginning to comprehend His 

Word. For that I am thankful. I am reading His

Scriptures daily. It is the finest literature there is and His words are very inviting once you get started. I hope to continue reading, so that I may fully understand Jesus' life and His eternity."

At the bottom of his letter Tony wrote, "He that believeth on the Son hath everlasting life" (John 3:36). 

As I recall, Wilson's favorite hymn is, "I Love

to Tell the Story." I believe that he is really living the message of this song, and that he will continue to win others to Christ. What if Brother Burnett had not gone to Alamo!

It is my earnest hope that every Indian Christian will love to tell the story of Jesus and His love and will faithfully win other Indians to know our Saviour.

I have already mentioned that my Indian name is Kimo, meaning Lion. I am glad that this name was chosen for me, because I want to try to live up to what it implies. Just as a lion is bold, I also want to be bold: bold in telling my people the glorious message of salvation, bold in winning them to Christ.

I want to relate one experience of mine that is very important to me. No matter how long I may live and be a witness for my Saviour, I shall always remember this one occasion as an outstanding event in my life. Let me tell you about it.

At Isleta we have our Sunday School in the afternoon, and then come back to our home about four-thirty after taking some of the others home. 

The Sunday I gave my heart to Jesus I was very happy, but did not know how to show it or even to say much about it. 

When we got home that afternoon I suggested to Ernest that we play Sunday School. Naturally 

I said I wanted to be Mr. Stumph, the missionary.

We went into our living room and the play Sunday School began. It was a very small Sunday School, with apparently just four of us present. Now I know there were five, for God was certainly there with us.

As John 3:16 was the verse we had been talking about in our class that afternoon when I decided to accept Christ, it was much on my mind. I used it. Ernest was unusually quiet and attentive when 

I explained the verse as nearly as I could the way my Sunday School teacher had explained it to me.

I asked Ernest if he wanted to become a Christian. I am not sure whether I was still playing missionary or whether I was really in earnest when I asked that question. Anyway something about Ernest's serious "Yes" made me question him closely. "Do you really mean it?" I asked him. "Yes," he replied, "and I'm not playing at all."

The next Sunday he told Mr. Stumph at Sunday 

School and let all the people know. 

I like to think of that Sunday afternoon in our home, where, because I had learned to love Jesus, God was able to use me as He had planned. I do not know all that God plans for my life, but this I know: I won my brother.
