Commodity Theory of Labor 

Mr. Garrett then proceeds to consider the question of wages in the railroad industry. He reveals a false perspective by starting out with the "commodity theory of labor." 

He refers to a "monopoly," a word dear to the hearts of all railroad propagan- MONOPOLY dists who have not yet been able to recognize the fact that the railroads can no longer have a complete monopoly of transportation. In connection with labor, the author refers to "a falling market," and "raising the price as the demand falls." There is no suggestion here of feeding the families of working men-no thought of humanitarianism in connection with what constitutes a just wage for the employees. It is purely and simply the age-old commodity theory of labor. He insinuates that all the railroad workers need do is to "imagine" that they have worked and their paychecks will be forthcoming.

Doubled Productivity for 1c an Hour Increase 

Following his commodity theory of labor, Mr. Garrett concludes that the "price of railroad labor has been raised to its highest point." Let us examine the facts.

Measured by any standard, such as the kind and character of work railroad workers do, the services they render, the output of their labor, the standards agreed to by the managements as fair and proper as evidenced by agreements made, and standards accepted in other industries, the railroads today are receiving more for their wage dollar than ever before in history. The railroad propaganda machine cannot even challenge this statement. Approximately 1,000,000 workers in the railroad industry today do what 2,022,000 railroad workers did in 1920, and in the aggregate they receive the paltry sum of lc per hour more for performing all of this service to the railroad industry. No important industry can show so small an increase in wages for so large an increase in productivity. The total paid to railroad employees, including executives, was 53% less in 1938 than in 1920 and 40% less than in 1929. The railroads are now receiving 88% more work per employee, 101% more work per employee's hour of service and 92% more work per dollar of wages paid, than they received in 1920. If the distinguished author of "Peace on the Rails" had examined into these facts, he would not have concluded that railroad workers enjoy "a fattening of the payroll by imaginary work." WAGE

If there be those who may feel that the author of this reply is partial to the interests of the railroad workers, then let us consider the words of three very learned men who sat on the President's Emergency Board in the fall of 1938 and who spent an entire month taking testimony and making a careful study of all the facts involving wages in the railroad industry. Here is what the Board said: "Examination of the data above detailed leads us consequently to the conclusion that the level of wages of railway labor is not high when compared with wage levels in other industries. Mr. Garrett reveals that he is of that class in America which readily admits its acceptance of the principle of collective bargaining but is inclined to take the view that agreements affecting working conditions are something less than commercial contracts, and hence their violation should be dealt with through a different procedure.

Readers of "Peace on the Rails" should ponder the fact that there are almost a million employees in the railroad industry whose cases may come before the National Railroad Adjustment Board. It is reasonable to assert, therefore, that the discussion of but six Awards cannot fail to yield an incomplete if not an inaccurate picture. Mr. Garrett's article gives the impression that of all the cases coming before the National Railroad Adjustment Board, the railroads had lost in almost every instance. Board, during the period from January 1, 1935 to September 20, 1939, only 1,478 cases were referred to referees. Almost two-thirds of the cases were settled by agreement between the representatives of the railroads and or ganizations sitting as the First Division of the Board. 

AGREEMENT 

It should be remembered, too, that all of the cases coming before this Board involve alleged violations by the railroads of contracts with their employees. Employees do not present cases which they feel are doubtful. Therefore, it is not surprising to find that, whether decided by the Board with or without a referee, a majority of the cases have been found to involve violations of contracts and have been decided in favor of the employees. 

Exhaustive Negotiation Before Board Receives Case BOARD CASE 

It is also important to understand the procedure followed by organizations such as the Brotherhood of Railroad Train men, before cases are presented to the 

National Railroad Adjustment Board. 

A member of the Brotherhood of Railroad Trainmen who feels that the agreement under which he is working has been violated by the railroad, presents his grievance to the local lodge of which he is a member. If the members consider his grievance has merit, it is approved by the lodge, which directs the local grievance committee of the lodge to handle the grievance with the subordinate officials of the railroad. If the committee failed to reach a settlement, it is obliged to report that fact to the lodge and the lodge will decide whether the decision of subordinate officials shall be accepted or rejected.

If rejected, it is referred to the general grievance committee having jurisdiction on said railroad system for review, and if, in the opinion of the general committee, the grievance has merit, it is approved and appealed to the general officials of the railroad.

If, after conference with the general officials of the railroad, the general committee is unable to make a satisfactory settlement of the dispute, the general committee may request the President of the Brotherhood for assistance. The President thereupon reviews the grievance, and if he decides that the case has merit, a Vice President is assigned to assist the general committee in the handling of its case with the higher officials of the railroad. If the Grand Lodge officer is unable to bring about an agreement, and if he and the general grievance committee feel that justice to the employee requires further prosecution of the case, the matter may be prepared for submission to the National Railroad Adjustment Board. This proposed submission must then be referred to the President of the Brotherhood for his

approval before the case can be sent to the Adjustment 

Board. 

Thus it will be observed that the merits of an employee's case have been thoroughly considered by the responsible representatives of both parties before the Adjustment Board receives the case. No one familiar with the facts would imply, as Mr. Garrett does, that these cases descend upon the carriers without warning and that the consequences of a decision by a "pro-labor" neutral referee are so transcending that "the precedent may apply immediately to thousands of fictitious days exactly like that one, and nobody knows how much it will cost the railroads. The railroads themselves do not know until the claims come in." Contrary to Mr. Garrett's assertions, the railroads are fully informed of all the ramifications involved in every case, for their capable officials, from the bottom up, have spent much time in negotiations on said grievances before they were submitted to the Adjustment Board.

Justice Deadlocked

It would be well at this point to reflect on some history of collective bargaining in the railroad industry.

Prior to the 1934 amendments to the Railway Labor Act, it was practically impossible to reach a decision on a number of important cases involving working agreements. Voluntary adjustment boards then in existence were composed of an equal number of representatives of management and labor. 

If the respective representatives voted as separate units against reaching a decision in an important case there were no means provided for breaking the deadlock. Millions of dollars were withheld from railroad workers because railroad managements placed their own interpretations on the contracts with their workers and no means were available whereby settlements could be obtained without resort to the strike. Mr. Garrett does not mention the millions of dollars which the railroads cheated their workers out of through this impossible set-up by which railroad managements violated their 15

contracts with impunity, knowing all the time there would be thousands of instances where no claims would be made by the employees and thereby enable the railroads to seek the cover of these false precedents. 

Under the 1934 amendments (present law), when a deadlock occurs, the representatives may agree upon a neutral referee and in the event they are unable to agree, such referee is appointed by the National Mediation Board, the members of which Board are named by the President of the United States and confirmed by the Senate. Neither labor nor management has anything to do with the selection of referees by the Mediation Board. 

Both stake their chances on the neutral referee. The only difference is that when labor loses a case it accepts the decision, but railroad management, with its long tradition of controlling public bodies, often cannot reconcile itself to the fact that it is not always right, nor does it always readily submit to the lawful decisions of public bodies established by law, when such decisions are against it. 

High-Priced Justice 

Mr. Garrett's complaint regarding the procedure before the National Railroad Adjustment Board is a familiar one to railroad workers. The railroads do not prefer a poor man's court, where for a nominal sum a poor man can receive justice. They desire expensive court procedure, with high-paid lawyers, knowing that a railroad worker cannot afford to spend $5000 in court litigation to win an award of $1000. Mr. Garrett says the Adjustment Board constitutes "a court above all other courts." By his own article he repudiates this false statement. Even after a referee has decided a money case against the railroad, it does not have to pay the award if it chooses to refuse. Employees may then sue and a federal court will determine whether the award is just and fair and should be paid.

In this situation, the railroads would like to have their employees oblige them by taking every money award (however small) into the courts. Railroad workers do not approve of that kind of "high-priced justice." Situations have arisen in the past where unscrupulous railroad managements have arrogantly refused to carry out the just awards of the Adjustment Board and it was necessary for railroad employees to threaten to strike to enforce such awards. Mr. Garrett does not like the idea of workers striking for their rights, even after those rights have been affirmed by government bodies. He rightly suggests that it would not look well for the railroads to be "in the bad position of courting a strike sooner than to pay a lawful award." We must politely tell Mr. Garrett that the laws of the land give workers the democratic right to strike, and we do not propose to place this right in the hands of Mr. Garrett or anyone propagandizing for railroad managements and Wall 

Street. We should like to ask Mr. Garrett what he, as 16

a democracy-loving American, thinks of an employer who violates his contract with his employees, a violation confirmed by a government body, and forces the employees to threaten to strike before the employer will comply with the decision. Such reprehensible conduct on the part of management fully justifies strike action on the part of the employees.

Garrett's Concept of Employee Rights Contrary to Prin ciples of Jurisprudence 

Mr. Garrett objects to a claim sustained by a referee becoming a precedent for others with similar claims, for, says Mr. Garrett, if these are paid, "nobody knows how much it will cost the railroads." Mr. Garrett does not compliment the railroads when he refers to thousands of cases, adjudicated by government tribunals, of carrier violations of employee contracts. We wish Mr. Garrett's facts, in this instance, were not true. Mr. Garrett apparently is content to accept the philosophy of railroad management to the effect that every employee claim should constitute a separate suit, although such course is contrary historically to established principles of jurisprudence. In the case of taxpayers' or stockholders' suits, it is not necessary for each taxpayer or stockholder to bring a separate action. Obviously railroad workers could not spend such extravagant sums in court costs in order to obtain justice and to compel railroads to live up to their contracts. Finally, why, Mr.

Garrett, should the principles of jurisprudence for railroad workers differ from that which holds for other citizens? 

Like Mr. Garrett, Workers Have Rights

Mr. Garrett does not like the agreements which railroad managements and railroad labor have voluntarily entered into. He does not understand them and he concludes that they are bad. A bit of history here might be illuminating to Mr. Garrett and to some of his readers. Let us assume that he contracted with his employer, the SATURDAY EVENING POST, to write an article on the subject of "Peace on the Rails," for which he is to receive a stipulated fee. If he worked under conditions to which railroad workers were subjected in the years prior to the time of the establishment of working agreements, the editor might have called Mr. Garrett to the office at 2:00 o'clock A.M., have him wait around until 4:00 o'clock A.M., and finally dismiss him. If he then sued for the time so spent and won his case, would he consider that he was being paid for "imaginary work"? Again, the editor might order him to scrub the floors of the publishing house and deny him compensation for this service. Let us assume that Mr. Garrett sued the editor for time spent in scrubbing the floors and that he won an award. Would Mr. Garrett feel that such award constituted a "fattening of the 17

payroll"? Again, let us assume that after having entered into the contract with Mr. Garrett, the SATUR-

"Peace on the Rails," and paid that individual for the story. Mr. Garrett, no doubt, would sue the editor for a breach of contract, but would he call the recovery "time paid for, but not worked," and would he feel that he was "fattening the payroll by imaginary work"? 

The above illustrations are comparable to those which prevailed on the railroads before railroad employees had the protection of working agreements negotiated by their unions. Mr. Garrett, it seems to us, stultifies himself in his characterization of these working agreements. 

He says: "If a man does with his left hand the thing that by the rules should have been done by another with the right hand, his status changes and he is entitled to another rate of pay, or an extra day's pay."

When Mr. Garrett brings into a discussion of wage scales in the railroad industry the subject of lawful awards resulting from violations of contracts between the carriers and their employees, he is no more logical than he would be if he would group together all monies received by enterprisers from court awards resulting from violations of commercial contracts and then call the sum "time paid for but not worked," or "pay for imaginary work." Of course, he would not do that, because it would be a vital thrust at capitalism and our free system of private enterprise. But why does he assault these principles when they relate to the contract rights of working men? 

Mr. Garrett's whole attack upon railroad labor may be well summed up by saying that he does not believe working men's contracts are as sacred as other forms of contracts.

Right for Capital, But Wrong for Labor 

Apparently Mr. Garrett had in mind the railroads' complaint against the mileage basis of pay for train and engine men. Every dollar of revenue the railroads receive from the American public is calculated on the mileage basis, and without regard to the time required for transport. Why is that a righteous principle for the railroads, but a bad principle for railroad workers? Although the principle has not operated to give railroad workers a fair share of the increase in their productivity, it has tended to shorten the work day and to offset the dangerous trend in this country whereby the increasing productivity of labor is intensifying wealth concentration to the detriment of our economic system.

A New Low in Propaganda Technique 

In a sense, it is difficult to answer the propaganda of the railroads, for, as in Mr. Garrett's article, few assertions of fact are made, and only enough facts are given to induce the readers to draw the desired false inferences. A splendid illustration of this type of propaganda is Mr. Garrett's article, "Peace on the Rails." He heaps opprobrium and abuse upon the glorious "Casey" of the railroads. He discusses several cases, involving separate and distinct individuals, which cases were brought before the National Railroad Adjustment Board, but he creates the impression that the same man is involved in each case. He uses the name "Casey" for each claimant.

He bolsters this deception by such expressions as, "Casey is still a yard man," and "and again he was drunk," "he has been fired again," "Casey is this time in a far-away region," "this time Casey is an engineer," 21

"Casey is once more reinstated...."

Mr. Garrett finally draws for the reader the false conclusion that "Casey" has lost eleven and won thirty-two out of forty-three cases. Not only is the proportion of cases won by the employees wrongly represented, but to the casual reader the impression is given that "Casey" is an habitual drunkard, a completely irresponsible man, who repeatedly violates the rules, and who, in almost every case, is reinstated by the Adjustment Board. That is the impression the author desires his readers to gain, but of course it is shamefully false. Shorn of its false embellishments, the net of what Mr. Garrett stated in his article is that a score of men out of a million railroad workers had won their particular cases before the National Railroad Adjustment Board. Mr. Garrett's garbled presentation of facts marks a new low in vicious propaganda.

After falsely leading his readers to believe that "Casey" has a long record of violations with impunity, Mr. Garrett declares that the only reason why "morale and discipline in the operating department of a railroad" has not broken down is because: "Pride of craftsmanship more than one hundred years old does not go to pieces all at once."

War Against Labor's Democratic Rights 

Mr. Garrett and his readers can be assured that pride of craftsmanship among railroad workers would break down almost at once if Mr. Garrett's attitude and philosophy prevailed in the management of railroads. 

Whether knowingly or not, Mr. Garrett is playing a part in the nationwide attack which has been launched against the principle of collective bargaining. This attack began on the National Labor Relations Act and it is continuing with the hope that the Railway Labor Act and the National Labor Relations Act will fall and that the old rule of "tooth and claw" will be reinstated in labor and capital relationships. Mr. Garrett's role in this drama against democratic rights of labor is well portrayed by the article herein criticized and by his subsequent article in the SATURDAY EVENING 

POST, in which he attacks the National Labor Relations Act.

Mr. Garrett attempts to discredit a tribunal created by government for the settlement of disputes that otherwise would be deadlocked between capital and labor. 

Deadlocks between men must be settled either by lawful tribunals or by strife, and we do not want strife. Mr. Garrett refuses to recognize the lawfulness of awards handed down by a government body, and accuses railroad workers of taking something to which they are not entitled when they insist upon lawful
