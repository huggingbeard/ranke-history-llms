A proposal has been advanced by officials of Colonial Williamsburg, Inc. relative to speeding construction of the new mental hospital at Dunbar on the outskirts of Williamsburg, which I feel merits full exploration. 

In brief, this proposal would involve an advance of funds by Colonial Williamsburg in sufficient amount to permit completion of the Dunbar Hospital as rapidly as construction could be undertaken and call for repayment of this sum by the State over a period of years. The tentative suggestions have been offered that the funds be made available on a loan basis or that the land on which the buildings would be constructed be leased to Colonial Williamsburg until the cash advances could be amortized by rentals or some similar method. It is the view of the sponsors of this plan that the early completion of the hospital would result in substantial savings to the State which would more than compensate for the amortization cost. 

Adoption of such a plan would, of course, reserve our long-standing policy of financing capital improvements through current appropriations. also would raise the question of whether similar construction should be undertaken for other institutions where there is a continuing need for physical improvements. I respectfully request the Council to make a thorough study of the proposal advanced by Colonial Williamsburg and submit its findings and recommendations for the consideration of the Governor and General Assembly as early as practicable prior to the convening of the 1954 session.

Upon the receipt of this letter the Council immediately decided to study the subject itself rather than referring it to a committee of the Council. 

A subcommittee, composed of Messrs. Perrow, Button, Harrison, Crockett and DeJarnette, was appointed to act upon matters which might arise between sessions of the Council. 

BACKGROUND 

Much background information is necessary to understand the many aspects of the study. The site of the Eastern State Hospital is in the city of Williamsburg and comprises approximately 207 acres together with buildings in various stages of repair. It is one of the oldest mental institutions in America. One of the largest capital outlay projects upon which the State has entered is the transfer of the entire Eastern State Hospital at Williamsburg to new structures located on the Dunbar Farm some distance outside the city. Toward accomplishment of this objective the State has made large expenditures. 

The College of William and Mary, the second oldest institution of higher education in the nation is also located in the city of Williamsburg. 

The College was destitute when hostilities ceased in 1865 and for many years thereafter its future was clouded with doubt. Under a series of able presidents the affairs of the College came to be placed upon a more stable footing and its continuation and enlargement was assured. In 1887 the General Assembly made an appropriation to the College, and in 1906 the College became a State institution. 

The city of Williamsburg is in an area with a rich historical past, whose significance is known by all those familiar with the history of the United States. Practically all physical traces of this historic past had been lost at the time when the late Dr. W. A. R. Goodwin conceived the happy project of interesting one of the country's most generous philanthropists in the restoration of colonial Williamsburg. Bishop Goodwin's project was crowned with success beyond his fondest dreams and today the corporation which was created to restore historic Williamsburg exists as a monument to these two men. Colonial Williamsburg, Incorporated, has conceived its purpose to be the restoration, from the original plans, of the buildings, streets, and other aspects of a city which existed in the early period of American history. Its attraction for tourists is unique. This has helped to make the tourist industry one of the greatest income producers in the 

State. 

To effect and provide for the transfer of the hospital from the present site in the city to the Dunbar Farm the General Assembly in the early 1940's provided funds which, with subsequent appropriations, have amounted to more than $6,000,000. The transfer is still far from completion being only 29.5% complete. In 1944 the General Assembly authorized the conveyance of the 207 acre tract to the College of William and Mary when the property is no longer required for hospital purposes. At the current rate of transfer it will be many years before the property becomes available to the College. 

The hospital property contains many sites such as Colonial Williamsburg has restored. The present buildings are not in harmony with the restoration so far achieved in Williamsburg. Colonial Williamsburg is therefore interested in effecting the transfer as speedily as possible in order to complete the restoration and for other purposes and the College cannot, of course, obtain possession until the transfer of the hospital is completed.

The hospital cannot be transferred, Williamsburg cannot take on its complete historic appearance, the College cannot use the property, and the savings to the State of a unified operation at Dunbar cannot be achieved, for a long period of time under present conditions. 

The Proposals of Colonial Williamsburg 

The Council was concerned in learning the nature of the proposals entertained by Colonial Williamsburg and set a meeting with their representatives for this purpose. At that meeting, representatives of Colonial 

Williamsburg made the following offer: 

1. That arrangements be made to lend the State $23,000,000 for the purpose of constructing Eastern State Hospital on the Dunbar site and thus complete the transfer. 

2. That the interest rate on the obligation be fixed at a figure below that generally payable for like obligations. 

3. That the loan be repaid over a period of years. 

4. That Colonial Williamsburg be permitted to acquire the present hospital site at a price to be agreed upon by those concerned. 

It was stated that this plan would save the State fourteen million dollars by having the entire hospital constructed at once with additional economies resulting from operation in one place rather than in two places. 

The Council requested information as to the intended uses of Colonial 

Williamsburg regarding the 207 acre tract. Colonial Williamsburg advised the Council that it intended to do several things with the property, if acquired: (a) A portion of it would be immediately restored, this being the area shown on certain maps of the old city; (b) A portion of the property and the building thereon would be developed as a community hospital; (c) A portion of the property, together with certain buildings thereon, would be developed, in appropriate architectural details, as quarters in which students from all over the country might be housed at low cost for educational visits to the Williamsburg area; (d) A portion of the property and buildings thereon would be used for purposes, such as storage, incidental to the operation of the restoration; and the remainder of the property would be developed as circumstances required.

The Council was dubious as to whether the proposal could, under the Constitution, be effected without a vote of the people. Colonial Williamsburg employed special counsel who thoroughly investigated this question and advised that, in their opinion, the creation of an Authority which would issue its bonds, construct the facilities, and lease them to the State Hospital Board would afford a legal means of accomplishing the objective. 

The Council subcommittee then requested counsel for Colonial Williamsburg to prepare and submit its financing plan, free from legal objection, to it as soon as convenient. Subsequently, Colonial Williamsburg presented a plan to the Council for financing the construction of the hos-

pital, involving the creation of a hospital Authority with power to issue revenue bonds. Colonial Williamsburg also expressed its willingness to bear the expense of litigation testing the validity of bonds issued by such an Authority, further, to acquire the bonds at a favorable interest rate to the State, and to hold them until maturity. These factors make this plan unique. 

The Council presented this plan to the Attorney General with a request for his advice as to its legality. The Council is advised that, in the opinion of the Attorney General, the plan is legal and that the bonds would not be obligations of the State under the provisions of § 184-b of the Constitution; they would constitute revenue bonds payable solely from special revenues, in this case the rental of the buildings by the institution from the Authority. A similar distinction has already been upheld in a Virginia case involving the issuance of highway bonds. 

CONSIDERATION OF PROPOSALS 

Upon the solution of the legal obstacle, the Council was informed that Colonial Williamsburg was in the process of preparing an offer for the 207 acre tract. It had been indicated that this offer would be based upon negotiations with the College by Colonial Williamsburg. 

Shortly after the Council had ascertained that the legal difficulties could be overcome, a meeting of the subcommittee of the Council with the President of the College and the Rector of the Board of Visitors was held on October 5, 1953. At that time, the entire proposal was presented by the 

Council subcommittee to these officials of the College. It was indicated that a substantial offer would be made the College for the property involved. It was pointed out that negotiations were still under way and that, at the proper time, a meeting of the several interested parties would be held for the purpose of acting on the entire plan. The Council did not, during this time, consult the State Hospital Board, the Governing Board of the Eastern State Hospital, these matters being deferred until the Council had a complete proposal before it. 

While the Council had the matter in abeyance, pending the receipt of an offer for the property from Colonial Williamsburg, a meeting of the Board of Visitors of the College of William and Mary was held in Williamsburg on October 17, 1953. At that time the Board of Visitors adopted a resolution stating that they were "unalterably opposed" to the transfer of any of the hospital property. Immediately, Colonial Williamsburg advised the Governor that this action of the Board defeated any attempt to effect the transfer of the hospital. Thereafter, the Board of 

Visitors of the College, through the Rector, wrote the Governor, stating, in essence, that while the College required the property for its purposes, the overall needs of the State might require a different use and disposition of the property. These were transmitted to the Council, which renewed its consideration of the subject. On December 17, 1953, the Council requested an opportunity to meet with the Board of Visitors of the College on January 10, 1954. Between the two dates the Council was advised that the College might well obtain a very substantial sum to compensate it for the renunciation of its claims to the Eastern State Hospital property. 

At the joint meeting of the Council and the Board of Visitors of the College on January 10 in Williamsburg, the entire proposal, in all its aspects, was presented to the Board of Visitors of the College. It was stated on behalf of the Board that many of these matters had not been known before.

It was pointed out on behalf of the College that the transfer of approximately 11 acres of the property to Colonial Williamsburg would permit the restoration of the historical sites. It was pointed out on behalf of the Council, however, that the major purpose of the undertaking is the immediate transfer of Eastern State Hospital to a new site with greatly improved treatment for patients, which we are advised will save very substantial sums in capital outlays, and result in substantial economies from centralized operation. It was stated that the realization of this objective was contingent upon the adoption of a plan of financing offering savings which could not be duplicated. Few investors, it was noted, care to enter into binding commitments where the known interest return is less than the usual market rate. It was finally pointed out that the transfer of the hospital depended upon such a plan of financing, and the financing depended upon the transfer of the entire tract. The Board of Visitors was requested to consider this matter and to advise the Council as to whether they would agree to dispose of the 207 acre tract under a plan whereby the consideration therefor (which would be generous) might be appropriated to the College either as endowment or for capital outlays. 

Before the Council left this meeting, the Board advised it that the College has 1200 acres, a portion of which is unsuitable for development as a college campus. The Board further advised the Council that the tract of 207 acres is required for educational purposes. In conclusion, the 

Board stated that it would consider the presentation made by the Council and the two groups met in separate session. 

Upon conclusion of the session of the Board, the Council was advised that the Board had adopted the following resolution:

Upon consideration of this resolution and the negotiations which the Council has had, the Council reports that the proposal for the immediate transfer of Eastern State Hospital from the present site to the Dunbar Farm cannot be accomplished in accordance with the original offer made by Colonial Williamsburg. The proposals advanced by Colonial Williams burg, with their consequent advantages to the State and to the city of Williamsburg, involve the acquisition of the entire tract. The first paragraph of the resolution of the Board defeats this. 

The Council feels that the proposed transfer by the College of William and Mary of the eleven acre tract referred to, in consideration of repeal of the 1932 legislation, is a matter which is not germane to the subject of its study. It will not aid in transferring Eastern State Hospital to Dunbar Farm. It will not give Colonial Williamsburg, in the near future, possession of the property which is desired. It will not permit development of the area in the manner above discussed, resulting in the provision of a community hospital which will be of great benefit to the inhabitants of the city of Williamsburg, and the provision of facilities to accommodate young people who might thereby be enabled to visit the colonial capital of Virginia. It will not provide the funds which it was hoped could be used by the College in meeting many of its pressing present needs.

The Council is convinced that the proposition submitted by Colonial Williamsburg, provided the purchase price is adequate, is for the best interest of the Commonwealth, yet the Council is regretfully forced to the conclusion that the proposal of Colonial Williamsburg for the transfer of Eastern State Hospital to the Dunbar Farm location cannot be effected for the present. In view of this situation, the Council has no present recommendation, but it is urged that the Governor continue to work with the problem in the hope that something may yet be worked out whereby the best interests of the College of William and Mary, Eastern State Hospital and Colonial Williamsburg will be promoted. 

The fourth paragraph of the resolution adopted by the Board of Visitors of the College of William and Mary requests that the correspondence be published. The Council is glad to comply with this request and, with the consent of the Governor, attaches this correspondence as an appendix to this report.

Dear Governor Battle: 

I am writing you in view of the recent statement released to the press by the Board of Visitors of the College of William and Mary under date of October 17, 1953, with respect to the Eastern State Hospital property in Williamsburg.

As you know, Colonial Williamsburg has been actively engaged during the past year in an effort to help find a solution satisfactory to yourself, the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council, and all other interested parties for expediting the removal of the Eastern State Hospital from Williamsburg.

The interest of Colonial Williamsburg in this project stemmed originally from its desire, for obvious reasons, to see all of the Eastern State Hospital patients removed from Williamsburg. This desire has, quite naturally, been shared by the City of Williamsburg. 

Another impelling factor in the interest of Colonial Williamsburg in this project has been its desire to acquire the Eastern State Hospital property in order to make possible further steps in the completion of the Restoration.

Colonial Williamsburg first expressed its interest in the removal of the Eastern State Hospital from Williamsburg in 1934. We learned at that time of the State's ownership of the Dunbar site and its plans for the eventual removal of the entire hospital to that site. We were also advised that if funds for utilities at the new site could be obtained, the proposed development at Dunbar might be expedited. 

As a result, Colonial Williamsburg in 1936 contributed $25,000 to the State of Virginia toward the utilities at Dunbar, and in accepting this contribution the State agreed to move the more disturbed patients from Williamsburg to Dunbar as promptly as possible and to erect no new buildings at the Williamsburg site.

Although the first appropriation for buildings at Dunbar was made in 1936, progress since that date, as you know, has not been as rapid as was originally hoped, due to the intervention of World War II and for other reasons. As a consequence at the present time only about 700 patients are located at Dunbar, as compared to about 1400 patients still remaining in 

Williamsburg. 

In the latter part of 1950 the College of William and Mary initiated negotiations for the sale to Colonial Williamsburg of the Hospital property in Williamsburg. These negotiations were not concluded only because the 

State could not promise possession at any definite date. Having in mind these negotiations with the College and in view of the various considerations set forth above,, we approached you over a year ago as to the possibility of expediting the complete removal of the Hospital to Dunbar. 

With the benefit of your cooperation, these conversations led in succession to a survey and report by a Richmond architectural firm indicating an estimated saving of over $14,000,000 to the State if the removal to Dunbar could now be completed in a single operation instead of being prolonged over a period of twenty years or more; to the reference of the entire matter to the Virginia Advisory Legislative Council; and to an exploration of the legal problems involved by special Richmond counsel retained for 13

that purpose and acting in consultation with a Sub-Committee of the Legislative Council and with the Attorney General. 

As you know, the plan which finally resulted from this combined effort contemplated the creation of a State Hospital Building Authority which would issue approximately $23,000,000 of bonds for the immediate completion of the facilities at Dunbar, without creating any State indebtedness. Proposals under this plan were still under consideration, subject always to the condition of an agreement between Colonial Williamsburg and the College of William and Mary for the purchase of the Eastern State Hospital property, when the Board of Visitors released its statement of October 17, placing on record its "complete and unalterable opposition to the transfer of all or any part of the lands of the College of William and Mary and the improvements thereon, to any person, firm or corporation whatsoever."

This action by the Board of Visitors has made it impossible for Colonial Williamsburg to pursue the matter any further and has automatically voided the proposals we have been discussing with you and the 

Virginia Advisory Legislative Council. I wish to thank you, on behalf of my associates and myself, for your courteous consideration of these proposals and for your cooperative attitude throughout our extended discussions. 

With Kindest personal regards, Sincerely yours,

Dear Governor Battle: 

The letter of November 30, 1953, addressed to you by direction of the Board of Visitors of the College of William and Mary in session on November 29, 1953, with respect to certain of its land, has been variously interpreted as an effort to re-open the question of the proposal of Colonial Williamsburg to make available funds for removing Eastern State Hospital to Dunbar, and as an offer on the part of the College to sell certain of its lands to Colonial Willamsburg. 

The letter was intended to state, and we believe did state, only thisif the Governor and the General Assembly determined that it was in the public interest so to do, the College, in order to expedite the restoration and reconstruction of certain properties of historical significance owned by it, would convey these properties for such purpose. 

As has been previously stated in the Press and elsewhere, it is the considered judgment of the Board of Visitors that the present and future needs of the College require as much or more land than it now owns, and consequently, the public interest demands that the College retain all of the land to which it holds title in order that it may adequately discharge its responsibility to the citizens of our Commonwealth, and especially to those who reside in that area of Virginia where the College is situated. This judgment must have been held by the General Assembly, when it enacted Chapter 248 of the Acts of 1944 authorizing the transfer to the College of the property on which Eastern_State Hospital is situated, and by the Honorable Colgate W. Darden, Jr., who was then Governor of Virginia, and the State Hospital Board, when Mr. Darden executed and delivered and the State Hospital Board caused to be executed and delivered in its behalf the Deed dated September 5, 1945, by which said property was transferred to the College.

Nevertheless, the Board reiterates, if the Chief Executive and the General Assembly of our Commonwealth, in their combined wisdom, are of the opinion that the public interests which will be served by the surrender of the College's properties of historical significance, as named in my letter of November 30, 1953, for the purpose of reconstruction and restoration are superior to the public interests which will be served by the retention of these properties by the College for the purpose of meeting the needs of higher education in Virginia, the College will convey so much of its lands as may be reasonably necessary for such purpose to whomsoever the Governor and General Assembly may direct, upon such terms and conditions as to them may seem right and proper.

In the event it is determined that these properties should be conveyed for the purposes above stated, we believe that a premise of any such transfer should be: that following the consummation of such transfer the

College will be in as good a position as it enjoys prior to such transfer. 17
