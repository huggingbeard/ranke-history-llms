The Problem 

Within the last few years creditors have taken a vast number of life insurance policies as collateral security. In many cases this has not been done alone on the ground that life insurance is a valuable form of security, but often partly because it was the only security which the debtor had available to replace other collateral which had rapidly depreciated. As a result the use of life insurance as collateral has become of such importance that careful consideration of the special nature of this form of security is necessary.

Life insurance differs from other forms of choses in action used as collateral in several important respects. In the first place, in a life insurance policy there is usually a third party who is interested, namely, the beneficiary, whose rights in the policy must always be considered. In the second place, at the time the life insurance policy is transferred as collateral, it may have little or no actual cash value, whereas immediately after transfer it may become of great value because of its maturity by reason of the death of the insured. In the third place, many life insurance policies contain a number of privileges and options that may be exercised during the lifetime of the insured by the person having title thereto, such as, for example, the right to take the cash surrender value, the right to secure loans either in cash or for the purpose of paying premiums, the right to change the beneficiary designation, the right to convert the policy to some other form of insurance, the right to make the policy a paid-up policy for a lesser amount, etc. 

The failure to take into consideration the special nature of life insurance policies in making the transfer as collateral has 1

in some instances resulted in loss to the creditor and in other cases has caused expensive litigation to determine the interest of the beneficiary in the policy upon the death of the insured. 

The writer has undertaken to study this problem from the standpoint of the insurance company which he represents, from the viewpoint of the insured in order to advise policyholders, and also from the creditor's side of the problem because of having been privately employed by a number of banks to assist them in securing proper title to life insurance policies deposited as collateral.

The Assignability of a Life Insurance Policy 

The courts have generally agreed that in absence of a provision prohibiting the assignment of the policy contract, it may be freely assigned as any other form of chose in action by any party having a definite interest therein. Most of the modern insurance policies have been drafted on the assumption that they are freely assignable, and have merely included provisions stating either how the assignment is to be made, or providing that the insurance company will not be deemed to have notice of the existence of an assignment unless a copy of the assignment is filed with it or some other formality adhered to. Therefore, under most of the modern policies, it is recognized that the insured and beneficiary may make an assignment of their interest in the policy, so far as the insurance company is concerned.

Who Should Execute a Transfer of the Policy as Collateral 

The insurance contract usually involves three parties: the insured, the beneficiary, and the insurance company. The transfer as collateral involves some form of transfer of the policy in which these three parties are interested to a fourth party, the creditor. Such transfer is usually to secure an obligation of the insured, although occasionally it is to secure a joint obligation of the insured and beneficiary, or may be made as an accommodation for some third party.

It is a popular notion that the insured owns the policy if he has paid the premiums. However, the courts with almost no exception have agreed that in absence of a provision giving the insured the right to change the beneficiary, the beneficiary has a definitely vested property right in the policy, even though the right of the beneficiary to receive the proceeds will depend upon whether the beneficiary is living at the death of the insured.❜ 

Therefore, where the interest of the beneficiary is vested, the insured cannot defeat the interest of the beneficiary by an assignment without the consent of the beneficiary. 

Many of the life policies issued today reserve to the insured the right to change the beneficiary designation. However, only a few policy forms in use specifically reserve to the insured the right to assign the policy without the consent of the beneficiary, even though he has reserved the right to change the beneficiary. 

The courts usually make a definite distinction between a change of beneficiary and an assignment of the policy. The right to change the beneficiary is the power to appoint a payee of the policy. Most policies reserving this power to the insured provide that it may be exercised by observing certain formalities, such as the requirement that a written notice of change of beneficiary be filed at the home office of the insurance company with the policy for indorsement of the change thereon by the company, which change may or may not require the consent of the insurance company. An assignment is a transfer of the policy itself. Most policies do not require a specific procedure to be

The Beneficiary as a "Real" Surety 

Where the consent of the beneficiary is necessary to a transfer of the policy as security for an obligation of the insured, it becomes important to understand the position of the beneficiary in such a transaction. If the policy does not reserve to the insured the right to change the beneficiary designation, so that the beneficiary has a vested interest, or if the insured has reserved that right but the law of the particular state requires the consent of the beneficiary to the assignment notwithstanding, then the beneficiary is in the position of a surety. 

That is, her interest in the policy has been pledged to the creditor for an obligation of the insured. For example, in the Oklahoma case cited below the court said: "If the terms of the policy and the loan contract created the relationship of creditor and debtor between the insurance company and the insured, and it required the consent of the beneficiary to enable the insured to hypothecate the policy, then the beneficiary stood in the relation of a surety towards the holder of the insurance policy. This principle of law would apply if the insured could not hypothecate the policy as collateral security for the loan made pursuant to the terms of the policy, without the consent of the beneficiary." 

In this case, the policy was being assigned to the life insurance company for a loan, but the rule is the same where the assignment is made to any other creditor. This rule is supported by the following cases: Ala. Cal.

Where the beneficiary is not personally liable for the indebtedness of the insured, but merely her interest in the life insurance policy is liable, the beneficiary is called a "real surety". 

A real surety will be discharged under the same circumstances as a personal surety. Any change in the contract for the performance of which a surety is liable, which change is made without the consent of the surety, will either discharge the surety entirely 10

or to the extent the surety is injured by the change. A good statement of this general rule of law as it applies to a real surety is contained in the syllabus to Rowan v. Manufacturing Company, 33 Conn. 1 (1865), in which it was said that: "Where a person pledges his property as security for the performance of the contract of a third party, the property stands in the position of a surety; and any changes in the contract which would have discharged a surety upon the contract will discharge the property pledged as such security." 

It is a general rule that the following changes in the contract of indebtedness without the consent of the surety will release the surety either entirely or to the extent injured: 

(a) EXTENSION OF TIME. Limitations on Assignments by Certain Beneficiaries 

Since the consent of the beneficiary to an assignment of the life insurance policy is usually essential or desirable, it is important that the signatures of the proper parties be secured and that those who sign are competent to do so. 

A minor is under the same disabilities in connection with a contract of this kind that he is in connection with any other form of contract that he may make. In general he may repudiate the pledge of his interest in the policy at any time before reaching majority, or within a reasonable time thereafter." Therefore, the signature of a minor should never be accepted to an instrument of transfer of a life insurance policy for collateral purposes, or in connection with any agreement in relation thereto. If there is a minor beneficiary and the insured has reserved the right to change the beneficiary designation, the policy should be changed so as to eliminate the designation of the minor as a beneficiary. After the transfer of collateral has been made, the minor may be again designated as beneficiary subject to the transfer as collateral.

Often a policy to be assigned as collateral will have what is commonly called "contingent beneficiaries". For example, the policy may be payable to Mary Smith, wife of the insured, if she is living at the death of the insured, but if she is not then living, in equal shares to such of the following children of the insured, namely, Jane Smith, Margaret Smith, and Robert

If the signature of the beneficiary is required, in view of the previous discussion, it is just as necessary to secure the signature of the "contingent beneficiaries" as it is to secure the signature of the "primary beneficiary"." All are beneficiaries, but who will actually collect the proceeds of the policy depends merely upon the order of the death of the beneficiaries.

If the policy is payable to a trustee and transfer of the policy requires the signature of the beneficiary, the question always arises as to whether the trustee is competent to join in the assignment. A trustee does not act in his own right but always as a fiduciary for the beneficiaries of the trust. Therefore, a transfer of the policy by a trustee will depend entirely on whether the power is conferred upon the trustee by the terms of the declaration or agreement creating the trust. If such power is not conferred the interest of the trustee in the policy must be eliminated, if permitted by the trust agreement, before the transfer; otherwise the transfer may be invalid. The interest of the trustee may then be reestablished in regular course subject to the collateral transfer after it has been completed.

If a policy, payable in the form of income, is assigned to a creditor as collateral, the creditor may find that he must accept payment in the form of income, which may continue over a long period of time. This is usually a disadvantage to the creditor and also to the beneficiary who may be entitled to the balance. Where this problem arises, the policy should be changed, if the form of policy will permit, so that the amount due the creditor will be paid in one sum. Usually, the balance may be paid in the form of income to the beneficiary. No definite plan can be suggested here for accomplishing this, as policies and agreements for paying income vary so greatly in their terms. In such case, the creditor should advise the insurance company of his desires and permit the insurance company to suggest the necessary arrangements in view of the provision of the particular policy involved. 

The Methods of Transferring the Policy as Collateral There are several ways in which a policy may be transferred to a creditor as security, the proper method of transfer in a particular case depending upon the purpose of the collateral. 

In some cases the insurance may be voluntarily secured by the insured for the purpose of paying his debt without any requirement on the part of the creditor that the insurance be carried. 

In other instances, the insurance may be required by the creditor, in which case the creditor will require a valid lien on the insurance, and possibly the entire control over the collateral. 

(1) Designation of Creditor as Beneficiary 

The simplest method of making a policy payable to a creditor is by designating the creditor as beneficiary. Usually the policy is so written as to provide that the death claim shall be payable to the creditor, his executor, administrator, or assignee, as his interest may appear; balance, if any, to a designated beneficiary or to the estate of the insured. It should be noticed that, under a policy written in this manner, the creditor usually does not receive any of the proceeds until the death of the insured. 14

Under the provisions of most policies written in this manner, the policy will not give to the creditor any control over the insurance during the lifetime of the insured, such as the right to take the cash or loan value in the event of default in the indebtedness. Furthermore, if the policy matures as an endowment, the policy may provide that the endowment value will be payable to the insured. If the insured reserves the right to change the beneficiary designation, this will give him the power to terminate the interest of the creditor in the policy at any time. 

This is perfectly proper in those cases where the insured is carrying the insurance on his own responsibility for the purpose of liquidating the debt at his death. It should be kept in mind, however, that a debt does not necessarily mature upon the death of the insured. Therefore, the insured should have the creditor agree that he will accept payment through life insurance at the time of his death, if the insured wants to be certain that his life insurance will operate to pay the debt and release other collateral held.

If the creditor is designated as beneficiary in the policy, it is always important to the insured that the beneficiary designation specify that the creditor is to be a beneficiary, as creditor, only to the extent of the indebtedness. Otherwise, the creditor may collect the proceeds as an ordinary beneficiary and refuse to apply the amount collected on the debt, and then prove against the estate of the insured for the whole debt. Under the laws of most states, the insured may designate any beneficiary regardless of whether the beneficiary has an insurable interest in the life of the insured," and, therefore, it may not necessarily be assumed that the amount payable to a beneficiary is in satisfaction of indebtedness. This difficulty is most likely to occur where the insured has made a policy payable to a close friend or relative to whom he owes money.

Occasionally a creditor may take out insurance on the life of the debtor and pay the premiums on the insurance so secured. 

Where this is done, the creditor should be the owner of the insurance and should be the beneficiary of the full amount under the policy regardless of the amount of the policy. Of course, the rules of law governing insurable interest require that the creditor p. 152 14 

Vance on Insurance, Second Edition, 15

shall not carry insurance on the life of his debtor which is disproportionate to the amount of the debt involved. If there was a proper insurable interest when the creditor secured the policy, there is, in most states, no further question of insurable interest raised by the fact that the indebtedness is later reduced or eventually paid off entirely. Since the insurance in such case belongs to the creditor, the amount paid to the creditor under the policy will not reduce the indebtedness. (2) Collateral Assignment

The most usual method of transferring a policy to a creditor as collateral is by means of a collateral assignment. This may be either of two types. It may be a simple form of assignment reciting that the policy is transferred to the creditor as collateral, or it may be a form containing, in addition, provisions giving to the creditor certain control over the insurance during the lifetime of the insured.

Under the former, the creditor usually merely acquires a lien on the policy. Some of the assignments of this nature provide that the lien of the creditor shall be only upon the amount payable as a death claim. If the assignment does so provide, it would seem clear that the creditor would have no control over the insurance during the lifetime of the insured. He would merely be in a position to retain his lien, but would have no right to make loans under the insurance, or surrender the policy if the debtor defaults. Even under a form of assignment which is not limited to the death claim but which merely purports to place a lien upon the policy, there is considerable question as to what extent the creditor may control the insurance during the lifetime of the insured. Undoubtedly, under the laws of many of the states, it would be necessary for the creditor to foreclose his lien by judicial proceedings or follow some other statutory regulation in order to secure either the loan or cash value of the policy. If the parties intend, at the time the assignment is made, that the creditor shall be placed in a position to foreclose the lien, it is probably advisable to include in the assignment some agreement to the effect that the creditor may merely take the cash or loan value. There is usually nothing that can be accomplished by a foreclosure sale because the cash value of the policy is definitely fixed by its terms and will usually repre- 16

sent the amount for which any party would purchase the policy on execution sale.

On the other hand, if the insurance is carried voluntarily by the insured, the insured may not desire to give to the creditor the right to borrow on the policy or secure its cash value. If such is the case, there would seem to be no objection to using a collateral form of assignment which will give to the creditor a lien only on the amount that may become payable at the death of the insured.

If the insurance is required by the creditor in order to have adequate security for the indebtedness, the creditor will usually desire to have some control over the insurance in event of default in the indebtedness, or in the event of non-payment of the premium on the insurance. For example, in event of default in an interest payment due, the creditor may wish to be in a position to borrow against any loan value provided in the policy in order to pay the defaulted interest item. Or, it may be desirable to borrow to pay a premium due on the policy and thereby prevent its lapse. The more serious situation may occur where, because of default in the indebtedness, it becomes necessary to liquidate the collateral. Cases have occurred where the debtor has disappeared leaving the creditor holding an assignment, which does not authorize the creditor to surrender the insurance. However, the collateral assignment may be so drafted as to include provisions authorizing the creditor to surrender the insurance in event of default, apply the cash value on the indebtedness, and thereby avoid expensive and troublesome foreclosure proceedings. (3) Absolute Assignment

Realizing that a mere beneficiary designation or simple transfer of a policy as collateral usually does not describe the indebtedness, nor the conditions upon which the creditor holds the collateral, nor give to the creditor control of the collateral, it has become the practice of many creditors to have the policy transferred by means of an absolute assignment. However, even though an assignment is absolute on its face, the parties are always in a position to show that the assignment was not in fact absolute, but was collateral, and, in doing so, are in a position to show the terms on which the policy was given as 17

collateral. This means that if an absolute assignment of a policy is taken and there is no further written agreement defining the purpose of the assignment, the parties are in a position, if a lawsuit results, to testify as to the conversation at the time the assignment was taken, in order to show to what extent and under what conditions the policy was to be held as collateral. 

This arrangement always leaves a question of fact, and this will usually be determined by a jury, whose sympathies will be with the beneficiary. Therefore, if nothing further is done, the creditor may not have been placed in any more secure position by having taken an absolute assignment. 

On the other hand, an absolute assignment may place the creditor in the position of being the owner of the insurance as between the insurance company and the creditor. That is often desirable, provided the purpose of the absolute assignment is fully set forth in an agreement between the insured, the creditor, and the beneficiary; otherwise the creditor may become liable for having dealt with the insurance under the absolute assignment in a manner not authorized by the parties. 

For example, acting under the absolute assignment the creditor may surrender the insurance, and shortly thereafter the insured die. The beneficiary may then sue the creditor for damages on the theory that the assignment was in fact collateral even though absolute on its face and that when the policy was so assigned it was understood that the policy was to be held by the bank but not surrendered for its cash value.

Relative Merits of Collateral and Absolute Assignment 

Forms 

There are many questions left open by a mere transfer of the policy to the creditor. What is the indebtedness to be secured by the collateral? What is the amount of the indebtedness?

May the indebtedness be increased and the policy held for such increased amount? What relation does this collateral have to other collateral? May some of the parties to the original debt be released without releasing the beneficiary's interest in the policy as collateral? Can the creditors collect all of the insurance at maturity and credit the debt and pay the balance to the beneficiary? Can the creditor surrender the insurance or secure a loan? Can the creditor collect dividends? Can the creditor collect disability benefits? If the creditor pays the insurance premiums, can the creditor recover such payments? 

Whatever form the transfer of the policy may take these questions should always be settled by written contract between the parties, so that the determination of such intention will not be left to inferences to be drawn from the nature of the transaction and the oral negotiations of the parties. If a collateral assignment is used, the contract covering these questions may be included in the assignment itself, which should be signed by both the insured and the beneficiary. If the parties use a beneficiary designation or an assignment which does not fully set forth the terms on which the assignment is made, there should be a separate agreement between the insured, the beneficiary, and the creditor describing fully the purpose of the transfer and covering each of the problems referred to in these questions. 

Whether it is preferable to use such a collateral assignment, or whether it is preferable to use an absolute assignment with a separate agreement, is a matter which may be subject to con- 19

siderable debate. If a collateral assignment is used, the insurance company will, of course, have to be given notice of the complete nature of the contract between the parties. On the other hand, if the assignment is absolute in form with a separate agreement defining the purpose of the assignment, the creditor may be placed in the position of being the absolute owner so far as its relations with the insurance company are concerned, but will be limited in its control over the policies by the terms of the separate agreement between the creditor, the insured, and the beneficiary. In some respects, the latter is preferable because under such an arrangement the creditor does not need to become involved in a discussion with the insurance company regarding the detailed terms of the agreement upon which the policy is held as collateral. If it happens that the control given to the creditor is to be exercised only in the event of the default of the debt, it is a decided advantage not to have to prove to the insurance company the fact of default in the indebtedness. 

If the absolute assignment is used with a separate agreement between the parties, the insurance company will be discharged by having dealt with the creditor as owner, provided the assignment is sufficient for that purpose. Whether the creditor has dealt properly with the insurance under the terms of the separate agreement will be a matter to be settled entirely between the creditor, the insured, and the beneficiary. 

Where an absolute assignment is used for collateral purposes, knowledge of the insurance company that it is so used may place it on notice of the fact that the creditor is not in fact the absolute owner, and therefore place upon the insurance company the burden of determining the actual extent of the transfer. 

This involves the always difficult question as to how far the knowledge of local agents is the knowledge of the company. 

To avoid this problem some companies include in their absolute assignment forms a power of attorney authorizing the assignee to deal with the policy fully as attorney for the insured. There is a practical difficulty of handling these assignments because usually the assignee will not sign as attorney for the insured in submitting papers to the insurance company in the course of dealing with the policy. The necessity of securing new papers for this alone always causes confusion. Therefore it is suggested that, instead of the power of attorney, there should be a pro- 20

vision merely authorizing the assignee to deal with the insurance company in its own name as owner. 

Control of Policy Before its Maturity 

Modern life insurance policies contain numerous rights which may be exercised during the lifetime of the insured. For example, the right to make premium and cash loans, the right to apply dividends on the policy in various ways, the right to change the form of the policy, the right to receive disability benefits, the right to surrender the policy for its cash value, etc. 

Where the insured is voluntarily carrying insurance to liquidate his indebtedness in event of his death, there is no reason why the creditor should have any control over the policy while the insured is living. However, there has been some difference of opinion as to how far a creditor should be given the control of the insurance during the lifetime of the insured, where the creditor has required the insurance as collateral. It has been argued that the creditor should not be given the right to terminate the policy and therefore leave the insured without protection. 

On the other hand, some creditors have been confronted with an awkward situation where the debtor has refused to protect the insurance or left the community, leaving the creditor holding a policy which he could not surrender so as to apply the cash value on the indebtedness. If insurance is to be of value as collateral, a proper amount of control must be given to the creditor for his protection. Perhaps it is well to limit the control of the creditor only to the time when the debt is in default. It is inadvisable to include a condition of this kind in any assignment filed with the insurance company, because evidence of default will have to be furnished to the insurance company, which may often be difficult to establish to the satisfaction of the insurance company. This is one of the reasons why it has been suggested that a form of assignment be used which will place the creditor in the position of the owner of the policy as between the insurance company and the creditor, and include any limitations in a separate agreement between the insured, the beneficiary, and the creditor. If the creditor violates this agreement he will be liable for such violation which will generally give the insured and beneficiary the desired protection. 21

Whether the creditor should be placed in a position to secure the disability benefits and apply them on its indebtedness has also been subject to controversy. It should be pointed out here that under some policy contracts the disability payments will reduce the face amount of the policies, and some policies provide that while disability is being paid there are no loan or cash values. Where a policy of this kind is assigned for collateral, it is obvious that, unless the disability benefits are also included in the assignment, the value of the policy as collateral would be jeopardized in event of disability of the insured. It should also be noted that, under the terms of some policies, if the insured is disabled for a specified length of time, the policy will mature as an endowment and the face of the policy will be paid. The disability income may or may not be continued after the date of the payment of the policy as an endowment. Presumably, such policy matures as an endowment because of the disability of the insured and, therefore, the endowment value may be held to be a disability benefit. That being true, an assignment of a policy of this kind which excepts from the assignment the disability benefits may prove disastrous to the creditor. 

Application of Policy Proceeds to the Indebtedness In transferring policies as collateral, altogether too little consideration has been given to the question as to how the proceeds of the policy will be applied. Usually the insurance policy is only a part of the collateral held. From the standpoint of the creditor, and often from the standpoint of the insured, it is desirable that the debt immediately be canceled by the payment of the life insurance as a death claim. However, as previously pointed out, the interest of the beneficiary in the policy may be in the position of a surety. Such being the case, the beneficiary may be able to require the creditor first to resort to other collateral held, before resorting to the proceeds of the insurance, or, if the insurance proceeds are applied on the debt, the beneficiary may be entitled to contribution from other sureties, if any, for her loss, may be subrogated to other collateral held by the creditor, and may have a right against the estate of the insured for any amount of the debt paid from the insurance proceeds. 

For example, in Farracy v. Perry, 12 S. W. (2d) 651 (Tex. 22

C. C. A. 1929) it was held that the creditor must resort to other collateral first. A policy on the life of Perry, payable to his wife with right reserved to the insured to change the beneficiary designation, was assigned to a bank to secure personal indebtedness of the insured and partnership indebtedness of a firm of which the insured was a partner. The court said "While Mrs. 

Perry had no vested right in said policies during the lifetime of her husband, but had only an expectancy, on his death, however, her expectance became a vested right to the entire proceeds of said policies, subject only to the rights of the bank. The bank had only a lien on said policies regardless of the form of assignment.

The debt due the bank . . having been incurred before the policies were transferred to it as collateral security and... Mrs. Perry not being in any way personally responsible for said indebtedness, and, on the death of L. C. Perry, the title to said policies and the proceeds of same having vested in Mrs. Perry and become her separate property, subject only to the rights of the bank, said policies, her said separate property, occupied the position of a surety for the payment to the" bank of the obligation of the partnership and Perry, and the fact that the obligation was greater than the amount of insurance payable, "did not... deprive her of the privilege of taking all necessary steps to protect her said separate property. In order to do this, under the well established principles of law of both our state and federal courts, she had the right to have the partnership collateral held by the bank applied to the ... partnership indebtedness to said bank, before so applying any part to her said separate property."

It has also been held by the supreme court of North Carolina in Russell v. Owen et al., 203 N. C. 262, 165 S. E. 687 (1932), that if the debt of the insured has been paid out of the proceeds of insurance assigned as collateral, the beneficiary is entitled to be subrogated to other collateral held by the creditor. 

In this case the insured secured two loans from the Equitable 

Life Assurance Society on the security of two deeds of trust on real property. He also secured two life insurance policies each payable to his wife, as beneficiary, if living at his death, but if not then living to his estate. Both policies reserved to the insured the right to change the beneficiary designation. Both the insured and his wife joined in an assignment of these policies 23

to the insurance company as additional security for these loans. 

On the death of the insured the insurance company applied the proceeds of the policies to the payment of the bonds secured by the mortgages on the real property. The court said "In the instant case no change of beneficiary having been made by the insured while the policies were in force, the interest of" the beneficiary "named in each policy at the time it was issued, ceased to be contingent upon the death of the insured; upon his death it ripened into a vested interest, with the result that, subject only to the rights of the creditor of the insured, the Equitable Life Assurance Society of the U. S., under the assignments executed while the policies were in force, by both the insured and the beneficiary, the proceeds of the policies became and were the property of" the beneficiary, "free from all the claims of the representatives of her husband or any of his creditors except the claims of the Equitable Life Assurance Society of the United States, as assignee of the policies. In Pearshall v. 

Bloodworth, 194 N. C. 628, 104 S. In the instant case the claim of the 

Equitable Life Assurance Society to the proceeds of the policies was as assignee, and not as beneficiary. • 9 "The policies were assigned by the insured and beneficiary, jointly, to the Equitable Life Assurance Society, as additional security for bonds executed by the insured as principal, and the beneficiary as surety. These bonds were also secured by deeds of trust on lands owned by the insured. After the death of the insured, the bonds were paid out of the proceeds of the policies of insurance. Thus, the property of the surety, with her consent, was applied to the payment of the bonds, on which the insured was liable, as principal. By reason of these facts, the" beneficiary "became a creditor of the estate of her husband" in the amount due on the bonds and paid out of her property. "As such creditor she is entitled to be subrogated to all the rights of the Equitable Life Assurance Society under the deeds of trust which" the insured "had executed as security for said bonds."

The court said, “A fundamental error in the defendant's argument is the assumption that the assignment as collateral was an extinguishment, pro tanto, of the beneficiaries rights. In the case of some forms of assignments which are used, it is contemplated that the creditor will not collect on the death of the insured more than the amount of the indebtedness. On the other hand, many assignments are being used with the idea that the creditor will collect the whole amount payable under the insurance policy and undertake to dispose of the balance. Of course, if the creditor pays the balance to the wrong party, he will be liable to the party who was actually entitled thereto. Under some policies an assignment operates to change the beneficiary and, therefore, the transfer may operate to eliminate the interest of the beneficiary. Under other policies, the assignment may not affect the interest of the beneficiary except to the extent of the interest of the creditor in the policy. Therefore, the disposition of any such excess should be specifically provided for in either the assignment itself or in a separate agreement between the insured, the beneficiary, and the creditor, in order that there may be definite authorization for the payment to be made by the creditor.

Premium Payments 

How to determine whether the premiums are paid is one of the problems which confronts every creditor who has required insurance to be carried by the debtor as security. The insured may default in any agreement with the debtor to pay premiums, 25

just as he may default in his indebtedness. However, the creditor is usually desirous of knowing if there is going to be a default in the premium so that pressure may be brought to bear on the insured to pay the premiums, or so that the creditor may advance the premium before the policy lapses, if the creditor so desires. 

Usually the insurance company cannot give this notice for the reason that it never knows whether the premium is going to be paid until the days of grace have expired. Furthermore, payments are often made through local offices and the remittances do not reach the home office until several days after payment. A duplicate premium notice may be sent to the assignee and in some states this is required by law. That, however, does not advise the creditor that the premium will be paid by the insured. The safest method of handling this is to require the insured to present his premium receipt to the creditor within a given number of days before the grace period for the payment of premiums expires. This will give the creditor time to determine what action he desires to take before the policy lapses. Often the creditor will want to pay the premiums to prevent lapse of the insurance, if the debtor is unable to do so. As a matter of justice, courts have generally allowed such premiums to be added to the debt." However, it is always much better to have a definite understanding with the insured and beneficiary on this point at the time the policy is transferred as collateral.

Some policies which contain a loan value also contain an option, available at the election of the parties owning the policies, that if a premium is not paid when due the company will automatically make a premium loan against the policy to pay the premium when due. It is usually an advantage to the creditor to have this option made operative at the time the policy is assigned to him. This will prevent the policy from lapsing as long as there is sufficient loan value to pay the premiums. The creditor, in the meantime, can take such action as is necessary

If desired, instead of the absolute assignment, the assignment may recite that it is made as collateral security and may include the terms of the foregoing agreement so that only one form need be signed. However, if that is done, the control of the insurance by the creditor should not be limited to a time after default in the indebtedness. If it is so limited, proof of default will have to be furnished to the insurance company before the creditor can deal with the policy, and the furnishing of proper proof may be difficult.

Policies vary so greatly in their provisions that the terms of each individual policy should be considered at the time the transfer as collateral is made. The form of agreement suggested should be reviewed by local counsel before being used to be certain it meets with the requirements of the laws of the state involved. Furthermore, the parties may not desire to agree to all of its provisions, which may be varied to meet individual needs. A DISTINGUISHED BANKER was addressing a group of young men. "Listen carefully," he said, "for I am about to give you the golden key to freedom and happiness. "Have the courage, once each year, to strike a true balance sheet of your life. In opposite columns, set down what you had hoped to be and to achieve against your actual accomplishments. 

Write down fairly the sum of your possessions, your income and the income which your family would continue to enjoy were your support withdrawn. Total your debts and obligations and then subtract what you actually have from the amount of estate which you had hoped to accumulate. Look deficits fairly in the face. Not by dodging responsibilities, but by facing them, is freedom of mind gained."

Wise and sane advice, which, if followed, leads to a more serious consideration of life insurance than ordinarily it receives. 

With facts plainly assessed, insurance becomes a matter for definite planning, rather than to be left to haphazard decision, based on importunity. 

This is the attitude toward life insurance which the Phoenix Mutual Life Insurance Company of Hartford, Conn. seeks to advance. On its part, the company has recognized the need for intelligent co-operation, by training its limited force of representatives in the principles of life insurance service. The true function of a life insurance representative is not that of a salesman, but of a trusted counsellor. Specialized knowledge of insurance is required and the ability to study problems from the view-point of the insured.

The responsibility is the greater because the business of living is never wholly an individual matter. On each life some other lives must lean; and the assurance of their continued welfare is charged as a duty not only to the one they trust, but to the life insurance representative who advises him, and the company in which he places his confidence.
