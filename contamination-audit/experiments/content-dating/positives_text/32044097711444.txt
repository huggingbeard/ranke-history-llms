COMMENTARY ABSENTEE VOTING Introduction 

South Dakota's basic laws with regard to absentee voting were enacted in 1913 (Chapter 200, Session Laws). 

They were revised slightly in 1919 and 1925. Codified in 

Title 16 of the South Dakota Code of 1939, they were modified to a greater extent in 1944 (Chapter 2, Session Laws), and again amended in 1953 (Chapter 75, Session Laws), 1955 (Chapter 55, Session Laws), and 1957 (Chapter 85, Session Laws). The underlying provisions of the state's laws on the subject of absentee ballots and voters are, however, nearly fifty years old. Since 1913, however, the urbanization and industrial development of the state, the broadening of the franchise, and, more recently, the use of voting machines, have had a great impact on political parties, campaigns, and elections. Moreover, the increased mobility of South Dakota's population and the large numbers of men and women from the state serving in the armed services have lent great significance to the absentee vote. 

In an effort to keep abreast of the times, the Legislature has amended and added sections to that portion of our election laws dealing with absentee voting, but there has been no comprehensive overhauling. As a result, some provisions which remained unchanged in fifty years are unsuited to modern conditions, and many of the attempts at uddating have proved inadequate in practice. There are. of course, many provisions which remain adequate without further amendment or modifications, and others have been modernized in a suitable manner. 

Recognition of Need 

For several years, the need for revision of South Dakota's absentee voting laws has been recognized by various groups, agencies, and individuals. Typical of the expressions was the report of the Legislative Research Council's Committee on State and Local Government, which recommended that the 1961 Legislature provide for a thorough study of absentee voting laws and practices during the 1961-63 biennium. The report (Report of the Legislative Research 

Council to the 1961 Legislature, September, 1960) stated that in the study of election laws during the 1959-61 interim "...it was frequently pointed out that certain facets of the state's absentee voting laws and practices need attention and improvement. 

The Thirty-seventh South Dakota Legislature concurred in this recommendation, and in order to effectuate it, adopted louse Concurrent Resolution No. 5, 1961, calling for a study of the matter (See Appendix XIII for text of the re-

-6- solution). The Executive Board of the Council, in carrying out the mandate of HCR 5, created the LRC Committee on Elections. 

Absentee Voting in General Although the South Dakota Consitiution guarantees the suffrage to residents of the state who meet certain qualifications, it goes without saying that not every qualified voter is so situated as to be able to present himself at the polls on election day. The number who are unable to do so is particularly high in time of war, and the framers of the Constitution, although not providing directly for absentee voting, recognized in Article VII, section 6, that electors should not lose their right to vote because of absence from the state in the military or naval service. As far back as the Civil War, provisions were made by states for absentee voting of persons in the armed forces--although the first general provision for casting ballots in absentia was not adopted until 1896 when the Vermont statute was enacted.

Although they constitute the largest number of absentee electors, and were the first for whom states made provisions for casting absentee ballots, soldiers and sailors are by no means the only group of qualified electors who cannot be physically present at the polls on election day. There are also numbers of ill and disabled persons, college and university students, migrant workers, persons operating commercial carriers, businessmen away on trips, and others who are unavoidably and necessarily away from their election precincts at election time. 

Because it would be unjust to disenfranchise the several classes of people merely because of their physical location on election day, every state except three (Pennsylvania, South Carolina, and Mississippi) has adopted some sort of general absentee voting law. The breadth of the provisions vary--in a few, they apply only to certain elections, and in three states they apply only to members of the armed forces (New Jersey, Maryland, and New Mexico).

Although the detailed procedures vary, the laws generally provide that the absent voter make application to the proper official in his own voting district for the privilege of voting in absentia, receive a ballot, validate himself before a notary, mark his ballot, and mail it to the proper election authority in his home district.

Possibilities of Fraud Although the study conducted by the Legislative Research Council did not grow from any specific or implicit charges of fraud, there have been complaints to the effect that corruption is possible, and that fraud is encouraged by the state's present absentee voting laws. These complaints have brought insistent demands for reform of the laws on the subject. 

Since an honest electoral system lies at the very basis of popular government, it follows that whenever doubt is cast on that system, steps should be taken to determine if there is corruption, and, to the extent that it exists, to eliminate or correct those statutes which permit it. and the one that 

1961, which

The position taken by the Committee appears to have been contemplated in HCR 5 authorized the study, is not one of criticism of the present laws, except to the extent that defects may be clearly shown. 

The mention of possibilities of fraud, as in the two court cases cited below, should not be interpreted as implying that the Committee on Elections considers the state's absentee voting system has allowed or encouraged corrupt election practices.

In other states, the courts have been explicit in their judgement of certain absentee voting law provisions as means to fraudulent ends. In Kentucky, for example, a 1957 elections case later upheld by the Court of Appeals, prompted this statement from the judge:

As is true of many good laws, the absentee voting law has been and is being used in a manner and as a means to ends that were never dreamed of by the strongest supporters thereof. This law is abundant with opportunities for misuse. Loosely interpreted and loosely applied, the legislation provides a means by which county court clerks can become political dictators. It is a method by which the votes of unqualified persons may be obtained, by which legal voters may be coerced, by which "floaters" may be voted "on the table," by which secrecy of the ballots may be invaded, and by which alteration, forgery and false swearing may be accomplished in obscurity, all tending to destroy the freedom and equality of election. (Courier 

Journal, Louisville, Oct. 2, 1957, as quoted in

Kentucky Legislative Research Commission research publication 52, 1957).

It is interesting to note that the Kentucky law about which the opinion speaks is very similar in context to the absentee voting laws of South Dakota. A similar opinion was rendered in the Louisiana Supreme Court with special regard to use of voting machines in the absentee voting process:

The absentee voting law, as written, when adapted to use with voting machines, in addition to exposing the voter to intimidation and other forms of reprisal, presents a ready-made pattern for vote fraud, such as vote buying, and constitutes such a serious situation that the matter addresses itself to the legislature for prompt correction at its next session. (Dowling v. Parish Democratic 

Committee, 1958, 235 La. 62, 102 So. 2nd 755).

Prevention of Fraud 

Absentee voting is not a problem of South Dakota alone. Absentee voting in every state where it is permitted has created unusual problems in the prevention of fraud and the achievement of good election administration. According to a special committee of the American Political Science Association created at the request of the President to undertake a study of voting in the Armed Forces (House Document No. 

407, 82nd Congress, 2nd Session, 1952) fraud in absentee balloting has been mainly of two kinds: (1) deliberate disqualification of an unduly high proportion of the vote, and (2) undercounting or overcounting of the vote.

Notwithstanding the dangers of fraud, the special committee said, absentee voting privileges are essential for servicemen and other citizens. In its report, the special committee made several recommendations for safeguards to prevent corruption in absentee voting. The report stated that state and local activities for absent voting would likely be unsatisfactory unless three requirements are met: (1) that

state election laws are simple, clear, and orderly; (2) that election officials are capable and honest; and that a strong sentiment exists in favor of giving qualified electors a chance to vote and have their votes counted.

It is not, of course, within the province of the LRC Committee on Elections to make recommendations which would purport to change attitudes, and the selection and instruction of election officials is not part of the law which the Committee was assigned to study. The first requirement for prevention of fraud--simple, clear and orderly laws--is, however, a matter of direct concern, and properly a subject for Committee recommendation.

A Special Problem: Absentee Voting by the Physically Handicapped 

A special problem exists in the procedures spelled out by the state's absentee ballot laws, in that they make no provision for assistance to persons who are physically unable to mark a ballot. This oversight has prompted Representative Deane Davis to suggest that in any revision of absent voting laws, a special provision be included to provide for assistance to such persons by one or more responsible friends or relatives, who would certify as to the validity of the ballot when not actually marked by the absentee voter. 

Recommendations by Sioux Falls Republican Women's Club The Council office received a list of six specific recommendations for the voting of "shut-ins" for modifications to the South Dakota Absentee voting laws, which was considered by the Committee on Elections. The recommendations. received are listed below: 

1. Only persons deputized by the county auditor, city auditor, independent school district clerks or town clerks, as the case may be, shall be permitted to obtain applications for, or notarize absentee ballots. 

2. Two persons of opposite political beliefs or opposing issues, working as a team, shall obtain applications, and when voting absentee ballots, one of the persons shall be a notary public. 

3. All requests for absentee ballots shall be signed by the persons obtaining such applications and shall be dated at the time applications are signed. 4. 

A uniform system should be established to handle requests for applications and issuing absentee ballots. 

On the master registration list, a check mark should be placed in front of the name of each person requesting application for absentee ballot and the date stamped when each ballot is issued. 

A daily record should be kept of the ballots showing names of persons deputized to be responsible for voting them, noting date of issuance.

5. All absentee ballots shall be accounted for and returned to the county auditor, city auditor, independent school district clerk, or town clerk, as the case may be. If ballots have not been voted, the proper notation for not voting should be made on the envelope. 

6. In cities or towns where voting machines are used, all absentee ballots shall be delivered to a special election board for tabulation on election day, thereby eliminating delivering absentee ballots to the various boards and running them through the machines.

ELECTION SUPPLIES AND PROCEDURES 

In addition to the principal subject of study--absentee voting--the Committee reviewed several ancillary subjects which affect elections in South Dakota. Particular attention was paid to the workings of the Single Voter Registration Act, adopted in 1961, which had its first major state-wide trial in the primary election of June, 1962. 

Special attention was also given by the Committee to certain of the statutes dealing with election supplies, with a view toward improving the economy and efficiency of the procedure in South Dakota.

The problem of slowness in providing the county auditor with returns for the elections for which registration the county auditor has responsibility was also considered. 

The same problem also relates to the return of the registration and poll books and other election materials. 

Recommendations have been made by the Committee for changes in all these areas.

RECOMMENDATIONS 1. The Committee recommends consideration by the 1963 

Legislature of two draft bills to the absentee voting statutes to provide for closer controls on the application for, securing of, accountability for, and voting of, absentee ballots (Appendices I and II). 

Based on its studies, which included receipt of and careful review of comments from city and county auditors throughout the state, public hearings, and consideration of laws of other states, the Committee has determined that certain significant changes should be made in South Dakota's absentee voting laws. Spelled out in Appendix I (a bill to amend present law) and Appendix II (a bill to provide new requirements for uniformity in accounting for absentee applications and ballots), these changes are primarily designed to provide better accountability of applications for absentee ballots, better controls on the procedures of securing and voting these ballots, and better controls on and accountability for the ballots themselves. The draft bills would provide for a "team" approach to the securing of applications and ballots for voters in the county where they vote when the voters are physically unable to go to the poll.

They would establish more workable procedures for assistance to absentee voters who are physically incapable of marking their own ballots. They would repeal the present law which allows voters to cast their ballots at the polls after voting absentee. Finally, the recommended bills set out other "house cleaning" changes which if adopted will bring the absentee voting procedure into line with the 1961 

Single Voter Registration Act.

2. 

The Committee recommends consideration by the 1963 Legislature of a draft bill to modify the voting machine lav to allow the recording of all absentee votes within a county on a single voting machine, or on the various precinct machines at intervals during the polling hours (Appendix 111).

As it was originally adopted in 1959, the voting machine law provided that absentee ballots should be recorded on machines at times during the election day when the machines were not otherwise in use. However, the absentee voting law allowed the absent voter to cast his ballot in person after submitting an absentee ballot, leading to the obvious conclusion that if the absentee ballot were recorded before an elector appeared in person, the result would be that he would either have to be turned away from the polls, or his vote recorded twice. For this reason, in 1961, the Legislature modified the voting machine law to provide that all absentee ballots should be recorded after the polls had closed for personal voting. This in turn created problems: the number of absentee ballots in some precincts is so high that more than an hour was required to record the absentee ballots, adding to the fatigue of election boards, and holding up the reporting of results. 

Since this Committee has already (in Recommendation # 1, above) recommended repeal of the section which permits voting in person after having submitted an absentee ballot, the need which occasioned the 1961 change will have been removed if the recommendation is adopted. The Committee therefore recommends that the law be returned to the wording of 1959, with the additional proviso that if the county auditor thinks it advisable, he may deem all absentee ballots to constitute a seperate voting precinct, and have them all cast on a single machine.

3. The Committee recommends that the 1963 Legislature correct an erroneous reference to the pollbook in the 

Single Voter Registration Act (Appendix IV) and eliminate conflicts in other parts of the law which explicitly or implicitly require the use of two pollbooks (Appendix V). 

In section 22 of the Single Voter Registration Act, the word "registration" is used to denominate a book when the obvious intent of the Legislature, judging from the further reading of this and related sections, was that the book should be a "pollbook. Since confusion has resulted from the erroneous terminology, the Committee has prepared and recommends adoption of a bill to correct the matter. 

The use of two pollbooks at election is considered unnecessary by county auditors inasmuch as there is a registration book for each polling place and one pollbook can be used to check against the master registration list. The savings in printing costs alone in the larger counties is substantial if one pollbook is eliminated. Also, one less pollbook provides that one of two poll clerks is eliminated, a saving which would be enjoyed at every voting precinct. 

The Committee concluded that because the county auditors from larger counties believed that sufficient information is available from one pollbook and the registration book to provide cross-checks, the need for two pollbooks does not exist. The Committee, therefore, recommends passage of the bill to decrease the number of pollbooks required at elections from two to one.

4. 

The Committee recommends that the 1963 Legislature adopt bills which would promote efficiency and economy in elections by: (a) permitting the use of metal seals in lieu of padlocks on ballot boxes (Appendix VI); 

161 authorizing the use of metal-topped, canvasbodied ballot boxes (Appendix VII); (c) providing for uniformity in the tally sheets used in primary and general elections (Appendix VIII); and (d) repealing the requirement that the Secretary of State furnish special election pencils. 

At best, elections are costly, and at worst, they become exorbitantly expensive. Part of the reason for the expense is the cost of equipment and the transportation of the equipment. Two examples of the costly equipment required are the padlocks for ballot boxes, necessary to preserve the secrecy and integrity of elections, and the boxes themselves. 

The cost of delivery of one bulky box to the precinct voting places, especially those which are distant from the county seat, is high--the sheriff or his deputy can transport only a few at a time. The Committee therefore recommends that metal seals in lieu of padlocks be authorized and that the use of collapsable, metal-topped, canvas-bodied ballot boxes be permitted by the 1963 Legislature. 

The matter of different tally sheets being used for the general and primary elections was, the Committee believed, previously solved. The law relating to the conduct of primary elections, however, specified a form for tally sheets which did not conform to that used in general elections. The 

Committee recommends adoption of a uniform tally sheet for use in both primary and general elections. 

Chapter 92, Session Laws of 1951, provided that the 

Secretary of State provide elections pencils. In October, 1951, the Attorney General rendered the opinion, however, that inasmuch as no funds had been appropriated for election pencils, the Secretary of State was not obligated to provide the pencils. Since 1951, no Legislature has appropriated money for the purchase of the pencils, and the law has become a "dead letter." The Committee recommends repeal of the law.

5. 

The Committee recommends adoption by the 1963 Legislature of a bill which would permit destruction of voted ballots when the need for their preservation and retention has expired (Appendix X).

Present South Dakota law is either silent or confusing regarding when old, voted ballots may be destroyed by county and city auditors and others with elections responsibilities. Since this is the case, most officers, in order to protect themselves, never destroy the ballots, even decades after their possible need has expired. One result is that this collection of paper is literally crowding the people out of many courthouses and city halls. In order to clarify the situation, and permit destruction of the old voted ballots once they can no longer serve any useful service, the Committee recommends a bill to authorize destruction of these ballots.

6. 

The Committee recommends adoption by the 1963 Legislature of bills to promote more rapid report of election returns by (a) providing that the returns of elections and attendant elections materials be delivered immediately to the county auditor subsequent to an election for which he has responsibility for the registration of voters (Appendix XI), and 

(b) requiring the canvass of elections in independent school districts immediately following the election rather than at the next regular meeting of the board 

(Appendix XII). 

County auditors expressed interest in having legislation passed to provide that the returns for which they have responsibility are delivered to them immediately after an election. With the advent of voting machines, there is no need for the three day delay, even in the more populous counties, in the delivery of the returns to the county auditor. In order to cross-check the eligibility of voters, it is necessary that the auditor have access to the poll and registration books to compare the absentee voting with the personal voting at the polls; there seems to be little reason for any delay. The Committee recommends the adoption of a bill to eliminate the three day period.

It was suggested to the Committee that a period of more than a month could pass between the time of the election and the canvassing of the election by the independent school district board. The need for this delay, also, no longer exists. Because the county auditors now have responsibility for the independent school district voter registration, they see advantages in completing the elections process as soon after the election as possible. The 

Committee recommends passage of this suggested bill.

Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board. The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
