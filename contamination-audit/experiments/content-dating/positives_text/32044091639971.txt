IN The Necessity for an American School of 

Asiatic Studies BY ARTHUR UPHAM POPE Director of the Iranian Institute 

N unforgettable words our late President marked out one of the great issues before the world today: "The old term 'western civilization' no longer holds.

World events and the common needs of all humanity are joining the culture of Asia with the culture of Europe and of the Americas, to form for the first time a world civilization."

The days of our isolation are over; the most stubborn and fanatical are without a hearing, their alarms and their phrases have been dissipated by grim facts. We tried, with an earnestness of effort born of both fear and ignorance, to cut ourselves off from the rest of the world. We thought the world could be fragmented and that we could live safely and successfully in one of its cells. Tariff walls, currency restrictions, trade quotas and all the other instruments of disintegration brought the world to an almost fatal impasse.

The disaster of war has at last thoroughly ploughed under these old fallacies. Men deaf to reason and common sense have learned the hard way, by bloody and costly war, that a compartmentalized world will not work. Men everywhere now know that they must rebuild their one world and this time make it work.

Measured by sheer magnitudes and perhaps ultimate power, Asia is certain to play a decisive role in shaping this new era. Peace, we learned, was indivisible. So is prosperity and so, also, is poverty. Political confusion and economic impoverishment are inevitable if we try to exclude or improperly to exploit Asia. More than half of the human race live in Asia. "Things happening in Asia, opinions formed in Asia, and decisions made in Asia will largely determine the course of events everywhere in the world." Here in *Owen Lattimore, Solution in Asia, 1945, p. 1. 1

Asia are to be found resources in variety and quantity that surpass the rest of the world. Here are those vast, impregnable heartlands, that despite the menace of new weapons will probably maintain a maintain a considerable degree of military inviolability. Here are talents and capacities, submerged by centuries of poverty, misrule and economic depletion; yet no longer content to remain dormant or subservient.

Here are social and political ambitions that will no longer be gainsaid. Here are smoldering resentments that conceal dangerous possibilities. For fifty years revolt has been brewing in Asia. Scores of able observers have been describing its course and warning us of the consequences which we have ignored. Asia is no longer willing to play a subordinate role, or to have her wealth and population fed into the grist of the western economic mill. She is increasingly aware of the greatness of her own past, her social systems, her religions, her philosophies and her arts. If she is aware of her own deficiencies and frustrations, she is also becoming aware of her own power and critically conscious of the seamy side of western civilization, its fallacies and its limitations. 

The painful and unnecessary conflict in Syria is proof that economic and political interests can no longer be advanced in Asia by force or by any unilateral fiat. Asia is eager for cooperation, but determined that it shall be in part at least on her terms. Skillful and friendly cooperation can be immensely rewarding. Any other approach is sure invitation to trouble.

A post-war boom is certain; civilian production everywhere will be boosted and speeded up, rapid circulation of goods and money may create exciting illusions of real prosperity. 

But unless this prosperity involves the systematic and effective incorporation of the material and cultural resources of Asia, it will be temporary. Our productive capacity expanding beyond our consuming capacities threatens to become a frankenstein that may create havoc at home. The bankers and industrialists seem to be of one mind that America must export seven or eight billion dollars' more of goods after the war than she did just before, unless we are to risk a chronic unemployment of not less than 15 millions—an intolerable load that no state could carry for long without inviting confusion and disaster. Currency stabilization, development 2

of trade with South America and Russia, public works, and other operations and certain obvious improvements in the economic setup will accomplish a great deal. But some of this is superficial and more of it temporary, until we can involve in this economic rehabilitation Asia itself. How is this to be accomplished?

These mutual necessities call, first of all, for a profound reconsideration of our attitudes and procedures in relation to the vast potentialities of Asiatic trade. The record of the white man in Asia is not a pretty one. By and large, he has been an exploiter and an arrogant and contemptuous one. His economic methods have been crude, wasteful, uncoordinated. He has too often catered to the immediate demand with little obvious interest in the long-time mass developments which are essential for a stable economy. Profoundly convinced that we are of course a superior people and that all others, particularly Asiatics, are "lesser breeds without the law," we have taken little trouble to understand them, and have too often merely sought for quick returnsshort credits and shorter policies; and we have not always been intelligent or scrupulous about the means, and have scarcely recognized the proprietary rights of the indigenous population.

Our efforts at economic cooperation with Asia have too frequently been wasteful, incompetent and sometimes downright ludicrous. One American firm is reported to have spent four million dollars in India, in good, solid American fashion, pounding on one slogan. All Americans know how superior such technique is. But the slogan "Keep that schoolgirl complexion," was a mystery to most Indians. There, alas! there are few schoolgirls, and in the soap sense, no schoolgirl complexions! One of the agricultural implement companies, however, decided to do it "the Oriental way, way," showing how realistic and subtle they were. So in a great 

Muhammadan city they staged a colossal procession, with elephants and drums, much scarlet and gold and all the works-proving that the Americans could think in terms of Oriental pomp and magnificence. The streets were deserted and the shutters closed when the procession passed. The next day, the servants of the company were not even respectful. Instead of good will the company had aroused the hot resentment of the entire population. The procession had been staged on the most sacred day in the Muhammadan 3

calendar, compared to which our Good Friday is a happy holiday! On that day, no one may eat or drink-tears, fasting and prayer are the only appropriate activities: trumpets, drums and raucous cries of "Buy our wares" were unspeakably offensive.

These blunders might have been only trivial were not India vital for our own economic welfare. As the New York Times said in a recent editorial (May 27, 1945), "For American business men India is one of the mightiest potential markets on the globe, about to enter upon an industrial era that will release the latent energies of one-fifth of the human race. 

She will want machinery for farm and factory; she will want tens of thousands of products that America can provide. We of this country have a stake in India. For our own wellbeing, if for no higher reason, we can no longer think of 

India as outside our world."

Vast as India is, she is only a part of Asia. Yet, on account of the long English tradition in India, it is probably easier to do business there than in other parts of the continent. Judgment, tact and, above all, a real knowledge of the character and habits of the people desirable everywhere, are indispensable in all of Asia. Our common error has been to take our own psychological patterns into the Orient where they do not fit and to assume that good will and bright ideas are enough. The maintenance of a volume of Asiatic trade that shall meet our national necessities requires groundwork, study of sentiments and traditions of the peoples with whom we propose to trade, people whose sentiments literally take precedence over the profit motive or even over actual need. 

If we ignore or flout their sentiments and habits, rivals more realistic and understanding will get hold of the trade; but we are slow to learn.

Doing it the Oriental way requires more than good will and a bright idea. One manufacturer thought to flatter the sentiments and habits of his hoped for customers in the Near East by making miles of cloth ornamented with a handsome Arabic inscription copied from an ancient ceremonial grave cloth in the Metropolitan Museum. What the inscription was about they never made out, but it was a sacred text from the Koran, about as appropriate and acceptable to the Muhammadans as a quotation from the Lord's prayer on a bathing suit would be for us. Other advertisers in India were sure. that the endorsements by our movie stars would be as effective

there as here. But Indian sentiment on such matters is quite the reverse of ours and the method only piled up hopeless sales resistance.

Again, an agricultural implement company persuaded a Rajah in Northwest India to buy fine, deep-bladed plows, "so much better than those crude, primitive, wooden plows that your backward people use to so little avail," the agent argued. The plows were bought and very efficiently plowed up the strong saline subsoil and ruined ten thousand acres for a generation!

Lots of Americans have felt there was no necessity to think or even to open their eyes very wide when approaching Oriental problems. Some of our reporters and journalists, skillful and dependable as they are in many situations, seem to lose their wits as soon as they enter the Asiatic scene, particularly West Asia. Quite a number of correspondents passing through Iran have reported watching with fascination "the procession of veiled Persian women." They never saw one veiled Persian woman; there are veiled women in New 

York but none in Teheran. They had read about veiled women and seen them in the movies; they saw what they expected to see not what was there.

Bankers and the general public have been equally unrealistic. Fifteen years ago the vice-president of a famous bank was considering investigating the hints that Iran might want to negotiate a loan of about $300,000,000—a mere trifle in view of the fact that the nation has an infinitesimal public debt and plenty of resources to back such a loan. It was concluded, however, that Iran was remote and unreal; so something safe and familiar and near was chosen like Ivar Krueger and his match-works, with well known consequences. 

One company built a railroad in Persia. Had it been built in America it would have been hailed, as it was, as a thoroughly efficient and honest job. It would have given the 

Persians a first-class railroad at minimum cost. 

But no attention was paid to the Persian situation or to political or social needs, primary purposes for which the road was built. The result was a loss of millions and a long train of prejudice and resentment-all unnecessary.

There are forty-eight American agencies in Iran-thirtyeight of them are represented by Arabs. But for good or bad reasons, the Iranians strongly dislike the Arabs. How

much has this little oversight cost, aside from being a constant irritation to the Persians?

Our Raisin Growers' Association sent to China a surplus raisin stock, worth hundreds of thousands of dollars. Nobody bought because the big packages were worth a three-weeks' wage. When they put them up into the tiny packages, they printed on the box the "Sun-maid." But everybody in Asia knows and has known for 5,000 years that if the sun shines on a maiden, she will have a baby right away. And the

Chinese girls weren't going to take a chance, nor would their mothers approve of the traffic in these dangerous little seeds that might provide an unwanted or an unexplained baby! 

These are crude but amusing examples of a wrong point of view, of wrong procedures, of unnecessary failures. In each instance a few minutes' conversation with an expert would have made possible a profitable transaction. How is the situation to be remedied? How are we going to work out the most effective cooperation with Asia, to our economic advantage, for our political security and our cultural enrichment?

First of all, our attitude on Asia has got to be changed. 

We have to be more flexible, objective, open-minded, respectfully curious even, about the peoples with whom we intend to deal. The arguments against unrestricted Asiatic immigration, the memory of such phrases as "Asiatic cholera," the World Tour advertisements of the merely exotic, the past appeals of our missionaries to "save the heathen in their darkness," all have created prejudices that have thrived in the vacuum of ignorance. Our soldiers stationed in Asia have complained that there were no corner drugstores, pool rooms, to say nothing of the lack of bathrooms! All this has added up to a distorted and unreal picture of Asia. It is gradually, but too slowly, giving way, for there are as yet no fully adequate agencies at work to substitute a wiser view or sounder skills to control our relations. 

This is a problem which has not yet been solved by our universities. Oriental subjects have been taught in American universities for now 75 years, but they did not prepare the nation, either in attitude or in procedures, for the terrible reality that burst on us in '41, or the vast opportunities, problems and duties that face us now. How many in this country understood the power of Japan? Or who had gauged the heroic capacity of the Chinese people, through long years of

appalling suffering, to salvage with such courage as they did their cultural institutions, to maintain a resistance, despite tragic hardships and cruel insufficiencies of equipment? The 

Japanese were broadcasting in some half dozen languages, which no one in America could understand. We knew little or nothing about the situation in Burma and the Malay States: only one man of university status could be found in the entire country to teach the Malay language. 

An illuminating Survey of the facilities for teaching Oriental subjects in American universities was recently completed by the late Professor John F. Normano of the Research 

Bureau for Post-War Economics. 

Dr. Normano found that the present instruction in Oriental subjects is still in the grip of over-specialization: courses are "isolated, scattered, non-systematic, incidental." Asiatic economics, so urgent for our present welfare, is shockingly neglected. Many institutions complain of "shortage of competent instructors," and "it is obvious that the qualitydeficiency in the field is lamentable. The exotic and sentimental are in many places emphasized at the expense of a concentrated and thoroughly disciplined program of instruction and research.' 

To quote further: "Current studies in the Asiatic field do not show any definite purpose." "No American university has ventured beyond the traditional offering of isolated courses on history, geography, language." "None shows a well-rounded curriculum leading to interrelated study and knowledge. Disciplines are still considered as watertight compartments, and the departments concerned continue their traditional isolation." "Coordination and integration are completely lacking despite some promising announcements.

The recent surge of interest in things Asiatic has not given an impetus to new courses or to appreciable changes in curricula." The analysis of various surveys already made on this subject "does not reveal any attempts by American universities and colleges to present integrated studies of Asia or of the larger regions thereof." "A comparison of the situation abroad is rather unfavorable to the United States. The 

Soviet Union and Great Britain are now far in the lead in 

Asiatic studies."

Dr. C. O. Arndt of the U. S. Office of Education comes to . 

similar conclusions: "Though there is evidence of growth in the study of the Far East since 1940 on college and university

levels, a keen awareness of the importance of this area when judged by course-offerings exists in comparatively few institutions." "There is an urgent need of integrating courseofferings on the Far East in colleges and universities, and thus developing a balanced program of studies."* The failure of the universities to foresee this problem Dr. Arndt regards as a disturbing evidence of a lack of social sensitivity. 

Many important studies in Asiatic languages, literature and history are maintained at a number of our large universities but these studies are to a considerable degree devoted to the basic and difficult problem of the languages, the indispensable foundation on which all specialized studies will have to build. But it is only a part of the total complex, and meets only a few of the most urgent necessities. It is well within the fact to say that our economic future in no small degree depends upon the economies of Asia, and the economy of Asia is a very deep and complicated question, into which enter all kinds of factors, social, psychological, as well as material. Yet only three institutions in the country were giving serious courses in the economics of the Orient the last year.

In no university is there a really compre hensively organized Department of Asiatic Studies. Oriental subjects are taught in a number, but in none are they planned as mutually supporting courses. This is particularly unfortunate because the more serious recent studies in Asiatic culture and affairs show to a degree that we little understood how closely interrelated the various problems and cultures of Asia are. Politically, we have been increasingly aware of this. The cry "Asia for the Asiatics" has had a real meaning.

But in the history of Asiatic cultures as generally expounded, these problems have been painfully, and, from an intellectual point of view, disastrously ignored. The efforts to construe the beginnings of Chinese culture without reference to the essential contributions of western Asia has failed.† And on the other hand, a knowledge of Chinese contributions to the 

Near East since the 5th century A.D. is equally indispensable.

Cultural missions are again, as in the previous centuries, going back and forth through various Asiatic countries, even in wartime. The regional economies are sometimes admirably

The Iranian Institute and School for Asiatic Studies in 

New York, now offering for graduate students a choice of approximately 250 courses in Asiatic subjects to cover a training period of three years, is at present the only specialized teaching institution in this field in America. 

Several universities are planning integrated courses, but it is slow work, because of the established administrative procedures and the demarcation of subjects in more or less. water-tight compartments. If we are going to get an adequate knowledge of the Orient that shall guide us culturally, politically and economically, it would seem to call for new training schools: specially designed schools that could equip leaders, teach teachers, and provide our business interests, for example, with the adequately trained personnel that they are going to need. The number of enterprises that would be served by a school of this kind are quite surprising, for the Orient will provide careers for thousands of Americans in a great variety of undertakings if and only if they are properly trained.

There are vast development projects for which Americans are especially well equipped. Many of these have been going to the Swedes, the Czechs, the Swiss and other Europeans who have made a careful study of the conditions of work in the Orient to which they have adapted themselves with realism and skill. Besides the bankers, engineers and exporters, there are many groups that can profit by the services of a well organized School of Asiatic Studies. There are the various consultants, the salesmen and resident agents; then there is the scholar, the archaeologist, the historian, the museum worker and the anthropologist and sociologist, all of whom need special training, which only a school designed for the purpose can adequately supply. philanthropists, Then there are the administrators, teachers; the surveyors of all kinds, specialists in transportation and industry, in oil, forestry and agriculture. Our government is going to need an ever increasing body of agents in the diplomatic and consular services. Journalists and correspondents can profit by instruction in such a school. The government is even now sending creative writers to the

Orient to interpret the life and problems there in terms of fiction. All these need basic training.

And finally, such a school should train teachers in the major subjects so that in this country they can undertake their work intelligently; in preparatory schools, high schools as well as advanced teaching in the universities. 

One of the first requirements that such a school must meet is a knowledge of the languages. "Language is a steed that carries one into a far country," say the Arabs. "American lack of facility in the Asiatic languages is a good gauge of the shallowness of American expert knowledge of Asia."* In many of our contacts with the Orient there can be no substitute for an adequate command of the language. An engineer working in a province is crippled if he has to depend upon interpreters, and there might not be enough to go around, in either capacity or dependability. It is a well known trick for a rival firm to corrupt or buy off another man's interpreter. Negotiations on great occasions can be carried on through third parties; but the sound development of our relations with Asia depends upon more than a few conferencesit depends upon continuous, friendly and natural intercourse. 

In such situations, unsuspected problems and unsuspected solutions may float quietly to the surface that would have stubbornly resisted frontal attacks by conventional measures. 

It is of greatest importance that we shall learn to view the Orient not with our own habits and prejudices but as they see themselves and their problems. Their values are often quite different from ours and sentiment controls business in the Orient in a way that we have failed to appreciate. We are, for example, convinced that in any product quality, price, service and delivery are the decisive factors; in the Orient these may be quite outweighed by personal considerations and by other attitudes that may seem to us irrelevant. But in Asia these are of the essence of life. These are not things to be guessed at.

The apt quotation, a joke in the vernacular, or even a slang phrase may at the right moment be more valuable than a threat, a bribe or even a superior product. A gesture of respect toward the established values in regions where social customs so different from our own, may bind a whole group in grateful loyalty. A knowledge of the land, the people, * Owen Lattimore, Solution in Asia, 1945, p. 5. 10

manners and customs, and their past as well as their future can make residence and work in foreign lands profoundly satisfying and enjoyable, so that agents, instead of impatiently calculating the next return sailing, will find it more. than worth while to take longer residence, and such residence is necessary for the finest kind of cooperation. 

We have great faith in effective sales promotion but in the Orient that is not enough nor will it meet our own necessities of the immediate future. We must accurately gauge the tremendous nationalistic impulses that have been sweeping Asia with increasing force for 40 years. They have now almost the power of tidal waves. Will they recede or has a permanent tide set in? The latter seems likely. So American enterprise in the Orient must understand this new nationalistic fervor and come to terms with it, which is quite possible; where joint enterprises are undertaken they will not succeed if the American intention is to conduct all its work on its own and then pull out when it is replete with profits, leaving nothing for the natives to carry on with. Those concerns which make it clear that they expect proper return for services, ideas, capital but that they intend that their enterprises shall also be beneficial to the people concerned, that natives will be employed wherever possible and trained up to carry on in the future, will be greeted with gratitude, with an eager spirit of cooperation that will guarantee success. 

The scientific and industrial advances in this country will keep our development projects in the lead indefinitely so that as soon as one operation can be successfully developed in any one region, new and more advanced enterprises will be needed and acceptable.

The promised trade boom with Asia can easily run its course. The vast populations of Asia cannot sustain a permanent and profitable market unless their own economic status is materially changed. These countries must be industrialized and thus their standard of living and their purchase power permanently elevated. Their primary industrialization is not a threat but a guarantee of profitable trade relations in the future. But with the industrialization we must cooperate, not merely on long term credits but by participation, guidance, actual partnerships. These are requirements to which Americans are not too well adapted and for which they have little experience. There are centers of wealth in various parts of Asia eager for such partner- 11

ship, but they can be maintained successfully only when our agents can enter naturally and deeply into the life of the people and be partners socially and culturally, as well as over the counting-boards.

Americans planning work in the Orient are at a certain serious disadvantage in competition with European agents. 

They lack the specific training which the latter enjoy and also the tradition of long-term residence in the Orient. Many 

British families have, for instance, had a record of centuries of work in various parts of Asia. It seems to them natural and interesting; they accommodate themselves to the regions to which they are sent and their knowledge and first-hand experience of the Orient have deeply enriched the countries they represent. Our agents, knowing so little of the East, regard it as a place to be dreaded, not enjoyed. This is largely the result of ignorance. Proper background and training will show to any alert American that the great cultures of the Orient have much to offer him; that these countries are charged with intense and dramatic interest; that they are regions of the greatest and most powerful changes as well as rich in ancient wisdom. How to live in the Orient, to adjust to the hazards of climate and social intercouse are no particular mystery and, once understood, can make life in the favored parts of every country of Asia gratifying as well as profitable.

We must have men both there and at home who are able to gauge the trends of economic and political development. 

What are the social, political and economic habits that may either stabilize or abruptly change the total picture? It is expensive to guess. It is not too difficult to understand. In short, the economic rehabilitation of Asia is clearly of vital importance for our own prosperity and for world peace as well. The human and material resources there are vast and inexhaustible, the potentialities quite incalculable, although for the moment blocked by poverty, inefficient methods, want of primary industrialization, maldistribution of population, political difficulties and disbalanced world economy. All these, even the local over-population problems, are remediable; and many of them greatly ameliorated within a decade.

power of the many hundreds of millions throughout Asia can be raised and that fairly promptly, if the right methods and principles are adopted by enlightened and cooperative world opinion. What role America will play in this destined renaissance will be in precise proportion to our understanding. To this end we need trained men-experienced and tested in Oriental affairs; we need new methods of fact gathering, research analysis and interpretation. There are not enough equipped men to go around. They must be shrewdly selected and trained by realistic, flexible-minded, thoroughly competent men with vision and imagination, scientific and practical at the same time.

Such men, and we have them, though not in abundance, could in a few years supply the much needed personnel and help formulate the enlightened policies for the formidable and closely interrelated problems of Asiatic economic rehabilitation. Our wealth, and prestige, our good will and freedom from aggressive political ambition has given us immense power to help settle these problems and settle them right. 

But unless these qualities are supplemented by knowledge and skill of the highest quality they will be impotent and they might spell conflict and disaster. The necessities plainly call for a specialized, expertly planned School of Asiatic Studies on a scale comparable with the great Oriental training schools abroad.

This country thus urgently needs first class facilities for training leaders, teachers and participants in this great project of Oriental rehabilitation. Alas! there are not enough experts to go around, and scattered specialists do not meet the problem. The Iranian Institute and School for Asiatic 

Studies has proven the necessity of integrated courses, the efficacy of a well balanced culturally unified faculty that know how to cooperate in the interchange of information, of plans, and in actual teaching work. For example, we do not believe in teaching a language in vacuo; language is indeed the indispensable instrument of approach, but it is also the expression of a way of life. A language must be related to other forms of expression, social customs, manners, religion, art, superstitions even, and all must be reviewed in the light of history and tradition. The Institute has proven that many stubborn problems, not only in cultural history but of immediate necessities, can be solved through skillful cooperation of specialists working as a team, a difficult but necessary art, 13

that requires sincerity and genuine devotion, but one richly rewarding when achieved. The vice-president of one of our large concerns recently complained that in the Near East there are one hundred thoroughly trained English agents, pressing certain interests; but not one American. France has her great Ecole des 

Langues Vivantes Orientales, on which she leans heavily for the proper administration and development of her colonial empire and overseas trade. Britain has the famous London 

School of Oriental Studies with its faculty of 70 and supplementary faculty of 60 more; with 700 registered students, and with ample government subsidy. Russia's training of Oriental agents is of the most thorough sort. America cannot hold her own or make that contribution to Oriental rehabilitation that will be mutually profitable for all nations unless we can at the earliest possible moment, establish on an adequate scale such a School. This is not only an opportunity for economic benefit but for sensible and humane service which can be of profit and security to the nation as well as a contribution to universal prosperity and international peace. 14
