GEORGE DEWEY 

Admiral George Dewey was born in Montpelier, Vermont on December 26, 1837. By a special act of the 1937 Legislature it has been decreed that the 

State of Vermont shall pay tribute to one of its most distinguished citizens in this Centennial of his birth. Norwich University is glad to cooperate with the 

State in this remembrance of a great admiral, for Dewey's first military training was received as a Norwich cadet at the old institution in Norwich, Vermont from 1851 to 1854. When the nation was acclaiming the most brilliant naval achievement of modern times, Dewey returned to his native State, and to his first military college, to honor, and to be honored by, the "home folks". 

In 1899, he laid the cornerstone of Dewey Hall, erected in his honor by Norwich University, on the site occupied by the college since 1866. When numerous colleges were conferring the degree of LL.D. upon him, Norwich gave him the highest military distinction within its power the degree of Master of Military Science. As the Military College of the State of Vermont, we now feel honored in being able to give this booklet to our alumni and the citizens of this State in appreciation of one of the nation's most distinguished men 

Admiral George Dewey.

George Dewey's ancestry has been traced back through the nobility of France and England to a mythical stage of pagan history. His first American ancestor, having changed the name from Duee to Dewey, settled in Dorchester, Massachusetts, in 1634. This Thomas Dewey later became one of the founders of Windsor, Connecticut, and from his second son, Josiah, the branch of George Dewey descended. His great-grandfather, William Dewey, was one of the volunteers at Lexington, and his brother, Simeon, was with Ethan Allen when he demanded the keys of the fortress at Ticonderoga. There are many stories told of Dewey's boyhood, and most of them have been slightly dramatized by successive narrators. He was a pretty active boy, and not at all afraid of trouble. The district school of Montpelier which he attended was somewhat unruly, and various masters had given it up in despair. 

When Z. K. Panghorn assumed his duties, he determined that he was going to maintain discipline. So, when young George and his comrades set up the inevitable challenge, Mr. Panghorn bided his time, and then proceeded at the psychological moment to inflict quite severe corporal punishment upon George and another ringleader. When the teacher accompanied the boy home to his father, and the evidence of the trouncing was shown, Dr. Dewey, with his usual good sense, offered to continue the chastisement if George did not think he had learned his lesson. George thought he had. He later followed Mr.

Panghorn voluntarily over to Johnson to his new academy, and there prepared for entering Norwich at the age of fourteen. He seemed in later life to be very grateful to Mr. Panghorn. However, when George's father was contemplating a little discipline for the disobedient boy who had tried to ford the Winooski with his horse and wagon instead of going around by the bridge, and had been upset in the rapid stream, George told his father that he ought to be glad that he wasn't drowned, and by this plea escaped punishment. George's frequent plunges through the thin ice, his blind-fold racing down the State House steps, and his near drowning when he established a swimming record for his day, all indicate a vigorous, adventurous boyhood. He was known as "the blackeyed cuss", and like Robert Burns, was greatly moved by a Life of Hannibal. 

When in make-believe he stormed a neighboring hill instead of the Alps, George's army sometimes consisted only of his sister Mary. 

At fourteen, George wanted to go to sea. His father wanted him to stay home. The military academy at Norwich, Vermont, was the compromise between father and son. This school rivalled, and in the judgment of some, surpassed West Point in those days. Southerners especially thought highly of this institution which was indescribably poor, but which seemed to graduate good officers. There were only two buildings then-The North and the South 

Barracks. And there were only two courses, the Collegiate and the Scientific. 

But even in those days, which seemed so remote and peaceful, there were at least two versions of any university: the university as it sees itself through the 

Faculty and the Catalogue, and the university as it is experienced by the student.

Much has been said about the conditions of Dewey's transfer from Norwich to Annapolis. In all our research, we can find only a statement that the appointment in those days was primarily political. The Vermont Representative was not disposed to appoint Dewey, who was an alternate. Admiral 

Dewey says: "There was no vacancy for West Point from Vermont. Otherwise, I might have gone into Manila Bay on an army transport instead of on the "Olympia". But it happened that there was a vacancy at Annapolis. A boy by the name of George Spaulding, of Montpelier, received the appointment at first, but decided that he would not take it. My father, through his influence with Senator Foote, had me made Spaulding's successor. Spaulding became a distinguished clergyman."

George Dewey entered the Naval Academy on Sep tember 23, 1854, and graduated in 1858. Of the sixty who entered, only fifteen graduated, and even by the end of the first year, twentythree had been plucked. 

He graduated fifth in his class, and at the end of the two-year cruise, was third in standing. At the Academy, Dewey was nicknamed "Shang". Disputes between mid-shipmen were settled by fists in off hours, but when Dewey was called an opprobrious name by another cadet at mess, Dewey didn't wait a second, but sprang around the table, and beat him down under the table. Dewey was fined ten demerits, but assured that he had done the only proper thing. He was satisfied. Dewey always felt profoundly grateful to Annapolis and spoke of it with admiration. In a day when chewing tobacco was common, Dewey had the fortitude to break himself of the habit when he observed on his first Mediterranean cruise that the British officers did not chew. Even as a young man, he had decisiveness and integrity of character. After his Mediterranean Cruise on the Wabash, Dewey was commissioned Lieutenant, and on May 10, 1861, he reported for duty on the old side-wheeler Mississippi, on which he served until it was set on fire by the batteries of Fort Hudson in March, 1863. Farragut's indomitable character made a very great impression on the young naval officer, who was called upon to perform trying duties at the age of twenty-four. Dewey could have served under no better man, for David E. Farragut became the naval hero of the Civil War. Speaking of him years later, Dewey said: iversity, scheduled to open in September, 1938. Whenever I have been in a difficult situation, or in the midst of such confusion of details that the simple and right thing to do seemed hazy, I have often asked myself, 'What would Farragut do?' When it became evident that under the gruelling fire of the Fort the Mississippi must be abandoned, Captain Smith and Lieutenant Dewey spiked her guns, and went over the side. Dewey risked his life under the fire of sharp shooters to save the life of a seaman struggling to keep afloat with an arm paralyzed by a bullet. After this, Dewey was ordered up the James River under Commander McComb. In 1864 he was attached to the North Atlantic blockading Squadron, and in 1865 he was commissioned Lieut.-Commander for meritorious conduct in attacks on Fort Fisher.

Dewey always speaks of his own life with a detachment characteristic of a man of action. He never thought of himself as a hero, though after the universal acclaim of his Manila Bay victory, he did say that he had become a hero too late in life. He never confused a star of destiny with the chances and changes of war, and he always gave full credit to those who toiled with him in the preparation for the victory that followed. In 1867, he married Susie B. 

Goodwin, the daughter of New Hampshire's fighting governor. 

Five years later she died, shortly after giving birth to their only child, George Goodwin Dewey. In the succession of appointments after the Civil War-with the 

European Squadron, as an instructor at Annapolis, as Light House Inspector, in command of the Dolphin and the Pensacola, Dewey fulfilled all the routine duties of naval life in times of peace with integrity and efficiency. 

Buying soap or tobacco for his men, awarding contracts, and building up an inferior navy to the point where it became an efficient fighting machine for a great nation took the greater part of his life, and these duties were an ultimate test of his devotion to the naval service. On May 23, 1896, he was promoted from Captain to Commodore, but continued President of the Board of Inspection and Survey until a fleet was ready for him. 

Before Dewey's appointment to command the Asiatic Squadron, two other Norwich men had commanded United States fleets in the Pacific: Commander 

Josiah Tattnall, and Rear-Admiral Carpenter. Political pressure had been brought to bear on Theodore Roosevelt, the Assistant-Secretary of the Navy, to appoint Commodore John A. Powell to command the Asiatic Squadron, but

Roosevelt knew that Dewey was the better man for the job, and when Senator Proctor of Vermont called on President McKinley, he left with the assurance that Dewey would receive the appointment.

There was a general expectation of war with Spain. Financial interests became confused with national idealism. Newspapers and magazines were flooded with articles on Spanish injustice and the misery of oppressed peoples: 

Then the U. S. S. Maine was destroyed in Havana Harbor, and Spain no longer had the chance to negotiate. The cynics raged about an imperial policy, and the idealists talked about the liberation of oppressed peoples. The Navy had prepared for action. The first thing Dewey did was to change his base from 

Japanese to Chinese waters. Upon his arrival at Hong Kong, he learned that the day before, February 15, 1898, the battleship Ma'ne had gone down with a loss of 266 of her crew. Newspapers in the United States were printed in red ink. The war feeling ran high. The Orient became very near, and its glamor moved men and women almost as much as the tyranny of Spain. When 

Dewey's fleet went into action in Manila Bay, the slogan was "Remember the Maine".

But Dewey had a job on his hands. Sentiment was not enough. He procceded, when neutrality was observed by China, to move down to Mirs Bay.

November, 1937 He bought supply ships, and all the coal he could get. Yet when he moved on toward the Philippines, there was only 60% of the war strength of supplies in his fleet, and he was 7,000 miles from home. The British were enthusiastic; the Germans were truculent. The Kaiser wanted the Philippines, and it took great tact as well as manly determination to keep the Germans in their place. After the Battle of Manila Bay, Dewey nearly had another war on his hands—in fact, he offered it to the flag-Lieutenant of the German fleet upon five minutes' notice. They took the hint, and observed the embargo. But, of course, the great event was the Battle of Manila Bay on May 1, 1898.

FROM ADMIRAL DEWEY'S OFFICIAL REPORT ON THE Battle of MANILA BAY. 

* * 

FLAGSHIP "OLYMPIA", MAY 4, 1898. 

* * * The squadron left Mirs Bay on April 27 * Arrived off Boliano on the morning of April 30, and finding no vessels there, proceeded down the coast and arrived off the entrance to Manila Bay on the same afternoon. The "Boston" and "Concord" were sent to reconnoitre Port Subic. * * * A thorough search of the port was made by the "Boston" and "Concord", but the Spanish fleet was not found. * * * 

Entered the south channel at 11:30 P. M., steaming at eight knots. After half the squadron had passed, a battery on the south side of the channel opened fire, none of the shots taking effect. The "Boston" and "McCulloch" returned the fire. The squadron proceeded across the bay at slow speed and arrived off Manila at daybreak, and was fired upon at 5:15 A. M., by three batteries at Manila and two at Cavite, and by the Spanish fleet anchored in an approximately east and west line across the mouth of Baker Bay, with their left in shoal water in Canacoa Bay. 

The squadron then proceeded to the attack, the flagship "Olympia”, under my personal direction, leading, followed at distance by the "Baltimore", "Raleigh", "Petrel", "Concord", and "Boston", in the order named, which formation was maintained throughout the action. The squadron opened fire at 5:41 A. M. While advancing to the attack, two mines were exploded ahead of the flagship, too far to be effective. The squadron maintained a continuous and precise fire, at ranges varying from 5,000 to 2,000 yards, countermarching in a line approximately parallel to that of the Spanish fleet. 

The enemy's fire was vigorous but generally ineffective. Early in the engagement two launches came out toward the "Olympia" with the apparent intention of sinking torpedoes. One was sunk and the other disabled by our fire and beached, before an opportunity occurred to fire torpedoes. 

At 7 A. M. the Spanish flagship "Reina Christina" made a desperate attempt to leave the line and come out to engage at short range, but was received with such galling fire, the entire battery of the "Olympia" being concentrated upon her, that she was barely able to return to the shelter of the point. 

The fires started in her by our shells at this time were not extinguished until she sank. * * *

The three batteries at Manila had kept up a continuous report from the beginning of the engagement, which fire was not returned by this squadron. 

The first of these batteries was situated on the south mole head, at the entrance to the Pasig River. The second on the south bastion of the walled city of 

Manila and the third at Malate, about one-half mile farther south. At this point I sent a message to the Governor-General to the effect that if the batteries did not cease firing, the city would be shelled. This had the effect of silencing them. 

At 7:35 I ceased firing and withdrew the squadron for breakfast. At 

11:16 A. M. I returned to the attack. By this time the Spanish flagship and almost the entire Spanish fleet were in flames. At 12:30 P. M. the squadron ceased firing, the batteries being silenced and the ships sunk, burnt, and deserted. 

At 12:40 P. M. the squadron returned and anchored off Manila, the "Petrel" being left behind the point of Cavite. This duty was performed by Commander E. P. Wood in the most expeditious manner possible. 

The Spanish lost the following vessels: Sunk, "Reina Christina", "Castilla", "Don Antonia de Ulloa"; burned, "Don Juan de Austria", "Isla de Luzon", "Isla de Cuba", "General Lezo", "Marqes del Duero", "El Correo", "Velasco", and "Isle de Mindano" (transport); captured, "Rapidio" and "Hercules" (tugs) and several small launches. I am unable to obtain complete accounts of the enemy's killed and wounded, but believe their losses to be very heavy. The "Reina Christina" alone had one hundred and fifty killed, including the Captain, and ninety wounded. I am happy to report that the damage done to the squadron under my command was inconsiderable. There were none killed and only seven men in squadron slightly wounded. * * Several of the vessels were struck, and even penetrated, but the damage was of the slightest, and the squadron is in as good condition now as before the battle. 

I beg to state to the Department that I doubt if any commander-in-chief was ever served by more loyal, efficient, and gallant captains than those of the squadron now under my command. * * * * * On May 2, the day following the engagement, the squadron again went to Cavite where it remains. * On the third, the military forces evacuated the Cavite arsenal which was taken possession of by a landing party.

GEORGE DEWEY. 

The whole world, excepting Spain and Germany, rejoiced. A wave of acclamation encircled the globe, and the name of Dewey was on every tongue. 

But Dewey kept his head. He refused to make war on women and children, and in the long negotiations conducted through the Belgian Consul in Manila, M. Edouard Andre, with the Spanish Commandant Augustin, who was relieved by Jaudenes because he told his Government the truth about Manila, Dewey showed an executive power no less valuable than his military acumen. 

He kept his men supplied with fresh beef, and arranged for the surrender of Manila in such a way as to save the Spanish officers from being shot when they returned to Spain. Before Merrit arrived with the American land forces, Dewey was in a precarious condition-7,000 miles from his naval base, with an unfriendly German fleet, and an internal struggle within the Philippines demanded intrepidity and good sense. Dewey did believe that his victory was a judgment of the Lord upon Spain, but he never did any more damage or injury to anyone than was necessary. 

On August 12, the Spanish-American War ended with a protocol in which Spain ceded Porto Rico and the Island of Guam to the United States. In 

October, 1898, peace commissioners met in Paris, and on December 10th, the peace treaty was signed. The treaty was ratified with one vote to spare by the United States Senate on February 9, 1899. The Philippine Islands were ceded to the United States for $20,000,000, and since that day we have used every agency to make these peoples a credit to themselves. Supreme 

Court decision on a five to four basis fixed their status politically, and American idealism has given them facilities for education and hospitalization never dreamed of before we took them over. Even baseball was taught, and the tribal chieftains learned that in baseball the chieftain didn't do all the batting while his menials ran the bases. The principal cities of the various Islands are now a credit to American socializing genius. Dewey's method in victory helped greatly to make this possible for them and for us. 

On March 3, 1899 George Dewey became the first Admiral of the United 

States Navy. The whole world rejoiced. When Admiral George Dewey came to the new Norwich in Northfield, Vermont, the best orators of the day extolled his achievements, and everywhere great crowds wedged in close to the stand on which the national hero listened to the flowery oratory of the day. 

In his "Autobiography", we catch a glimpse which makes us understand the 

Triumphal Arch erected for him; "Dewey arches, Dewey Flags, and 'Welcome Dewey' in electric lights on the span of Brooklyn Bridge! The great city of New York made holiday.

Its crowds banked the piers, the roofs, and Riverside Drive when the "Olympia", leading the North Atlantic Squadron, which won Santiago, proceeded up the North River, and they packed the streets for the land parade in token of public emotion, while the gold loving-cup which came to me with the freedom of the city expressed the municipality's official tribute. In the presence of this spectacle, which was without equal, my emotion was indescribable. I was no less

The Norwich University Record deeply affected when I stood on the steps of the State House at Montpelier with the grounds filled with Vermont ‘home folks', and when on the steps of the east front of the Capitol I received from the hands of the President the sword of honor which Congress voted me."

Anyone who studies the life of Dewey finds himself an admirer of the man-even though he is not prone to hero-worship. And as we come now to the reversal of popular esteem, which fortunately was only temporary, we pity the great man and feel disesteem for the crowd. After the pageantry was over, and the "captains and kings" were silent, Dewey made the tremendous mistake of marrying a woman whom he loved-Mrs. Mildred (McLean)

Hazen, and instead of selling outright the house which the public had given him in Washington (as several generals had done), he deeded it over to his new wife. At the worst, this is a pardonable error. But he also announced his presidential candidacy. American people of his day felt outraged, and Dewey realized the permanent truth of the proverb, "Sic transit gloria mundi". But 

Dewey was still indomitable-still every inch a man, and a great one. Every day he went to his office in the Navy Department, fulfilled his routine duties, and keeping in touch with the living science of naval warfare, helped to prepare the American people for a realization of the uncertain tenure of all national relations, and built up the United States Navy for its part in the World War. 

When he died, on January 16, 1917, President Woodrow Wilson said: "The whole nation will mourn the loss of its most distinguished naval officer, a man who has been as faithful, as intelligent, and as successful in the performance of his responsible duties in time of peace as he was gallant and successful in time of war. It is such men that give the service distinction. and the nation a just pride in those who serve it."
