The Present Republican Opportunity 

Is the Republican party looking for a leader or a stooge? Everyone knows which the old guard would prefer. But how about the "better elements of society" that are supposed to constitute the bone and sinew of the party? In this portentous crisis are they politically and morally asleep or have they abdicated their inherent rights of leadership in favor of those omnipresent professional politicians who haunt political gatherings in order to gain enough influence to make bipartizan deals with similarly motivated Democrats for their mutual enrichment, at the expense of the nation?

One of the greatest assets of the Democratic party is the present Republican party organization leadership. If the G. O. P. allows this aggregation of political fixers to pick its candidate, it can count on having a nominee who will miss being President by as wide a margin as he will miss being right. From the standpoint of these professional politicians, the ideal candidate is a man who, with enough simulated sincerity to give his counterfeit coin something of the right ring, voices vague generalities and copy book aphorisms that he never has even attempted to embody in a concrete program. But this year, if such a candidate is chosen as titular leader of the Republican hosts, he will be all but unnoticed by the grim millions, who with disillusioned eyes will be looking through, around and beyond him, to see the tail that is wagging the dog, or in other words, to see the partizan bosses and financial superbosses who will dictate the appointments as well as the legislative program of any administration that bears his name. In this unprecedented crisis, the masses of the voters will regard with mingled feelings of 5

bitterness and contempt any party that has nothing more hopeful to propose than a return to the economic system corroded by greed, that for nearly a decade has been cracking and crumbling before our eyes. 

As a loyal Administration Democrat, who however is an American before he is a partizan, I cannot ignore the fact that one of the most pressing needs in our present fateful race with time, is an opposition party, or better still a patriotic Coalition, with an adequate constructive program and a dynamic leadership, to replace the spiritually and politically dazed Republican party. While a host of Republican prestidigitators, realizing that something drastic must be done, have been working themselves into a fine frenzy trying to resuscitate the stricken elephant, apparently their genuflections, chants and incantations have been largely fruitless. But even if, by some miracle of political magic, they should succeed in this difficult enterprise, apparently no satisfactory steps toward national salvation could be counted on to result therefrom for the simple reason that we landed in the depression as a result of a series of Republican Administrations, and up to date the G. O. P. has developed no new program or leadership that can be considered even approximately adequate to the present emergency.

Other groups of enthusiastic political medicine men and midwives with equal solicitude are trying to assist at the birth pangs of a radical third party. Even though they should succeed, it seems extremely unlikely that in the present world-wide spiritual and economic collapse, a class-conscious third party could measure up to America's moral and material needs. At this critical juncture, neither a Republican resuscitation nor the birth of a radical third party but a political resurrection would seem to be the only hope of the nation. 

But what does that mean and how can such a miracle be achieved? 

Manifestly the first step in the direction of a new and constructive political orientation must be the sacrifice of partizanship, class consciousness and personal selfishness on the altars of country and the God of nations. President Roosevelt has gone a long way in that direction. He has Republicans in his cabinet and a host of other Republicans as officials in the various Federal Emergency Agencies. He has risked his political

life repeatedly in daring and unpopular efforts to save millions of people from imminent bankruptcy and despair. However, nothing is clearer than that the steps taken by his Administration, up to date, are not enough. It requires little insight to see that if America is to achieve a permanent recovery, one (or both) of our great political parties must "find its life by losing it" in a resistless Coalition Crusade, to mobilize the self sacrifice, intelligence, courage, faith and other spiritual resources of the nation, behind a sound and adequate program of fundamental economic reconstruction.

Moreover, before either party can launch such a Crusade it must achieve first, what is known in medical circles as a correct diagnosis or, in theological parlance, as "a conviction of sin;" and second, a new and more inspiring vision of the higher possibilities of individual and national life. The Republican party, because of its present state of leaderless confusion and manifest inadequacy, presumably would find it easier to pray-"God have mercy on me a sinner," than would its victorious and more self-satisfied opponent. Unfortunately, however, up to date its closest approximation to this repentant state of mind has been such symptoms as self-pity, a tempest of resentment and bitterness between factions and a well developed and well deserved case of inferiority complex. Not until Republicans come to realize that they deserved all they got at the last Presidential election, is there the slightest hope of their being able to make a good recovery-moral or political. 

Senator Borah in an interview in the Chicago Tribune last January, epitomized, in a sentence, the sins of omission of the Republican "old guard," which has controlled not only the Republican party but also the country, during the greater part of the last half century. "They had the power," he declared, "but they refused to act." It would be difficult to sum up as briefly their sins of commission. 

How about the Democratic party? It cannot be denied that serious mistakes have been made by the Administration. Indeed this was foreseen and predicted by the President as being inevitable in an emergency program that had to be hastily drafted by fallible human beings to meet a national crisis that had been gathering momentum for more than a

decade. Moreover in addition to this generally recognized fact there seems to be a growing feeling that the Administration has inaugurated enough drastic reforms and expended enough billions of dollars, to have brought about a successful constructive revolution, had there not been lacking some essential factor in the remedies used. As a matter of fact, millions of people are wondering how the Administration could have come so near to gaining a victory over the depression without quite doing so-how it could have won so many battles without winning the war. 

It has been further suggested by Administration critics that this anomalous situation has been due, among other things, to the President's apparent willingness, at times, to rate "political availability," personal friendship and status in the Roosevelt-Farley faction of his party, above outstanding ability, as qualifications for the highest positions of public trust, and to his too great reliance on the advice of a galaxy of brilliant young theorists, as yet undisciplined by the years, and uninitiated into the practical wisdom of the costly school of experience. Also, it has been suggested that his record of having appointed to office more members of the opposition party than has any President since the Civil War, has failed to elicit the enthusiastic response that might have been anticipated, largely because it was not carried the one step further required to make of his Administration, a Coalition government like that of Washington, composed of the ablest obtainable statesmen, regardless of party, faction or personal predilection. Moreover, recognizing that these are only secondary considerations, the more fundamental suggestion has been made that if the President had relied more on that Divine guidance invoked in the closing words of his Inaugural Address and less on the arts of the politician and the ingenuity of human advisers and associates, he and the nation would now be farther along the way to a recovery of what was best in the past, as well as to a discovery of infinitely bigger and better things for the future.

This last criticism seems to be the basic one on which rests whatever of truth there may be in the others. During the first year of the 

New Deal, fear of unloosing still further whirlwinds, that the Harding and Coolidge Administrations had sown, restrained and silenced, the

selfish, partizan passions of men and made them more amenable to the dictates of reason and right, than they had been for a generation. At that moment a Coalition Administration, that was essentially an official Committee of Public Safety, that made the President's prayer for guidance its daily invocation and aspiration and that called upon every individual citizen, even the humblest, to do his utmost to pull his weight in the ark of our national safety, could have fanned into a consuming and purifying flame the latent fires of patriotic self-sacrifice that ordinarily are evoked only in the less confusing crises of war. It thus seems clear that if the President had been a prophet as well as a statesman, in addition to the social and financial readjustments he has brought about, he might also have awakened this spiritually somnolent nation. 

However, it would be neither intelligent nor fair to ignore the fact that when Franklin Roosevelt assumed the Presidency he was faced with a nation-wide condition of agricultural near-bankruptcy, industrial and commercial near-collapse and complete financial paralysis. And even that was not the worst. He found himself at the head of a nation suffering from sleeping sickness of the soul. Obviously his first job was to put into operation such emergency policies as would stave off acute collapse and give us time to wake up, perceive the imminent danger of the situation, diagnose its hidden causes deep within ourselves and begin to cooperate with a Higher Spiritual Power in the stupendous task of doing with Its help what we so signally had failed to do "on our own," namely, utilize our unlimited national assets to bring about an equally unlimited and diffused national prosperity. Manifestly President Roosevelt has come nearer to doing his part of this emergency job than have state or local officials or than have most of his fellow citizens. For three perilous years he has warded off threatened acute disaster, saved millions of farmers from impending bankruptcy, kept the families of millions of unemployed workers from hunger, cold and despair, helped nearly every kind of legitimate business to keep its head above water and resuscitated a collapsed banking system.

Indeed, can it not be truthfully said that the most tragic feature of this situation has been, not the inevitable fraction of error on the part

of those Federal officials, on whose shoulders was laid the heaviest peace time burden of responsibility borne by any group of men in a thousand years, but the failure of those of us who have borne no such burden, to function more constructively as individuals and through local and state governments? Manifestly before we criticize the limitations of the daring and difficult achievements of the President and his helpers, must we not in common fairness ask ourselves, what have the rest of us done to help our distraught country in this supreme crisis? Are we even now aware of the imminent and overwhelming nature of the danger hanging over us? Do we realize that our economic breakdown is but an outer expression of a prior spiritual collapse within ourselves? Are we willing to renounce our idolatry of human systems and system makers? 

Are we willing to give up greed, fear and partizanship and ask the

Almighty to show us the way back to spiritual sanity, economic health and happiness? Until we are ready to do these things, President Roosevelt cannot save us! Though Washington, Jefferson, Jackson and Lincoln all were to rise from the dead-they could not save us! Until we fulfill the Almighty's clearly stated conditions, even He cannot save us! 

Spiritual laws are as immutable as natural laws. Honesty, the spirit of "live and let live" and patriotic self-sacrifice are the only known foundations of a permanent prosperity. This is not a new doctrine. It is but a fragment of the transcendent truth expressed in Holy Writ— "Seek ye first the Kingdom of God and His righteousness and all these things shall be added unto you." There is no other way! Clearly the 

American people must share with the President the responsibility for our nation-wide failure to recognize and act on the truth that the spiritual factor is the decisive factor in the present crisis and that, until we build our individual and national policies on a prior or accompanying spiritual recovery we have not yet made a start toward a permanent economic comeback.

If someone could give the rank and file of the badly demoralized party of Abraham Lincoln and Theodore Roosevelt a truthful and detailed diagnosis of the spiritual causes of its present moral and political collapse, together with a sound constructive program and a dynamic

leadership, it seems probable that they would respond to such a challenge with alacrity, sincerity and enthusiasm. With a view to helping encourage a movement among Republican voters, in this direction, cer- ▸ tain suggestions are here presented for their sympathetic consideration. 

As President Roosevelt and "Jim" Farley are two of the most astute politicians of this generation, it seems highly improbable that Mr. 

Fletcher, Mr. Slemp or any other opposition political managers, unless they can acquire some higher political cards than the Administration now holds, can take enough tricks to win the game. The crucial question appears to be, have the Democrats drawn all the political trumps and face cards from the deck? I think not. To my mind the political ace and King and Queen of trumps still are being held back by the God of battles for the leader who is willing wholeheartedly to take his leads from Him. For example, our people must be given a new and higher ideal than the orthodox American ambition to make money, and then still more money, with which to buy enervating luxuries, flatter one's vanity and ruin one's children, or than the Fascist or Communist ideal of sacrificing all that is highest in individual and family life to a modern Moloch, in the form of a political and economic dictatorship. 

But it will be difficult to do this if, from the cradle to the grave, we continue to permit ourselves and our children to be inundated from the press, and the university chair with a steady stream of propaganda in favor of uncivilized and demoralizing economic superstitions. 

Following are some of the crude and sinister superstitions which long have been poisoning the economic thought and life of the nation and helping to prevent our people from seeing the spiritual and economic "truth that makes men free." One of the oldest of these is the utterly untenable theory, that man is primarily an economic creaturethat his dominant desire or motive is to achieve economic well being. 

Strangely enough this manifestly false theory is held by reactionaries and communists alike. In order to realize its essential unsoundness, one has only to look about. Obviously the vast majority of our people are seeking pretty much "everything but" their economic well being. If for example during our high water mark of prosperity before, during and

after the World War, wage earners had been motivated primarily by the desire for economic well being, most of them, when the depression started, would have possessed homes of their own and money in the savings banks. But instead of laying-by a part of their abnormally high war and post war wages, an overwhelming majority of them, in order to get "what they wanted when they wanted it," promptly spent every dollar they could get. Millions also mortgaged their future by buying a lot of useless junk on the popular but perilous plan of "paying $5.00 down and $5.00 a week until the day of their death." Such people are not primarily economic creatures but creatures of impulse. Before we can hope to create a permanently prosperous economic system, these fatuous and childish impulses must be channelized and transformed by a nation-wide spiritual renaissance that will supply the long looked for "moral equivalent of war."

Another current economic superstition is the theory that we are entitled to charge, for service or goods, "all that the traffic will bear." 

This is not a "profit system" but a self-destructive system of extortion, commonly called profiteering. It is piracy, reduced to terms of cunning instead of force. It is this ruthless rule that has transformed many corporation officials, who as fathers, husbands and friends are kind, considerate and generous, into business automatons, mere cogs in machines for grinding out illegitimate profits. Many of these men feel that in their fiduciary capacity, as corporation officials, it is not a crime but a duty to exact from customers the uttermost farthing for the smallest possible service or commodity and from employees the last kilowatt of energy for the lowest possible wage. An economic system that establishes the right of the strong and privileged to exact from others more than a fair return for goods supplied or services rendered, is endorsing a principle that ultimately can be counted on to destroy it. When the few organize to exploit the many they are establishing a precedent which is not apt to be forgotten when the many gain the opportunity to reverse the process and expropriate the few.

A more modern economic superstition is the belief that the present crisis, like others in the past, is due, not to such soul maladies as greed,

extravagance, cowardice, stupidity and crookedness but to a "business cycle-whatever that may be. This fantastic theory, that a healthy economic system necessarily must collapse every so often, is built upon the baseless assumption that such entirely unnecessary economic diseases as underconsumption and overproduction are periodical and unpreventable. On the contrary these pathological conditions can be prevented as soon as we can, in time of peace, tap and harness, from the deeper sources of the soul, more intelligence and an even greater voltage of economic motive power than can be generated from the caveman instincts of greed and grab. Such an achievement will bring about a spiritual renaissance that will insure to every worker economic justice and a career open to talent, industry and character. But is such an achievement possible? 

Unquestionably such near-miracles have taken place all down the ages and now are taking place, on a wholesale scale in a number of countries, through the instrumentality of that interdenominational movement in the direction of first century Christianity, called the Oxford Groups.. 

The crucial question is can they be brought about today on a large enough scale to save and transform civilization before it is destroyed by the toxic poisons of greed, fear, class and racial hate and the savage lust for place and power?

Still another widely held economic superstition is the belief that the present crisis is a natural and inevitable aftermath of the World War. As a matter of fact, it was not the war we helped to win, but the war we and all the other combatants lost and still are losing, that caused this economic collapse-the war against the sinister enemies within our own wills and hearts and minds. 

As long as these and other demoralizing economic superstitions are promulgated as sound American doctrine by the press, by learned college professors and by leading professional and business men throughout the nation, so long will public opinion and public morals continue to be polluted at their source. When man's selfishness is justified by having the stamp of nation-wide approval placed upon such doctrines as "every man for himself-the devil take the hindmost" in a ruthless "struggle for existence," we are reverting to the ethics of the jungle.

This doctrine has been accepted by millions of otherwise civilized and humane people because they have been made to believe that higher ethical standards are impractical. On the contrary, sound ethical standards are as practical as the multiplication table and as immutable as the law of gravitation. Could anything be more obviously impractical than the present pagan system, based on greed, which rapidly is disintegrating the world over? When men start by being "intelligently selfish," their next logical step is to become intelligently cowardly in their attitude toward the known crookedness around them, with the probable result that they will become first defenders and then temporary beneficiaries of this more profitable crookedness. This is a part of the well known process by which every previous civilization has decayed and died and as a result of which our boasted civilization is now more desperately ill than most of us are willing to admit.

But what is there that can be substituted for the classic economic doctrine of "every man for himself and the devil take the hindmost"? 

A multitude of honest, earnest men and women have been genuinely puzzled during the past 100 years, as to which if any, of the many proposed answers to this question, is practical. We of this generation are fortunate in being able to see the test of experience being applied, on a nation-wide scale, to almost every one of the proposed answers. Russia is endeavoring to demonstrate the workability of an autocratic form of communism; Mussolini and Hitler are experimenting with equally autocratic forms of State Socialism. The sovereign peoples of Great 

Britain, America, France and the other nations that have retained their popular forms of government, apparently have become dazed at the rapid disintegration of their capitalistic civilizations. They are beginning to wonder whether or not mankind is to be limited permanently to a futile choice between an unsuccessful Bolshevism, an equally unsuccessful Fascism or Nazism and a Capitalism which all over the world is breaking down before our eyes? Fortunately such is not the case.

Theoretically at least, it is possible to build a civilization that combines all that is best in both individualism and the various forms of socializa-

tion. And yet the key fact must not be overlooked that such a constructive achievement can only come as a result of a spiritual awakening within ourselves as individuals. It ought to be self-evident that no nation is going to be right until a sufficient number of its individual citizens are willing to sacrifice whatever selfish and craven impulses and interests prevent them from being and doing right. From which it follows, as the night the day, that what America needs primarily is, not a new system or a new racket that is guaranteed to enable each one of us to get rich quick at the expense of the rest of us, nor a new political party based upon any of the fantastic schemes now being promoted for producing prosperity by political and economic magic, nor yet a "superman," who is supposed to be able to change 120 million fear racked Mammon worshippers into a patriotic, courageous and prosperous people, but a good old fashioned "conviction of sin" and a willingness to be not merely reformed but transformed. Unless we are willing to obey the Almighty's definite and detailed instructions for us as individuals and nations, not even God Himself can save civilization from further collapse. All successful New Deals and New Orders have to be started, not at the ballot box, nor in armed uprisings but in the well springs of the will in masses of individual men and women. 

In the light of these basic verities, what are likely to be the paramount issues in the coming presidential campaign? The issue, which in importance transcends all the rest is the bed rock issue between those who believe that by pledging "our lives, our fortunes and our sacred honor" we can, with God's help, find a triumphant way out of the depression for the whole nation, and those on the other hand who believe that this crisis is a ruthless endurance test, in which the weak, the idealistic and the honest will be liquidated and only the toughest, the cleverest and the crookedest will survive. The reactionary slogan, "recovery without reform," to many of its advocates, means recovery of political and economic control by the conscienceless crew of frenzied financiers who so recently have wrecked our national prosperity. But although that is what frequently was and is meant by this crass expression of unintelligent self interest, it cannot conceivably be its per-{ manent result.

If the predatory and parasitic classes should succeed in putting into operation this ruthless and ruinous policy, they simply would be sawing off the limb on which they are sitting. The American people sometimes have been slow but they never have been supine. If they once come to realize that they are being brutally exploited, they can be counted on to "rise like lions after slumber, in unvanquishable number." When they see the plain path of patriotic duty, they can be relied upon to walk in it, though hell itself rise up to block the way! On a showdown, instead of submitting weakly to recognized tyranny they would be more likely, like Sampson, blindly to pull down the pillars of organized society on all our heads. I cannot believe that they will ever go red but I am sure they will never go yellow!

But however important it may be to realize that the American people as a whole, with God's help, can win this desperate conflict with hunger, cold, disease and despair, such a theoretical belief is not enough. There is another outstanding question of equal importance-in order to win this fight, are we willing to pay the price-whatever that price may be?

This issue is going to call out in our people a new type of patriotism—a patriotism as self-sacrificing and daring in the field of economics as has been the patriotism of the past on the field of battle. At the next election, neither a reformed Republican party, nor even a constructive Coalition party will have so much as a fighting chance, unless it can show itself at least as willing and determined as has been the present administration, to underwrite victory-cost what it may! Our people may not be convinced that the present Administration has found the best methods of getting us out of the depression but they cannot help admiring the courage with which it has risked political danger and defeat by putting through emergency measures that, for the time being, have saved millions of innocent victims of the depression from hunger, cold and despair. No party can win the next election by deliberately aiming at a lesser national achievement. You cannot compromise with hunger and cold.

There are three possible ways of outflanking the Roosevelt cohorts, first by offering a more practical program of economic recovery than

theirs, second, by proposing a higher and more alluring ultimate ideal than the one they are aiming at, and third, by an organic combination of these two. Several obviously insincere policies highly favored in certain reactionary circles, can be relied upon to defeat any candidate who adopts them. One is that of insistently talking about "balancing the budget" without proposing any definite method of doing so. Another is the similarly disingenuous proposal to reduce taxes, without suggesting any plan for achieving that highly desirable result. A third is the demand that the government "stop its present orgy of spending," without offering any suggestion as to how our ten or eleven million unemployed are to be cared for more economically. "The fool though a wayfaring man" ought to be able to see that until jobs can be given these victims of the depression, our governments, Federal, state and local will have to feed them or shoot them. One of our Governors is being lavishly praised because his state, by shirking its unmistakable duty to help furnish emergency relief to its unemployed, threw practically all of that responsibility on the Federal and local governments. If a similar ostrich. policy were to be inaugurated on a national scale by our next Chief Executive, it speedily would transform millions of unemployed into hunger marchers and the nation into a bedlam of economic chaos. 

The reactionaries and their political stool pigeons appear to believe that they can ride into power on a negation. Apparently they propose to make the Mistakes of the New Dealers their paramount issue. They seem to have overlooked the fact that the Administration has made more successes than mistakes, and to have forgotten the sound axiom, "you cannot beat something with nothing." Moreover, is it not possible that millions of voters next November will be more afraid of idleness, bankruptcy, hunger and cold and all the rest of the crushing legacy of Harding, Coolidge and Hoover than of the shortcomings of the present Administration-obviously shortcomings more of method and temper than of motive.

In the very nature of things there are in America and in every other nation, three distinct schools of political and economic thought. The one demands that we let things drift, on the baleful theory that sooner or later enough of our "out of luck" and "out of work" classes will be

destroyed to restore our economic equilibrium. Another demands that we "change the system" in order to "enable the individual to live on a higher moral and material plane." This school of thought naturally takes the position that the solution for our most pressing economic ✓ problems lies in socialization, in a change of method that does not necessarily involve a prior change of motive, or in other words, in taking people as they are and seeing how high they can lift themselves by their bootstraps. The third demands that we "change the motivating spirit in the individual," so that an even deeper and more constructive change can be made in the system-a change wrought by persuasion from within rather than by compulsion from without. This school of thought holds that if the Christian spirit of trusteeship or stewardship can be made to dominate a people it can express itself constructively in a variety of ways; in an illumined system of voluntary cooperation as advocated by 

Kagawa, in a God-guided socialization, in a transformed and transfigured individualism, or best of all in a combination of the three that will be better adapted to the multiple needs of civilized man than conceivably could be any one hard and fast system. 

Without the principle of stewardship the politician or bureaucrat, whether he be Republican, Democrat, Socialist or Communist, is all too apt to regard a public office not primarily as a public trust but as a private snap. Without the principle of stewardship, the officials of cooperative enterprises such as stores, banks, insurance companies, live stock and grain dealers associations and even the officials of fraternal and religious bodies, like the managers of capitalistic enterprises, are similarly prone to regard their positions of responsibility and authority, not primarily as opportunities for public service but as means of personal advancement. In other words, without the inspiring, revivifying principle of stewardship every organization tends to become a racket. Its ideal ends tend to become subordinated to the selfishness, vanity and love of power of officials and "members of the organization." Many of us have seen this process of moral disintegration taking place in various organizations with which we are connected. What the world needs most today is a new motivating spirit, like that of Christian stewardship, that will transform individuals, corporations, and political,

labor, farmer and religious organizations into instruments for promoting primarily the public welfare or the Kingdom of God on earth, and only incidentally for promoting the individual fortunes of officials and employees holding positions of trust.

How can this spirit of stewardship be made a creative controlling factor in the political and economic life of the nation? Obviously not until this spirit has come to dominate a multitude of individual men and women, can we expect a higher, creative spirit or motive power to become manifest in the platforms and candidates of either or both of our two great parties?

Apparently there will be at least four paramount issues in the approaching campaign-finance, taxation, agriculture and unemployment.

It is high time that intelligent voters of all parties did their bit, by endeavoring to contribute to the general fund of constructive thinking on these vital questions. Until we have made a real start in the direction of permanently solving these basic and organically related problems, it would be a costly folly to waste time and effort, or to get into needless controversies among ourselves, on secondary issues. If we can create a stable dollar and permanently cut pre-depression interest rates in half;* maintain remunerative prices for farm products, without subsidizing the farmer;† drastically reduce confiscatory local taxes by county consolidation and other steps in the direction of governmental efficiency and economy;‡ reduce unemployment by creating a mass buying power more nearly equal to our powers of mass production, by means of an expanded security program financed by a national consumption tax,‡ and as a result of all these constructive measures make a genuine start in the direction of balancing the budget—a permanent and basic recovery will have begun.

These policies therefore can be regarded as necessary first steps or what the socialists call a "minimum program." The party that shows us the soundest, quickest and most economical way of achieving them, undoubtedly can win the next election. Indeed is it conceivable that a

political party can lose, that is able to show us, first, how to save from impending disaster our four largest groups of voters-debtors, taxpayers, farmers, and wage earners; second, how to get business off the present "dead center"; third, how to safeguard and underwrite the legitimate property rights of all classes; and fourth, how again to open wide the gates of opportunity to the talent, industry and character of the millions of baffled, befuddled and embittered young men and women, to whom life so far has meant only one long series of incomprehensible and undeserved defeats and disillusionments. 

However, before we can work out a program that can be counted on to harmonize present antagonisms and reintegrate our rapidly disintegrating civilization, and before we can even understand and wholeheartedly support such a program, we shall have to achieve as individuals and as a nation, a new and more constructive spirit and motive. For generations mankind has been haunted by the childish assumption that whenever a nation can be induced to vote in favor of an ideal economic system, based on brotherhood, such a system can be created regardless of whether or not its citizens possess sufficient intelligence, character, mutual good will and faith in God and man to enable them to produce and operate such a system. Strangely enough few even among our leaders today realize that the evils of the present system are merely the economic expressions of such disorders of the soul as acute selfishness and fear, moral lethargy, moral myopia, moral cowardice, a demoralizing materialistic complex and a paralyzing incubus of unbelief. As long as the 

Thearts of leaders and voters are set, first and foremost, on achieving personal and partizan success and acquiring easy money, material luxury, special privileges (whether for labor, the farmer or capital) and all the other soul-destroying grist that most of us have worked, voted, hoped and prayed that the mills of the gods would grind out for our especial benefit-just so long will our efforts to achieve a permanent national recovery end in futility and failure. As a shadow picture on a movie screen can only be changed by changing its film, so our economic system can only be fundamentally changed for the better by first ridding ourselves as individuals of those demoralizing, disintegrating and disruptive

passions and complexes that have created an environment in which racketeers, profiteers and grafters, can spawn and thrive. 

Communists think they are preaching a heroic and adequate gospel when they declare that nothing short of a revolution, that will do away with the profit system, can save a nation. But a revolution that only changes the selfish struggle for profits into an equally sordid struggle for higher salaries, position and power in a bureaucracy or a military machine, has failed before it starts. In our present supreme crisis nothing short of a spiritual renaissance that begins in human hearts and minds and thereafter inevitably expresses itself in changed laws and institutions, can save the "Grand Old Party," can save America or can save the world.

CHAPTER II FINANCIAL SANITY 

When one tries to compute the losses of the nations of Europe and America, as a result of the breakdown of predatory finance and the economic system of which it is the shaky and crumbling foundation, the mind stands aghast at the incredible magnitude of the total. For several years most of these nations have been making herculean efforts to find a financial system that, in normal times, can be counted on to promote instead of sap the national prosperity and that in a crisis will not crack and collapse. Obviously the world is in desperate need of less financial hysteria and more financial sanity. Without it our individualistic civilization is doomed. But financial sanity can only be gained by first achieving a moral sanity that is willing to give as well as to receive a square deal. In other words, financial sanity means financial fair play.

By almost universal consent financial sanity, or fair play, stands for several fundamental principles, such as "sound money," "a balanced budget, "no abnormal inflation,” “an elastic currency,” “a stable dollar" and "a redeemable currency." Unfortunately, however, these principles are more widely endorsed than understood. Let us briefly endeavor to get at the real and vital truths expressed by these and certain other generally accepted financial slogans which have been coined and given wide currency by economists, financiers and publicity experts. 

Financial fair play unquestionably means "sound money." Unless the unit of value can be relied upon, even in the gravest crisis, to maintain as nearly as is humanly possible its normal buying power or value, it inevitably will become a source of economic confusion and danger. 

But maintaining its value unquestionably means preventing its increase in value, as well as its decrease. If the unit of value can be kept reasonably constant in buying power, this stable financial yard stick will do 22

FINANCIAL SANITY 

23 more than will any other one factor to stabilize the whole financial and economic structure. In other words, sound money, or money with a permanently stable buying power, is the key stone to the arch of a permanent prosperity.

Financial fair play also means a balanced budget as a normal objective and condition. Undoubtedly, there are times when it is necessary for nations, as for individuals, to borrow and even to borrow heavily. But after a war, an economic collapse or any other justifiable resort to credit is over, until the debt incurred has been liquidated, to persist in borrowing is to become a credit addict. Borrowing, like dope taking or surgery, while useful or even necessary in an emergency, is fatal as a habit. Moreover, a nation that permits itself to print irredeemable paper money with which to pay current expenses, cannot too quickly put a stop to that dangerously seductive practice, which, if persisted in, is certain to prove fatal.

Financial fair play means no abnormal inflation of currency or credit. Such inflation tends to upset all rational financial calculations and to inject into the economic life of the nation an element of uncertainty that works like fever in the blood. Indeed, few economic blunders bring more fear and confusion in their train than does abnormal currency or credit inflation, or even a prospect of such inflation, unless possibly it be its opposite, the equally unwarranted and destructive process of abnormal currency or credit deflation. Both of these processes are dangerous economic diseases and should be avoided as such. From which it follows that when abnormal currency or credit inflation or deflation has occurred, a return to financial health can only be achieved by means of a process known as "reflation," or in other words, by an immediate movement in the direction of a sound, normal monetary condition. 

Financial fair play means an elastic currency that will furnish a never failing supply of credit for all the legitimate needs of business, industry and agriculture that are backed by sound security. The business man, industrialist, or farmer whose credit is dependent, not on the soundness of the security he has to offer but on abnormal fluctuations in the money market brought about by speculators, gamblers and frenzied

financiers, is hopelessly handicapped by an unsound, uncivilized and intolerable financial system.

Financial fair play means credit on the easiest terms that are economically sound. Every needless difficulty or burden imposed on the debtor class and every element of uncertainty injected into the problem of securing promptly, when needed, the credit to which one is entitled is a direct attack on our individual and national prosperity. For example, the old custom of loaning money to cattle breeders and feeders only on 90-day commercial paper, was an injustice that frequently inflicted cruel hardships on borrowers and sometimes bankrupted essentially solvent farmers. Likewise, the vicious old custom of limiting the lifetime of mortgages on farms and urban real estate to five years, was a sordid injustice to farmers and the owners of urban homes and other kinds of real estate. It was a means of extorting from them additional fees and unfairly high interest rates by holding over them, like a sword of Damocles, the fear lest at maturity they might not be able to renew the unpaid portions of their loans. Present day 20-year mortgages, that are amortized almost imperceptably year by year, have offered millions of self-respecting hard-working people a release from this gross form of financial injustice.

Financial fair play demands a financial system that is not less sound, nor "just as good," but immeasurably sounder than the so-called "gold standard." Several times during the past century, in both England and 

America, when subjected to severe strains, the so-called "gold standard" has collapsed. Its last collapse in the United States occurred March 3, 1933, when every bank in the country closed its doors. During that acute and tragic crisis, although we had nearly one-half of the world's gold, an inverted pyramid of approximately 100 billion dollars worth of currency and bonds "payable in gold," was found to be resting on a narrow base of approximately four and one-half billion dollars worth of the yellow metal. Anybody but a "frenzied financier" ought to have been able to see that such an inverted pyramid, until its ultimate collapse, always was in a perilously unstable state of equilibrium. Indeed, such a contraption, by no stretch of the imagination, could properly be called

FINANCIAL SANITY 

25 a gold standard. It was a standard composed of 412% gold and 9512% confidence. It was about on a par with the so-called "50-50 rabbit sausage, composed of one horse and one rabbit." In other words, it was essentially a confidence standard which, as bitter experience finally taught us, was based chiefly on "misplaced confidence." In the light of these facts, it is apparent that when anyone demands a return to the gold standard, he is proposing that we place our national prosperity, and our national security on board an unseaworthy ship which several times in the past, on encountering heavy financial weather, has foundered. 

Financial fair play means a redeemable paper currency. Economists are practically unanimous in condemning as unsound all known types of irredeemable paper currency. Moreover, it also has been demonstrated repeatedly that it is disastrous to attempt to make a currency redeemable in any one commodity, like gold, of which there is not a sufficient quantity to sustain, in a depression or panic, more than a fraction of the currency and credit supposed to be based upon it. The unfounded nature of the pretensions of the advocates of the "gold standard" is made clear by the fact that there is not enough monetary gold available in the world to supply even a 50% gold reserve with which, in a crisis or panic, to redeem the currency, bank credit and bonds in the United States alone. If we are to have a financial system that will weather every storm, manifestly other forms of wealth—not instead of gold but in addition to gold—must be used to form a Gibraltar-like foundation for that system. In such a system apparently the chief function of gold would be that of settling international balances, until we could provide for the settlement of most of them by means of a new system of international financial bookkeeping.

What conceivably could be sounder than a currency secured by ample quantities of the cream of the wealth of the nation-billions of dollars worth of gold, silver, iron, copper, wheat, corn, tobacco, gilt-edged securities, farm lands and city real estate-each dollar of this currency to be issued only as loaned on at least double its value of tangible wealth and redeemable in SPECIAL bonds based on this abundant, varied and impregnable security? This brings us to the consideration of what long

has been known in financial circles as "asset currency." For many years the Federal Reserve System has been experimenting with an "asset currency" based on well-secured short time commercial paper. From years of experience it has become clear that this sound principle can be so developed, safe-guarded and expanded as to supply the United States with the safest, most economical and most efficient financial system the world has ever seen. In order to prevent disastrous inflation or deflation of this redeemable paper currency, a constitutional amendment could be passed providing that whenever the price level of commodities rose a certain number of points, the Treasury would be required to sell bonds and retire currency and that whenever the average price level dropped a certain number of points, the Treasury would be required to buy bonds in order to put more currency in circulation. 

Financial fair play means credit at the lowest rate of interest compatible with sound finance. Every increase in the interest rate, above such a minimum, not only is a tax levied by the creditor class on the debtor class but likewise is a paralyzing hindrance to a normal national economic development. What is the "lowest rate of interest compatible with sound finance?" If loans are to be made by private citizens, banks or other loan agencies on a commercial basis, evidently the lowest permanent rate will have to include not only cost, including risk, but also a profit. However, if loans are to be made by a government, to its taxpaying stockholders, manifestly the interest rate could become merely a service charge to cover all items of cost, including possible losses. It is interesting to note that this suggested financial policy is in harmony with the teaching of Holy Writ, which repeatedly forbids "usury or increase." 

Some New England Cooperatives have found it practical to loan their reserves to members at a service charge of 2%. The Federal Land banks, in normal times, have found it possible to make and collect loans at a cost of only 1% above the cost of money to them. The Reconstruction Finance Corporation has demonstrated that a still smaller percentage will cover the cost of investigating and buying first mortgage bonds of well-managed railways, banks and industrial corporations. 

However, as it would seem only fair that all the people, through their

27 government should share directly, as well as indirectly, in the benefits of the proposed new financial system, a Federal tax of 2% could be levied on all loans made and all bonds bought by the government bearing the proposed nominal interest rate of only 1%. Such a policy while holding at 3% the total cost of government money, in interest and taxes, would bring into the Federal Treasury an enormous revenue. For example, of the seven and one-half billion dollars worth of farm mortgages outstanding in 1935, doubtless at least five billion dollars worth of them would be sufficiently well secured to make them suitable for the government to take over. Such a procedure would mean a saving to the farmers of at least $100,000,000 a year in interest and an additional revenue for the Federal government of an equal amount. Similarly the eight billion dollars of mortgage bonds on Class 1 railroads now outstanding, if taken over by the Federal government at a combined cost of only 3% in interest and taxes, would mean an even larger saving to the railroads that would take a lot of them out of the red and increase enormously the value of their stocks. This also would net the government an additional revenue of $160,000,000 per annum. In a word, if the Federal government would issue and loan $25,000,000,000 of redeemable currency to its citizens, on twice their value in the best possible tangible security and would charge for the use of this money a service charge of 1% and a tax of 2%, the government would derive therefrom an annual income of $500,000,000 while at the same time giving to business, industry and agriculture a desperately needed financial stimulus of incalculable value.

Bond issues requiring more money than the government considered it safe to loan on the security offered, would have to be floated, as at present, in the commercial money market. Likewise 90-day commercial loans, made on the basis of the credit rating of merchants and other borrowers, would have to be handled by commercial banks. But any farmer having a farm or storage warehouse receipt for grain, cotton, tobacco, or other non-perishable crops and any urban dweller having improved real estate or any other kind of non-perishable property, that could be classed as "gilt-edged" security, would be able to get the amount

of money to which such security entitled him at a combined cost, in interest and taxes, of only 3%.

As a concrete illustration of the benefits that would come to every individual business man and farmer, borrowing from the government or selling bonds to it in accordance with the proposed new "asset currency" system, take the following item of financial news from the 

Chicago Tribune of December 19, 1935. Prior to that date private bankers had been demanding, from the Great Northern Railroad, an underwriting fee of 1% and interest at 5%, on a ten year bond issue for one hundred million dollars. Considering these charges too high, under existing financial and economic conditions, President Jones of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, was quoted as telling the President of the Great Northern that the RFC would let him have the money at 4%, without any underwriting fee. That the road was entitled to this low money rate was indicated by a further statement of Mr. Jones that it is "one of our best systems" and "never in receivership" and that the bonds in question were being quoted on December 16, in New York, at 108 on a "when issued" basis. In accordance with the suggested asset currency system, the Great Northern could have been loaned this money at a combined rate of interest and taxes of only 3%. 

This would have meant a net saving to the railroad, on the offer of the bankers, of $3,000,000 the first year and $2,000,000 each year thereafter and would have meant a net income to the government, in taxes, of $2,000,000 a year.

In the light of the present superabundance of capital, the whole theory and practice of financing business, must be rethought and revamped. Up to date, the bond merchant and mortgage holder have been receiving the lion's share of the gross income of industry, commerce and agriculture. Take for example, a business man who starts a new mine or factory and invests in it all his available capital of perhaps one million dollars. He also puts in his brains, energy and hard work. When necessary, he thinks and plans night and day to overcome the numerous difficulties and solve the myriad problems, technical, administrative and financial that arise. After a time, when more money

29 is needed, he issues bonds bearing 5% interest, or more, for an amount equal to his original investment. If the enterprise is barely able to get by, he gets nothing on his investment-the bondholder taking all the net income. If it is reasonably successful, he and the bondholder receive approximately the same income. Only in case he makes an unusual success does he make more than the bondholder, and in case he encounters temporary reverses, so that he cannot make both ends meet, unless he can borrow more money or sell out at a sacrifice, he loses his entire investment while the bondholder either can recoup himself from the proceeds of a forced sale or buy in the business for a song. 

In other words, in our present financial set-up, the business man assumes practically all of the risk, does all of the work, furnishes all of the brains and finds that the dice nearly always are heavily loaded against him in favor of the bondholder. No wonder that the creditor class, during the past quarter of a century, rapidly has been absorbing the wealth of the country and establishing a veritable dictatorship over American business, industry and agriculture. The Rockefellers avoided this pitfall by establishing their own banks and financial houses in order to finance themselves. Henry Ford, after a narrow escape from financial hi-jackers, finally found it imperative to do the same. What these two business geniuses did for themselves, the Federal Government can do for all of our business, professional and laboring men and farmers by establishing a financial system, based on "asset currency," with the cream of the wealth of the nation as its impregnable foundation. What an incalculable impetus could be given to the national prosperity, by thus reducing, and in some cases cutting in half, the interest rates on loans based on gilt-edged security! If this were done, much of the money now being poured in a golden flood into the coffers of frenzied financiers, many of which already are full to overflowing, would remain in the hands of business men, farmers and others, to be used as active buying power and increase the effective demand of the nation for the surplus products of America's farms, mines, forests and factories.

CHAPTER III  

The politicians of both parties are looking anxiously, even desperately for a satisfactory agricultural policy. This is a new departure for the Republicans who, until three years ago, had little or no difficulty in lining up the rural voters of the North and West on Civil War memories, regardless of live issues. It also involves a similar but more recent change for the Democrats who, prior to the Supreme Court's decision against the A.A.A., apparently were more permanently tnan happily married to their hastily espoused emergency agricultural policy. But today both of these gay political Lotharios seem to be heart free to embrace the agricultural policy that promises to bring most benefit to the nation as well as the largest number of farmer votes to the party sponsoring it.

If political leaders could be induced to pay less attention to the mythical "farmer vote" and professional rural vote shepherds, and more attention to the devising and enacting into law of practical agricultural policies, undoubtedly they would be able to evoke a deeper and more lasting loyalty among country people. In the present crisis the farmer is looking not for a handout nor for "easy money" but for justice. He is neither a moron nor a political mercenary, offering to sell himself to the highest bidder in dollars and cents of the taxpayers money. He has been told repeatedly to "work out his own salvation" and has shown himself more ready to do so than has any other class. However, he realizes that he, like every other good citizen, is entitled to the government's assistance in this task and that the only rational limit to that assistance is the limit automatically reached when the government begins to enrich his class at the expense of the others. As a matter of fact he constitutes one of the few classes that consistently has refrained from 30

asking the government to go beyond that limit. The shipping industry frequently has demanded and received heavy subsidies and of recent years, like Oliver Twist, constantly has had its hand out for "more." 

Our manufacturers began by demanding help for "infant industries" and ever since have been demanding and receiving a larger and larger measure of help for the lusty giants into which those "infants" have grown. High finance has been granted one special privilege after another, including the governmental functions of issuing money and controlling credit until, as direct results of its sordid and bungling domination of the business world we had the crash of 1929 followed by the present unprecedented depression. 

The average farmer would be quite willing to renounce his bonuses, on condition that all other special privileges likewise would be withdrawn as fast as possible without making bad matters worse. Indeed as a dirt farmer, I am strongly of the opinion that during the approaching campaign, if one political party attempts to outbid the other in dollar and cent bonuses and the other, refusing to compete in this sordid contest, offers the farmer a plain, unvarnished "square deal," and appeals for his support on a basis of devotion to his family, his country and his God, the politicians will be astounded at the resulting tidal wave of farmer votes that will be rolled up in favor of such a selfrespecting policy.

It is reported that a day or two before the last Congressional election, Chairman Fletcher of the Republican National Committee was asked how the farmers of the Middle West were going to vote. "It is like this," he is said to have replied. "A day or two ago, farmer Smith, of Speedunk, Iowa, received a letter from me urging him to vote for the immortal principles, historic traditions and sound policies of the party of Lincoln, Grant, Harding and Coolidge. The next day he got a letter from the A.A.A. containing a check for $300.00. That is how he is going to vote!"

As an economist and a farmer I must insist that this is a long way from being a fair statement of the case. Like most city people, the distinguished gentleman quoted apparently knows little or nothing about

the post-war tragedy of the farmer. Here are a few of the salient facts concerning that major national disaster. The depression knocked agriculture cold several years before it even began to affect business. Indeed agriculture was potentially bankrupt during the whole hectic period that business and labor were enjoying their feverish, post-war prosperity based on a national orgy of stock gambling. The calamitous condition of agriculture, at that time, was concisely described by Virgil Jordan, Chief Economist of the National Industrial Board, in an address before the 10th Annual Meeting of the American Farm Bureau Federation, on December tenth, 1928. Allowing farmers the wages of skilled labor and the same interest rate on their capital being paid on government bonds, "the industry as a whole," he declared, "in the last crop year, fell short by more than five billion dollars of paying its own way as a going concern." "There is no way of concealing the fact," continued Mr. Jordan, "that this basic American industry, which feeds and clothes the nation and leads all others in capital invested and value of product, is broke and has been every year since the war." Following are some startling figures that tend to corroborate these statements. In 1920 the value of farm crops decreased over three billion dollars and in 1921 decreased nearly three billion five hundred million dollars more, while as a result of the disastrous losses by agriculture during a period of eleven years, a shrinkage occurred in the capital value of farm lands of over twenty billion dollars.

The present depression has taught the farmer certain facts that all the eloquence of a generation of Democratic spellbinders had failed to get across to him. Among other things it has taught him that while for a half century he has been fed a liberal supply of high sounding "principles," traditions and campaign promises, the Eastern manufacturer, in lieu of this cheap line of sales talk, has been allotted billions of dollars of tariff tribute money, from the pockets of one hundred and twenty-five million ultimate consumers. Non-partizan experts have estimated the quota of this tariff tribute money annually paid by farmers, at one thousand million dollars. Moreover when six million farmers, in spite

of their greatly increased efficiency at production and their utmost efforts at economizing, were about to go broke, and implored the Harding and Coolidge Administrations for relief, they were plunged deeper into debt by means of government loans and told to "stop dabbling in politics" since "the government could do nothing for the farmer." That statement was and is absurd on the face of it. It is true that the government cannot legislate the farmer out of all his troubles. With his own strong arms, clear brain and undaunted heart he must conquer, or be conquered by, a never ending succession of difficulties and pests-droughts, hailstorms, hurricanes, sunless summer months and blistering seasons of heat, boll weevils, Canada thistles, chinch bugs, army worms, grasshoppers, corn borers, and "the locusts mile deep swarm." But it is quite as true that there are certain other varieties of troubles from which, in the very nature of things, the farmer cannot hope to extricate himself without the help of government. Indeed how without that help can he ever expect to get lower taxes, lower freight rates, a stable dollar, electric current at a fair price, a credit system adapted to the essential needs of agriculture, fair tariff schedules, protection from profiteering "combinations in restraint of trade," and foreign markets for his surplus crops? The last straw of insult added to injury, that finally roused the farmers to the necessity for united political action in behalf of their farms and firesides was President Coolidge's veto of a "farm relief" measure which, after repeated failures, they finally had got through both Houses of Congress.

In the light of this brief resume of recent agricultural history, it is not difficult to understand that when President Roosevelt actually gave the farmers something approaching equality with the manufacturers, in governmental help, they should have felt strongly impelled by gratitude as well as by self-interest to rally to the support of his Administration. 

In other words, it was not a bribe, as has been intimated, that lined up the farmers for the Roosevelt Administration but a belated and only partial righting of half a century of gross governmental injustice. 

Following is a comparison of some of the results, in the corn belt, of the Hoover and Roosevelt agricultural policies. When President Hoover

came into power the price of corn, in Central Illinois, was approximately seventy cents a bushel; shortly before he left the White House, its price in the same region, was fifteen cents a bushel. On the other hand President Roosevelt by loaning the farmers forty-five cents a bushel on stored corn, reducing the interest rate on farm mortgages and other New Deal policies, accomplished some equally striking but more desirable results. He speedily put many millions of dollars into circulation in this fertile but financially stricken region; he reduced the outflow of interest money by tens of millions of dollars; he doubled and trebled the price of corn; and by enabling the farmer to hold his corn, instead of selling it to pay taxes and other pressing obligations, he made it possible for him, a few months later, to reap the benefits of still higher scarcity prices caused by the drought, of which otherwise the speculator would have been the sole beneficiary.

The cotton belt and the wheat belt have been helped approximately as much as has the corn belt by present emergency agricultural policies. 

Unquestionably as long as our political contests are conducted on the basis of votes given for benefits received, President Roosevelt is entitled to the continued support of the farmers-unless the Republicans, not only outpromise him, but also show by their votes in Congress and by their policies in the states controlled by them, that they stand ready to do more for them than will the Democrats. However, such a reversal of Republican agricultural policy sounds highly improbable unless they can discover some form of "farm relief" that will not alienate the traditional Republican support of business, political and journalistic leaders in the Eastern states, most of whom never have regarded the farmer as an equal whose interests should be fostered by the government as diligently as have been those of the manufacturer. Therefore it would seem that the Republicans cannot hope to regain the farmer vote in the West and Middle West, unless they can propose some better method than that of subsidizing the farmers as heavily as they have the manufacturers, steamship companies, and other recipients of rich special privileges. That being the case, they may be interested in the following brief outline of an agricultural policy that obviously could be relied upon to conserve,

at one and the same time, the soil, the farmer's legitimate financial interests and his self respect.

Among the multitude of agricultural problems facing us, the outstanding one ever since the World War has been that of preventing or disposing of the farmers' surplus crops. First of all we should recognize that there is such a thing as a normal and highly desirable crop surplus. 

Such a surplus, if wisely limited and stored, can be transformed from a national blight into a national blessing. By providing ample reserves of feed for live stock and food for people it can be made to insure the nation, in the event of possible crop failures, against food shortages and famine prices.

However, if we are looking, not for a nostrum but for a permanent cure, we must attack this highly complicated problem from several angles. We must retain for the farmers such of our home markets as they can supply at reasonable cost and do all in our power to develop profitable export markets.* We must carry on a wide range of experiments in order to develop new and profitable industrial uses for agricultural products. We also must find the fairest possible way to increase by billions of dollars per annum, the buying power of the masses;† and finally we must attack the problem of the surplus at its source. This last proposal should be subdivided into three policies; first, that of reforesting millions of acres of unprofitable farm lands that are better suited to timber production than to growing other crops; second, that of reseeding to grass, in the semi-arid regions, millions of acres of unprofitable farm lands, which never were adapted to anything but grazing, and third, that of sowing each year, approximately oneseventh of our fertile farm lands in nitrogen gathering crops, like the clovers, alfalfa, soy beans, vetch, cow peas and lespidesa, to be plowed under as fertilizer, in order to conserve our greatest single national asset, the fertility of the soil. It is a significant fact that the basic principle of this last policy is almost as old as civilization and has the sanction of Holy Writ, the Mosaic code having commanded a rest for the land

every seventh year. However, instead of allowing all the land to lie fallow every seventh year, this principle apparently can be applied best today by seeding one-seventh of our arable land, each year, in nitrogengathering crops to be plowed under as fertilizer. These three proposals long have had the support of all competent agricultural authorities and are so sound, both agronomically and economically, that they could be counted on to arouse little or no opposition on the part of urban consumers of farm crops. And yet, if carried out systematically, they unquestionably would not only help conserve soil fertility but also would help prevent the production of disastrous crop surpluses that force down the prices of farm products below the cost of production. 

The present Administration has rendered an invaluable service to the farmer and the nation by demonstrating, at a cost of several hundred million dollars a year, that ruinously low prices for farm products can be prevented by a nation-wide reduction of crop acreage. As we have at last learned this expensive lesson so well that it has become a matter of common knowledge, apparently we are ready for the next step. In other words the pump having been liberally primed, it now seems to be up to the farmer to keep pumping. All that most farmers have ever asked is that the mighty mechanism of the Federal government be utilized "to help the farmer help himself." Therefore as a considerable part of the heavy disabilities unjustly laid on agriculture now have been removed, it would seem that the next logical step is for the government to give the farmers leadership and moral support in the great task of working out a more permanent and less costly method of crop acreage control. Two important preliminary steps should be taken, first, the 

Federal government should induce the states to remove all state and local taxes on farm lands being used to grow nitrogen gathering crops for fertilizer and second, a practical and economical method should be worked out by the U. S. Department of Agriculture to have enough legume seed grown at low cost to enable the Federal government to furnish free seed as its contribution to this nation-wide soil conservation project. Such a permanent agricultural policy would be a three-cornered cooperative enterprise, in which the landlord should furnish free land, the tenant free labor, and the government free legume seed.

After the necessary preliminary work has been attended to, a nationwide drive could be launched, somewhat as Liberty Loan Drives were conducted during and after the war, to line up the farmers for a national policy of voluntary crop acreage control that would be equally sound economically, agronomically and Biblically. For this patriotic national effort the Federal government could mobilize, not only all of its multiple agencies but also the powerful influence of such organizations as the U. S. Chamber of Commerce, the American Bankers Association, the 

American Federation of Labor, and the leading farm organizations. It then could appoint in each state, a Crop Acreage Control Board of leading agriculturists, with the Governor as Chairman Ex-Officio. Each 

State Board could appoint Township Boards - each Township Board to be composed of a leading banker, a leading business man and three farmers. Like the local Draft Boards during the war they could be given authority to classify the farmers and to grant reasonable exemptions, while the force of organized public opinion could be brought to bear on recalcitrants. Every farmer complying with the request of his government could have a plaque stating this fact placed on his gate post, so that those having no such plaques would be recognized as slackers or parasites who were profiting from a crop control policy which they were unwilling to do their part to help make succeed. Most of the farmers undoubtedly would fall into line, as did nearly everyone during the Liberty Loan Drives--buying bonds whether they wanted them or not. 

In situations like these, local public opinion backed by the highest state and Federal authority is all but all-powerful. Moreover, it is not necessary that such a plan should be one-hundred percent efficient. Nothing is.

The experience of the cotton growers throws much light on this suggestion. In order to protect themselves from ruinously low prices, the cotton growers repeatedly, and without government subsidies or leadership, have made voluntary wholesale reductions in cotton acreage. 

In 1905 they made a reduction of 4,105,000 acres and in 1915 and 1921 they made similar reductions of 5,120,000 and 5,369,000 acres respectively.

For more than a decade I have believed and stated in speeches, in farm journal articles and at Congressional Committee Hearings that, as a part of a well balanced agricultural policy which would include a remunerative price for farm crops and a reasonable reduction in taxes and interest, the average farmer, without being subsidized, would be willing to sow from fifteen to twenty percent of his arable land in legumes. Now that farmers are beginning to receive for their crops something more than the cost of production and have had their tax and interest burdens noticeably reduced, I feel confident that they are ready, to begin to put into effect such a policy. Given competent national and local leadership, unquestionably they would enlist by the millions in such a corrective and constructive national project. 

Judging by past experience we can safely rely upon the intelligent and patriotic support of an overwhelming majority of the farmers for any agricultural or other governmental policies that are sound and practical. The farmers never have shown any inclination to be sordidly class conscious. During our early history, when the country was chiefly agricultural and the Congress was composed largely of farmers and country town dwellers, one might have expected the enactment of legislation that was selfishly agrarian. On the contrary, taking a patriotic national viewpoint, these representatives of the rural districts fostered urban commerce and industry, at the expense of agriculture, by enacting tariff legislation for the benefit of our then "infant industries." These "rube” fathers of the Republic were wise enough to realize that in the long run, the best way to promote the prosperity of any great economic interest is to build up the other essential economic activities of the Nation, not yet normally developed or that temporarily are in a depressed condition. 

If later when urban manufacturers became able, not only to hold their own but to carve out for themselves new and ever richer spheres of influence in the markets of the world, they had manifested the same far sighted patriotic spirit, they would have favored a gradual decrease in the tariff schedules made for their benefit instead of demanding the unfair and unpatriotic increases in them which have been brought about during the past half century.

The nation need have no fear that the farmer will ever refuse to do his full part in carrying out any sound and patriotic agricultural policy that may be inaugurated by Uncle Sam. From the start he has been the backbone of the nation in time of peace and its faithful defender in time of war. He long has been open to criticism for allowing himself to be deceived by the specious propaganda of big business into voting against his own legitimate interests, but he never has been, and I am confident never will be, open to criticism for prostituting his ballot to the attainment of selfish and unpatriotic advantages for himself and his class.

CHAPTER IV  

Today America is being offered the biggest business opportunity the world ever saw. Has she enough faith, courage, insight and initiative to seize this golden opportunity? Or is she so sunk in the ruts of routine, so poisoned and paralyzed by greed and fear and so fettered by partizanship and economic superstition that she is unwilling to make the political and economic changes required to enable her to take possession of the veritable Eldorado of prosperity that lies spread out before her in an unparalleled panorama of potential markets?

For more than a decade six million American farmers have been facing constantly increasing needs of fertilizers, fences, implements, hardware, paint, cement, roofing and lumber. The present aggregate of these needs amounts to billions of dollars. These six million farmers and their wives, together with twenty odd million children, could also spend to advantage hundreds of millions of dollars on badly needed clothes, household equipment, education and medical care. There are also sixty or seventy million inhabitants of our cities and towns, among whom are millions of underpaid and unemployed workers, who are in similar need of billions of dollars worth of pretty much everything that they normally use, to replace worn out goods of all the fifty-seven varieties. In addition to these enormous requirements of the ultimate consumer the heavy industries also need repairs and renewals amounting to many more billions of dollars.

We pride ourselves on being a practical nation and yet for years we have been ignoring this steadily enlarging opportunity to ride back to prosperity on a tidal wave of new business activity. Why do we continue to ignore this glittering prize that lies so easily within our reach? 

Are we suffering from buck fever, heart failure, softening of the brain or plain spiritual dry rot? 40

This anomalous situation calls to mind the old story of the philosopher and the fisherman on a lake in a rowboat. The philosopher enquired if the fisherman knew anything about philosophy. He did not. "How sad," replied the philosopher, "there is a quarter of your life gone. Do you know anything about science?" He did not. "Another quarter of your life gone," observed the philosopher. "Do you know anything about art?" He did not. "Another quarter of your life gone," lamented the wise man. Just then the fisherman began taking off his coat. "Do you know how to swim?" he asked. The philosopher did not. "Then all your life is gone," said the fisherman, "for this boat is sinking." 

Manifestly America has "everything but❞—the one thing needful to enable her to utilize to advantage her practically unlimited assets and opportunities. If we had that one missing factor undoubtedly we should be the most prosperous nation the world ever saw. Among our assets are enormous supplies of raw materials together with surpluses of nearly every other variety of material wealth; we are descended from enterprising off-shoots of the best racial stocks of Europe; we have the most extensive and expensive educational system on this planet; our industrialists have created the most efficient known system of mass production; our form of government is one of the best ever devised. And yet, in spite of all these invaluable assets and the many crucial battles against the depression that have been won by the Administration during the past three years, we still are perilously far from having won the war against possible economic collapse and wholesale disaster. However hard we try to ignore the sense of impending danger, in moments of insight there come to some of us Christ's devastating words to the rich young ruler, "One thing thou lackest!" Nothing could be clearer than that in America today, both individually and nationally, a vital something is lacking. If such were not the case, this fabulously favored nation would be setting an example and setting the pace for the other nations of the world.

In another respect, we are like the rich young ruler. America has many virtues and good works to her credit. We entered the war without selfish ambition. After the war we made no attempt to acquire any

part of the spoil of conquered territory. We were lavish in our gifts of food to Belgium during the war, to Poland and Austria immediately after the war and later still to Russia for the starving peasants of the Volga Valley. Our millionaires have given more money to education, philanthropy and charity than have those of all other nations combined. 

Before the crash, labor in America was receiving the highest wage on record and the average standard of comfort among the masses was the highest in human history.

But in spite of our many favorable moral factors, and material advantages beyond those of any other nation, our civilization unquestionably is desperately ill of the same disease of which every previous civilization has died. Indeed we are in such dire straits that it is good news to hear that we lack only one primary necessity which, if gained, would enable us to supply all the others. For if we can discover what that "one thing" is and get it, undoubtedly all that is worth salvaging of the civilization of the past, can be saved and transformed into something immeasurably bigger, better and more permanent. What a dramatic and alluring opportunity! Shall we call in the ablest diagnosticians and offer a million dollar honorarium to the one that can tell us the cause and cure of our trouble? Indeed is there anyone who can explain why, at this particular juncture, when for the first time in human history methods have been developed that enable us as a nation to produce all the necessities and comforts of life in such abundance that every legitimate need of every worker in the national hive can be amply supplied-suddenly the whole structure should begin to crack and crumble over our heads? 

What is the weak link in our chain? By the simple process of elimination it is becoming every day more evident that as we have every other necessary factor the only thing lacking is the spiritual element. That fundamental need manifests itself in three outstanding national shortcomings that are effectively blocking national recovery. 

The first is the absence of a dominant desire on the part of most of us for equal justice to all. Doubtless the reason for this anti-social attitude is our instinctive realization that, if everyone were to get his just share, none of us would get more than our just share. Obviously

too many of us are not only willing but anxious to prosper beyond our deserts. Indeed how many of us are not trying our utmost to get from society as much as possible, regardless of how little we give to society? 

Our second outstanding national shortcoming is the unbelief, even of those who sincerely favor economic justice, in our ability under God to attain it. On a show-down most of us believe that in politics the bosses are more powerful than the Almighty and that in business our financial overlords exercise a similar supreme power. Our third national shortcoming is the unwillingness, even of those of us who are attracted by the principle of economic justice and believe it to be attainable, to pay what it costs.

For example, many of the rich are unwilling to be taxed as heavily as are the wealthy classes in Great Britain and many of those who are not rich are equally unwilling to accept a temporary cut in salaries or wages, in order to help make a success of our present supreme national effort to get business off the "dead center" so that we all can begin to make a living again. And whether rich or poor, most of us are unwilling to sacrifice partizanship, as have a majority of the political leaders and voters of Great Britain, even for the purpose of creating an emergency coalition government, to bring about desperately needed but unpopular reforms that, if proposed by a one-party government would defeat that party at the next election. Recent experience in 

Europe has shown that when such reforms become imperative, they either must be put through on the moral and political authority of a patriotic nonpartizan coalition or imposed by the military authority of a dictatorship. Our forefathers pledged their "lives, their fortunes, and their sacred honor" to the founding of this great nation on a basis of equal rights and equally binding duties. Several generations of their descendants have manifested the same spirit whenever the sacrifice, even of life itself has been necessary to preserve this government one and indivisible or to maintain the inherent and sacred rights of individuals and minorities. Until we of this generation are ready to do likewise we have not so much as made a start toward a permanent and basic recovery.

Most of the policies and planks so far proposed for the platform

of the present Opposition party are hopelessly inadequate and futile. 

It is high time the "outs" recognized that they have not the ghost of a chance of winning the next election, unless they can convince our voters first, that they are at least as sincerely desirous, as is the present Administration, of giving a square deal to every legitimate interest; second, that they have at least as lively a faith, as has the present Administration, in our ability under God and our popular form of government, to achieve this inspiring objective; and third, that they are willing to pay the price of such an achievement, even though that price involves the spending of billions of dollars and the scrapping of discredited economic superstitions, worn out political shibboleths, personal greed and partizan lust for place and power. General Grant won the Civil War, although as able or abler strategists had failed to do so, because he acted on the principle that a quick victory, though a costly one, would be cheapest in the long run. If some of President Roosevelt's predecessors had acted on that sound principle, our people would have fought their way to victory over the depression, years ago, at a cost which might then have seemed staggering but which would not have amounted to more than a trifling percentage of the almost incalculable total cost of our present long drawn out period of economic disintegration. 

Civilization's present "riddle of the sphynx" is that of making adjustments in the realms of economics and government, as rapidly as they are being made in the fields of mechanics, chemistry, agriculture and industry. Stalin, Mussolini, Hitler and Kemal Pasha all have been trying desperately but unsuccessfully, to achieve this difficult feat. In this life and death "race with time" by the nations of the world, the closest approximation to success has been achieved by Great Britain, Norway, Sweden, Denmark, Finland and Switzerland. This has been done, not by a fuller concentration of power in the hands of so-called supermen but by means of a greater unity of moral and economic purpose on the part of all classes and a patriotic cooperation by members of all political parties. In other words the partial victories of these nations over the depression have been due primarily to certain subconscious spiritual qualities possessed by their citizens rather than to any change in the system in the direction of a supercentralization of autocratic power.

Unquestionably the most difficult and dangerous economic problem facing the American people is that of unemployment. No matter how many other economic problems we solve, until we have made a real start at solving this one, civilization is still in peril of a national brain storm that may at any time result in an acute and nation-wide political and economic collapse. This chronic and wholesale unemployment is only one aspect of the amazing new problem of making mass consumption keep pace with our rapidly increasing powers of mass production. 

The "Townsend plan" and the "Social Credit plan" were designed primarily to furnish solutions for this problem, and the former, in the absence of any sounder plan, is gaining millions of backers. While I realize the imperative necessity of providing increased purchasing power for the masses, I am unable to see any social or economic justice in the Townsend proposal that we impose "transactions taxes," on millions of people earning less than a thousand dollars a year, with which they are supporting families, in order to give more than twice as much to others over sixty years of age, most of whom are without dependents. Nor can I see any social or economic justice in the Social Credit proposal that the government pay a stipulated minimum income or dividend to all our wealthiest citizens as well as to millions who already are receiving a living wage. But I do realize that it would be not only sound ethics but also the best conceivable business investment for our Federal, State and local governments to so increase the buying power of the submerged tenths of our population as to provide work for an everincreasing number of unemployed and thus to help enable us as a nation to begin to consume all that we can produce when operating at something like full capacity. The recently enacted federal security law was an epoch-making piece of legislation. Our next step is that of perfecting its program. I am confident that we shall find it sound economics and sound statesmanship to pay a subsistence pension not only to all veterans of industry and veteran home makers whose incomes are below a modest minimum but also to all other adults who are incapable of supporting themselves; to see to it that all children are nourished, clothed, housed and taught how to make an honest living and become intelligent citizens; to have the government contribute toward the es-

tablishment and maintenance of a system of insurance against illness, that will provide first-class medical care not only for the unemployed and unemployable but also for families having incomes that, while adequate for normal needs, in times of illness cannot be stretched to meet the cost of necessary hospitalization and medical care; and to offer emergency jobs, in government conservation and construction corps, to all able-bodied, unemployed men or women at wages similar to those now being paid WPA workers. When no work for which they are physically or mentally qualified is available, instead of being put "on relief" they should continue to receive, not a "dole" but a somewhat smaller wage than that received while actively employed. In other words, in order to conserve the self-respect and morale of unemployed workers who are physically and mentally fit, we should segregate them completely from such non-workers as children, the incapacitated and the aged by adopting Bismark's principle of "the right to work." Some such program as this would enable us gradually to close a large part of the fatal gap between a subnormal consumption and a steadily increasing potential production.

There has been a running fire of discussion, during the past few months, as to which is giving the best results, the Hopkins or the Ickes method of putting the unemployed to work. Obviously each has its merits. For example when we take into account the fact that emergency relief work is being paid for with borrowed money, of which there is not enough to put half of the unemployed to work, it becomes clear that it would be unfair to the others to pay substantially more than present WPA subsistence wages, to those fortunate enough to get government relief jobs. In other words it unquestionably would be better to put four million men to work at low wages than two million at double those wages. On the other hand, certain factors in the problem are being overlooked by those who maintain that the construction of permanent public improvements is a less desirable form of relief employment than boondoggling, raking leaves and other activities of doubtful or only temporary value, because of the fact that a considerable percentage of the cost of the former goes for building materials instead of for labor.

These critics of the Ickes method fail to recognize that labor is the chief item of cost in building materials and that the fraction of the cost that legitimately goes for other items is counterbalanced many times over by the permanent value of the roads, schoolhouses, bridges, airports, viaducts, tunnels or whatnot that are built partly or wholly with Federal relief money. Moreover the benefit to the morale of the workers, from being employed on work of permanent value, is too important an asset to be ignored, as is the further fact that the labor utilized by private enterprise in the manufacture of the building materials in question is helping to solve the extremely difficult and crucially important national problem of getting business off the "dead center" and again operating normally.

All of which leads up to the key fact that if, from now until the end of the depression, all unemployment relief work were to be paid for at approximately the WPA scale of wages, and only work of real and permanent value were to be undertaken, this nation, which potentially is rich beyond the dreams of avarice, could to advantage steadily and rapidly increase the number of unemployed being given jobs, until a large part of our present involuntary unemployment would be brought to an end. Thereupon naturally and inevitably wages would begin to swing back to higher levels. 

In the present economic era, for the first time in human history, the demand of the human heart that all those having subnormal earning power be supplied with the necessaries of life, coincides with the demand of the head that society make an incredibly profitable investment by enabling those who otherwise would be ill-clad, without proper medical care, badly housed and undernourished, to consume a much larger share of the nation's surplus products. 

Among the many possible sound investments, in public works projects, one stands out as of unique strategic significance. A new and partly self-supporting type of suburb could to advantage be laid out, adjoining a large number of cities and towns, on rivers and lakes and along the seaboard, preferably in the South. In these suburbs every home should have a garden in which could be grown fruits, flowers, vegetables and chickens. Tens of thousands of inexpensive garden homes could be built

annually, that when rented for a moderate price would prove veritable godsends to unemployed and part-time workers. 

In such a garden home an insufficient income, like an old age pension, could be more than doubled by a little well-planned outdoor exercise that would be, not a drudgery but a thrilling game of chancewith a bountiful nature acting as banker and most of the dice heavily loaded in favor of the players. These suburbs could be laid out according to scores of different designs, to suit the tastes of all sorts and conditions of people. Some could be built to meet the needs of writers, teachers, artists and others, anxious to try the thrilling experiment of growing most of their food and perhaps some of their clothing, during their part-time working hours, while still retaining abundant leisure to devote to study or writing, music or metaphysics or whatever they might elect to do, to round out and enrich their lives. Others could be located under climatic conditions conducive to the health and happiness of invalids, cripples or the blind. But probably the greater number would be built to meet the requirements of that enormous class of intelligent and self-respecting men and women who annually are being consigned to the economic scrapheap because they have passed an arbitrary age limit. These communities would offer such veterans of industry the nearest modern approach to a Garden of Eden, as a substitute for the hell of enforced idleness and poverty, supplemented by charity, which they otherwise would be facing. 

What a tranquil and alluring prospect would be opened up by such a colony, in any one of our Southern States! With a soil capable of growing two or three crops a year and several green vegetables, even during the winter months; with a climate in which the fuel requirements are from fifty to seventy-five per cent less than in the North, and a part of which frequently can be supplied by the simple process of felling a few trees; with fish in nearby waters to supply a modicum of food and sport; and with all the urban attractions available in an adjoining city or town-imagine if you can, how such a possible future home would look to a bronzed and calloused workingman, from Detroit or Chicago-just fired because he has passed the fifty-year-old dead-line, and given a pension that could not conceivably be stretched to meet the

high cost of living in those populous centers. To a vast multitude of such victims of a ruthless industrial system, a home in such a community would look like a tropical island to a hungry and spent swimmer battling for his life in a tempestuous and shark-infested sea. 

Alluring as are the possibilities of a few hundred such economic oases, scattered throughout the country, unquestionably those possibilities can only be realized in communities planned, built, colonized and supervised by thoroughly trained, practical executives whose deepest loyalty is not to a theory, party, clique or class but to the individual men and women for whom such communities would be built, and to those spiritual values for which their rescue and rehabilitation stand. Without such a competent and devoted leadership it will be possible in a hundred different ways to fall just short of the possible success inherent in such a plan. In order to furnish a continuous and effective stimulus to government officials, carefully supervised individual enterprise should be encouraged by liberal government loans, and in every other legitimate way, to compete with government agencies in the construction of such projects. 

Here the crucial question arises how is the huge proposed security program to be financed? Those of us who have to pay heavy taxes from perilously shrunken incomes have been wondering how long our townships, counties, municipalities, states and the nation can continue to pay the present heavy cost of relief to the unemployed and the unemployable. But strangely enough, there is in our problem an even more important factor than that of cost which must be taken into account-the basic factor of the much greater cost of the economic loss involved in present-day subnormal consumption. As a result of the enforced idleness of men, machinery and equipment, our nation every year is losing vastly more than all the relief agencies of the nation have spent during the entire depression. In other words, if by some such constructive program as that proposed here, we could stop this incredibly costly WASTE of time, obsolescence, depreciation, interest, insurance and business momentum, our relief bills would be a comparatively small item that could be classified, not as an expense but as our most profitable national investment. Ten million idle men and the machinery, mines, shops, ships,

railroads and money that their idleness renders inactive or only partially productive, means a prodigious daily national loss. This multiplied by approximately 300 working days per year makes a total annual loss which we should be seriously underestimating if we were to figure it as merely the difference between our present national output and that in the relatively prosperous days before the depression, since our steadily increasing powers of production makes our potential annual output today vastly greater than it was ten years ago. The only reason that we have not heard more of this gigantic annual national loss from unemployment, is that so few people think nationally. Most of us have been so obsessed by our personal interests that we have not taken the time or trouble to figure out the state of the nation. Not until a majority of the American people come to realize that, when a ship that has no lifeboats is sinking, the only way to save anybody is to save the ship and thus save everybody --and not until then-can we expect to have a sufficiently patriotic and intelligent public opinion to make possible the putting into operation of a successful Coalition government and a sound recovery program. 

But even though many recognize that the proposed security program is the most profitable conceivable national investment, we must not forget the collateral fact that many heavy taxpayers, owing to drastic reductions in their incomes, find themselves with little or no money to invest. 

Consequently the major part of the tax burden of financing this new security program can not with impunity be laid on business enterprises that already are in the red, or dangerously near the red. In other words, this enormous new tax should not be permitted to become a wet blanket on that business recovery, without which apparently there can be no quick and permanent way out of the depression. 

It is difficult to overestimate the strategic importance, at the present stage of the crisis, of refraining from increasing the tax burden of the anxious and nervously jumpy business men, who like goldfish in a glass bowl, for several years have run their noses into an unseen but impassable barrier whichever way they have attempted to go. The tax problem of the nation is not unlike the problem involved in the burden that must be carried by a soldier on a forced march. It is of vital importance that he be allowed to carry his load of equipment on his back

rather than tied to his feet. Today our business activities are analogous to the soldier's feet. They are the part of the nation that we are counting on to carry us out of the depression. So long as they are directed by loyal hearts and clear heads they should be left as unfettered and unburdened as possible.

Indeed one of the most important tasks facing the American people is that of vigorously promoting legitimate business, while at the same time seeing to it that, as a result of these efforts, no doors of opportunity are opened to unregulated monopoly, predatory finance or other forms of profiteering. As business now is conducted, regulation by such agencies as the Federal Trade Commission, the Interstate Commerce Commission and the Federal Reserve Board, undeniably is a highly necessary governmental function. Those negative critics who content themselves with denouncing such "government interference" as an evil, without proposing any better way of protecting legitimate business and the consuming public from the vastly greater evils of "unfair competition" and other illegitimate business practices, are either uninformed, insincere or worse.

It is strongly inferred by these advocates of unregulated speculative and predatory business that without the possibility of unearned winnings or loot always dangling before their eyes, business man cannot be made to function effectively. Is it true that a majority of our industrial leaders are unwilling to employ their capital, brains and energy for a fair and reasonable reward? Is not the inference untrue and libelous that they can be spurred to activity only by the prospect of immoderate and immoral profits; that they are primarily not producers but gamblers and grabbers to whom business is a huge game of chance, a carnival of speculation and loot? Indeed is not business fundamentally one of the greatest and noblest of the creative activities of man, rather than a twilight zone of intrigue and spoliation?

Of recent years there has been an instinctive groping toward a higher plane of business activity on the part of hundreds of industrial leaders. 

They have sensed the hidden element of weakness and potential catastrophe in present-day trends, without being able to see what they are in search of because apparently they were not willing to pay the price of

creative vision in sacrifice and self-discipline. For example, there are literally hundreds of great industrial enterprises in this country, such as the B. & O. Railroad, the American Cast Iron Pipe Co., the Standard Oil group, the Steel group, the International Harvester Co., and other colossal conservative groups that for years have been trying out various methods of employee participation in profits or management or both. 

Employees generally have regarded these new methods as mere "sops" or devices for bolstering up a shaky economic system because, up to date such innovations, instead of being based upon some constructive economic or ethical principle like that of cooperation or stewardship, have been dependent upon the caprice or selfish interest of capital. Moreover these promising experiments are open to criticism from still another angle. Even if they were endorsed by organized labor they could be counted on eventually to arouse in the minds of the consuming public a lively suspicion that they constituted a species of conspiracy on the part of labor and capital to levy tribute on the ultimate consumer for their mutual enrichment.

However, if capital should once become willing to share its profits, above a fair minimum percentage, with both employees and patrons, a new type of business corporation would thus be created which, by combining the cooperative principle with the most successful business methods, would be able to steer a safe course between the Scylla of inefficient management, on which so many cooperative enterprises have been wrecked, and the Charybdis of present-day capitalistic class war, with its attendant strikes, sabotage, economic depressions and threatened economic chaos. Nothing would seem to be more apparent than that in our national economic set-up, as the emphasis becomes shifted from speculative and predatory efficiency to productive efficiency and distributive fair play in the interest alike of the individual, the Commonwealth and the common good-wealth, security and human happiness will increase by leaps and bounds.

Obviously such a system would tend to eliminate both profiteering and economic tyranny, the two most obnoxious features of present-day industrialism. And we must not overlook the fact that no elaborate

changes in wage scales, no lavish expenditures on welfare work and no bonus schemes that do not involve these basic readjustments will be at all adequate in the present crisis. There are times when palliatives are worse than futile; when they irritate and exasperate. We are living in such times. Nothing in the way of reform that does not measure up to the requirements of man's inherent and universal sense of fair play with regard to these two basic features, can do anything but add fuel to the smouldering fires of nation-wide discontent. Every intelligent man knows that the rich extremist is more dangerous than the poor one; that Bolshevism is a reaction from Czarism, Kaiserism and Bourbonism; that the determination to get by might what cannot be obtained by right, in every age has been learned by the weak from the strong, and has culminated in the red rabies of revolution only when tyranny has become a habit and injustice has been exalted to the dignity of a vested interest and a national institution. 

However, if to-day, as in past national emergencies, a majority of our business men show themselves to be conservative by instinct, hardheaded by training and spiritually alive and alert, the present crisis will prove to be both an awakener and an opportunity. But if by any chance they fail to measure up to this patriotic challenge, more radical and less reliable leaders inevitably can be counted on to forge to the front, and the responsibility for what follows will be on the shoulders of those who have shirked an unmistakable duty in an unparalleled crisis. Obviously the only "safe and sane" policy for business leaders at the present time is to line up the business world in the interest of fair play, based on sound business methods and standards, as against the get-rich-quick, get-poor-quicker, methods of the business adventurer. 

During the Third Liberty Loan Drive, at Youngstown, Ohio, I was taken through one of their great steel mills. With keenest interest I watched each step in the process of steel making, until finally I saw the forms removed and red hot steel ingots standing before me-beautiful as flame and weighing I knew not how many tons. I well remember how thrilled I was when told that, while polished and hard on the surface, those glowing shafts, on the inside, were still molten. All

during the war and during the even more difficult days of moral confusion, after the war, I took those great steel ingots as emblems of our American business men- —on the surface polished and hard but inside molten with the fires of latent patriotism and spiritual purpose, only awaiting a great occasion and a great leader—to flare forth with dynamic power!

It is difficult to understand why so small a percentage of business men seem to realize that their economic interests are diametrically opposed to those of profiteers and financial racketeers whose unfair gains come chiefly from corruptly secured monopoly privileges, crooked financing, and unfair competition. The great mass of legitimate business men run the risk of losing one year what they made the year before or of having one generation lose what was made and saved by the preceding generation. But those whose profits come from some form of special privilege, do not run the same risks. Like armor-clad knights in the Middle Ages they are safe from the deadliest dangers that menace and destroy their less well protected fellow soldiers of fortune. 

Senator Borah recently has said some eloquent and challenging things about the dangers inherent in unregulated monopoly. But so far as I am aware no one has diagnosed our unsound and unjust financial system in such a way as to put the business men of America properly on their guard against the present sinister monopolistic control of money and credit by a small group of financiers. This group has been authorized to exercise, for private profit, the governmental prerogative of issuing money and the dangerous autocratic power of arbitrarily extending or withholding credit. As a result a small coterie of financiers has come to dominate nearly every important economic function and activity of our national life. Unless the more intelligent business men, professional men, laboring men and farmers can unite on a common platform and under a common leadership, to break the strangle hold of this financial near-dictatorship, our individualistic civilization is doomed. 

In working out the problem of financing the proposed social security program as a means of augmenting the purchasing power of our poorest classes, the vitally important fact should not be overlooked that a con-

sumption or sales tax is only paid once whereas a production tax, unless by some strange combination of circumstances it cannot be passed on by the producer, also is paid by the jobber, by the wholesaler, by the retailer and finally by the ultimate consumer. During this process almost invariably a profit is tacked on at each transfer, so that finally it is pyramided into a much larger total than the original tax received by the government. In this case the ultimate consumer is the victim who pays not only the tax but also the additional charges levied by all those through whose hands the article has passed. If in certain cases, the production tax can not be added by the producer to the price of the product, it may turn out to be the proverbial last straw that breaks the profit cycle, puts the enterprise in the "red" and stops the producer from functioning. In which case it will become a fatal drag on business at the very moment when national recovery depends largely upon our ability to encourage and stimulate business. 

Not only must we stop laying new tax burdens on our confused and discouraged business men but we must do our utmost to relieve them, as well as the farmers, of any unfair and unnecessary tax burdens they now are carrying. For example a policy that would effect a tax reduction of several hundred million dollars per annum in our state and local taxes would be that of consolidating our counties into one-fourth or one-fifth of their present number and having the enlarged counties managed by county managers, each with a competent staff of employees. 

Such a businesslike reform, once it had been worked out on an efficiency basis, doubtless could be counted on to cut in half our state and local taxes, other than those for education. 

Possibly ways can be devised for raising a part of the cost of this incredibly profitable security program by new taxes on nonproductive wealth. Indeed when the depression is over and the well-to-do classes again are receiving normal incomes, perhaps most or all of it can and should be thus raised. But I am convinced that during the present crisis a major portion of it will have to be raised by a consumption or sales tax similar to, but far less burdensome than, the taxes proposed in the Townsend plan and the Social Credit plan. The strongest objection to

such a tax always has been that it is a tax on the poor as well as on the rich. But what taxes are not finally paid by the ultimate consumer? 

Moreover, the fact that the advocates of the Townsend "transactions taxes" are largely recruited from the laboring classes who would have to pay those taxes without directly benefiting from them, indicates that the much lighter tax here proposed would arouse little serious opposition from those classes. Indeed is it not highly probable that as soon as the backers of the Townsend plan come to realize how much better it would be to increase the buying power of all dependent classes, instead of just that of the aged, they will switch their allegiance to the security program having its benefits most widely and most fairly distributed. 

Let none of us imagine however that the American people can ever be satisfied with the achievement of mere security. The ancestors of most of us came here because they believed that on this new continent OPPORTUNITY was written across the skyline—offering to the oppressed of every land a career open to talent, industry and character. And while our first duty undoubtedly is the urgent, emergency task of saving our people from hunger and cold, bankruptcy and despair, our second and more lasting obligation is that of once more opening wide the gates of opportunity to the children of even the humblest of our citizens. Until that has been done the American spirit and genius will not again be able to function freely and creatively. 

We have been warned vociferously against a "Challenge to Liberty" by those who seem to be perturbed because this generation has been called upon to exchange the restraints of a comparatively simple agricultural and industrial system for those of a more complex economic organism that finds it necessary to "shackle cunning as we already have shackled force." Still others are apprehensive and bitter because they imagine that they see in the trend of recent Federal legislation a challenge to our rapidly disintegrating capitalistic system. Apparently they are unaware of the fact that the old individualism founded on greed and grab is gone, never to return, and that henceforth no individualism will be tolerated that does not safeguard the rights, the initiative and the welfare of the weak as well as of the strong.

Mr. Average Voter would be more impressed by the arguments of these administration critics, if he felt that they represented the sincere efforts of disinterested patriots to defend those vital spiritual realities for which the words Liberty and Individualism really stand-deathless principles for which our forefathers fought and died and which our generation likewise stands ready, if need be, to defend with our lives. 

But when the Liberty, so ostentatiously defended by these professional patrioteers consists of an economic system which under Harding, Coolidge and Hoover was in process of destroying the individual initiative of six million farmers and transforming them into financial serfs, growing the nation's foods and textiles at less than the cost of production, and when in our industrial centers this so-called liberty frequently degenerates into a license to exploit without let or hindrance the lives and labor of men, women and children, fair-minded citizens are more apt to be shocked by their sacrilege than stirred by their fervor. 

It is incredible that so many reactionaries should blindly refuse to see that the old individualism of greed, is dying before our eyes. It is equally amazing that so many radicals refuse to see that this dying individualism of greed is going to be replaced, not by an exotic and despotic communism or fascism, based on resentment, fear and class hate but by a new individualism, instinct with the American spirit and based on the Christian, creative principles of solidarity and stewardship.

CHAPTER V  

Present conditions indicate that America is fast approaching a showdown, that in the near future we shall see a definite turn, either toward a permanent recovery or toward a more disastrous collapse. Until it becomes apparent that we are reducing unemployment on an ever increasing scale, while at the same time making similar headway at balancing the budget, the signs of economic convalescence so manifest on every side, cannot be regarded as proofs of a permanent return of economic health. In other words, until we succeed in diminishing at one and the same time the symptoms of economic disease and the amount of credit dope we are pouring into our system, it is impossible to say which of our improved business conditions are vital and permanent and which are artificial and temporary. During this period of peril and uncertainty every consideration of expediency unites with every patriotic impulse to warn us, each and all, to stop "playing politics," to stop playing the short sighted, selfish games of creditors, capitalists, veterans, farmers or wage earners, to stop "playing possum" which is what most of us are doing and to enlist for the duration of a crisis that is worse than war, in a supreme national effort to achieve a permanent prosperity based upon a square deal. 

We face this crisis divided into many political groups-Constructive Conservatives, Progressives, Radicals and Reactionaries; Watchful 

Waiters, New Dealers and squealers; Advocates of a New Order and 

Beneficiaries of the Old Disorder; Socialists, Communists, Technocrats,

Townsendites, Coughlinites, Farmer-Laborites, The Army of the Unemployed and the Army of the Unemployable. Pathetically few of us are qualified to participate constructively in the spiritual renaissance that alone can save and transform civilization. 58

Unless this nation is able, in the near future, to exorcise the devils of depression, it will find our present difficulties and dangers becoming every day more menacing. Judging by recent happenings in Europe and New Zealand, unless some new and constructive factor is brought into the American situation, the next administration is apt to become revolutionary whether it be labeled Democratic or Republican. How revolutionary, apparently will depend upon how undernourished, badly housed, ill-clad and exasperated the masses of our people have become. 

Not to recognize these facts is to ignore the teaching of every European upheaval since the World War. Several of them have been non-Communist but all of them have been revolutionary, in that they have been subversive of both the so-called capitalist system and of popular government. Indeed, without firm and skillful leadership on the part of our Chief Executive, backed by an alert, intelligent and organized public opinion, Congress at any time may switch from Democratic to Radical or from Republican to Fascist almost overnight as a result, not of an election but of a campaign of high pressure lobbying and a flood of letters and telegrams from some aggressive, highly organized revolutionary minority.

In view of these facts, manifestly the immediate duty of sane Americans, regardless of party, is to do their utmost to help the President, as long as he is President, in his superhuman task of achieving a diffused and unshakable national prosperity in the welter of world chaos that threatens to engulf civilization. This can best be done by supporting all Administration policies that are sound and adequate, while at the same time advocating vigorously such other policies and leadership as are felt would be more adequate to the nation's needs. Such a nonpartizan, patriotic policy should be easy for Republicans to adopt, inasmuch as President Roosevelt has gone a long way toward making his Administration a Coalition Government. He has Republicans in his 

Cabinet and a number of Republicans hold some of the highest positions in various New Deal Organizations, while a large number of Republicans hold responsible positions in all the Federal relief and other emergency agencies.

The civilized world has admired the President's restraint in refusing to bolster up the confidence of the electorate with glittering but unfulfillable promises. The extraordinary effectiveness of his radio talks has been due largely to his custom of telling the people, not what he is going to do but what he has done. After years of procrastination by three preceding administrations, while civilization was cracking and crumbling from base to dome, a dazed and disillusioned nation was heartened to see constructive emergency policies, apparently as powerful as the destructive forces to be overcome, rapidly being crystallized and put into operation. Most of our people realized that in a crisis like the one President Roosevelt inherited from his predecessors, risks had to be assumed and experiments had to be launched, if wholesale and imminent disaster was to be averted. And while during this transitional period of suffering and confusion, it was inevitable that certain elements should be led astray by the Utopian schemes of demagogues and mirage mongers, the nation is to be congratulated that from the start an overwhelming majority of the electorate has been, heart and soul, behind the President in his courageous efforts to steer the ship of state midway between the hidden reefs of Fascism and Communism. However, in spite of these well-known facts, today, after three years of nation-wide, rapid-fire emergency operations, most of which have been successful, an increasing number of people are finding it impossible any longer to be satisfied with the assurance that we are "on the way” and earnestly and even insistently are demanding to know where we are going. Moreover, they are anxious to hear from the President not only what are our next transitional goals but also what is the major objective that he and we are aiming at. "Without a vision the people perish." Our people are hungering and thirsting, not for irridescent mirages such as have led astray half the peoples of Europe but for a clear-eyed vision of the higher and happier civilization, which they instinctively realize is waiting just over the horizon, for the generation that can muster enough character, intelligence and Faith to create and operate it. 

I am far from intimating that to President Roosevelt or to anyone else has been or ever will be given a vision of the detailed plans and

specifications of the ideal future state. It seems highly improbable that any man again will be permitted to descend from Sinai with the laws and institutions needed by his own and future generations minutely worked out and inscribed on tablets of stone. And yet if, in the words of John Stuart Mill, "all political revolutions, not effected by foreign conquest, originate in moral revolutions," then is the work of the prophet or spiritual architect more creative and vital than that of any legislator or executive ruler. Indeed may it not be truthfully said that the world counts its steps up the difficult steeps of progress, not by the number it can boast of astute political strategists or of men of blood and iron, but by the number of creative leaders it has been vouchsafed? 

Lesser men can be found to formulate and carry out ingenious "policies of realization," to translate into laws and institutions the architectonic visions of the social seer-their work is chiefly selective and adaptive, his alone is creative and fundamental. 

As a next transitional step is it not the manifest duty of those Republicans and Democrats who are aiming at the same general objectives as the President but who differ from him as to political or economic methods, to create a liberal Coalition second line of political defense to ⚫ function vigorously and constructively in place of the present opposition party which a reactionary leadership and platform have hopelessly discredited and weakened. Such a Liberal Opposition Coalition should stand for popular government, free speech, a free press and policies of economic reconstruction that not only are sufficiently drastic to meet the present emergency but that also are sufficiently fundamental and constructive to constitute the beginnings of a prosperity that will be more permanent because more widely diffused than any we have had in the past. The fact that both of the two great parties have reactionary wings which usually are in control of the party machinery and frequently dictate both platforms and candidates, and the further fact that the present Democratic leadership has not yet clearly explained, even to its supporters, its ultimate objective or the steps by which it expects to attain that objective, tends to make the present liberal Democratic leadership too purely personal to give a satisfactory assurance of stability and perma-

nance. Therefore until there has been created another liberal party or Coalition to serve as a political spare tire, the way will be left open, in case of a sudden shift in political sentiment, for a revolutionary political upheaval, planned, incited and led by some highly organized Communist or Fascist minority. The strength developed for the half truth embodied in the Townsend plan and for the efforts of the late Huey Long to array class against class, no longer can be ignored by intelligent and patriotic citizens. The responses evoked by these appeals to emotionalism and unreason demonstrate that the patience of untold millions of our people is perilously near the breaking point. 

Here the queries naturally suggest themselves: Why divide the liberal forces of the country into two opposing Coalition parties? 

Would they not be enoromously more effective fighting under the same banner? I am not proposing such a division; I am merely recognizing that it already exists. There can be no doubt that logically the liberals of both parties should be battling shoulder to shoulder. But it is no less certain that a majority of them in both parties psychologically are still under the spell of meaningless partizan labels and prejudices each partizan group fatuously waiting for the other to see the light and join its ranks. As soon as both liberal Democrats and liberal Republicans. decide to subordinate partizanship to patriotism both will come to realize that the creation of a resistless non-partizan Coalition is the inevitable next step in the direction of national salvation. Indeed is not the increasing pressure of events likely to bring about such a merger more quickly than any of us expect? And will not the party that first signifies its willingness to take that next step deserve and receive the enthusiastic endorsement of a grateful electorate?

In direct opposition to the proposed realignment of the forces of orderly progress two portentous extremist movements, like storm clouds, today unmistakably are taking form on the horizon of our national life. 

We literally are being inundated with propaganda from both reactionaries and radicals, each functioning under a variety of more or less misleading labels. The one insistently demands "recovery without reform,"

while the other as insistently demands policies that in effect mean reform without recovery.

The demand that the Administration stop all efforts at reconstruction and devote its energies to bringing about recovery, is like insisting that a physician cure a disease without trying to remove either its cause or the accumulated poison it has generated. In reality it is a plea for relapse instead of recovery-for another shot in the arm, instead of a manifestly necessary surgical operation. In face of this widespread demand it cannot be denied that a certain element in our population is looking, neither for a New Deal, a New Order nor a New Spiritual Leadership, but for a new racket to replace the profitable old rackets that have gone to the scrap heap. By its demands for a "superman" to bring about "recovery without reform," this element apparently is seeking a leadership that can rationalize or camouflage the swine and the tiger in human nature, and bring back the "good old days" when the cunning and the unscrupulous were able to prey with impunity upon the idealistic and the weak. 

Unequally yoked together with these extremists having definite Fascist tendencies, are a more moderate group for whom the phrase "recovery without reform" has an entirely different meaning. This group apparently is solicitous lest certain members of the "brain trust," instead of focusing their energies on the superlatively important, immediate task of finding the quickest, soundest and most permanent possible solutions for crucial national problems, should jeopardize the whole process of necessary reconstruction by using their temporary positions of influence and authority to experiment with grandiose and impractical economic theories. Such critics should make their criticisms specific and should accompany them with constructive proposals. They also should publicly dissociate themselves from the Fascist minded critics of the Administration who so vociferously are advocating recovery by means of a return to the sinister reactionary principles and practices that brought about the present economic collapse. 

The Communist movement in America is older and more widespread in its ramifications than is that of the Fascists. Out of deference

to American economic, political and religious sensibilities, most of its operations, like those of the Fascist movement, are being energetically but clumsily camouflaged. The various organizations, rural, educational, religious and political, through which it carries on its steady work of propaganda, do not agree on any clear-cut positive objective but stand for a variety of disingenuous policies such as, discrediting the good along with the bad in our collapsing capitalistic system; broadcasting fairy stories about fictitious Soviet successes in the realms of government and industry, and promoting unworkable political and economic schemes that, if adopted, would sink the nation in a sea of bankruptcy. These zealots of reform without recovery, apparently have learned nothing from the long drawn out Russian tragedy of fanatical tyranny, nationwide poverty and wholesale starvation.

However, our chief task is not that of discrediting the Communists, or the Fascists. Like the profiteers and plungers of Capitalistic High 

Finance, they have done a thorough enough job of discrediting themselves. The crucial question is, have we anything better-have we the higher truth that can harmonize present antagonisms, reintegrate our rapidly disintegrating civilization, enable us to salvage what remains of the rich social inheritance of the past and realize for ourselves and our children the immeasurably richer possibilities of the future? The present cataclysmic crisis is nothing less than a last call from Nature and from Nature's God, for the only possible remedial action that can stop the secretion and get rid of past accumulations of deadly moral poison in the vital organs of our national life. The future of America depends upon our willingness to submit to a thorough spiritual and economic diagnosis and to stand the gaff of whatever individual "soul surgery" and national reconstruction are necessary to effect a cure. 

While certain reactionary political groups take the position that the evils of our capitalistic civilization are neither fatal nor curable, practically all the advocates of "revolution by force" have gained a strong tactical advantage by recognizing that if present pathological conditions are allowed to go from bad to worse they are certain to prove fatal but that if dealt with resolutely they can be cured. Indeed these dynamic

truths constitute the fulcrum with which they have pried loose, in the mass consciousness of Europe, mountains of inertia, moral indifference and bovine stolidity and transformed them into veritable political avalanches that have wrecked economic systems, dethroned rulers and turned nations upside down.

Unquestionably these misguided extremists have rendered real service by broadcasting these crucially important facts. But when they proceed to point us respectively to Russia, Italy, Germany or Utopia for the cure, the instinctive reaction of the American people coincides with their considered judgment in recognizing that none of these melodramatic and incredibly costly experiments yet show any signs of succeeding. As long as Stalin, Mussolini and Hitler can prolong the present transitional era, by keeping the enthusiasm of their militant followers up to the point of living largely on prospects and promises, their creaking, top-heavy systems may endure. But eventually their efforts to achieve great spiritual objectives by purely material, human, and sometimes desperately inhuman means, must fail. They have overlooked the obvious fact that it is as impossible to make a successful socialized economic system out of antisocial individuals, or a prosperous and warless world out of parasitic and predatory people as it is to make a perfume out of onions and asafetida. For while justice and brotherly love can be talked about glibly by self-centered, self-willed egotists, those great spiritual realities can only be manifested in and through men and women who are dominated by them. "Only he who has can give.” 

Millions of Americans are wondering what are the first sure steps toward recovery. Until we recognize that there is no easy way out of the present cataclysm-that political pink pills and financial dope will not do the job-we ourselves are a part of the disease rather than of the cure. Until we realize that our breakdown is too deep-seated to be remedied by patchwork reforms, we are going to continue to refuse to make those costly sacrifices which all classes must make if this crisis is not to end in wholesale tragedy. Indeed, until as a nation, we have invoked the principle of "equality of sacrifice" which did so much to get Australia started on the road to recovery, we have not even begun to

lay the foundations of a bigger, better and more permanent prosperity. "Equality of sacrifice" means that every class must be willing, or must be compelled, to sacrifice to the public welfare, not only every unfair advantage but also such of its normal privileges and perquisites as temporarily are incompatible with the public welfare. In other words, it means nothing less than "pledging our lives, our fortunes, and our sacred honor" to the achievement of victory over the depression. But unless such a pledge means vastly more than most of us so far have realized, its effect will be both superficial and ephemeral. Edith Cavel, in a flash of insight before she faced the firing squad, caught a vision of the great truth that "Patriotism is not enough." Millions of young lives sacrificed in the World War, not as we first dreamed, to establish a New Order, but as we now realize-a blood sacrifice to the old disorder-point to the tragic fact that human heroism, without limit and at its best, is not enough. If civilization is to be saved, a wisdom higher than man's highest and a power more creative than that generated by a willingness to die for country, conscience or "cause" must be evoked. 

All down the ages a transforming spiritual power has enabled selfish human beings to be changed and used as spiritual "leaven in the lump" of a sordid, so-called civilization. Two outstanding modern examples of this have been the Quaker and Wesleyan movements. The former has been a quickening force in all the politico-spiritual movements of the recent past, like that for World Peace, while the latter, in the 18th century, was the most potent single factor in averting an English revolution by force and in bringing about a bloodless and constitutional evolution of the British Empire into a far-flung union of democratic states. 

During the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries the souls of the millions were enchanted and led captive by the ideal of human liberty. 

Generation after generation poured out their treasure and their blood to realize for themselves and their children this enthralling vision. What we of this generation are seeking is a spiritual experience that will release in the souls of present day men and women even more resistless energies than did the ideal of human liberty in the breasts of our forebears. Recognizing that "politics is the science of the possible" we are

looking for some body of men who can generate in present day leaders and voters at least as dynamic a motive power in behalf of right, justice and brotherhood as machine politicians in our great cities and present day European dictators are able to generate by appealing to the caveman instincts of greed, fear, hate and personal and national vanity. 

For many years I sought eagerly not only in America but throughout Europe for a political party, a reform movement, a labor union, a church or any other organization that was able to make good men and women get up as early, stay up as late, work or fight as hard and get as much kick out of life as do the denizens of the underworld, the crafty cohorts of grafting politicians, the fanatical apostles of Communism or the no less fanatical soldiers of fortune in the world of high finance. 

It was obvious that spiritually vital individuals are to be found in all religious denominations and political parties but I was unable to discover a spiritually dynamic movement, that seemed adequate to the present crisis, until I ran into that modern expression of First Century Christianity known as the Oxford Groups. This army of "spiritual revolutionaries" not only is helping to change individuals in forty different countries but is bringing about nation-wide spiritual awakenings in Great Britain, Canada, South Africa, Switzerland, Norway and Denmark. Indeed it appears to be the only constructive force in the world today that is growing faster than Communism. 

From twenty centuries of human experience, it is possible to say that the Christian way of life is not a mere ideal conjured up by the creative imagination of its Founder but a practical possibility for us all. 

There are today enough people actually living on this basis to demonstrate its entire practicability. When real and vital it not only works, but works miracles in individual lives, in family life, in business activities, in politics, and even in that last refuge and stronghold of the unregenerate self, a standardized and ossified religion. I am confident most people will agree that anything which actually gives individuals victory over personal spiritual defeats, that enables families to be "happy

at home," that enables business men and politicians to be successful though honest and that enables nations to defend their legitimate objectives primarily by the power of Right, instead of by cunning or material might, is what the world needs most.

In order to arouse this contagious and invincible spirit, a deeper appeal must be made than that involved in the logical but uninspiring argument that, in such a crisis, patriotic sacrifice is the soundest investment and unselfishness is the only intelligent self-interest. Without a spiritual leadership that can evoke the deeper and more potent energies of the soul, we never shall be able to defeat and replace with a vital constructive power those destructive, partizan and bipartizan organizations of profiteers, racketeers, grafters and credit vampires that not only are preying on all the productive classes of the nation but long have been undermining the foundations of civilization. 

Is such a spiritual leadership going to be offered to the American people before it is too late and if so, will we, as individuals, have enough spiritual discernment to recognize its incalculable value and enough patriotic self-sacrifice to follow it, regardless of cost, to victory? Upon our answers to these questions depends the future of America and of western civilization.

Most radicals seem to realize keenly the need of a new spirit or motive in the nation but are all too apt to limit it to the economic field and to see it as a need, not of all of us including themselves, but chiefly of "the rich." For example, they say, "Ever since the World War, the wealthy classes of Great Britain have paid an income tax of 80%-why are not ours required to do the same?" This query, while pertinent as far as it goes, fails to take into account a deeper and more subtle aspect of the problem. Until as in Australia all classes, even the poorest, are willing to do not only their bit but their utmost to "pull their weight" in the Ark of our national safety, we have not yet fanned into a flame that unconquerable, sacrificial spirit which alone can carry us to victory. The principle behind the British Graduated Income Tax is sound. In a time like this, our wealthiest citizens should count it a privilege to be able to pay as taxes a higher percentage of their incomes than do or can the

less prosperous classes. But that is not the whole truth, in a time of supreme crisis the principle brought out in the story of the widow's mite also must be invoked. This widow, out of her poverty, rose to heights of loyalty and sacrifice that the rich rarely if ever attain. Undoubtedly 

America can win this fight without the negligible amounts of money supplied by the widow and the orphan but it is equally certain that we never can hope to win it without the sublime sacrificial spirit which manifests itself most strikingly in the lives, labors and devotion of that most patriotic class in the nation, the self-respecting poor. 

Moreover, in a crisis like this anyone but a professional politician ought to be able to realize the imperative necessity of sacrificing partizanship as a necessary preliminary to adequate, national, remedial action. 

Manifestly the present collapse is too stupendous for any political party to handle alone. This fact was discovered several years ago in England where the classes that had most to lose, took the initiative in temporarily scrapping their traditional party system and creating a Coalition or "National" Government which for several years has served as a species of political receivership. According to a member of that Coalition Government this was not primarily a political act but a spiritual achievement and one that has been productive of invaluable practical economic results. The sacrifice of the gold standard likewise was a British spiritual achievement that has been fruitful of invaluable economic results. 

Indeed as a result of these major policies and others of less heroic proportions, such as the conversion of a large block of the national debt at a lower rate of interest, the British Empire has been saved from bankruptcy and started on the highroad to recovery. Partly as a consequence of these policies, during the past two years Great Britain has been the only great nation in the world that instead of raising taxes, has been reducing them. However, these splendid national achievements are only a start. Apparently before the British nation can go much farther on the road to basic recovery, the sacrifice of partizanship and financial superstition will have to be made more complete while pride, greed, sentimentalism, materialism and similar basic causes of present abnormal economic conditions will have to be exchanged for a new national spirit

or motive. Nothing seems clearer than that unless this is done, further progress toward a greater and more permanent prosperity in Great Britain is bound to be slowed up and ultimately stopped. 

Another vital step in the direction of recovery in the United States will be the underwriting by national leaders of victory over the "depression." In this hour of supreme peril, no leader or party without 

Faith in the nation's ability, under God, to gain that victory, should or will receive serious consideration from a dazed but determined nation. 

It was by instilling his own faith and courage into our people that President Roosevelt won his first crucial battle with national financial collapse.

After these necessary preliminary steps have been taken, we must face the practical question, in what new economic methods will the new national motive or spirit express itself? The old individualism, based on greed, is disintergrating before our eyes. Are a materialistic Communism and a soulless Fascism its only possible alternatives or can some new master principle like that of Christian trusteeship or stewardship be invoked to revitalize and reconstruct our economic life? Stewardship, while the antithesis of selfishness, in no sense is synonymous with the sentimental altruism of the impractical theorist. Moreover, it applies not only to money but as well to intelligence, influence, time, energy and everything we have and are. Contrary to the credo of the one-idead enthusiast, it can be expressed not only in terms of voluntary cooperation or socialized effort by community, state or nation but also in terms of individual initiative. In other words it stands, not for a method but for a "quality of life”—a life that transforms whatever it touches and polarizes it with a resistless urge toward the highest spiritual ends. The socialist who condemns individualism, root and branch, because of the manifest crudities and cruelties in our present individualistic system is attaching chief importance to a method rather than to the spirit or motive behind the method. In like manner, the individualist who opposes even a moderate degree of socialization, because of the graft and bureaucratic inefficiency which be believes to be inherent in governmental activities, makes the same mistake of attaching chief im-

portance to a method rather than to the motivating quality of life expressing itself in and through that method. 

The truth is that neither Christian individualism nor a God-guided socialization ever has had a fair trial. Probably neither one will become the sole instrument of man's future progress. It seems more probable that some day the best features of both will be expressed in a Christian trusteeship that will create an economic system more splendid than any dreamed of by the most ardent apostles of a materialistic socialism, of the quasi-anarchy of extreme individaulism or of an anti-Christian Communism based on hate, resentment and terrorism and limited in its efficiency by the murky intelligence and warped character of selfcentered, self-indulgent and self-important autocrats and bureaucrats. 

Indeed, all man-made Utopian systems are the pathetic products of futile human efforts to do by changes of method what can only be done by inner spiritual change. As Dean Inge says, "No political alchemy will produce golden conduct out of leaden instincts." After a life-long study of capitalism, socialism, communism, syndicalism, the single tax and trade unionism, I have been forced to the conclusion that basically there are only two kinds of government and only two kinds of economic systems-God-guided and misguided. Human wisdom "unillumined by a spark" never yet has made a real success of an individual life, of an economic system or of a government. 

Fortunately, this does not imply that victory over the present depression must be postponed until a majority of our voters have been transformed spiritually. From the moment we, as a nation, are able to find and are willing to follow a God-guided leadership, victory is assured. The Almighty's promise to spare Sodom if ten righteous men could be found in the city and Christ's emphasis on the leaven that leavened the entire mass of meal, both unmistakably indicate that, at the start, the Almighty's majorities are spiritual rather than numerical and that genuine Christianity stands, first of all, not for a quantitative movement but for a contagious and resistless quality of life. 

While waiting for the necessary constructive spiritual leadership to develop throughout the nation what is the duty of plain patriotic citizens? ?

One thing is clear. Before we can hope to see through the fog banks of half-baked thinking that are rolling in from every side, or the smoke screens of sordid and fanatical propaganda that are being belched forth from a thousand subsidized sources and before we shall be able to recognize adequate leadership when it is offered, most of us, church members and pagans alike, will have to undergo a spiritual purging that will involve the sacrifice of partizanship, greed, fear, moral indifference and other similar taints of the soul. When we thus have gained a sufficiently impersonal or national point of view to be able to work and vote and spend our money, not as capitalists, farmers, veterans or wage earners nor as Democrats or Republicans but as stewards or trustees of our wealth, ability, votes and influence, a new and dynamic quality of life will begin to manifest in and through us and we shall find ourselves being energized and utilized by a resistless Power that works for those "far-off Divine events toward which the whole creation moves." 

To those who are not accustomed to take into account the spiritual factors of life, all this may sound hazy, indefinite and beside the mark. 

As I have proposed neither a new party nor a new fool-proof system, they may find it difficult to decide just what I am driving at. Nevertheless, if one is looking, not for a nostrum but for a cure, my proposals are simple and easy to understand. I have not proposed the abolition of individualism but the transforming of an individualism denatured by greed, fear and tyranny, into an individualism shot through with spiritual purpose and power. I am proposing the abolition, not of the profit system but of a self-destructive system of profiteering or extortion. 

I have not opposed a reasonable degree of socialization because a Godguided socialization would not only supplement but also foster a higher form of individualism than that which the world has known on the half savage plane of gouge and grab. 

As an essential preliminary I have suggested the sacrifice of partizanship to patriotism, by the creation of a constructive non-partizan coalition to mobilize the spiritual resources of the nation for the stupendous task of reconstructing our national life on a higher, happier and more prosperous level. As definite constructive proposals, I have suggested

the adventure of faith involved in underwriting victory over the depression and have indicated that this major objective can only be attained by invoking the principle of "equality of sacrifice." I also have declared that if as a nation we are to function creatively, stewardship must be substituted for greed or personal ambition as the dominant spirit and motive power of our national life. 

What, in a nut shell, do these suggestions involve and point to?

They involve nothing less than a spiritual renaissance and they point to nothing less than the coming of the Kingdom of God on earth. 

Strangely enough, this spiritual renaissance which is no longer an irridescent dream but a rapidly growing present day reality, arouses no enthusiasm among advocates of "revolution by force" nor fear among those who would maintain the old order by force. And yet it involves profounder changes than any other revolution so far attempted or proposed. Tinkering with economic or political methods and systems is child's play compared with the deep inner changes in individuals and the stupendous national and world transformations that inevitably will result from the changes in spirit and motive involved in the present unmistakable beginnings of a world-wide spiritual awakening. 

We are faced with two alternatives, that of co-operating with Deity in the creative task of salvaging what is good in the crumbling heritage of the past and working it into a higher civilization or, that of allowing the evil in the old order to disintegrate and destroy the good along with the bad. This generation, by the costly path of wholesale suffering, is merely rediscovering the world-old truth, frequently ignored but never evaded that the wages of sin, whether individual or national, are misery, degeneration and death. This tragic truth is written in the blood and ashes of every previous civilization. It applies not only to the crimes and vices of the "submerged tenth" but as well to the transgressions and derelictions of the elite and the alert, both inside and outside the church. Indeed the sins that destroy civilizations are not primarily the grimy, sordid sins of the underworld but the wellthought out, well-executed and temporarily successful wrong-doings and craven compromises of the so-called "better elements of society."

When individuals and nations learn that a civilization built on greed is a civilization built on a volcano, they will have made a start in the right direction—"The fear of God is the beginning of wisdom.” But not until they have learned the additional truths, on which the creative principle of stewardship rests, that "He that loseth his life shall find it" -will they be able to recreate civilization on a higher, happier, more permanent and more prosperous basis. The nominal Christian who sheathes the "sword of the spirit" in a slothful and cowardly ease while ruthless financial autocrats dominate business, partizan and bipartizan bosses control politics, and theorists, denominationalists and ritualists rule the religious world,-is a caricature not a Christian. 

It is important to realize that in the present set-up, certain people occupy key positions that carry with them tremendous responsibilities and opportunities for service. For example, who can doubt that nationwide political and economic near-miracles would result if even a small nucleus of business and professional leaders in each community would surrender their short-sighted and lopsided human wilfulness to the Will of God and begin to live the principle of Christian stewardship? 

One of the first results undoubtedly would be a gradual, bloodless, constructive revolution. But what would be the process? Would these

Christian Crusaders be required to "divide up" their working capital with the unemployed and the unemployable? Or would they not rather be guided to utilize the greater part of it, together with their time, energy, courage and managerial skill, in giving to the unemployed as much employment as possible without dissipating their capital, while at the same time devoting at least a tithe of their incomes to the more fundamental task of helping to finance a militant Crusade in behalf of the Gospel of the Kingdom of God on earth?

As millions of young Americans voluntarily risked their lives to help win the World War and millions more will be expected to do the same in the event of another war, is there any sound reason why a few thousand financially successful Americans, who are too old for active military service, should not volunteer to take whatever financial risks are involved in participating in the greatest creative adventure the world

ever saw, the God-like adventure of rebuilding civilization on a basis of Faith, courage and stewardship?

Here the practical question arises, suppose a minority of our business leaders were to begin to run their enterprises on this new basis how long would they be able to compete with the "labor skinners," "tax dodgers," fake advertisers, dealers in shoddy goods and all the other unscrupulous adepts at "unfair competition?" In dealing with this problem we should recognize that the intuitive guidance, which comes with faith and the spirit of stewardship, would make a business manager not less but more efficient, and at the same time would tend to increase the efficiency of his entire organization. However, if in spite of such advantages these spiritual pioneers should find it impracticable to introduce certain more humane and ethical methods until their competitors did likewise, it would become their duty to cooperate with all other good citizens, in so "changing the system" as to make these beneficent economic changes possible to the few by making them compulsory for all. 

If in this process it should be found desirable to socialize some natural monopoly, such as a railroad or a telegraph system, the spirit of stewardship would be as necessary an attribute of its government appointed manager as it would be of the manager of a private enterprise. Indeed we never can hope to save civilization by "socializing the system" unless we also are able to imbue with the spirit of fair play and patriotic self-sacrifice the men who are to operate that system. But when a majority of our citizens begin to put right above supposed expediency, we no longer will find any insuperable political difficulties or insoluble economic problems blocking our progress. A nation composed of men and women anxious to give an honest equivalent for what they get, always can and always will find ways of doing so. 

As practical proposals, I have suggested a financial system that will mobilize and utilize the financial resources of the nation for the benefit of the nation as a whole, instead of for the enrichment of a small, privileged class of credit manipulators. But obviously this can not be done until, as a nation, we have had a spiritual renaissance that

I have suggested that the farmers† be mobilized, under the best obtainable national and local leadership, to carry out the policy of sowing approximately one-seventh of their arable land in nitrogen gathering crops in order to conserve soil fertility and prevent the production of unprofitable surpluses. If this policy is properly presented to the farmers, it is inconceivable that they should insist on being paid rent by the government permanently for this one-seventh of their land as the price of their cooperation in carrying out a great patriotic project, of which they will be the only direct beneficiaries and from which they also will benefit indirectly, as much as will any other class. To be sure, it will be far easier to persuade them to give up their present subsidies, if at the same time a real beginning can be made at putting an end to the special privileges of all other classes. 

I have suggested an enlarged security program to be financed chiefly by a consumption tax. But unless a majority of the ultimate consumers are willing to pay such a tax, in order to achieve the dual objective of caring for our needy classes and reducing unemployment by closing the fatal gap between a subnormal consumption and our rapidly augmenting powers of production, their shortsighted selfishness will prevent us from thus achieving a permanent and ever-increasing national prosperity. 

I also have indicated that drastic reductions in local taxes can be made, by consolidating our counties and having them administered by competent county managers as economically and efficiently as possible. 

But this enormous saving of the taxpayers' money cannot be effected unless we can develop a militant public opinion that is stronger than the selfish personal and partizan interests that today uphold our costly and antiquated system of county government.

APPENDIX A HISTORY OF THE FIRST FARM RELIEF BILL* 

The history of the first farm relief bill is a story of economic tragedy and political trickery hard to parallel. In 1921 I was pressed into service by the American Farm Bureau Federation to collect from the American farmers a gift of surplus corn to help feed the starving peoples of Europe. Over seven hundred thousand bushels of corn were contributed, milled and exported to Poland, Czechoslovakia, Austria and other countries where the old, the young, the poor and the sick were suffering from slow starvation otherwise known as undernourishment. The Washington representatives of these stricken countries repeatedly told me that while they were most grateful for this generous gift from the American farmer, it was only a drop in the bucket in comparison with their enormous and desperate needs. They insisted that they could furnish sound security if we would sell them on credit the additional foods and textiles required.

On discussing this problem with leading exporters, bankers, Government officials and farmers, it became apparent that no financial mechanism existed to meet this new international emergency. The farmers were being smothered in their surplus crops and the business stagnation, resulting from their reduced purchasing power, was costing the business men more every month than the total value of those surpluses-while millions of people in Europe were undernourished and under clad for lack of the foods and textiles which on our farms, were being burned for fuel, sold below cost, or allowed to rot in the fields. Accordingly a plan was worked out, modeled largely upon that of the "Foreign Trade Financing Corporation," the organization of which had fallen through,

Credit Men's Association, numerous Chambers of Commerce, Honorable Herbert Hoover, and many other "best minds." These "best minds" all agreed that Europe not only needed our surplus crops, but also had ample security to offer for the necessary credit to finance their sale abroad and that both Europe and America would greatly benefit from such a sale if some financial mechanism could be created to act as the connecting link between our bursting barns and Europe's empty stomachs and shivering backs. It seemed to me then, as it has ever since, that if the plan itself was as practical as the most competent men in the United States had agreed it was, and if private enterprise would not undertake the job-that the Federal Government should do so. 

In the Spring of 1921, with this idea in view, I had drafted and introduced in the U. S. Senate, by the Chairman of the Senate Committee on Agriculture, a bill to dispose of the farmer's surplus crops abroad on well secured credit. The Harding Administration's response to this proposal was, first a smile of incredulity, then a sneer, then relentless opposition. For three months the battle over this plan occupied the center of the national legislative stage. The administration forces first attempted to sidestep the issue by trying to force an adjournment on July 5th. The President went to the Capitol, to urge this adjournment upon members of the Senate, but in spite of the acceptance of this proposal by administration leaders, and even by some prominent opposition leaders, most of the Southern and Western Senators flatly refused to adjourn. By preventing this "masterly retreat," the newly organized "agricultural bloc" gave the country the first concrete demonstration of its power.

After a protracted debate on the bill, in which such large caliber senatorial artillery as Senators Lodge, Edge and Kellogg, led the Administration's opposition to its passage with their accustomed vigor and authority, it became painfully apparent to administration leaders, that

nothing short of some master stroke could possibly head off the increasingly popular demand of the farmers for relief from a situation which rapidly was bringing about their wholesale impoverishment. Again the 

President himself was induced to take a personal hand in the contest. 

It was widely advertised that he was going to cut the Gordian knot by introducing a "compromise bill," which would furnish relief to the farmers with the left hand, and to the railroads with the right, by loaning enough money to the farmers on sound security to tide them over this period of "temporary financial embarrassment," and by loaning the railroads half a billion dollars on a mass of railroad securities of doubtful value. This bill finally was introduced by a leading Western 

Senator in a deprecatory way, as though he had a presentiment of the fact which came out in the next election that by so doing he was cutting his own political throat. A storm of protest greeted the proposal. Farmers everywhere declared that what they needed was not more credit, but markets, a chance to sell their crops for something above the cost of production. For be it remembered that at this time corn was selling in 

Illinois for as low as 38c, in Iowa for 30c, and in states farther West for 20c and even 15c a bushel, whereas according to the best authorities, it had cost farmers more than 50c a bushel to grow it. 

Through the hearts and minds of millions of American farmers surged a feeling of bitterness and resentment that they had "asked for bread and been given a stone." All they were asking was that the Government loan one hundred million dollars as a basis, on which four hundred million dollars' worth of debenture bonds could be issued and sold, in order to create a revolving fund to finance the exportation of the farmers' surplus crops from year to year. 

When, in the course of the debate on the administration's "Compromise Bill," it became apparent that this much-heralded measure probably would be defeated, a member of the Cabinet sent for me and asked what I thought of it. I replied that its plan to advance the farmers temporary emergency loans at a reasonable rate of interest was good as far as it went, but was far from being adequate, as it made no provision to get rid of the farmers' surplus crops by selling them abroad on credit.

"Why," I urged, "will not the Administration accept an honest-to-God compromise bill-incorporating with its domestic credit feature the Export Credit plan, everywhere recognized by the farmers as their chief hope of salvation during the present temporary European food shortage?" As a result of this conference, such a combination or compromise bill was drawn up, and became known as the first Farm Relief Bill. 

It received the support of Administration Senators, conservative Democratic Senators, and of the agricultural bloc and, to the consternation of its enemies and the surprise of its friends, finally on August 4th, passed the Senate by a unanimous vote.

Immediately following this, a gentlemen's agreement was entered into, pledging the leaders of both the House and Senate, to pass the measure through the House as it had passed the Senate. Whereupon a number of the leading champions of the bill left Washington, assured that the battle was won.

They had hardly got out of the city, however, when the enemies of the bill began a secret attack upon it. Instead of being referred to the 

Committee on Agriculture, in the House, as it had been in the Senate, the bill was referred to the House Committee on Banking and Currency. 

This Committee immediately had hearings at which the enemies of the bill were invited to testify and the friends of the bill were not permitted to be heard. As a result of this unprecedented procedure, the Committee, on August 11th, reported the bill back to the House adversely. 

When this became known, the friends of the measure rallied to its defense and it was passed by the House, in Committee of the Whole, by a two to one vote. It later was taken up "in conference" by the duly appointed conferees, three from the Senate, and three from the House. 

Three Senators and the Democratic House Conferee voted to carry out the will of the Congress of the United States as expressed by a unanimous vote of the United States Senate, and a two to one vote of the House, but the two Eastern Republican House conferees brazenly threatened to kill the entire bill unless the Export Credit feature was eliminated. The date already set for adjournment gave no time in which to bring the matter up again in the House, in order to allow that body to

overrule this treacherous action of its misrepresentatives. As a consequence the friends of the farmer were forced to yield, and accept a bill which gave the farmer more dope in the form of domestic credit while depriving him of the one thing he needed more than any and all other things combined, namely: foreign markets for his surplus crops. 

Who killed Cock Robin? Would two individual Congressmen, on their own initiative have dared to block legislation endorsed by a unanimous vote of the Senate, and a two to one vote of the House? If not, what powerful interest or organization was responsible for the appointment of those men as "conferees," and for their brazen effrontery in thus thwarting the expressed will of overwhelming majorities of both 

Houses of Congress? 

It was currently admitted that as Administration Senators and Congressmen did not dare to vote openly against the Export Credit feature of the "Farm Relief Bill," and as the secret and sinister influence of the International Loan Brokers of Wall Street was too powerful to be resisted, this cowardly means was adopted of assassinating in the dark a measure they dared not vote against in the light.

This Export Credit plan would have helped business and industry as much indirectly as it would have helped the farmers directly. Practically all the business and industrial leaders who were conversant with its provisions openly acknowledged this fact. Indeed, so far as is known, there was only one financial interest in the country that was antagonistic to it, a powerful group of International Loan Brokers. These men, through their lobby at Washington, declared repeatedly that Europe had no security to offer for the proposed American credits to European importers. Yet at the very time they were reiterating this argument, they were loaning their own money or that of their clients (at high rates of interest, plus a high commission) in practically every country in Europe, on the selfsame security that they insisted was not good enough for Uncle Sam. The truth was they realized that if, instead of allowing the 

Government to extend credit abroad for several hundred million dollars, they could loan that amount, they would make in commissions alone, tens of millions of dollars. It is small wonder, therefore, that no argu-

ments in favor of Government credits to foreigners, even on approved security, sounded convincing to them. 

While in accordance with this Export Credit Bill, not a dollar of our Governmental loans would have gone abroad, as automatically every penny would have been expended in this country for our surplus farm crops, unfortunately millions of dollars of the money loaned abroad by private financiers did go abroad and was spent for Argentine corn, wheat, cattle and hogs—thus rehabilitating Argentine agriculture, while America's surplus crops continued to be burned for fuel, to rot on the ground or to be sold at from one-third to one-half their cost of production.

When we consider first that less than one-half a billion dollars' worth of surplus crops, constituting about 5 per cent of our total farm output, have reduced the value of the other 95 per cent at least two billion dollars nearly every year since 1920; and secondly that Europe owes us over $20,000,000,000 that we never can hope to collect, until European nations begin to get on their feet economically, is it not self-evident that it would have been the part of business prudence and common sense, to say nothing of common decency, for America to have helped rehabilitate Europe, at least to the extent of selling her on well secured credit such of our surpluses as had been demonstrated to be, not national assets, but national liabilities? 

Mr. Julius Barnes, formerly Director of the United States Grain Corporation, and afterward President of the United States Chamber of Commerce, in a speech before the Illinois Chamber of Commerce on December 8, 1922, stated that if we could extend Europe credit to the extent of even the comparatively small sum of eighty million dollars, to enable it to buy our surplus grain, the price of wheat would rise from twenty to thirty cents, the price of corn from ten to fifteen cents, the price of oats about ten cents, and the price of rye probably thirty cents a bushel.

Moreover if anyone doubts that the sale abroad of even a part of our surplus crops would raise the prices of grain in this country very materially, he has only to recall the results obtained in 1922 when Congress voted an appropriation of twenty million dollars, with which to buy wheat, rye, milk and corn products for the starving Russians of the Volga Valley. From the day Mr. Hoover began to buy food to ship to 

Russia, the price of grain began to rise. Every day that Mr. Hoover continued to buy, the price of grain continued to rise, and the day Mr. 

Hoover stopped buying, the price of grain stopped rising. During the period that these supplies were being bought, in spite of the fact that only a small part of this money was spent for corn products, the price of corn rose, and remained considerably over ten cents a bushel higher. 

There were in the country at that time, according to the figures of the United States Department of Agriculture, one billion five hundred million bushels of corn. An increase in price of 10 cents a bushel on this amount of corn meant a gain to the farmer of one hundred and fifty million dollars. In other words, the government's gift of twenty million dollars' worth of our surplus crops, netted our farmers a profit of one hundred and fifty million dollars on what was then left of the corn crop, without taking into account the enormous increases likewise caused in the prices of the various other food crops. 

The only half-way plausible argument that ever has been made against the Export Credit plan, was to the effect that if the Government were to extend two or three hundred million dollars in credits to foreigners, on apparently sound securities, it was possible and even probable, that some losses would be incurred. After the World War

the Baldwin Locomotive Works "dumped" twenty million dollars' worth of locomotives abroad on credit, and succeeded in collecting every dollar due on them; similarly the Chicago packing houses sold about fifty million dollars' worth of lard and other meat products abroad on credit, and were equally successful in making collections; and a syndicate of bankers, brokers and planters in Birmingham, Alabama, shipped thirtyeight million dollars' worth of raw cotton abroad, on credit and likewise collected every dollar for it. However, if in exporting large quantities of our surplus crops abroad on credit, our Government had incurred some losses, such losses could easily have been taken care of from a sinking fund, created by raising the interest rate one per cent on the credits extended to foreign importers.

What possible argument then remains to be urged against such a policy? Apparently only this, that any legislation, however sound it may be economically and however fair it may be ethically, if it succeeds in raising the prices of the farmers' crops might raise the cost of food and clothing to consumers. This is an argument which has been greatly overworked. Everyone who will stop to think will recall the familiar fact that when the price of corn drops, the price of corn meal does not drop proportionately; and conversely, that when the prices of the farmers' crops rise, the price of food to the consumer does not rise proportionately. There are a number of other economic factors such as interest, storage and the cost of processing, advertising, transporting and retailing, some of which are even more influential in determining the prices of the finished products than is the cost of the raw materials. 

But suppose the price of food to the consumer automatically did rise and fall with the price of farm products—would that constitute a valid reason for allowing seventy million supposedly self-respecting urban Americans to be either on the farmer's "charity list" or on the farmer's back-taking food and textiles from him at less than the cost of production? Is not such a procedure pretty apt to look, from "between the corn rows" like either a form of charity or a form of larceny? When it is done with the farmer's consent, it is charity, but if it is done by economic and political force, what is it but legalized loot?

After the session of Congress, in which the Export Credit Bill was assassinated, Western Republicans fearing that it might prove politically dangerous to reintroduce and pass a farm relief bill of Democratic parentage, ditched the Export Credit bill and substituted therefor the McNary-Haugen bill which provided for the levying of a production tax on the farmers, called an "equalization fee," to be used as a species of export bonus. Whereupon, in both the 69th and 70th Sessions of Congress, the Export Credit bill was re-introduced by Senator Joseph T. 

Robinson, Democratic leader in the Senate and by the late Representative 

Oldfield, Chairman of the Democratic Congressional Committee. 

Finally after repeated defeats, the McNary-Haugen bill was passed by the 70th Congress and vetoed by President Coolidge. As a result of this action, the Coolidge Administration in 1928 stood convicted at the bar of public opinion of having killed, as did the Harding Administration, a farm relief bill passed by Congress, without giving the country any substitute legislation in its place. 

For years the Democrats had been vainly trying to get Western Republicans to collaborate with them in drafting and passing, over the President's veto, a composite, non-partizan farm relief bill, minus the equalization fee. Many Democrats were willing to accept the debenture plan, the allotment plan, the reforestation plan, the acreage limitation plan or the export credit plan. But a large number of them, realizing that the equalization fee was merely another tax to be added to the farmer's already crushing and confiscatory load of taxes, voted against the McNary-Haugen bill. As the burden of the tariff has been borne not by the manufacturers but by the nation, they urged that the burden of farm relief likewise should be borne not by the farmers but by the nation. The average farmer whether in the East, West, North or South, does not care a straw what brand of relief he gets if only it is sound, constitutional and will save him from bankruptcy and his wife and children from the poorhouse.

During the past fifteen years world economic conditions have so completely changed that the Export Credit plan no longer is a practical solution of the farmer's problem. But when it was introduced into Con.

gress, conditions in both America and Europe were so favorable to this policy that its passage would have put billions of honestly earned money into the pockets of six million American farmers, and would have set the wheels of business and industry turning prosperously throughout the length and breadth of the land. Not in all history has there been a situation in which so simple and inexpensive a device could have unloosed so many pent-up energies and brought prosperity to so many deserving people on two continents.

After we had spent billions of dollars to help win the war and had loaned to the Allies billions more, both during and after the war, the sudden decision of the Harding Administration to do nothing more for Europe, not even to sell her on well secured credit, surplus crops which were proving themselves to be not assets but disastrous liabilities, was one of the costliest political blunders of our generation. Our attitude was not unlike that of the cautious young man who had blown his girl to a dinner, taken her to a show, dropped in at a cabaret for a midnight supper and taxied her home, but resolutely refrained from kissing her good night on the grounds that "he already had done enough for her." 

What that girl thought of him will be the none too flattering verdict of history on the Harding Administration—and us.

APPENDIX B MARKETS FOR MORATORIUMS 

On June 29th, 1931, a day or two after President Hoover's tentative proposal of a moratorium on interest and amortization payments on European war debts, the following constructive suggestion was offered in an address by the author before the Institute of Public Affairs, at the 

University of Virginia. "President Hoover's proposed international war debt moratorium is a welcome constructive note in a world bedlam. However, in his efforts to help the debtor nations of Europe, up to date the President has overlooked a unique opportunity, latent in his proposal, to secure compensatory advantages to the United States. "The debtor nations of Europe are in desperate need of credit, and the proposed moratorium undoubtedly would give them time in which to catch their financial breath. But there is another side to the picture -America is in equally desperate need of markets for its surplus products. Millions of American, as well as European, unemployed workers and farmers are facing hunger and ruin. Fortunately, it is possible, with one master stroke, to give Europe the credit it needs and America the export markets it needs-by making the President's generous offer contingent upon the purchase by the debtor nations of the amount of their deferred payments, in American surplus products. "This would mean that England, France, Austria, Italy and the other debtor nations, each year would buy from America, instead of from Russia, the Argentine and elsewhere, approximately two hundred and fifty million dollars worth of foods, feeds, textiles, copper and steel products and other surpluses from our farms and factories. This is the kind of friendly give and take which from time immemorial has formed the warp and woof of a sound diplomacy and made the wheels of commerce turn. 89

"The details of such a plan can be worked out, provided President Hoover has the will to do so, in other words, provided he is as interested in saving the American farmer, working man and business man, from disaster, as apparently he is, in protecting the enormous foreign investments of our international loan brokers. "If the original Hoover proposal goes through, without the addition of some such collateral agreement as that suggested, the debtor nations of Europe will profit heavily and our international financiers will profit in much the same way that the holders of second mortgages would profit if the first mortgage holders were to step back, bow low and say, "After you, my dear Alphonse!”
