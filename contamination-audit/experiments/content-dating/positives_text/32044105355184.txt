NAHANT CHURCH 

UNDAY services were held at Nahant as early as 1820, in a small stone schoolhouse standing on land just back of the present Public Library. The Rev. Samuel Joseph 

May conducted them and taught the children of the Summer residents during the week. Soon, however, the increase of Summer visitors to Nahant, after the opening of the Nahant Hotel about 1820, and the construction of many cottages for private families, suggested to Mr. William Havard Eliot a plan for building a chapel where different religious sects might assemble and "unite in the worship of God." 

A site for the church was given by Mr. Cornelius Coolidge, who had bought land and built several houses between the years 1820 and 1830. The necessary funds were collected by Mr. Eliot and the plans were prepared by Isaiah Rogers. The subscription paper, dated July 24, 1831, read in part as follows: "Whereas it has been proposed to erect, in some convenient location in Nahant, a suitable structure for the accommodation of persons disposed to attend religious services on Sunday in this place; and whereas it is understood that a neat building for this purpose would not exceed fifteen hundred dollars, the land on which it is being placed being offered by the owner gratuitously; the subscribers agree to contribute the sums set against their names.'

Unfortunately, Mr. William H. Eliot, the originator of the movement, died after an illness of a few days, at the age of 35. 

His brother, Samuel A. Eliot, carried on his plans and the church, built at the cost of $2,476, was ready for use in the Summer of 1832. Mr. Eliot, inspired during his travels in Greece by a love of classic architecture, had adorned his own new house, now owned [3]

by Mrs. George H. Mifflin, with portico and columns. The new chapel, under the same influence, showed a porch with four Tuscan columns and two doors at each end of the building, three windows on each side and three steps leading from the ground to the portico. There were twenty-four pews, with side seats equivalent to eight more pews. Sixteen of these pews were sold immediately to the following original pew owners:

In 1835, Mr. John A. Lowell bought a pew and at the time of his death in 1881 was the last of the early proprietors. The pews corresponding to those owned by Mr. John A. Lowell and 

Mr. Samuel Hammond are still occupied by descendants of the same name.

The first service was held in the new chapel on July 8, 1832, by Rev. John Gorham Palfrey, D.D., from Harvard University.

During the succeeding one hundred years ministers of many denominations, "differing in creeds, in spirit one," have held services every Summer. An old record says, "To have organized the movement for combining the three prominent sects of Unitarians, Episcopalians and Orthodox in one service, required originality and a freedom not likely to be found out of the Unitarian circle." Of the fourteen who signed the first agreement, all but three, and of the first seventeen pew holders, all but five were Unitarians.

The church was enlarged in 1846 by the addition of twenty new pews, ten on each side. This change gave it the shape of a cross. In January, 1862, the chapel was badly damaged by a severe northwest gale, and for five years the question of rebuilding was discussed. It was not, however, until 1869 that it was decided to replace the old structure with a new and larger one. 

During that Summer, services were held in the Village Church, which had been built in 1851. 

At the opening service in the new church, which the Rev. 

Andrew P. Peabody, D.D., of Harvard University, held on 

Sunday, June 27, 1869, there was sung the Dedication Hymn, [4]

for which Mr.

George Peabody had composed music and the following words: 

Father Almighty, unto Thee

This humble altar now we raise, Where those who wander here may bring 

Their offerings of prayer and praise. 

Emblem of Faith and Christian Hope,

Long may it stand upon this steep, Where Thy creative power is seen

In all the wonders of the deep. 

Here may we listen to Thy voice, Heard in soft murmurs on the shore, Or in the thunder of the waves 

That break amid the tempest's roar. 

Here may we view Thy works sublime, Marvels of earth and sky and sea, And breathe this pure health-giving air, With grateful hearts, O God, to Thee. 

Oh, bless this house! and may it be 

A portal leading to Thy throne, Through which Thy children may discern 

The glories they can make their own. 

Here let all worldly strife be stayed, And here be found in Thy Son's name 

That "Peace on earth, good will to men" 

Which angel hosts did once proclaim.

INVOCATION 

Father Almighty, fill our souls 

With heavenly influence from above, That we may do Thy will on earth, And dwell with Thee in endless love.

This hymn is still sung at the closing service each year. 

The new chapel cost $25,571, and the Communion table, chairs in the chancel, lectern, litany desk, font, sermon desk, large Bible and prayer book for the chancel were given by different benefactors. A bell, purchased in 1834 by subscription, was hung in the attic of the little chapel, but since the roof proved nearly impervious to sound, the bell was soon removed to a sort [5]

of campanile in the rear of the church. It was finally housed in the tower of the new church. A fortunate purchase of land from the Lodge heirs was made in 1869, increasing the original lot on the northeast side.

In 1853 an organ had been installed in the old church, but in 1879 a large new organ, made by Hook and Hastings, was given by Mrs. William Appleton and Mrs. J. Arthur Beebe in memory of Mr. Appleton. After fifty years this is still in good condition.

An old record says, "The singing was conducted by a voluntary choir and superior to that Boston afforded in 1832. Sometimes by a choir from the village, sometimes by singers from Boston. Before the organ superseded the tuning fork, it was difficult at times to secure any singers. The effect of these services on the Summer residents of Nahant has been permanent. 

The regular attendance morning and afternoon of the heads of families accompanied by the younger part of the community prevented neglect of the Sunday. The observance of Sunday at 

Nahant in this respect will compare favorably with Newport, where it is not considered the correct thing to drive or make any show on that day."

In June, 1869, it was voted that "the thanks of the proprietors be presented to Francis C. Loring, Esq., Francis C. Loring, Jr., Mrs. Frederick R. Sears and Miss Tuckerman for the acceptable manner in which they have conducted the music and singing for many years.' 99 

Mrs. Sears, who had a beautiful soprano voice, sang in the volunteer choir beginning in the early seventies, and later was an active member of the music committee, a connection of over forty years. Associated with her at different times through this long period were the Misses Loring, Miss Sally Perkins Cary, Miss Charlotte H. Guild, Mrs. R. H. Hodges, Miss Alice 

Russell, Miss G. S. Cary, Mr. H. J. Hayden, Mr. John P.

Putnam, Mr. Fessenden and Mrs. James H. Beal, who from 

1889 until her death in 1920 was keenly interested in the church music, and compiled and presented to the church a set of hymn books. In 1888 the volunteer choir gave place to a paid choir. 

The first record of an altar committee is in 1888, when Miss Russell, Miss Codman and Miss Lawrence were appointed a committee to arrange any flowers which might be given to the 

---

church. For a number of years Mrs. Frank Merriam and Mrs.

Charles T. Lovering had charge of the altar, Mrs. Lovering serving from 1902 until 1928. 

In 1833 Colonel Thomas H. Perkins wrote Mr. Eliot that he had caused a picture to be painted by Fisher the subject;: "Our Saviour Addressing the Multitude on the Seashore". and wished to have it hung in the new chapel. This large picture, with its life-sized figures, was placed in a recess back of the reading desk. Some adventurous committee, intending to redeem the dazzling colors of the picture, had changes made in the foreground and in some of the figures, but the new paint by some mischance ran over the picture, and gave the appearance of coal tar. To remedy this a curtain was hung, which year by year closed more and more over the sacred scenes until finally it. totally obscured the painting, which was then removed from the frame and carried to Boston, where it narrowly escaped the Great Fire of 1872. In 1876 it was brought back and hung in the new chapel, but further record of it is lost. 

In 1886 Mr. Amos A. Lawrence generously gave $1,000 and helped to raise a sum of $3,454 to pay off a debt which had been incurred at the time of building the new chapel. This money was. given on condition "that no debts shall hereafter be incurred for the church services, or for its repairs, or for anything which could jeopardize the property or cause any embarrassment to those who hold pews or worship here." 

In 1904, three windows over the altar were filled with stained glass from designs, probably her last, by Mrs. Sarah W. 

Whitman. Gradually the old windows were replaced by beautiful stained glass to conform with the ones over the altar. The three windows in the right Transept were given by Mrs. Frank 

Merriam in memory of Mr. and Mrs. Joseph S. Lovering, and the three windows in the left Transept were given by Mr. 

Boylston A. Beal in memory of Mrs. James H. Beal. The smaller windows in the nave of the church, eight in number, were given by Mr. Arthur S. Johnson in memory of Mr. Samuel 

Johnson, Mr. Edward C. Johnson and Mr. Wolcott H. Johnson.

In 1905 Mrs. Beal gave the large new pulpit in memory of 

Mr. Beal. Two altar vases had already been given by Miss Mary 

Russell, the prayer book holder by Mrs. Frank Merriam, and the white altar cloth by Mrs. William Appleton. [7]

In the church was lighted by electricity through the generous gift of Mr. and Mrs. Arthur Perry, and in 1930 Mrs.

William G. Thayer gave the new Episcopal hymn books in memory of her mother, Mrs. William Sigourney Otis.

In 1850 Mr. T. B. Curtis asked permission of the proprietors to arrange for a school for boys in the church, having eight sons himself, and agreed to become responsible for all damage done while it was so occupied. It was from this school that the little Rice boy, who had been given to his father by the courts, was spirited away by his mother. Judge Robert Grant remembers seeing three men suddenly rush into the church, take the boy out to a waiting wagon behind two fast horses, and drive. furiously away. Henry Cabot Lodge, an older boy, was sitting on the fence outside, and gave such clear testimony when the kidnappers were caught that a gold watch, suitably inscribed, was given to him by his father. This watch he carried to the day of his death.

Two services were held on Sunday until about 1882, when it was voted to have only one service at 11 a.m., “there being so few people at Nahant."

When a tablet to the Founders of the Church was dedicated on July 22, 1877, the Rev. Andrew Preston Peabody again preached the sermon. Dr. John Gorham Palfrey wrote these verses for the occasion:

The thoughtful sires assembled here- Differing in creed, in spirit one- A modest temple joined to rear, To seek the Father through his Son. 

They hoped that long 'neath smiling skies, 'Mid Nature's hush, by silent sea, Accordant praises here might rise, Fountain of light and love to Thee.

The sires have passed beyond the veil; 

Yet while the years their circuit run, Accordant anthems here shall hail 

Th' Eternal Father through his Son.

This hymn is still sung at the opening service each year. 

Dr. Peabody said in part of the Founders: 

William H. Eliot-The real founder of the Nahant Church, had the esteem and confidence of all who knew him. He was a [8]

leader in the reform of church music and established a quartette choir at the West Church, the first in Boston. He built the 

Tremont House in Boston, which became the originator of the 

American Hotel System.

William Prescott-Long regarded as the head of the legal profession in this State, a man of large and varied culture, whose fame as a lawyer hardly surpassed his reputation as a scholar. 

Jonathan Phillips-A man who regarded wealth as a stewardship from God, a relief fund for need and suffering. 

Nathaniel P. Russell-A Treasurer of the Massachusetts

General Hospital, the West Church in Boston, and the Bunker Hill Monument Association, and the first Treasurer of the 

Nahant Church. 

Charles Bradbury-Held in high esteem by his associates. 

Thomas Handasyd Perkins-The merchant prince of his time, and for many years surpassing any other man in New England, if not in the United States, in the extent of his operations. 

He left a liberal endowment for the Institute for the Blind, which bears his name. He was an early purchaser of land at 

Nahant and liberally gave up the finest site on the Peninsula for the Hotel.

Frederic Tudor-One of the first men to build at Nahant and one of the chief benefactors of the town, the fine shade trees being all planted by him, and many others through the Peninsula, where it was supposed no trees would grow. 

William Appleton-A high-minded, large-hearted Boston merchant.

Samuel A. Eliot-Chief magistrate of his native city, representative in Congress, a liberal benefactor and judicious manager of public philanthropic institutions, Treasurer of Harvard, a man of signal integrity, purity and loftiness of character and firm religious belief.

Samuel Hammond-An honorable record as merchant, citizen, neighbor, friend and father.

David Sears-A man of great liberality in public and private life, charitable, literary and religious-a man respected and beloved. 

Peter C. Brooks-His motto might have been "Let your moderation be known unto all men." An ample fortune was ob- [9]

tained by prudence, caution and thrift. Liberal and generous in his charities.

Cornelius Coolidge-Donor of the land on which the church stands, and from his other lands at Nahant he gave the stones for the foundation. 

William H. Prescott-The Historian, will never be forgotten while histories last. 

Edward H. Robbins-A physician and man worthily honored and beloved..

A list of the clergymen who have preached in the Nahant Church would include outstanding names from all denomina tions: Chandler Robbins, John Gorham Palfrey, Bishop Clark of Rhode Island, Samuel Longfellow, Samuel K. Lothrop,

Bishop Vinton, Bishop Eastburn, Bishop Huntington, Edward 

Everett Hale, Phillips Brooks, Bishop Potter, James Freeman

Clarke, President Mark Hopkins of Williams, Bishop McVickar, Leighton Parks, Brooke Herford, George Hodges, Dr. George 

A. Gordon, Bishop Lawrence, President Tucker of Dartmouth, President Faunce of Brown. For a short time the Rev. Patrick

Strain of Lynn and other priests held mass there for members of the Roman Catholic faith, until they built a church for themselves.

Baptisms and weddings have had their place in the history of the church, and there have been two funeral services. To recall the names of all who have been connected with the Nahant Church would mean the naming of many well-known families in Boston through the past century. Prescott, Motley, Agassiz, Longfellow, Sears, Grant, Lawrence, Quincy, Warren, Lowell, Amory, Gray, Chadwick, Blanchard. These and others have served and worshiped there. 

The first secretary and treasurer, Mr. William H. Eliot, was followed by his brother, Samuel A. Eliot, who served four years.

Then Mr. Nathaniel Pope Russell kept the books for twelve years and Mr. John E. Lodge for fourteen. Mr. Samuel H. 

Russell had a service of thirty-three years, Mr. Frank Merriam of twenty-nine years and Mr. Lawrence Curtis of seven. Mr. 

George H. Mifflin was a trustee for twenty-seven years, and for twenty-five of these, engaged all the clergymen who preached during the Summers. [ 10 ]

Bishop Clark of Rhode Island, dean of the preachers, came to Nahant first as a young clergyman in 1836, and preached in all forty-four times. He had as an assistant in 1892, Mr. Walter 

Russell Breed, who then began coming to the Church and has preached twenty-six times. This record is equalled by that of 

Mr. Samuel A. Eliot, grandson of the Founder, who conducted his first service in Nahant in 1894, and still continues his interest and loyalty to the Church of his forefathers. 

Mr. Howard M. Dow was organist for over thirty years, and gave an organ recital on the new organ in 1879. More recently Mr. Percy F. Baker has had charge of the music and choir for over twenty-five years.

A record hardly equalled in any other church is the continuous service of Charles F. Johnson as sexton. He took his father's place when the latter went to the war in 1863, and since then has not missed a Sunday during sixty-nine years of faithful and devoted service.

The Nahant Church has for one hundred years sought to fulfill the religious needs of the Summer colony. It had to be nonsectarian, it has had no regular clergyman, it has depended for its existence on the loyal support and interest of those for whom it exists, and this support has come from the taxes on pews and sittings, and a few generous gifts and legacies, for there has never been a collection taken up during a service. 

The records of this unique church as herein set forth are unsurpassed. No other church to our knowledge has for one hundred years opened its doors to all denominations and worshiped together in peace and harmony. It is now hoped that the coming generation will take up the work so faithfully carried on by their predecessors, and make it, "though differing in creed, in spirit one," a yet more glowing example of worshiping God.
