Election Agreements 

Election agreements, in which all matters prerequisite to the holding of an election are stipulated, are authorized by Section 9(c) (4) of the statute. Such agreements provide for a quicker resolution of the question of representation than if a hearing is required, and at the same time conserve Agency time and money. It is clear from General Counsel memoranda submitted to the Task Force that diligent and continuing efforts have been made over the years to increase the percentage of election agreements. The figures indicate the success of this effort. The percentage of elections conducted by election agreement in recent fiscal years shows: 80.1 percent in 

FY 1972, 81.0 percent in FY 1973, and 81.8 percent in 1974. For 

FY 1975 the figure was 79.8 percent.

Obviously, election agreements depend upon the willingness of the parties to agree. However, the initiative for negotiating and obtaining the agreement must come from the Regional Office personnel. 

The percentage varies somewhat among the Regions, and it is known that some personnel are more successful in obtaining election agreements than others. While it is difficult to pinpoint the reasons for successful negotiation of election agreements, continuing efforts to do so are worthwhile. The Task Force has been informed by the General 

Counsel that the Division of Operations Management has recently begun a Regional Office survey of the successful techniques that have been used in securing such agreements and will impart the results of its review of these techniques to all Regional Office personnel. - 5 -

Consents and Stipulations 

One aspect of election agreements merits special mention. For many years Board regulations have provided for two kinds of agreements: (1) the consent agreement in which the parties accept the Regional Director's determinations as final on all issues which arise; and (2) the so-called stipulated agreement, in which the parties reserve their right to have all issues which arise determined finally by the Board. 

For every fiscal year through 1964, the number of consents exceeded the number of stips. In 1966, for the first time, stips were more numerous than consents, and the preponderance has increased each year since then. In FY 1975 there were 992 consents and 5,854 stips. 

Since stips, by definition, go to the Board for final resolution, they extend the time for resolution of the representation question. None of the memoranda submitted to the Task Force indicates that the Board has determined, or sought to determine, the reason for the decline in consents and the increase in stips.

III. Hearings on Petitions The Problem of Delay 

Section 9 of the statute provides that, when a petition is filed, the Board shall investigate such petition and if it has reasonable cause to believe that a question of representation exists shall provide for an appropriate hearing upon due notice." As noted, election agreements preclude the need for a hearing, but in the absence - 6 -

of such an agreement, the statute clearly makes a hearing mandatory before an election can be held. The Task Force is informed that recommendations have been made to Congress that the statute be amended to permit elections without hearings in certain situations. 

The Task Force sees no way that such elections can be directed under the present statute, and therefore any such recommendation by the Task Force would be beyond its jurisdiction.

On occasion a problem which arises with respect to hearings is that a party who has no genuine issues uses the hearing to delay the holding of the election as long as possible. This is one of the principal complaints which has been made both to Congress and to this Task Force. 

Data submitted to the Task Force show the following national median figures: 9 days from the filing of a petition to the notice of hearing, 14 days from the notice to the completion of the hearing, 22 days from the close of hearing to the issuance of the Regional Director's decision and direction of the election, and 30 days until the election. 

Thus, in cases where a hearing is held (and the election is not held up because of review proceedings at the Board level), an election takes place in a median of 75 days from the filing of the petition. It is emphasized that this is a median figure. It is probably true that a few "horror" cases in excess of the median are responsible for most of the criticism of the delay in obtaining an election. But the Task 

Force believes that the median figure of 75 days can be reduced. Some spokesmen have suggested an outside even a mandatory days for the holding of an election after a petition is filed. Given the statutory hearing requirement, the Task Force believes an absolute 

number of - 7 -

figure is unrealistic. 

The Task Force does think, however, that certain changes in the time and method of processing the case can be made which will accelerate the election date. 

The Task Force believes that it should be possible to ascertain more quickly than is now true in many cases whether the parties will agree to an election or whether a hearing will be necessary. 

5. The Task Force unanimously recommends a time target of three working days after the filing of the petition within which the Regional Office will set either a conference date for the election agreement or a hearing date, and if the latter, will send out the notice of hearing.

preference, were listed and may be summarized as follows:

(1) an "open door" requirement that the union be given access to employees during the pre-election period on the same premises and with the same facilities available to the employer, whether or not the employer himself uses the premises and facilities for electioneering; (2) an "opened door" requirement that the union be given access to all nonproduction premises and facilities once the employer himself chose to electioneer at the work place; (3) an "equal entry" requirement under which the union would be given access to those particular premises and facilities which the employer himself has used for election purposes. At the present time problems of employer denials of access are dealt with through unfair labor practice proceedings, principally Section 8(a) (1). Under the proposal, such problems would be dealt with pursuant to the Board's power to adopt rules and regulations to implement its Section 9 authority over the election process. Presumably, if such a rule were adopted and then violated by an employer, the remedy would be to set the election aside if the union lost, rather than proceed through an 8(a)(1) charge. The Board's access rule would become part of the "laboratory conditions" for the conduct of elections. Just as the employer's speech right is regulated more strictly under Section 9 than under Section 8, so likewise the proposed rule would regulate his property right more strictly under Section 9 than it is now regulated under Section 8.

Vote and Impound Procedure

As noted, one of the principal criticisms of the election process is the delay that frequently occurs in the holding of the election. 

The delay is particularly long, as has been stated, when a hearing case is referred to the Board for initial decision. Another situation which may produce inordinate delay is when a request for review is made with respect to some pre-election ruling of the Regional Director, and the Board grants review but is unable to decide the case before the election date. The practice has been to postpone the election until the Board decision has been rendered. 

Once an election has been directed, the parties gear their campaigns in light of the election date. To postpone the election in such circumstances is frustrating to a very high degree. If the election is ordered months later, the parties must then crank up their campaigns once again. Union members of the Task Force assert that there may have been an irretrievable loss of employee support. Further explication is unnecessary, for it seems self-evident that undue prolongation of the 

- 11 -

election is inconsistent with the basic purpose of Section 9 to provide a prompt method of resolving representation disputes. In a larger context, it has the unfortunate result of creating cynicism about the capacity of legal process to fairly and adequately respond to legitimate expectations.

The Task Force believes that, once an election date has been set, the election should be held on that date. If there are pending issues which could affect the validity of the election, they should be resolved after the election has been held. The ballots cast in the election can be impounded and later counted, after the issues have been resolved, in whatever appropriate manner is indicated by the decision on the issues. The great virtue of this procedure is that it enables a registration of the employees' choice at the time when interest and momentum in both the union and employer camps are at their peak. While knowledge of the outcome may be postponed, the parties know that they will not again have to mount another electioneering campaign.

The vote and impound procedure would no doubt confront the Board with some technical problems in the holding of the election and segregation of ballots, but they do not appear to be of any greater magnitude than those which the Board has met and responded to over the years. Questions of voter eligibility can be dealt with by the usual method of challenging the ballots. The most troublesome problem would seem to center on a disputed issue of unit determination. 

But we are confident the Board could develop a balloting process under which the election could be held, whatever the eventual decision might be on the appropriate unit. An analogue is the Globe election 

12 -

procedure which has been used successfully since 1937. 

The Task Force realizes that, in a relatively few instances, the decision on the pending issues could result in a decision that no election should have been directed in the first place. Such a result could follow when the pending issue is, for example, one of coverage or exemption under the statute.

Obviously, it is an undesirable result if the parties have gone through with an election only to learn later that it was a nullity. But statistically such cases are sure to be a small minority of the total. On net balance, reducing the frustration of the statute and the parties in the great majority of cases clearly outweighs the unfortunate result in a few. This is simply one more example of the truism that no solution to a problem is likely to be perfect or all-encompassing. 

The Task Force believes the vote-and-impound procedure would have the desirable result of expediting the Board decision on the pending issue(s). If the election has been postponed pending a decision, the natural psychological tendency is to relax and proceed at a more leisurely pace. But if the election has been held and the ballots have been impounded, and the Board knows the parties are awaiting its decision so that the ballots may be counted, the case is in an entirely different posture, one which should generate a pressure which would probably result in an earlier decision. 5/ 8. The Task Force recommends that the Board develop procedures for holding elections on the designated date and impounding the ballots for later counting in those situations

Security of Impounded Ballots 

The Task Force considered the matter of the security of impounded ballots. In those situations where ballots must be preserved following the conclusion of voting, either because all ballots have been impounded or because certain challenged ballots are determinative of the outcome of the election, the Region is required to secure those ballots until a final resolution has been reached, See Section 11344 of the Board's Case Handling Manual. Under present procedure, the ballots are stored in a locked file cabinet in the Regional Office. 

Over the years, a few Regional Offices have been embarrassed by their inability to produce "secured" ballots at the conclusion of the postelection investigation. No one has suggested that this reflects poorly upon the Board's reputation for neutrality; it simply reflects poorly upon the Board's housekeeping practices. It is doubtful that any procedure can be 100% effective, but we do think that a procedure 

14 -

attended by considerably greater formality than now exists would tend substantially to reduce the number of these embarrassing situations and conversely enhance the Board's reputation for its ability to maintain security.

The view was expressed that perhaps the impounded ballots should be deposited in a bank safety deposit box rather than kept in the Regional Office. The Task Force decided not to make such a flat recommendation. 

9. The Task Force unanimously recommends that the Board address itself to the matter of security of impounded ballots, develop a procedure which will maximize their security, and then require that this procedure be adhered to in all Regions. 

Counting of Challenged Ballots 

The Task Force received from one of the Regional Offices a suggestion regarding challenged ballots which it thinks should be considered by the Board. Under present procedures, if the number of challenged ballots is sufficient to affect the outcome of an election, an investigation takes place, the eligibility of the voter is determined, and the ballot is then counted or not. Obviously, this procedure delays the final determination of the election result and requires considerable investigation time. The proposal is that a challenged ballot be cast in duplicate, the original with the voter's name being segregated, and a carbon copy (without the voter's name but identifiable as a challenge) being put in the ballot box. The ballots can then be counted as usual immediately after the election. 

If the challenged ballots, whether for or against the Union, would 15 -

under no combination affect the result, then the result of the election could be certified immediately without the necessity of investigation and individual determination. While it may be true that this procedure would work in relatively few elections, the time saved would be worth it, particularly since the procedure is one which could be implemented with no extra cost in time or money. 

10. The Task Force unanimously recommends that the Board consider the adoption of this procedure on an experimental basis. ས. election.

A New Procedure 

The Task Force has probably spent more time discussing postelection proceedings than any other subject. 

The Task Force reached two broad conclusions: (1) that the entire procedure should be accelerated and (2) that the objecting party should be required to assume a larger responsibility than is now the case. More specifically, the Task Force has forumulated the outline of a modified post-election procedure which it believes will achieve the broad objectives, and which it unanimously recommends to the Board for consideration. The procedure is summarized as follows. If adopted, 11. 18 - 

---

it should be included in the transmittal letter to the parties when the Regional Director -sends out the notice of election. 

The objecting party will submit his objections within 5 working days of the election, as he now does. Within 3 working days thereafter the objecting party must submit evidence in support of the objections. This evidence will consist of a particularized statement of the objection, including the name of the person(s) who committed the alleged misconduct on which the objection is based, the date and location of such misconduct, and a specific statement of the nature of the misconduct. must be accompanied either by affidavits or the names of the persons who will supply the necessary information to the Board Agent. Rule-Making 

One focus of inquiry by the Task Force was the possibility of increased rule-making by the Board. 

The best way to achieve significant improvements of representation cases is to reduce the amount of litigation in both the pre-election and post-election stages. The question explored by the Task Force was whether a significant contribution to this goal can be made through adoption by the Board, either by administrative or decisional rule- · 30 - 1

making, of firm and inflexible rules governing subjects which now are litigated and determined on a case-by-case basis. 

There are good historical reasons for believing that significant accomplishments can be made through this approach. For example, at one time it was not uncommon to litigate the question of jurisdiction in a high percentage of Board cases. See, for example, Polish National

Alliance of the United States of North America v. NLRB, 322 U.S. 643 

(1944). The later adoption by the Board of its jurisdictional yardsticks has virtually eliminated litigation of this issue and surely has promoted a rise in the number of representation cases disposed of by agreement without necessity for a hearing. In the same way, the 

Board's contract-bar rules at one time resulted in frequent litigation of the question of the reasonableness of the duration of a contract asserted as a bar. See Cushman's Sons, Inc., 88 NLRB 121 (1950).

The eventual adoption of a firm three year rule in General Cable Corp., 139 NLRB 1123 (1962), again has eliminated this as an area of dispute and litigation.

In view of these successes, it seemed worthwhile to consider whether there exist other areas in which the adoption of firm rules will eliminate litigation. The Task Force considered whether to recommend to the Board that it proceed through formal rule-making under the Administrative Procedure Act. 

17. The Task Force unanimously recommends that the subject of formal rule-making be further studied by the Task Force in its second year. The Task Force does believe, however, that there do exist numerous areas in which greater specificity can be achieved with a resultant decrease in litigation. - 31 -

These examples are illustrative only. Investigations 

The General Counsel deems the investigation-disposition stage of the processing of an unfair labor practice charge as commencing upon the docketing of the charge and as being concluded at the time of initial action by the Regional Office, i.e., withdrawal, dismissal, settlement prior to issuance of complaint, or issuance of complaint. If a charge is sent to the General 

Counsel's Advice Branch for "advice," however, the time that the charge is in "Advice" is not considered part of the 30-day investigation stage. (The time in "Advice" is included in the Agency statistics for the time to process such cases.)

From the inception of "time targets," the target date for the investigation and disposition of a charge has been 45 days. This time target usually has been met or bettered nationally. For example, during the first half of fiscal 1976 the median time for the disposal of 14,325 ULP charges in the investigation stage was 43 days. Of course, a median does not reflect extremes. The more complex or difficult cases often take much longer, and some Regional Offices, e.g., San Francisco (before the establishment of two Regional Offices in that area), where the intake has been extraordinarily heavy, exceed the median time on a more regular basis. - 39 -

Some observers have suggested that a few days might be cut from the investigative process by a quicker handling of the case between the docketing and the first contact by the investigator with the parties. In the present process in most Regions, the charge passes through more than one person on its way to the investigator, and often the investigator is out of town, or otherwise unavailable, at the time the charge is docketed. If the initial contact with the parties to arrange tentative dates for interviews were handled immediately by a supervisor, or other person, in the absence of the assigned investigator, some time might be saved. We understand that some Regions follow this practice. 

23. The Task Force recommends that time could be saved by placing more of a burden upon the charging party to establish a "reasonable basis" for its case by furnishing affidavits or live witnesses sooner or in greater numbers.

The Task Force also thinks that when such reasonable basis is not established, the Regional Office should quickly dispose of the case, without bothering to see the charged party for evidence of its position. To avoid delay in interviewing the charged party, however, a tentative appointment could still be set as soon as a charge is filed. 

24. The Task Force unanimously recommends that charges defective on their face should be quickly dismissed.

Other observers feel that the investigatory process could be improved through better communications between the investigating agent and involved parties and less secrecy on the part of the General Counsel. There has been objection, for example, to the practice of interviewing the charging party's witnesses in the absence of the charging party's attorney. There 8/

IV. Discovery 

Discovery is undoubtedly one of the most significant, controversial issues entrusted to the Task Force. We shall first canvass a variety of positions, and then attempt to arrive at some recommendations that will reflect a consensus of the group's views. 

A. Arguments In Favor Of Discovery 

Some argue that discovery will result in an 

Speed-Up of the Process. 

overall savings of time by reducing the number of contested issues and enabling the hearing on those that remain to be focused and hence briefer. 

The feeling is that if discovery were available, a respondent would realize sooner that the facts were against its position. The respondent would then presumably be willing to engage in more meaningful, and better prepared, settlement proceedings. These proceedings could then result either in the full settlement of the case or in the stipulation of certain facts. 

If all the facts could be agreed upon in this manner, there would be no need for an oral hearing and the case could be disposed of by written materials. If factual questions remain, then the hearing time would be reduced by saving the time which would have been used proving questions settled by discovery. 

Better Settlement Practice. As indicated above, the facts developed through discovery may aid in the settlement process. There is another aspect of discovery which will also aid in reaching settlements before hearing. 

The lawyers are forced to direct their attention to the case and hence will have a better grasp of it when conducting settlement negotiations. 

Better Definition and Resolution of Issues. If, through discovery, each party is aware of the other's factual contentions, then the preparation of the case for hearing can be focused on the contested issues and their legal 47

significance. As a result, the parties will not have to prepare for as many contingencies and should be better prepared on the issues which will arise. At the hearing, cross-examination should be more concise, because better prepared.

More Fair. A respondent is likely to feel at a tactical disadvantage if it is not aware of what might be raised at the hearing. As a result, it will have to overprepare by investigating issues which will not be raised, or if without suitable notice of the topics in contention, it will be unable to adequately prepare for rebuttal and cross-examination. comments are to the effect that the General Counsel has what amounts to discovery through his ability to investigate complaints, but the respondent has no correlative right. There is a strong feeling that trial by surprise is simply inconsistent with modern jurisprudence. 

Reduced Pressure for Discovery Under FOIA. So long as there is no functional provision for discovery, respondents who feel that it may be important for the development of their case will logically use the Freedom 10/ of Information Act to obtain as much information as they can. The difficulty with using FOIA in lieu of discovery is that it is not limited by the protections contained in most discovery provisions. Thus, if some suitable form of discovery were available it would reduce the pressures on respondents to make claims under FOIA and the pressure on courts to accede to them.

B. Arguments Against Discovery 

Fear of Reprisal. The most frequent argument against discovery is that it would hamper the investigation and resolution of complaints because employees would be intimidated from discussing the allegation for fear of reprisal by the employer. Under the present situation, a person is told during the investigation that what he or she says will not be revealed unless and until the person is called as a witness in the hearing. The feeling is that there are two benefits which result from this promise: (1) confidential informers can be protected by an agreement not to call them as witnesses; and (2) the person who does testify feels protected by the formality of the process. before an Administrative Law Judge, with many people around to hear what is said.

Tampering With Witnesses. Some feeling has been expressed that if a potential witness's statements were revealed too far before the commencement of the hearing there would be the possibility of witness tampering. In this case, the respondent would persuade the witness that his or her view of the situation was erroneous and that the testimony should be something different from what was told to the investigators. 

Viewed one way, we have the situation where a potential witness is intimidated into changing a story; viewed another, it reflects the benefits of discovery by arriving at the true and fully developed facts through pre-hearing revelation. Time-Consuming.

There are two thrusts to the argument that discovery will protract the overall process. One is that any provision for discovery will itself result in the development of new litigable issues in the form of wrangling over compliance and the scope of the requests. The second is that some forms of discovery are inherently time-consuming, since they call for compiling the information requested, coordinating the schedules of several busy people, or duplication of effort. - 49 -

Burdensome and Expensive to Comply. The argument is sometimes made that if discovery is available, the parties will want to gather far more information than is really necessary for the case. 

If that occurs, then the party supplying the information has gone to needless expense in preparing the excess; usually the excess is in the form of detailed business data. 

Moreover, furnishing the information may require the attention of essential people.

Overly Formal. One argument is that discovery is the stuff of the large, complicated, and potentially expensive civil suit whereas most labor cases are much narrower. Thus, the argument runs, the average labor case is better handled by a system which can provide an informal, but structured hearing, and arrive at just results relatively quickly. Under this theory, discovery would operate to overly formalize the proceedings and make them less flexible. It is also suggested that settlements prior to complaint might be reduced if the respondent knew it could later get a list of witnesses. 

Any time a discovery request is made by a respondent for anything not in the possession of the General Counsel's Office, it will potentially consume resources of that Office because the attorneys will need to be in on the decisions of what will be provided under the request. 

This may mean that either more resources must be supplied in order to comply with discovery, or that the attorneys will be able to devote less time to processing cases so that more delay would result. 

Board Resources. 

Defective Affidavits. Certainly the most discussed form of discovery is furnishing the respondent sometime before the hearing with the affidavits taken during the investigation. In addition to the general ones, there are 50 ·

two arguments against the early release of the affidavits. they are frequently not carefully drafted so that the affiant is not as precise and focused as he or she might be. As a result the affiant may ramble on about matters which will arise in the hearing. The second argument is that the issues may have narrowed after a full investigation. 

In either event, the information provided is beyond that in issue and may cause a broadening of the case. 

Some argue that discovery is not really necessary because its main benefits are available in the form of a bill of particulars. 

Once this gives adequate notice of what is alleged, the respondent would have it within his power to develop the relevant facts. Unnecessary.

Control of Regional Director Over Scheduling and Postponement of Hearing Dates 

The issuance of a complaint by a Regional Director officially places a case before the Board, and from that point forward, it is contended by some persons, the Regional Director should be no more than one party to a proceeding before an impartial quasi-judicial agency. The present procedure, they insist, gives the Regional Director too much influence over the original scheduling of a hearing date, and too much discretion with regard to the postponement of a hearing The Regional Director, acting as an adversary, might be tempted to use his powers in order to gain a tactical advantage over the respondent. 

One solution would be to have all initial hearing dates set by the Chief Administrative Law Judge, or the West Coast Deputy Chief Judge, after all parties are given the opportunity to arrive at a mutually satisfactory proposed date. The NLRB 

Rules and Regulations could also be amended so as to give the Chief Administrative Law Judge, or the West Coast Presiding Judge, the authority to receive and rule on all motions seeking postponement of hearings. As an alternative, a specific ALJ could be assigned to a case immediately after issuance of the complaint, and that judge could rule on all pretrial motions, including postponement motions. 

A contrary position is argued as follows. Although the Regional Attorneys do consult with the staff assistant to the Chief Administrative Law Judge, or the West Coast Presiding Judge, with regard to the scheduling of hearing dates, the final decision is actually made by the ALJ's Division. In most cases, regional personnel have no more influence over scheduling than respondents' counsel. 

Most Regional Directors require a stronger showing of good cause from their own personnel before granting a postponement than they require from respondents' counsel because there is constant pressure on regional personnel to expedite cases. 

However, outside parties are not necessarily told the reasons why a Regional 

Director postpones a hearing on his own motion. - 60 -

The present scheduling and postponement procedure is consistent with Section 3(d) of the Act, which gives the General Counsel final authority to issue and prosecute complaints before the Board. Any change in the present scheduling and postponement procedure would result in greater delays and increased uncertainty. Moreover, the present Chief Administrative Law Judge is apparently not enthusiastic about any change in the current procedures. 

While the Task Force recognizes the theoretical anomaly of lodging control over the scheduling and postponement of hearing dates in one of the parties to the litigation, a majority does not regard this as a major practical problem. 

We are satisfied to call the matter to the Board's attention, for whatever corrective action it thinks appropriate to ensure fairness to all. 

C. The Region 14 Docketing System Section 10258 of the NLRB Casehandling Manual sets forth a procedure for scheduling cases on a calendar call basis. At the present time, only Region 14 in St. Louis, Missouri, is employing any type of calendar call system for the trial of unfair labor practice cases. The mechanics of the St. Louis system are described below, along with a summary of its advantages and disadvantages. 

In the latter part of a given year, the Regional Attorney for Region 14 will mail to the Chief Administrative Law Judge in Washington, D. C., a list of proposed docket dates for the upcoming year. The letter from the Regional Attorney proposes two nonconsecutive Mondays out of each month for docket calls. The Chief Administrative Law Judge will generally approve the dates proposed, and he will allocate two ALJs to St. Louis for each week which commences on the designated Mondays. If necessary, additional ALJs are requested by the Region at a subsequent time. It is the sole responsibility of the St. Louis Region to schedule its cases for the coming year so as to obtain the maximum utilization of the ALJs allotted. 61-

Most cases which are to be tried outside of St. Louis are treated individually and they are not placed on the docket. However, short cases which are to be tried within 150 miles of St. Louis are sometimes placed on the docket and tried on Monday. After the trial of a non-St. Louis case, the presiding 

ALJ will proceed to the Region 14 office where he will try additional cases during the remainder of the week. Complex cases which are expected to consume five or more trial days are never placed on the docket. 

Complaints issued by Region 14 notify the respondent that its case will be heard sometime during a designated docket week. All cases are noticed for hearing on Monday, or as soon thereafter as the case can be heard. The time between the issuance of a complaint in Region 14 and the initial hearing date is only about three weeks. An attorney representing a respondent is not consulted as to the hearing date which appears in the complaint.

Once the complaint and the scheduled docket week are received by a respondent's counsel, the burden is upon him or her to seek postponements or accommodation. Counsel for a respondent can immediately call the NLRB attorney who is in charge of the case and seek to have a case heard on a particular day during the designated docket week. Counsel for the respondent can also submit to the Region a letter requesting that a case be heard during a subsequent docket week, but such a request will not be granted unless good and sufficient grounds are shown. The large majority of unfair labor practice cases in the St. Louis 

Region are postponed at least once due to written requests from attorneys representing respondents. However, when a case is postponed it is merely scheduled for the next docket week and it is not necessary for the Region to consult with the Chief Administrative Law Judge about a new date.

About ten days prior to the docket week, the Regional Attorney will compile, and circulate within the Region, a Tentative Trial Docket. The Regional Attorney 62 -

attempts to accommodate out-of-town counsel by scheduling them as early in the week as possible, preferably on Monday. After receiving the Tentative 

Trial Docket, counsel for the General Counsel who is going to try the case. will telephone counsel for the respondent for the purpose of additional settlement discussions, or, if settlement is impossible, for the purpose of working out witness problems and stipulations. The government trial attorney does, however, take the occasion to tell counsel for the respondent the day on which a case will probably be heard. Of course, only the trial time established for the first case on Monday morning is definite, but the Region has been quite successful in predicting the length of trials and most cases are heard at the expected times. If an attorney has been told that a case will be heard on 

Wednesday, he or she is not forced to come in before that time, but is often given the opportunity to do so. On the other hand, a case scheduled for Wednesday might not be heard until Thursday if prior cases take longer than expected. 

Frequently, last minute adjustments are made to the Tentative Trial Docket as a result of the telephone conversations between attorneys representing the General

Settlement

The central policy of the National Labor Relations Act is the encouragement of collective bargaining between unions and employers. The function of the 

National Labor Relations Board is to act as the guarantor of the rights of employers and unions to meaningful collective bargaining. As a result, the - 69 - 

---

Board may be said to be charged with two somewhat contradictory duties: first, to enforce the rights contained in the Act in disputes before the Board; and second, to encourage the parties to resolve the disputes themselves through collective bargaining.

The dual purposes of the Act enforcement and encouragement of collective bargaining require the Board to take two distinct roles. In enforcement, the 

Board acts as a prosecutor and seeks compliance with the law. In encouraging the parties to bargain, the Board acts more like a disinterested peacemaker and seeks to refrain from interfering unduly in the bargaining relationship. In fact, these two roles are inseparable as is evident in the Board's settlement function. At the same time that it must be certain any unfair labor practice is remedied, the Board tries to encourage the parties to resolve the case voluntarily.

All Board cases involve one of the three stages of collective bargaining the commencement of the bargaining relationship, the negotiation of a labor agreement, or the application of the agreement. The general problem which all 

Board procedures must therefore attempt to resolve is determining the degree to which the Board should interfere in the parties' bargaining relationship, if any, of striking the proper balance between the Board's prosecutorial and peacemaking functions.

There are, however, some problem cases, usually involving large numbers of discriminatees, which have required inordinate time before compliance was achieved. These cases often involve considerable time in investigation, settlement efforts, and then sometimes extended back-pay hearings before ALJs. Close attention to this problem is appropriate prior to the Task Force's final report. 

A principal problem in this area is assuring compliance with the 1 106

"'cease and desist" injunctive provisions of Board and Court orders. A long line of General Counsels has been quite selective in the utilization of the contempt mechanism -- usually opting to choose proceeding on new charges and further administrative hearings rather than to seek court sanction to implement the Court's enforcement decree. 

Whether or not this approach is correct, the Task Force believes the issues involved are important enough to require its reexamination. 

The General Counsel's very helpful memorandum of September 27, 1976˚, entitled "The Role of Contempt in Effectuating Board Orders," is attached and may serve as a starting point in our consideration of some of the difficult and complicated policy questions involved in making choices in this area.

Among the various questions, some of which are considered in the memorandum, are: (1) What standards should be applied by the General Counsel in choosing between instituting contempt proceedings and filing a new complaint? (2) Should the Board continue to oppose the intervention of charging parties in contempt proceedings? (3) What standard of proof should the General Counsel press for in contempt proceedings? (4) To what extent and how should Board orders in recidivist cases be tailored to make it easier to obtain a contempt finding by the courts?

In addition, the Task Force intends to inquire into whether there is adequate staffing organization within the General Counsel's office 107

to insure prompt determination of whether to initiate contempt proceedings and prompt and effective action when the decision to proceed for contempt is made.

Section 10 (g) provides that the commencement of proceedings for enforcement or review "shall not, unless specifically ordered by the court, operate as a stay of the Board's order."

The complaint, which forms the basis of a request for 10(j) relief, merely indicates reasonable cause to believe that the Act has been violated. Section 10 (e) relief, on the other hand, is based upon a final determination by the Board that unfair labor practices have been committed and that determination, at least insofar as it rests on factual findings, is subject only to limited review by the courts. E. 

Whether a significant issue in the case is one for the exercise of the Board's expertise. 

Issues of first impression are usually not presented to the courts in a contempt context for the obvious reason that the Board's position should be supported by a rationale formulated by the Board rather than one of its attorneys. Similarly, questions of a representational nature are best resolved by the Board. For example, in contempt cases involving withdrawal of recognition from an incumbent union and the unlawful recognition of a competing one, courts are apt to make rulings and even order elections, which are within the Board's province, or, worse, which are repugnant to prevailing Board law. To avoid such situations it may be necessary to forego contempt. Another example of an occasion for seeking the exercise of the Board's rather than the court's judgment is the case against one believed to be a successor to or in an integrated economic enterprise with the original respondent. Concepts of derivative liability in Board law are different from those in contract or tort law and an analysis of the issues may lead to the conclusion that the dispute is best resolved in a supplementary proceeding before the Board, e.g., a backpay proceeding, rather than before a special master, not versed in Board law. 

Whether the respondent is a repeated offender or had engaged in massive violations in the underlying proceedings.
