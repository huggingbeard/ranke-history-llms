FOREWORD 

Regionalization of the country's health care systems is more and more coming to be regarded as one of the most exciting and potentially valuable concepts for providing greater availability of the highest quality of care for all Americans. 

In hearings before the Senate Veterans' Affairs Committee, testimony has been developed to indicate that the Department of Medicine and Surgery of the Veterans' Administration is not only involved in such regionalization in concert with other elements of the private sector of the country's health care systems, but is planning and implementing its own regionalization process in the direct interest of the Nation's veterans it has been mandated by the Congress to serve. 

Senate Committee Print No. 4, Regionalization of Veterans' Admin istration's health care system was published by this committee in November 1971 as an interim report on the progress of the Department of Medicine and Surgery in applying the regionalization concept. It is revised at this time to reflect the concept of regionalization as applied to the medical care field in general and to the VA's health delivery system in particular.

The Senate Veterans' Affairs Committee believes that by these ef forts the Veterans' Administration's Department of Medicine and Sur gery has demonstrated a capacity not only to keep pace with its counterparts in the public sector, but to assume a leadership role in insuring better health care to veterans and nonveterans alike.

PREFACE 

In fulfilling the mission assigned to it by the Congress over the past quarter century, the Veterans' Administration's Department of Medicine and Surgery has developed the largest centrally directed health care system in the Nation. The services of that system are represented by some 150,000 physicians, dentists, nurses, and members of all other categories of professional, technical, and related health personnel working in 168 hospitals, 207 clinics, and 80 nursing homes in 179 separate cities and towns in 50 States. These services are supplemented, as necessary, by other fee or contractual arrangements with privately practicing physicians, dentists, and other public and private hospitals, clinics, and nursing homes to care for veteran patients who cannot be served within the system for various geographical or other reasons. During fiscal year 1972 this health care system provided inpatient care in nearly 1 million cases and provided over 9 million outpatient visits. 

Within the last few years the need to develop a method for insuring maximum service, utilization, and cost benefits of that system has been recognized. Because of the magnitude of both its facilities and the demands upon them, it was the decision of the Veterans' Administration and its Department of Medicine and Surgery that the process of regionalization can help achieve this goal.

REGIONALIZATION FOR HEALTH CARE 

Regionalization is a concept already in operation nationally in such areas as transportation, marketing, and communications. Its application to health care gained national understanding and acceptance and is being implemented as a result of two legislative measures enacted into law by the 89th Congress. The first was Public Law 89-239 passed in 1965 which provided for the establishment of regional medical programs as a functional effort in regionalization; the other, passed in 1966, was Public Law 89-749 which authorized the creation of comprehensive health planning organizations at both the State and local levels. Both laws, in effect, called for the establishment of cooperative community, State and/or regional efforts to not only identify health care needs, but to develop and implement plans to meet those needs in the most effective, equitable, efficient, and economic way.

To date, such efforts have helped to demonstrate how cooperative arrangements can achieve maximum utilization of institutional, professional, and financial resources. They have also served to show how expensive and wasteful duplication of equipment and personnel can be avoided, and how the all-too-prevalent selfish autonomy of various health care institutions and those who direct their services can be approached and altered.

INVOLVEMENT OF VA IN REGIONALIZATION 

First by voluntary action and subsequently by legislation, the De partment of Medicine and Surgery of the Veterans' Administration has become actively involved in this effort on the national level, and the elements of its system have done likewise at the regional and community level.

The initial voluntary effort was represented by the involvement of VA hospitals in the early development of regional medical programs in regions where those hospitals had already established themselves as an integral part of the local health care system. Further official involvement was provided for in Public Law 91-515 which amended and extended the original regional medical programs and comprehensive health planning legislation. Under those amendments, the

Chief Medical Director was directed to sit ex officio on the National Advisory Council of Regional Medical Programs Service, and each regional medical program was called upon to include a representative from local VA hospitals on its Regional Advisory Group. Similarly, State comprehensive health planning agencies with VA hospitals located therein were called upon to include VA representatives on their councils.

Another piece of legislation, also passed in 1966 as Public Law 89785, made possible a whole series of additional cooperative activities between elements of the VA health care system and those of the pri- (1) 89-836 O 73 - 2

vate sector at the community and regional level. Called the "sharing law" it provided that selected specialized VA medical resources might be made available to the nonveteran population of a community in which such health benefits were not otherwise provided by their own institutions, or were in too short supply to meet the community's total needs. Conversely, facilities or specialized services provided by medical schools or community hospitals in the private sector which might not be found in the local VA hospitals could also be made available to them. Thus, in effect, the new law permitted, and indeed encouraged, the institutions of the Federal and private sector of any community of functional health care region to exchange or share services as an alternative to the expense of duplicating them, or the dangers of going without them.

Within the framework of the VA hospital-medical school affiliations developed over the preceding 25 years, such sharing arrangements were quickly put into operation in highly specialized surgery, nuclear medicine, X-ray therapy, and other new, expensive, and complex areas. 

However, the sharing concept has taken longer to implement in nonmedical center areas where other VA hospitals also are found. 

This entire concept of total regionalization, including the Federalprivate mix, is best described by Dr. David Rutstein, professor and chairman of the Department of Preventive Medicine at Harvard Medical School, in his book "The Coming Revolution in Medicine" (MIT 

Press 1967). He writes:

The plan [regionalization] is in no sense a fixed blueprint of a medical care system-it may not predict what will happen. Instead it indicates what could happen if we took full advantage of our resources, knowledge and skills. Whether or not this plan, or details of it, be implemented, one thing is clear. The consequences of not planning well for the future of medicine are too grave to contemplate. Let me give you a simple outline of the plan. We shall emphasize the need for the regional structure of medical care programs, propose a more effective use of medical manpower, indicate ways in which the physicians' time may be more strategically applied, take advantage of rapidly advancing automation and technology, suggest added dimensions for medical research, and recommend a system of medical education to meet future needs. Not every hospital will be able to provide what every patient needs for the prevention and treatment of his disease. If this dilemma is to be resolved, we cannot build the medical system of the future around self-centered individual hospitals as we do today. Instead, through careful planning and efficient transportation and communications systems we have to weave together all medical facilities and personnel into regional medical service areas centered upon large technical hospitals that are affiliated with medical schools.

VA REGIONALIZATION-A BEGINNING AND ITS GOALS 

The catalyst for effecting regionalization within the Veterans' Administration's health care system was the appointment by the Administrator of Veterans' Affairs, Mr. Donald E. Johnson, of Dr. Marc J.

Musser as Chief Medical Director of the Veterans' Administration's Department of Medicine and Surgery in January 1970. The selection by the Administrator of Dr. Musser as his Chief Medical Director brought an experienced medical leader to the Veterans' Administration. Five years prior to that time, Dr. Musser accepted the responsibility for regionalizing the health services of North Carolina when he became coordinator of that State's regional medical program, established under Public Law 89–239. During those 5 years Dr. Musser

became one of the Nation's leaders in developing mechanisms for implementing health care regionalization. With the support and encouragement of Mr. Donald E. Johnson, Administrator, Dr. Musser proceeded to apply these concepts of regionalization to the U.S.-wide 

Veterans' Administration's health care system. 

By January of 1970 many VA hospitals, clinics, nursing homes, and other facilities and services were already included in some of the long-range plans of regional medical programs and comprehensive health planning agencies. Indeed, many had become active participants in early projects being carried out by some of the country's 55 regional medical programs then in operation. However, because of its specialized mission and the magnitude of the VA system, it was agreed. that the VA had to develop its own blueprint for regionalizing, to the extent compatible with the fulfillment of the overall mission of the VA, its own facilities and services to achieve the two major goals of Insuring the availability of a maximum range of health services to its veteran patients as close as possible to their homes, and do so with the highest possible professional quality, organizational efficiency, and the lowest possible cost. Maintaining a system that had come to provide the single greatest potential for the development of not only its own physician and allied health manpower, but a resource for training and supplying such critical personnel to the private sector; an unequalled base for cooperative clinical research; and a community resource for services, as necessary, under the sharing concept. 

CRITERIA OF VA REGIONAL DISTRICTS 

For planning purposes, the criteria for VA regional districts were established as functional geographical areas wherein- • The veteran population would be sufficiently large to produce a demand for VA medical care to support all general medical, surgical, and psychiatric services, as well as a full spectrum of special services to be found in any major medical area in the private sector.

Conversely, sufficient VA facilities and resources must be available to provide such services for the population to be served. • Two or more VA hospitals must be included with at least one of the hospitals having a full affiliation with a university medical center, or with a major accredited private practice clinic. 

While it was agreed that each regional district would be planned and operated as a comprehensive self-sufficient health care system, certain exceptions were recognized. • There could be inter-Regional District services where the population base of one or more provide only limited demand on certain areas of specialization such as organ transplantation, stereotactic surgery, blind rehabilitation, renal dialysis and care of spinal cord injuries. • Sharing or exchange arrangements or contract services could be negotiated with other Federal institutions or those in the private sector where VA facilities themselves cannot provide all of the services, or are not adequate to meet the demands.

Conversely, where an excess of VA capabilities may exist, full consideration should be given to utilizing them to complement or supplement the needs of nonveteran patients by similar arrangements.

It was also decided that the selection of hospitals to form a regional district would be primarily functional, and not necessarily be limited to geographical or political boundaries. As indicated, in making such decisions, factors relevant to the demands of patients, available services and the quality of the care to be provided will take precedence over administrative expediency or undue considerations of cost. Also, the established lines of patient flow and professional referrals will be encouraged within the regional districts so that each can be operated essentially as self-contained health care delivery system. 

ROLE OF VA FACILITIES IN REGIONALIZATION 

Under regionalization, the mission of each VA hospital, clinic, and other facilities is being identified to emphasize their strengths so that they can contribute in the most efficient way possible to the total health care system of the regional district. No facility's mission will be proscribed as an expedient to place it in a primary or secondary position to its colleagues. However, it is recognized that the hospitals affiliated with medical schools will generally represent the base of the regional district. The services of the general and specialized hospitals and other facilities relative to those base hospitals will be revised and strengthened as necessary to supplement or complement each other. A hospital, for example, to be truly supportive to the other cooperating hospitals may be expanded and its mission redefined as a modern chronic disease hospital. As such it could be further staffed and equipped to provide additional professional capabilities for providing care for arthritic, cardiac and pulmonary patients. 

DISTRIBUTION OF RESOURCES AND UTILIZATION 

As indicated, it is expected that regionalization will provide the framework for appropriate distribution of resources among all of the facilities within each self-contained regional district to include and effectively coordinate all specialized medical services. Decisions of how much services are to be distributed will take into account a number of critical factors including those involved with ease of access for patients, availability of necessary skills, and volume of utilization. In effecting such a distribution, regionalization must continue to be recognized as a method for the improvement of patient care through more effective utilization of resources within a health care system.

The desired results of regionalization in the form of proper and adequate utilization can be realized through the development and use of cooperative and mutually shared programs between and among all of the institutions of the regional district, but only if regionalization in each regional district provides- • Easier access to the system to enable patients to be admitted initially to any hospital or ambulatory care clinic.

• Increased patient mobility to improve transfer procedures that are immediately responsive to the needs of the patients in terms of placing them in the proper facilities where appropriate diagnosis and/or treatment consistent with their needs is available. • Increased staff mobility to insure that consultative services are continuously and readily available between and among all facilities. 

Optimum and efficient use of specialized services to insure that they are operated at the critical level of demand to keep them at the necessary level of proficiency. 

Improved data collection and transmission to provide standardized patient records and their rapid transmission to support clinical needs among all facilities. • Adequate peer review within the regional district designed to involve the representative staff and faculty of the cooperating hospitals and the medical schools utilizing their diverse professional skills. 

EDUCATION AND EXCHANGE OF MEDICAL INFORMATION 

Regionalization interrelates not only the institutions in a given regional district, but the professional and technical staffs working in or associated with them. Therefore, it follows that a common level of competence must exist in each of them. Such interdependence and collaboration makes the establishment of such standards a more easily achieved goal in each regional district as a result of— • Utilization of such teaching skills throughout the system which relieves each institution of maintaining its own educational programs, and allows it to take advantage of what is commonly available and concentrate its own efforts in training those needed for its own mission. A single system which facilitates the mobility, wide utilization of clinical staff, consultants, faculty, and technical personnel to transmit new knowledge. Development of teachers, and training programs in the various specialties and disciplines on a regional basis utilizing those sites and personnel best suited to provide training to meet total identified regional needs.

Permitting the application of the principles of scale economy in the purchase and use of highly qualified teachers and sophisticated training devices that could not be justified for a single hospital.

By the same logic, allowing for development and maintenance of programs for the exchange of medical information which utilize computer oriented data base, closed circuit television networks, telephone consultation service, and other new modes and electronic media.

RESEARCH 

The growing shortage of available Federal and private funds for biomedical research is causing an increasing level of concern in the country's medical community. In seeking ways of coordinating and

consolidating such activities, the type of collaborative research developed within the VA system over the past 25 years has gained prominence. 

In effect, the VA research effort has been regionalized for years in that it has encouraged the coordination of such activities in its facilities and among its own staff in collaboration with the country's medical schools. As a result, these efforts are regionalized not geographically, but professionally and scientifically. 

Under the regionalization plan, this type of effort will not only continue to be developed, but have the potential for operating within and between regional districts as considered appropriate by those directing and involved with this effort within the system. 

COST CONTROL 

After utilization, a major objective of regionalization is the control of the cost of medical care within the regional district to achieve not only a realistic cost benefit level. but to permit adequate funding allocations to those program areas that are most supportive of each hospital's mission and thereby the total delivery system within the regional district. 

This objective can only be realized through the evolutionary development and refinement of each regionalization process. However, it can be established that the management matrix must include— • Health system benefits related to availability of improved services and quality of care to more patients at a lower total and per patient cost resulting from increased demands; more rapid turnover rates; decreased length of stay; and increased occupancy rates. • Cost avoidance which will depend upon the proper assignment and maximum utilization of not only physicians, nurses, and other categories of health manpower, but employment of the most modern medical techniques including automated equipment and the use of computers and other technologies to replace obsolete equipment and methodologies, less effective techniques, and costly duplication of services.

Cost reduction which is closely related to cost avoidance, but reflects actual monetary savings through reduced costs of personnel services, equipment and materials, and the use of contractual services when saving can be realized with no effect on quality. 

DEVELOPMENT OF COMMUNITY RELATIONSHIPS AND INVOLVEMENT 

Regionalization will enhance the interaction between VA facilities and other public and private institutions and organizations and the communities they serve. Thus, over and above the increased and more efficient use of VA facilities for their own patients, regionalization provides the means for developing better community relationships by identifying all resources in the regional district, both of the public and private sectors. Then, through sharing arrangements made possible by Public Law 89-785, all health resources can be utilized to the maximum advantage of veterans living in that area.

ADMINISTRATION 

Successful operation of a regionalization program depends heavily upon the strength and quality of the administrative and professional leadership which exists and the firmness of the commitment of the several component institutions. To generate and maintain these, there must be appropriate involvement of each participating institution in planning, policy determination, and evaluation. 

Based upon these principles, each regional district has attempted to develop an organizational structure consistent with its local resources and needs. In general, this has followed the pattern of cooperative effort that has been so effective in regional medical programs over the past 6 years. In most instances, each region's cooperative group has identified and designated its functional leadership. Also, in their developmental activities, regional districts have emphasized three basic requirements:

Flexibility to vary any initial plan over time to provide the most functionally effective ultimate model consistent with the local goals and objectives.

Accountability, in this case to the Department of Medicine and Surgery, in terms of meeting all of the national goals and objectives set by the criteria of regionalization, and by the continued implementation of the congressional mandate to the Department. • Evaluation to establish a baseline and mechanisms to measure the development and progress of the regionalization effort in terms of increased quality and availability of medical care. 

Overall operational responsibility is vested in the designated leadership, as the regional district director, and various cooperative groups serve in effect as a "board of directors" or "executive and planning committees," and other advisory and developmental components. This organizational structure also provides for the necessary liaison with other community and areawide health interests. 

Thus far a number of organizational models have been developed. 

As these are evaluated over time, the most effective organizational structure will be identified. 

THE REGIONALIZATION PLAN 

Having applied the concept, criteria, and goals described herein to the total VA health care system, the decision was made to establish 37 regional districts. Under the plan, each would embody or have the potential for fulfilling the objectives outlined in this paper to provide a total health care system for veterans. 

As constituted, facilities of each regional district are within 100 miles or not more than 2 hours away from 88 percent of each of the population groups that constitute the 28 million veterans who are the potential patients of the total system. 

On July 1, 1970, only 6 months after the decision was made to regionalize the system, the first regional district was established in southern California. Since that time 36 additional regional districts

have been activated.
