Foreword Tins WORKBOOK provides study mat 

HIS WORKBOOK provides study-materials designed to aid the student in the Although it is particularly adapted for use with Vaughan's General Psychology, Revised (Doubleday, Doran & Co., Inc., 1939), many sections may be adapted for use with other beginning texts. Each chapter contains four parts: (1) Correlating Summary, (2) Projects, (3) Experi ments, and (4) Tests.

Each Correlating Summary indicates important relationships among the several chapters of the basic text, thus facilitating the student's comprehension of the textbook as a whole. After the first reading of an assigned chapter in the textbook, the student should turn to the Correlating Summary in the corresponding chapter of the Manual. During his reading of this passage he should turn to those sections in Vaughan's text for which chapter and page references are given, and integrate the discussion there with the subject matter of the chapter being studied. In this way the student will be helped to appreciate the various ramifications and applications of basic psychological principles. 

The Projects provide an opportunity for further reading and investigation to supplement the study of the textbook. The instructor may assign a certain project for a written or oral report, or the student may use one or several projects as the starting-point for the satisfaction of his personal interest in certain topics. 

Further, the questions and references make it possible for the instructor to extend the scope of his course according to his particular needs. The selected bibliographies are not intended to be all-inclusive nor even to include all of the "standard" references, but, rather, only those which will ordinarily be available and intelligible to the beginning student. If the instructor desires, he may assign the task of compiling a more complete bibliography for a certain project. 

The Experiments illustrate or demonstrate certain significant aspects of the textbook discussion. They are so planned that, except for a few homemade materials, they can be performed without any supplies beyond those provided

in the workbook. Most of the Experiments may be performed either by the instructor as group experiments or demonstrations, or by students working in pairs. The directions for each experiment are written in terms of an E (experimenter) and an S (subject). If the experiment is performed as a class experiment, the E will probably be the instructor, the S's being the members of the class. If an experiment is done by students working in pairs, one student will serve as E, the other as S. In each experiment, the opening paragraphs contain the experimental directions. In some experiments it will be advisable for the instructor to request the students not to read beyond these introductory directions before performing the experiment. This will eliminate the possibility that the student's performance in the experiment might be influenced by his anticipation of its outcome. At the end of each experiment there are questions which may be used in preparation of a written report. 

The Tests at the end of each chapter may either be employed to examine the student on his mastery of the text, or they may be assigned as self-tests to aid the student in his study of a certain chapter or his review of a certain section. 

The inclusion of alternate tests in many of the chapters makes possible their use in a variety of ways. For each chapter and for each Part of the textbook there are two kinds of tests. Certain tests are composed of essay-type questions; the remaining tests consist of objective-type questions, of which there are two forms. The True-False tests are one-sentence declarative statements, some of which are correct and some of which are incorrect. As the student studies each statement in turn, he decides whether, on the basis of the textbook discussion, it is True or False. He indicates his decision by writing on the blank line at the right-hand margin the symbol T (True) or F (False). The latter part of each objective-type test contains several Multiple-Choice or Best-Answer questions. Here there is an incomplete statement followed by several words or phrases, each designated by a number. One of these words or phrases would make the sentence an accurate statement, the others would make it incorrect or meaningless. The student decides which word or phrase is most appropriate, and writes its number on the line at the right of the question. 

The scoring of the tests will depend upon the individual preference of the instructor. Some teachers wish to make allowance for chance factors in objective-type tests by subtracting the number of incorrect answers from the number of correct answers to obtain the final score. The present arrangement leaves the scoring to the discretion of the user.¹ 

The practical value of this workbook will be enhanced if the student refrains from investigating unassigned sections. The effectiveness of the Experiments and Tests, especially, will be diminished if one or several students become familiar with them before they are assigned. It is the intention and hope of the authors that the student's use of this Manual will be a pleasure rather than an ordeal, and that with proper handling it will have the effect of increasing the student's enthusiasm for the study of psychology. PSYCHOLOGY UNSCIENTIFIC, PRESCIENTIFIC, AND SCIENTIFIC 

TH HIS FIRST CHAPTER is devoted to stating the subject-matter with which the psychologist is concerned and considering the methods by which he works. 

The student will note that the subject-matter of the psychologist is not at all unique or uncommon; psychology is defined as the study of human activities. 

It is the method of carrying on this study which is especially important in defining the field of psychology. In his approach to the study of human activities the psychologist is guided by the requirements of scientific thinking and the scientific method. 

The student should recognize and be able to characterize the different kinds of thinking which may be conducted upon the same question. For a discussion of the kind of thinking which results in erroneous or superstitious conclusions, the student should read the section on belief (pages 485-491). This should be contrasted with the discussion of scientific thinking (pages 494-500). 

At various points in the textbook the author calls attention to the ineffective thinking which is done with reference to many important current issues (see, for example, pages 577-580).

The descriptions of the experiments on the conditioned response and on reaction time are included in Chapter 1. to illustrate the application of the scientific method to actual problems. In reading about them, the student should attempt to compare the scientific treatment of these topics with commonsense ideas about them.

The results of the experiments on conditioning have had a great deal of significance in psychology, especially in the fields of learning (pages 309-311 and 443-444) and child training (pages 319-330). By referring to these sections the student can obtain an introductory insight into the manner in which scientific findings may be applied and extended. 1

AN ACCURATE MEASUREMENT of reaction time requires the use of very precise apparatus. However, a rough picture of relative reaction times to various stimulus situations may be obtained by means of the so-called chainreaction method.

DIRECTIONS: A group of S's, preferably not less than eight nor more than twelve, arrange themselves so that they stand in a continuous circle, with each S facing the center of the circle. One member of the group acts as timer; the timer stands as a regular member of the circle. The timer is equipped with a stop-watch or some means of recording time accurately in units no greater than seconds.

The procedure involves a predetermined signal given by the timer to the S on his right; this S responds by giving the signal to the S on his right, and so around the circle until the timer receives the signal from the S on his left. The timer measures the time which elapses from the instant at which he gives the first signal until he receives the signal from the S at his left. Each S must be careful to respond only to the signal from the S immediately at his left, and each S must be prepared to respond immediately upon receiving the signal. 

Several kinds of signals should be employed in this experiment; there should be five trials with each signal. For the measurement of tactual reaction time, the signal may be a tap on the shoulder or a squeeze of the hand. Visual reaction time may be tested with a signal such as extending the index finger from the closed fist. Auditory reaction time may be investigated by means of a signal consisting of a single, sharply spoken syllable, such as "Baa.” 

One part of the experiment may test what is known as choice reaction time. 

Here there will be two possible signals, each being assigned a specific response. 

The signal which is to be chosen is known only to the timer, and the response of the individual S is determined by the nature of the signal which he receives. 

A record blank should be prepared. The total elapsed time for each trial is recorded in each part of the experiment, and the average reaction time for that part of the experiment is computed. These averages are the arithmetic means of the times for the five trials in that part of the experiment. 

QUESTIONS: Compare the average reaction times for the various parts of the experiment. Are there consistent differences? If so, how would you account for them? 

Was there any evidence of a practice effect? Would you expect practice to influence reaction time? What other conditions might influence reaction time?

SYCHOLOGY WAS DEFINED in Chapter I as the study of human activities. 

It was shown also in Chapter I that there are numerous phases or aspects of this subject-matter, and this fact has resulted in the various points of view concerning the nature and methods of psychology. In studying Chapter 2 the student should make certain that for each of the points of view he knows what aspect of human activity is dealt with and by what methods it is studied. 

Throughout the textbook there are illustrations of matters which are of particular interest to one or another of the systematic points of view. It will aid the student's understanding of Chapter 2 if he will look ahead to instances of the areas included within each point of view. The introspective methods of the Elementarist, for example, are especially well-fitted for the study of such visual experiences as after-images and contrast (pages 93-95), or for the investigation of sensations of touch (pages 119-123). Other examples may be found in the discussion of problems of attention (pages 396-406), or in the analysis of the mental images which are present in thinking (pages 482-485) and remembering (pages 423-429).

The conflict between the Elementaristic and Configurational approaches originated in the study of perception and attention (pages 351-353 and 364373). Another example of the Gestalt point of view is represented by the concept of insight in learning (pages 440-443). Still another phase of Configurationism is to be found in the topological representation of human drives (pages 255-259). This may be contrasted with the Hormic school, which is represented by an inventory of the various urges (pages 216–241). 

The Functionalist's interest is revealed nowhere more vividly than in the discussion of the role of habits in everyday life (pages 330-346). Similarly, in 

James' discussion of the emotions it is the function of the emotional responses which is emphasized (pages 208–211). 

It was in the study of animal learning that Behaviorism had its starting point (see pages 439-444). The obstruction method of measuring animal drives (pages 253–255) is an example of the purely objective observation of behavior; the same attention to overt behavior-patterns may be noted in the studies of human emotion by means of the so-called methods of expression (pages 245-253). Watson's argument that thinking is sub-vocal speech (page 

483) is illustrative of the manner in which the Behaviorist handles what is usually thought of as a mental process. 13

The Psychoanalysts' interest in the role of the unconscious is shown very well in the discussion of mental conflict (especially pages 261-266). Furthermore, unconscious factors seem to be important in dreams, which is another field to which the Psychoanalysts have made notable contributions (pages 411-421). Any consideration of human motives or urges must take into account the psychoanalytic concept of the libido and the various forms in which it manifests itself (see, for example, pages 219-223). Jung's typology, based upon the characteristics of the introvert and the extrovert, has found a place for itself in the rating and description of personality (pages 611 and 636–638). 

If the student will turn to these various citations he will gain a greater understanding of the complexity which characterizes the subject-matter of psychology, and will be more appreciative of the interrelationships of the various points of view. He will understand, too, the meaning of and the reasons for the synoptic principle which is adopted by the author of the textbook.

The highly specialized functions of the various sense organs are suggested at the start of the chapter; the student should be alert for evidence of this specialization in the discussion of the various receptors. Moreover, the student should observe that this differentiation of function extends to the component parts of the sense organs themselves. For the description and illustration of techniques by means of which the sensitivity of the sense organs is determined, the student should refer to the section on psychophysical method (pages 595–601). For a further discussion and explanation of the effects of adaptation and habituation, see pages 315-318.

The discussion of specific energies (pages 145-146) provides further information on this point. 

The senses of sight, hearing, and touch are the means by which our experiences of space are organized. The combined action of the two eyes (binocular vision) and of the two ears (binaural hearing) are the bases for perceiving visual and auditory space (pages 381-388); the "local sign" is the basis for perceiving tactual space (pages 380-381). The kinesthetic sense is important in establishing patterns of muscular activity (pages 177–178). 

The sensory experience of hunger differs somewhat from other sensory experiences because it acts directly as one of the motives of human behavior (see pages 217-218). 25

DIRECTIONS: The pupillary reflex. S is seated comfortably in a position facing a moderately strong source of light, whether a bright window or artificial illumination. S closes his eyes and keeps them closed until told by E to open them. E holds a four-inch square of heavy cardboard directly over and against S's right eye, so that no light can shine directly upon the eye. S is instructed to open his eyes. HUMAN URGES AND MOTIVATION THE 

HE STUDY of human motivation is prompted by the desire to find out why people behave as they do. The discussion is made difficult by the obscurity of motives; the student should review here the general problem of getting information about motives (pages 71-75), and should appreciate the effect of this problem upon a systematic presentation in Chapter 6. The author points out that the classification of urges or motives is one of convenience, which fails to do justice to the complexity of human desires. Much of the psychologist's information about motives is obtained indirectly. Important values and goals are sometimes revealed in daydreams (pages 416-419), and inventories of interests, attitudes, and values (pages 613-617) provide some insight. 

The fact that motives are obscure and complex does not diminish their importance in human activity. Some psychologists make the hormic aspects of behavior the keystone of their systematic theories (pages 61-66). 

Many of the personal adjustments of everyday experience are made necessary because of conflicting motives (Chapter 7). Social living demands that the urges and emotions of the individual be reconciled with the wants of others (pages 9-10, 648-649, and 681-691) and with the demands of custom and convention (pages 655-660). The significance of motives may also be observed in their influence upon thinking; much of the thinking which people do concerning their problems is governed more by what they want or wish to believe than by reason or logic (pages 485–491 and 506–509).

The physical mechanisms in emotion have been studied already. The student should review at this time the parts played by the thalamus (pages 163-165), the autonomic nervous system (pages 170-174), and the glandular responses (pages 199-202). The disorganizing effects of emotion are well illustrated in the experiments of Luria (pages 267-271); likewise, emotional balance is considered to be one of the components of intelligent behavior (pages 534-537). Because the emotions have such a strong influence upon intelligent activity, the early training of the emotions in children deserves special attention (pages 322-330); the attainment of emotional maturity is essential if an individual is to make effective adjustments to his environment (pages 311-314). 61

THE FORMATION OF HABITS 

T IS DIFFICULT to limit the discussion of habit formation to a single chapter because the topic is concerned so intimately with many phases of human behavior. To a large extent, the development of the individual is the formation of habits, and it has even been remarked that a person is his habits. 

The student might think of this chapter as a general discussion of the ways in which the human individual is molded by his experience. The Functional school of psychology lays special emphasis on the importance of habits in human adjustment (pages 59-61), and yet the introductory discussion in Chapter 8 indicates that this same emphasis may be found in Watson (pages 

66-70) and Adler (pages 64-66).

Studies of the development of intelligence have provided much evidence for the Nature-Nurture controversy (pages 526-528). The "instinct hypothesis" has been another point of dispute in this controversy (pages 216–217). 

The phenomena of adaptation are simple illustrations of habituation. 

Adaptation occurs in vision (pages 96–97), touch (pages 121 and 123), taste (page 125), and smell (page 127). The necessity of becoming adapted to noise is one of the interesting problems of our contemporary civilization (pages 405-406). Attending is determined in part by adaptation (page 357). 

Habit formation and learning must be looked upon as related aspects of the same general process; the conditioned response (pages 26-30 and 443444) is the simple prototype of both. The factors which influence habit formation also determine learning; some of these similarities may be observed in the case of perseveration (page 433), repetition (pages 446-447), and reward and punishment (pages 447-449).

Evidence for the specificity of habits has been of great weight in the issues which arise in a discussion of the transfer of training, a matter which is important both in habit formation and in educational training (pages 449-451). 

The reading of Chapter 8 should reveal to the student the importance of habits in the more intimate problems of living. The training of the emotions and the development of emotional maturity have been discussed previously (pages 223-238). Habits are equally prominent in the development of character (pages 641-648) and in the organization of personality (pages 692–693). 95

Socrates is a man

(Conclusion) 

In this experiment, S studies the eighteen logical arguments which are presented below in the form of syllogisms. S's first task is to decide whether or not each syllogism is logically correct as it is stated, that is, whether or not the conclusion follows logically from the stated premises. In this part of the experiment, S should not concern himself with the correctness of either the major premise or the minor premise, nor with the truth or falsity of the conclusion. The questions in this test are to be answered by brief, concise statements of fact, which S writes on the blanks provided at the end of each question. Because the answers to the questions would not be the same for all localities, a definite community must be selected with reference to which the questions are to be answered. Usually, non-resident students are not sufficiently acquainted with the community where they are attending school to answer questions concerning specific details of the local civic government. It is entirely feasible for each S to reply to the questions of this test in terms of his own home community or any other community with which he is particularly familiar.
