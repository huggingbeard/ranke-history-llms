​JULIANA AS A PRINCESS 

Hardly ever in the history of a nation has the birth of a princess given rise to such rejoicing as the birth, on 

April 30, 1909, of Juliana Louise Emma Marie Wilhel mina, daughter of Queen Wilhelmina of The Netherlands and Prince Henry, Duke of Mecklenburg, who were married in 1901. The Netherlands people had so eagerly awaited an heir to the throne for eight years that when the message "A princess is born" was flashed throughout the country, the joy of the people knew no bounds. A successor to the throne had been given them, and everywhere throngs danced in the streets. The usually conservative Dutch could not be exuberant enough to express their elation.

Like her mother, Queen Wilhelmina, whose three halfbrothers died before she was four years old, Juliana was an only child; but in both cases the close bond between mother and daughter did much to overcome the many problems of an only child. Raised and educated under the personal guidance of their mothers, they both learned early the traits that form the Dutch national character, simplicity, tolerance, sense of duty, levelheadedness and dislike of pomp. They were trained from early childhood to be ready to assume sovereignty and the responsibilities involved. As Queen Mother Emma taught her

daughter, so did Queen Wilhelmina in turn teach Juliana that "the Queen belongs to the people." "It CAN be done..." Although an only child,

Juliana had a happy childhood. She was educated with several little girls of her own age, and was allowed as much freedom as possible. She was an intelligent child and, although generally obedient and willing, sometimes inclined to be unruly and stubborn. Once, when she was quite small, she very much wanted to water some flowers that she was tending. However, at the time, she was dressed in her very best; and her governess, afraid that she would spoil her dress, forbade her to do so. After several attempts on the part of the Princess to have her way, the governess quietly said, "It CANNOT be done and it MAY NOT be done." To which Juliana replied, crimson with anger, "It CAN be done, but it MAY NOT be done."

Stubborness is a family trait which Juliana also inherited. Years later, in 1938, when she launched a liner named "Oranje" after the House of Orange, the ship would not budge from the slip after the traditional bottle of champagne was smashed against its hull. Said Juliana's husband, Prince Bernhard: "What do you expect? You're dealing with an Orange and they only do what they want.”

Such little anecdotes about the Princess were, and still are, cherished by her people. Pictures of Juliana on her first pair of skates and on her first bicycle, as well as many others of her, were to be seen in practically every household. And this link between the Netherlanders and their Royal Family grew stronger as the years went by. 

Princess Juliana's education was thorough, yet marked with simplicity. She received her elementary schooling under the guidance of an outstanding Dutch teacher. Her

musical instruction was given by a well-known composer. 

At the age of fourteen, she was studying English, French and German.

On her 18th birthday, Juliana, according to the constitution, became of age and a member of the Council of State, the highest advisory body of the nation. From then on, she often represented her mother on various state occasions. "It's Jula, Madame..." 

That same year, and at her own special request, she entered Leiden University, the oldest of all Dutch universities, which was founded in 1575 by her ancestor, William the Silent, as a tribute to the heroic defense of that city against the Spanish troops in the Eighty Years' War with Spain. She became a member of the Women Students' Club and led the life of the average student.

Juliana lived with three of her most intimate girl friends in a small villa at Katwijk, a picturesque sea resort and fishermen's village on the outskirts of Leiden. 

She sometimes made the trip from The Hague to Leiden by train, and in every way shared the life of her fellow students.

An indication of her democratic spirit and her endeavor to be an "equal" with the other girls is shown in the following incident during her college life. She was invited to a fancy dress ball, but having no costume to wear nor time to order one, a friend offered to lend her one. 

Juliana accepted with enthusiasm, and that same evening the bell of the girl's house rang. As the maid happened to be out, the mother herself opened the door, and not recognizing her visitor in the darkness, abruptly asked: "Who is it?" A very timid but courteous voice answered:

"It's Jula, Madame. I've come for the costume that Lili promised me."

Juliana often refers to this period at the University as one of the happiest in her life. In subsequent years, she has attended, and still attends, many class reunions, and often has been seen marching, arm in arm, with her student friends in the traditional procession through the streets of Leiden at these anniversaries. 

When one of these girls later on was presented to her officially at Court, and started to address her in the prescribed way as "Your Royal Highness," the Princess started to laugh, and remarked that her name had not changed from "Jula" since her student days.

MARRIAGE OF JULIANA 

In 1936, the announcement was made of the engagement of Princess Juliana to Prince Bernhard of Lippe Biesterfeld. Having no previous inkling of it, the people first heard this news by means of the radio. That same evening, the young couple drove unattended through The Hague, not in an official car but in a two-seater. Bernhard won the instant approval of the people by his simplicity and charm. 

Their wedding on January 7, 1937, was a magnificent affair and was personally arranged by Queen Wilhelmina, who, although usually disliking any show of ostentation, found this occasion an outlet for her great happiness over the marriage of her beloved daughter.

Until the German invasion in May 1940, the young couple lived quietly in their palace at Soestdijk. One wing of this beautiful place, which had formerly belonged to Dowager Queen Emma, Queen Wilhelmina's mother,

had been entirely modernized as a wedding present from the Dutch people to their only princess. 

Their first child was born at Soestdijk on January 31, 1938. She was called Beatrix Wilhelmina Armgard (Beatrix meaning "She who brings happiness" and her other two names after her maternal and paternal grandmothers). A year and a half later, on August 5, 1939, their second daughter was born. She was named Irene, meaning "Peace." But even then the war clouds were hanging over them; and ironically enough when the little girl was baptized, it was on foreign soil (Buckingham Palace in England) while Holland was occupied by the enemy.

Directly after the Germans invaded Holland in 1940, Prince Bernhard received orders to take his wife and children to England. They left The Hague on Sunday, May 13th, and at IJmuiden they boarded a British destroyer. Their journey was marked with anxiety, as the destroyer was sighted by German planes and several attempts to bomb the ship were made. A magnetic mine was dropped in front of the vessel, but fortunately failed to explode. After a short sojourn in England, Juliana and her two daughters were taken to Canada. 

Never a better envoy. While in Canada, Princess

Juliana never ceased working for the Netherlands cause. 

One of the newspapers wrote, on the occasion of one of her visits to the United States, that "Queen Wilhelmina never had a better envoy to represent The Netherlands in Canada and the United States than her daughter."

She lived in a simple villa just outside of Ottawa; and whenever it was possible to leave her children, she made informal visits by plane or train to attend ceremonies, inspect military camps and hospitals, and in general ac-

quaint herself with the efforts of both the American and Canadian people in behalf of the war against the common enemy. By her dignity, simplicity and sweetness, she won the hearts of all who met her. She was a specia! favorite with the Dutch seamen and sailors, and she never failed to visit the Seamen's Home in New York and to have a word for every man present. 

Princess Juliana and her children also lived in Lee, Massachusetts, for a few months, where her modest way of living, including her daily shopping_tours, won the approval and respect of the stolid New Englanders. 

The third daughter, Margriet, was born in Ottawa, Canada, on January 19, 1943. Juliana's suite in the hos pital was declared to be extraterritorial by the Canadian Government during her confinement, in order that the royal child would not acquire, by its birth in Canada, Canadian nationality.

Soon after the liberation of Holland, the Royal Family returned to their homeland. On February 18, 1947, the fourth little princess, Marijke, was born.

Juliana becomes Queen. It was on May 12, 1948, that Queen Wilhelmina announced to her people that after the celebration of her Golden Jubilee as sovereign of the nation (August 31, 1948), she was abdicating and turning over the reins of government to her daughter, Juliana. Her fifty years of reign, especially the war years, had begun to take their toll. The Queen, who had courageously weathered a half century on the throne, felt that in justice to her health she should no longer assume the arduous tasks she had so competently fulfilled in the past, and that she would place this responsible burden on the younger shoulders of her daughter, who had been trained for this inevitable position since birth.

QUEEN JULIANA TAKING THE OATH TO THE CONSTITUTION, AMSTERDAM, 1948

On Monday, September 6, 1948, the inauguration ceremony took place in the Nieuwe Kerk (New Church) in Amsterdam. This was not a Coronation, as many term it, but an Investiture or Inauguration of the new Queen. (There is no mention in the Dutch Constitution of the word "coronation." During the ceremony, the crown was not placed on the head of the new Queen, but lay on a velvet-covered table, directly in front of the throne.) 

Queen Juliana took the oath on the Constitution there before the assembled Houses of Parliament. 

JULIANA AS A QUEEN 

The tasks of the Queen of The Netherlands are many. 

There is the representative side of opening exhibitions, attending ceremonies and visiting the eleven provinces of her country from time to time, thus keeping in personal touch with all her 10,000,000 subjects in Holland. To this end she regularly pays so-called "working visits" to specific areas of the country, during which she studies in great detail the problems of each area through attending discussion groups of officials, business leaders, farmers, factory workers, social workers, etc. 

The most important side, however, is the constitutional function. Of the 211 clauses in the Constitution, seventy pertain to the monarch, but the constitutional practice reduces them to the cardinal clause, "The Queen is inviolable, the Ministers are responsible." The combined 

Ministers, or Cabinet, stipulate and determine the government policy, as long as they have the entire confidence of the majority of Parliament. The Queen's task is to see that such a Cabinet is formed and that the cooperation between the Ministers and the representatives of the people

QUEEN JULIANA AT HER DESK

will be assured as much as possible. All her political acts must have the approval of the Minister concerned, who alone is responsible to Parliament for these acts. 

On December 27, 1949, Queen Juliana fulfilled the promise made by her mother during the war days in 1942 and again in 1948: sovereignty over the Netherlands East 

Indies was transferred to an independent Indonesia. 

To encourage and to warn. Queen Juliana has continuous contact with the Ministers, and she tries to make this as informal as possible. What are the bonds of a constitutional Queen with her Ministers? They have to keep her constantly informed of all state affairs. She has the right to be consulted, to encourage and to warn, but they remain responsible. In a constitutional monarchy, an intelligent Queen can give moral and intellectual leadership in matters of government. 

She remains the stable element amidst changing councils. During her rule, a Queen gathers a wealth of political experience that keeps her from being totally dependent on her Ministers. During her fifty years of reign, Queen Wilhelmina, her mother, gained a political view equal to the knowledge of the most experienced statesman. 

Not only experience, but intelligence and character, too, determine the royal influence. From her childhood on, Queen Juliana has been prepared for the task that was in store for her. A political education such as is given to few Dutch women fell to her lot. She has been trained in practicing impartiality, because during her reign she necessarily must be able to cope with changing political views. Moreover, she possesses an independent judgment. She is very intelligent, has good self-control and knows how to listen to "the other side of the story.'

Royal without being regal. She is very versatile in her conversations with visiting international dignitaries and guests, being fluent in English, French and 

German. Her charming, yet dignified manner, wins the love and respect of all who meet her. As one visitor expressed it, "To me the remarkable thing about Juliana is that she manages to be friendly and kind and sympathetic, and at the same time does convey about her a great dignity which comes not from a sense of her own importance but rather from an appreciation of the position she holds on behalf of the Netherlands people. She has a way of being royal without being regal." 

Every morning a large package of government documents arrives at Soestdijk Palace, together with an overall daily press review, from which the Queen may form an idea of the various views and convictions among Dutch and foreign circles. The documents are carefully studied, some signed immediately, others requiring further examination, and in some cases, the Minister of that department is invited to consult with the Queen personally. Thus, the various Cabinet Ministers arrive at Soestdijk Palace, some often, some less frequently. 

The Queen talks with her Ministers, often for many hours, and she listens, asks questions and gives opinions, but she does not decide. The ministers have that responsibility. If a new Cabinet has to be formed, however, the 

Queen does decide whom she wishes to be in charge of the selection. If the man of her choice does not succeed in forming a Cabinet which has the confidence of the majority of the political parties, he returns his mandate to her and she must find another to attempt the job. For-

JULIANA'S FAMILY LIFE 

Queen Juliana and Prince Bernhard and their four daughters live a very normal and happy home life. They enjoy doing things together, and the children are included in their activities as much as possible. 

Upon returning to Holland after the liberation, Juliana, in a radio talk, made a moving appeal to the people of Holland to help her in the education of her daughters by not paying too much attention to them. "Please treat them as children, not as extraordinary beings," she pleaded, "Their characters will develop more harmoniously and they will be happier because of it. I remember so well how miserable I used to feel when I was a girl and saw crowds waiting to look at me ... stare at me. 

I just craved to be an ordinary girl."

As a mother, Queen Juliana has been very successful in giving her daughters what she longed for so much in her own childhood, the companionship of sisters. The children have their meals with their mother and father, and like other children often have to be hurried a bit or told not to talk so much. The eldest princess, Beatrix, or Trix as she is called, is quite alert and talkative and finds no difficulty in entertaining those at the table with animated tales of happenings in her busy young life. 

She and her sister, Irene, often organize little performances to which they invite all the available grownups.

PRINCESS JULIANA WITH HER THREE DAUGHTERS IN CHATHAM, MASS., 1944

The invitations are handmade, and an entrance fee is charged and given to charity. At these performances, it is 

Irene, the quietest, who excels in acrobatic feats, and of course all the little princesses love dressing up in gay costumes. 

The "bungle cloth." Before Marijke was born,

Juliana explained to the new nurse that she had always wanted to bring up her children herself. And, with her usual sense of humor, she added, "Now with the fourth, I probably will succeed much better than with Trix, who was the first. Trix has been a sort of educational 'bungle cloth' to me." ("Bungle cloth" is a piece of cloth on which little girls are taught to sew and embroider, and which always shows earnest efforts if not always done right.) 

However, radiant Trix seems to prove that her mother's "bungle cloth" was a nice piece of work. 

The princesses go to progressive schools with children living in the neighborhood. At school, as well as in the 

Scout Movement, the princesses are treated just as the other children. They must learn to know the people in the world and to get as much experience as possible. 

Children of a royal family have the advantage, as well as the drawback, of having their lives predestined from birth, and naturally their education is directed accordingly. When, for instance, Beatrix decides she would like to dress as Sinterklaas (Santa Claus) and visit one of the children's homes on St. Nicholas Day, she must make all the preparations herself and the project is in her hands, a thing she loves to do. 

Marietta and Roswitha. To show the democratic way the little princesses are brought up, here is an example. At an afternoon ballet performance for a needy children's charity in Holland, two names were noticed on the program, Marietta van den Berg and Roswitha van

de Bos. In his opening speech, the chairman told the audience, "You may notice some well-known faces on the stage, as well as in the audience. If so, please refrain from any reaction." Sure enough, when the curtain rose, the delighted audience discovered that the two elder princesses, Beatrix and Irene, were performing with their little friends, and the very elegant names on the program were their own choice, to hide their identity. Queen Juliana was one of the proud mothers in the audience. 

These children, as other children, need some freedom to be happy. Therefore they often choose fictitious names when they are out walking. If they hear some passers-by make a remark about the royal children, they call to each other, "Cissy" or "Mina," loud enough to be overheard, and become quite amused when they see the people think they have made a mistake.

The two elder princesses are very much interested in art. Beatrix has her own little studio where she receives lessons in modeling. Her artistic ability is undoubtedly inherited from her grandmother, Princess Wilhelmina, who paints, and from her father, who is very clever at clay modeling.

They have a busy life as well as a happy one. First of all, there is school, then horseback riding three times a week; once a week, rhythmic dancing; finally, they also belong to the Girl Scouts. 

A "fifty-fifty" ruler. A very good description of the Queen's life is given by two of her four daughters. 

Just before their parents went to visit the Royal Family in England in 1950, the two elder princesses had a slight argument which went like this. One girl said, "Mother's work is looking after us, and her hobby is running the country." "No," said the other, "You are wrong. Mother's work is running the country and her hobby is looking

after us.

This slight dispute is a sure indication that Holland's Royal Family is a real family and a happy one. 

One could almost say that Queen Juliana is a “fifty-fifty” ruler, half Queen and half mother. That's how she herself sees her task. "For a Queen the task of being a mother is just as important as for every other Netherlands woman," she stressed in her inauguration speech in 1948. 

The royal home, Soestdijk Palace, is located in the heart of Holland and is more like a rambling country. estate than a palace. Not too formal, but graciously dignified, its left wing was furnished and modernized by the Dutch people as a gift to Princess Juliana and Prince Bernhard upon their marriage. On ascending the throne, Queen Juliana insisted upon living there and not in the Royal Palace in The Hague, the seat of the government. "This was given to us as our home, and here we are going to live,” she said.

With four young daughters growing up, this was a very wise decision on the part of the Queen. Although some of the Ministers and high officials wishing an audience with the Queen may find the 90-minute motor trip a bit tedious at times, the Queen here shows her "fiftyfifty” spirit, and often motors to The Hague or Amsterdam to receive diplomatic visits and other ceremonial calls.

One of the important wedding anniversaries celebrated in The Netherlands is twelve and one-half years, which is the "Copper Wedding" anniversary. This is important because it is the half-way mark to the Silver Anniversary, and also because the smaller children can participate in the celebration, whereas the Silver Anniversary is usually a grown-up affair. On July 7, 1949, Queen Juliana and Prince Bernhard celebrated their Copper Wedding Anniversary. The Dutch people naturally wanted to give their royal couple an appropriate present, and it was finally decided to restore the famous old castle, Huis ten Bosch (House in the Woods), in The Hague as the Royal Family had no suitable residence in this seat of the Dutch government. The living quarters of the 

Royal Family have always been extremely conservative compared to those of other royal dynasties. Soestdijk 

Palace, the private residence of Queen Juliana, was really meant to be a summer palace like Het Loo, the present home of Princess Wilhelmina. The Dam Palace in 

Amsterdam, where Queen Juliana stayed at the time of the Investiture, was essentially built as a city hall, and though it looks most impressive from the outside, it is very uncomfortable to live in.

The Hague has Noordeinde Palace, which has now been abandoned for living quarters. From the outside, it looks like a large whitewashed office building, and most visitors might pass it without realizing that it was a royal home which has harbored the monarchs of Holland since 1814. Part of it now houses an international college. 

So this left the Royal Family without a home in The Hague; and this city, with its diplomatic corps, felt this

No is No, Yes is Yes.

Queen Juliana tries to arrange her engagements to make sure she is at home when the children return from the local school. She also takes an active part in the running of her home. She discusses and plans menus, gives instructions to the domestic staff, arranges small intimate parties and film showings, and also helps at times to wash and dress the younger princesses.

Prince Bernhard shares equally with the Queen in the rearing of their children. Both are good disciplinarians. 

The Prince is the most severe. But if the children are in the wrong, both parents are very determined. The children know that an order is given only once, and that with Father and Mother, No is No and Yes is Yes! 

The Prince is cheerful, sport-loving, kind and without affectation. But at the same time he is endowed with a strong personality. When he was confirmed his father gave him a Bible text, to be written on his certificate, as was then the custom. These texts were sought to fit the individual. On Prince Bernhard's certificate was written: "For unto whomsoever much is given, of him shall be much required.--Luke 12:48." And, in Holland he has not disappointed the people. The Prince, a businessman before his marriage, has a quick and accurate insight into the most diverse matters. One of the chief features of his character is his strict conformity to his principles. 

This he showed in the war. Through his marriage, he had become Dutch, and like a Dutchman he fought, without compromising.

Aside from his purely representative duties as the Queen's husband, he also has functions in his own right.

For Holland, a Queen. The Queen at home has the wonderful ability of making everyone feel at ease. 

She is a good conversationalist and, more important, a good listener. She strives to learn something new from each visitor. With a word here and a suggestion there, she keeps the conversation going smoothly, often highlighted by her good sense of humor and intelligent logic.

It is this quality, combined with her Leiden University training, that enables Queen Juliana to draft most of her own speeches and give them that personal and appealing touch for which she is noted. 

The question has often been raised whether it is wiser to have a queen instead of a king. There are few Hollanders left who can remember living under a king's rule, King William III, Queen Wilhelmina's father, having passed on in 1890. These old-timers will agree with the rest of the nation that no king could have done as much for his people as the two women did who, in a rapidly changing world, were not only the symbol of their country's freedom but also the embodiment of the supernational virtues of decency, stability and devotion.
