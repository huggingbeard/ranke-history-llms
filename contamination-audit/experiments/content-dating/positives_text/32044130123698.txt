PREFACE 

In the United States today, one of the most difficult and devastating problems confronting law enforcement officers is the problem of traffic in narcotics or "dope"-"traffic" meaning the entire problem beginning with the actual illegal transportation of the narcotics into the country to its distribution among the perhaps thousands of addicts. The sad and shocking fact is that the bulk of these illegal narcotics is consumed by young people, young people so hopelessly entangled in the vicious habit that it is beyond the power of their human wills to overcome it. It is particularly to this group that the ministry of this book is directed. 

Mr. Jim Vaus, with his extensive background of experience in the under-world, is the ideal man to write an informative book of this kind. From many sources in the medical world as well as the law enforcement world, Mr. Vaus has uncovered startling facts about the narcotics problem, facts that will jolt young readers into a realization of the tremendous danger represented in toying with narcotics. Mr. Vaus was asked to study the problem from the standpoint of the effect of narcotics on various age groups, how dope addiction starts and what the present trend is, the actual traffic in dope, the degree of harm caused to the physical body from the point of view of the medical world, the effect upon the mental capacities of the individual not only from the medical viewpoint but also from that of the judiciary. 

Thus, it was to fulfill what they felt was a definite need that the publishers asked Mr. Vaus to write this fact-packed expose. It is their hope and prayer, as well as Mr. Vaus's that this book will be a means of pointing young peopleand older ones alike to a more secure foundation for happiness, the Lord Jesus Christ. With this ministry in mind, and this prayer on our hearts, this book is sent forth to the glory of God and the salvation of precious souls.

So widespread has the dope menace become that national magazines, with divergent reading audiences, have run articles on the subject. Some of them are: 

"A Short- and Horrible - Life," Life, June 11, 1951; Read er's Digest, September 1951. 

"Do Schools Teach Drug Addiction?" Catholic Digest, Febru ary 1952. "The Truth About the 'Drug Menace,'" Harper's Magazine, February 1952.

These headlines and articles are slowly awakening the American public to the fact that we have a major narcotics problem on our hands.

This is a shock to the American people because for a number of years dope has been a minor problem in the United States. Once before in our history, the use of drugs hit major proportions. In 1900 it was estimated that one out of every four hundred Americans was a drug addict. Narcotics were sold freely in drug stores and were widely used in patent medicines.

Public interest was aroused and the Harrison Pure Food and Drug Act was passed in 1914. For many years it proved effective in curbing the use of narcotics. By the early forties, drug addiction figures had shrunk to one addict in every three thousand people.

Unfortunately, this condition did not remain a fact. The willingness of Communist nations and nations inclined toward Communism to grow drugs for importation to the United States, the huge profits made by dope peddlers, plus America's growing decadent attitude toward sin combined to make narcotics again a major problem. "In 1948, addiction showed a huge spurt, particularly among young people," states Victor H. Vogel, M.D., medical officer in charge of the U.S. Public Health Service Hospital at Lexing ton, Kentucky.

One cause of the growth of addiction is the widespread use of barbiturates. Barbiturates occur in a variety of shapes, sizes and colors. Over 436 trade name types are sold on prescription in the Los Angeles area. The strongest acting drugs of this class are pentobarbital, secobarbital and amobarbital. 

The Chemical Division of the Department of Commerce reports that in 1948, 672,000 pounds of barbiturates were manufactured in the United States-enough to give every man, woman and child twenty-four doses. In 1949, 679,800 pounds of barbiturates were manufactured and in 1950 about the same amount. The amount of barbiturates sold each year is enough to put everyone in the United States to sleep every night for twelve nights. This quantity far exceeds medical needs. 

One can become addicted to the use of barbiturates. Dr. 

Harris Isbell, research director of the Public Health Service

Hospital at Lexington, states, "Despite statements to the contrary in standard textbooks, severe symptoms develop after abrupt withdrawal of barbiturates from chronically intoxicated individuals."

Furthermore, "Reports of deaths show that more people in the United States die of barbiturate poisoning than from any other kind of poison" (Facts about Narcotics-Vogel). Aimee Semple 

McPherson, founder of the denomination known as the Church of the Four Square, and for many years pastor of Angels Temple, Los Angeles, died from an accidental overdose of sleeping pills. So did Mrs. Ellen McAdoo Himshaw, daughter of the late Senator William G. McAdoo and granddaughter of 

President Woodrow Wilson. Another victim was Mrs. Evalyn 

McLean Reynolds, wife of former Senator R. R. Reynolds of

North Carolina and only daughter of the late Mrs. Evalyn 

Walsh McLean, owner of the Hope Diamond. 

Dorothy Krueger Smith, only daughter of retired General

Walter Krueger, World War II Commander of the Sixth Army, killed her husband, Colonel Aubrey DeWitt Smith, chief of the

Plans and Operations Division (Logistics Section) of the U. S. 

Army in Japan. Her attorney, Lieutenant Colonel Howard S. 

Levie, entered a plea of "temporary insanity" on the grounds that she was "doped" with paraldehyde, a sedative, at the time of the murder. She was, however, found guilty of first-degree murder and sentenced "to be confined at hard labor for the rest of her natural life."

Even children become the victims of barbiturates. In Dallas, Texas, a small child died from drinking phenobarbital, beer and soda water, given her by a baby-sitter and her boy friend. 

They wanted to see what would happen. They did! But the 'parents must share the blame, for they had made these drugs accessible. 

Next to barbiturates, marihuana causes the most trouble.

Marihuana is a plant with long slender leaves, which often grows wild. In some parts of the world the hemp plant preparations are known as hashish, and from this word comes the word "assassin."

The leaves of the plant are dried, usually hand rubbed into a mixture of small pieces and rolled between brown wheatstraw papers into a cigarette. Ordinarily a double thickness of paper is used to prevent the sharp edges of the plant from cutting through the paper. The ends of the cigarette are folded in to prevent losing the marihuana when it is being carried. At one time marihuana had a legitimate use but now it is strictly contraband.

Marihuana cannot be confused with ordinary tobacco, being greenish rather than brown and having a peculiar odor, much like burning weeds, when it is burned. The "sticks" are sold for fifty cents each, or three for a dollar in most large cities.

Because marihuana is considered non-addicting it has become the current vogue to "blast the weed." As one teen-ager said, "They had sort of a club in my school and you couldn't belong if you didn't smoke marihuana and pretty soon we were all using heroin instead of marihuana."

That boy's confession pointed out the fatal weakness in smoking marihuana. Smoking it soon leads to using heroin, and heroin is the most habit-forming of all drugs. It is a white, or slightly yellow powder, bitter to the taste, odorless and deadening to the one who becomes addicted to its use. 

Heroin is a manufactured derivative of opium. Because of its extremely dangerous nature, it is a forbidden import. Since early in the twenties no heroin has legally entered the United States. Yet the use of it is causing headlines across the country, and is prompting me to find out the facts of the situation. 

In a survey taken in connection with the writing of this book some interesting facts came to light.

San Francisco reported that the average number of arrests for addiction had more than doubled in the last ten years. 

But Michael Gaffey, Chief of Police, said that while accepting this as a fact, certain factors should be taken into consideration. 

He wrote: "It must be remembered that since the beginning of World War II our City has undergone a tremendous change both as to the total population and the social composition of our popu-

lation. Police service has also changed during the intervening years. This last factor is particularly important due to the recognized fact that narcotic enforcement as evidenced by arrest statistics is somewhat in proportion to the man-power and attention devoted to this phase of police work." 

It is no doubt true that the changing population and increased police force has played a part in San Francisco's rising narcotic arrests. However, other cities reported a similar rise. San Antonio reported that the number of arrests was "on increase." Philadelphia reported that the "number is doubled." Portland reported that there was "some increase due to growth and type of influx due to war." 

The Federal Bureau of Investigation Uniform Crime Reports show that in 1940 there was a total of 5,014 finger prints taken of those arrested for violation of the narcotic laws. In 1950, there was a total of 8,539. This is a steep rise. 

In my survey, the Chiefs of Police of the different large towns were asked if there had been a rise in the number of arrests of juvenile narcotic users. 

San Francisco was happy to report that there had been only two juveniles arrested on narcotic charges in 1952. This is a splendid record, especially in view of the fact that Los Angeles reports that the number of juveniles arrested on narcotic charges rose from nine in 1940 to over two hundred in 1951. 

Many cities, such as Cleveland, reported, "No juvenile problem involved in this city." San Antonio reported, "Only five to ten juveniles involved annually." St. Louis also reported low juvenile arrests, but it must be remembered that under the Missouri law a person who has not reached his seventeenth birthday is a juvenile. Philadelphia reported, "Very few under 18." Many who might well be classed as juveniles are not so classified because according to law after they are eighteen, they are adult. We have, however, many boys and girls eighteen and nineteen attending high schools. 

If they are arrested, they are not listed among juvenile statistics.

The real proof of rising juvenile addiction is the report of

the Federal Bureau of Investigation. The finger print cards in the Bureau show that in 1935 there were 9 arrested on narcotic charges who were fifteen and sixteen years of age as compared to 95 in 1950. In 1935, there were 78 arrested on narcotics charges who were between seventeen and nineteen as compared to 935 in 1950. It is an admitted fact that dope does menace our young people. Says Dr. Vogel of the

U.S.P.H.S. Hospital at Lexington, Kentucky: 

"In 1950 school authorities in several large cities, law enforcement officers, and narcotic hospitals suddenly realized that a great many young boys and girls were becoming addicted to narcotics. New York, Chicago and Washington were the first to feel this epidemic."

In 1950 in Chicago, it was estimated that one out of every five addicts arrested was a minor. The New York police estimated that there were at least fifteen hundred teen-age addicts in the city. The Detroit police arrested forty-eight dope peddlers who had been selling to high school students. In Washington, D.C., violations of the narcotic laws jumped 400 per cent. In 1951 in Detroit, the grand jury heard the testimony of one hundred youthful addicts.

These facts are appalling and yet added to them must be the realization that "narcotic traffic and addiction, unlike other crimes, does not lend itself easily to statistics. In a robbery, auto theft, or assault, and in most other crimes, there is a complaining victim-someone who demands redress. Through such complaints the police are able to assess the number and location of specific crimes in a community and direct police efforts accordingly. This is not so with drug traffic and addiction. The principal victim in this crime is the user" (Youth and 

Narcotics, Los Angeles Police Department, 1952).

The number continues to rise. Edward J. Bateman, chaplain to juvenile delinquents in Los Angeles, reported that in 1951, of the 1,392 juveniles who were taken to juvenile hall for the first time, 138 were drug users.

And the headlines continue. This is but one of the many which were printed during the compiling of this book:

FATHER FINDS Girl, 16, and Pal Dope VictimS 

"A father's shame at finding his 16-year-old daughter in what appeared to be a drunken stupor turned to heart-broken dismay today when he found she was using marihuana and turned her over to Norwalk sheriff's deputies" (Los Angeles Herald-Express, Feb. 

25, 1953).

Drugs have become a part of the American scene. What are we going to do about them?

A generation ago women did not smoke. Then a few began smoking, but they were careful not to smoke on the streets or in public. Nowadays young girls smoke any place they please and it is the pretty girls who are used to advertise cigarettes on the billboards, in magazines and on television. Dorothy 

Collins has achieved fame by doing a cigarette commercial.

The battle against the narcotic of nicotine has been lost. 

It has been lost because there is money to be made by selling cigarettes and the general public is apathetic.

There was a time in the United States when it took a little scheming to get drunk. One had to know a bootlegger. If he were arrested and went to jail, it took time to locate another bootlegger. Nowadays, in the state of California, alcoholic beverages are sold in the grocery store along with milk and coffee. And they are as easy to obtain in other states. The alcoholic has become a part of the American scene because there is money to be made in selling alcoholic beverages and the public is apathetic.

There is money, too, to be made in narcotics. So much money goes for heroin to be pumped into the arm that this business is rightly called the traffic of the golden arm. 

If the Christians continue to regard narcotics with the same degree of apathy that they have regarded alcohol and nicotine, it is only logical to go one more step and accept drugs as part of the social pattern. Such a step has already been suggested in an article in Harper's, November 1952.

Therefore, as we read the headlines of today, we must ask

ourselves, How long will it be before it is as legal to buy heroin as it is to buy barbiturates? How long will it be before it is an accepted social practice to "blast the weed"? How long will it be before the events related in the next chapter become a part of our accepted social pattern?

Chapter Two * 

Betty crouched on the bed, tossing, turning, writhing, trying to "kick the habit." Every nerve in her body screamed for heroin. Her eyes watered. She kept sniffing her nose. She was distressed in spirit, as one who had awakened from a bad dream with the dream still vividly real in her mind.

She couldn't stand being alone with her tormenting fears. 

She wanted someone to stay in the room with her. In order to have companionship, any companionship, she was willing to talk about herself. In rambling, jerky sentences, she told her story- "Why did I start taking dope? I don't know. Who knows?

I came from a respectable family. There were three of us children. Two older ones, and I was tag-a-long. There is ten years difference between my sister and I. "Back home in Kansas, we all went to Sunday school and on special occasions, like Christmas and Easter, to church. That is, we kids did. Dad was a lawyer and insurance salesman so he worked all kinds of hours, and liked to rest on Sunday morning. Mom liked to be with him so she used to stay home with him. "The folks had been to California on a visit when I was small; and after my brother and sister were married, they decided to move to the coast. Dad could only sell insurance on the coast, so he didn't make as much money as he had *This is a typical case. 22

back home. Mom decided she'd better go to work. She wanted to work because she didn't have any friends out here. 

She got a job with the telephone company and liked it. "Only, she had to work evenings. But then my dad often went out evenings to see prospects, and I was old enough to take care of myself, wasn't I? I was fifteen and getting along all right in school. I wasn't spending any time being teacher's pet but I got by, and I had got in with a gang. Nice kids. All of them. At least, most of them were." 

Betty leaned back and closed her eyes, quiet for a moment, thinking of her school days. In repose, she was pretty. Her dark hair was blue-black and though her face had a pathetic thinness, her features were regular. Physically, she was a normal, attractive girl and she might have lived a normal life, if her body began to twitch and she took up her story. "All we kids wanted was to have a good time. We didn't mean any harm, and most of us didn't harm anyone but ourselves and, perhaps, our families. Mostly, at first, we did silly things. We rode around town in one of the boys' jallopies, trying to see how quick he could jam on the brakes at a stop signal, and we laughed like crazy when he made it close, or skidded into the crosswalk. "Or we sat around all day in one of the homes where the parents were out and played records. If we found a record that was real George, we'd play it over and over and over. 

When we had the money, we went to the movies and we'd sit through it twice if there was a real hot love scene, or if it was a horror picture with real scary scenes. "Sometimes we talked about what we would be someday, when we got through school. All the girls wanted to get married or be movie stars. Mostly the boys just didn't want to go into the army; but anyway, we were all sure that somehow or other, we'd be happy, doing whatever we pleased. "I soon started smoking. Most of the girls did. Why not?

On the back of the magazines you see pictures of them opera stars, like Dorothy Kirsten and Risé Stevens, and those movie

stars, like Linda Darnell and Ruth Hussey, advertising cigarettes, so why shouldn't we kids smoke? "And we drank beer. You know, like they advertise on TV, where the fellow always has such a good time drinking it. We felt grown-up, doing what the grownups did. But maybe what they were doing wasn't so good for such kids to follow. But how were we to know?" She squinted her eyes, and her thin, suffering face questioned the examples of the grownups she had so innocently followed. "One day one of the boys got hold of some 'reefers' and after he tried one, most of the gang started smoking them. 

It's easy to smoke 'reefers' after you've been smoking cigarettes. It seems about the same. Especially as we all knew marihuana wasn't habit-forming. At least, that is what they say, because if you quit marihuana, you don't have to 'kick the habit.' But after you've tried your first 'reefer,' it's easy to get a 'yen' for another. "By this time I was having trouble with my teachers for not studying and for ditching. But I could always stare them down, as if they were unfair to say such things about me. It was fun to see the teachers squirm. I sure should have been an actress. Or maybe there is some actress in every girl." 

She cocked her dark head and smiled triumphantly because she had been able to fool her teachers and parents so long. "That term I barely passed, but I didn't care. I was looking forward to summer. I like summer. It's lazy. I'd get up late, like my folks did, and we'd lounge around in a bathrobe and have coffee and toast. They'd yak and complain about the government and high prices. Dad might tell how he'd outsmarted a man in some deal or Mom would tell how she got by with breaking some rule at work. Don't get me wrong. My parents were good respectable citizens, but I've noticed that everyone thinks he's smart when he gets by with something. 

And when I started smoking 'reefers' I didn't figure I was getting by with anything more in my way than they were in theirs. "After they'd go to work, I'd go to the beach with the gang.

Mom and Dad used to yak about the money I spent, but they always gave it to me. They knew teen-age girls were expensive and I was willing to be an expense. I liked to dress nicepretty blouses and neat skirts. Besides, I had to chip in when the gang bought marihuana. "One of the boys, Ben, had a 'connection,' but Ben wasn't telling where or whom he was. I was dumb in those days and thought the reason he wouldn't name his 'connection' was that he wanted to be a big shot and we'd have to have him along when we had a 'blast party.' But that wasn't his reason. For one thing, he was afraid that one of us would make a slip and he'd get 'busted.' "I knew we could be arrested for 'blasting the weed.' The kids used to laugh about the chance of being arrested, especially if we had some 'reefers' in the car when we drove by a traffic cop. But like I said, I was dumb in those days.

Jail didn't seem so very real to me. I couldn't have told you where it was, or what it was like. I think maybe it was because I was so dumb that I got in as deep as I did before 

I knew what was happening. "I suppose I was an average, dumb, good girl. When Mom said don't do this and don't do that, it was like listening to a recording. Mostly, it didn't seem real. She yapped at me not to get into bad company, but she didn't try to know my friends. Before I knew what had happened I was the bad company. "Anyway, back to Ben. He was the oldest boy in the gang, and was handsome, in his way. He had sandy hair, was tall, but too thin. And he had a teasing smile. I liked him. He didn't go to school but worked as assistant manager at a small neighborhood movie house. His job was more title than pay. "He'd come from a broken home. You might say it was plenty broken. His mom had left two husbands. I'm not a philosopher, and life is too thick for me to figure out, or to claim I've found any of the answers. But I wonder, unless a girl gets a special heel of a man, if she gains anything by getting a divorce and marrying someone else. It never seems

to me that these second marriages are so much better than the first. I'm sure my dad and mom disagreed plenty but they managed to settle their fights without a referee. Now, at least, they've got each other and the other two children turned out all right. But Ben's mother, she went through husbands like 

I go through a couple of 'reefers.' "She was a beauty operator; and mostly, when Ben was a kid, he had to shift for himself. That's why he got into the trouble he did. He began swiping stuff and did a spell at the reform school. And that's where he met the fellows who really put him wise. When he came out, his mother was between husbands, so he stayed with her for awhile. "One day he ran into some of his friends from the reform school and began ganging around with them. One of them dared him to take a shot of 'H.' No one dared Ben to do anything without his doing it, so he took a shot and was 'hooked. Soon after this, his mom married for the third time, and Ben left home. About this time he began making 'connections' for our gang. "You know marihuana affects different kids differently."

Betty sat up, and leaned on her elbows. As she talked about something which interested her, her eyes grew excited. She continued, "The kids do such crazy things. Sometimes, for no special reason, they'll beat up someone on the street, just for the excitement. "And you know, some fellows and girls don't like the 'weed' at all. I remember one girl who drifted into our gang, more to see what was going on than anything else. She had a nice home. Her mom didn't work and her dad was an elder or something in a church. After she smoked a 'reefer' she felt low and didn't come back again. I ran into her once on the street and asked her why. She said her conscience hurt her. 

Conscience! Mine never hurt me. Nor did the other kids'. She must have had her's trained. Most of the kids think it is fun to 'blast.' It makes you feel as if you are someone big. Lots of times, when I 'blasted' I'd go with Ben to a bebop concert, or we'd go to his place and play some bebop records."

She clasped her hands around her knees, her fingers clawlike and her long black hair half-covering her sober face, as melancholy memories crowded into her mind. "I remember the first time I saw Ben give himself a shot. 

I had gone with him to his place and he wouldn't 'blast.' I was mad at him, until after I'd smoked a 'reefer.' Then I forgot to be sore, and sat on the couch, listening to a record. 

He said, 'Baby, you can have your "weed," I'm taking something that really gives life a lift.' "He went into his room and came back with what he called an 'outfit.' He walked over to the table and spread it out, absorbed in what he was doing. I watched him, thinking how wonderful this new thing must be if Ben got such a bang out of it! Nothing, but nothing, ever made me feel, as I could see from his face, what he felt. I was keen to 'blast 'and when I was away from Ben I was frantic to be with him. But this new stuff — it was something! "He opened a capsule and carefully poured some white powder on a spoon. Then, with an ordinary ten-cent medicine dropper he dropped a little water on the stuff. He lit a match and held it under the spoon for a few seconds. Next, he put a small dab of cotton on the liquid, pressed the end of the dropper on the cotton, worked the rubber top of the dropper and drew the liquid up into the dropper. Out of a tissue he unwrapped a hypodermic needle and slipped it into the end of the dropper. My eyes must have been bug-eyed by this time. I knew what he was going to do. He was a 'mainliner' taking a shot of heroin. "So quick, that it showed plenty of practice, he slipped off his belt, wrapped it around the upper part of his arm, then doubled his fist and pumped until the vein stood up. He jabbed the needle into his vein and in a couple of seconds he was wrapping up his 'outfit.' "He sat down on the couch beside me. Soon he was feeling 'high.' I could see that 'H' really did something for him. 

Within a week, I was shooting 'H' myself. Queer stuff! After you've had a shot, you don't care about anything. You're

not even especially hungry. Oh, you eat, but it isn't important.

And I used to be crazy about clothes, but I didn't care about them anymore. The only thing that was important was 'H.' I had to have it. I only felt easy when I knew where my next shot was coming from. "For awhile I used Ben's needle but I was afraid to depend on him, or anyone, for anything as important as 'H.' I was desperate to get a needle of my own. About that time, Mom got sick. That annoyed me because Dad wanted me to stay home and look after her. Wars also increase the number of drug addicts. It was during the Civil War that the hypodermic needle was invented. "This new, quick way of relieving pain was widely used by many army surgeons in the Civil War, and as a result many soldiers started taking narcotic drugs" (Facts about Narcotics). 

After both World War I and II the addiction rate went up and mention has already been made of the use of narcotics by members of the United States Army in Korea. 

Contrary to opinion small towns are not immune from drug addiction. Maurice Helbrant was a Federal agent for many years and worked all over the United States. He was often sent to trace down a narcotic violation in a small town. He found that in small towns the use of narcotics could always be traced to some doctor. The doctor would prescribe narcotics for a patient in good faith in most cases, and the patient would become addicted. After the doctor's death, the patient would still be addicted. And by that time, he would have addicted a couple of friends.

The addict would soon learn how to receive narcotics through the mail. Then the "syndicate" would hear of the market, and a couple of strangers would come to town, and open up a lunchroom or pool hall. Soon there would be a scandalous drug situation in the town and someone would write the 

Narcotic Bureau. The Bureau would then send a Federal agent to investigate. The agent can arrest the peddler, but he cannot cure the addict. And as long as there are addicts, the "syndicate" will find a way to supply them. 

The reason the number of drug addicts continues to grow is that addicts make addicts. "Drug addiction is a communicable disease," states Dr. Victor H. Vogel. "It has been well established that each individual addict will cause four other persons to become addicts and these four make sixteen others, etc."

Some authorities believe that one addict encourages a friend to become an addict because he wants to sell him drugs in

order to support his own habit. Others claim that the addict knowing his own misery doesn't want someone else to be tied to the habit, but that the friend, seeing the apparent satisfaction in the drugs, wants to try it.

Addiction also springs from a desire to be one of the gang. 

Teen-agers will do many strange things in order to belong to the gang. They will wear blond streaks in their hair, or odd sox. In former days, they swallowed live goldfish or wore zoot suits. Today, to belong they smoke marihuana.

Smoking marihuana is the first downward step taken by many an addict. The claim is made that after smoking tobacco it is easy to smoke marihuana, because of the resemblance. 

Young people always enjoy a false feeling of safety when smoking marihuana, because from a pharmaceutical standpoint it is not habit-forming. By this is meant that if a person decides not to smoke it, there are no excruciating painful days trying to "kick the habit."

Actually, marihuana is dangerous because the young person soon gets to the place where he depends upon it for his pleasure. Also, in case after case, it has been the use of marihuana which has encouraged the teen-ager to try heroin. 

Another factor which leads to addiction is the maladjusted personality. Fellows and girls with psychological problems are considered addiction-prone. They have not faced the facts of life and adjusted to them. When the opportunity arises to find forgetfulness in the excessive use of drugs, they take it.

Unfortunately, drugs have become more and more easy to obtain. One girl led police officers to several places in the central Los Angeles area where barbiturates were sold over the bar. At one place an officer was able to make a purchase of benzedrine from the bartender. Marihuana and heroin were also sold at these places. The grapevine among addicts is well established. Once a person has become an addict, if he has the price, he has no trouble buying narcotics.

At first the use of narcotics causes a feeling of comfort and contentment. That is why they are given to patients who are

suffering in a hospital. Nervousness and worries vanish, problems are forgotten. However, the body soon builds up a tolerance. The user has to take more and more of the drug to have the required effect. In time, the drug merely makes the user feel normal.

After one is addicted, it is not the pleasure of the drug which makes him continue to take it, but the horror of the withdrawal pains. He reaches the place where, deprived of the drug, he suffers horribly. He feels as if his muscles were being torn from his arms and legs. He has violent pains in his stomach. He vomits and has diarrhea, often of a bloody type. 

He becomes depressed and wants to kill himself. 

The compulsion to have the drug grows stronger and stronger. In order to have it, he will share a needle with another user, without sterilizing it beforehand, and thus pass communicable disease from one to another. If he can't get a hypodermic needle, he will use a safety pin, a ten-penny nail, anything to make a hole in his skin in which to insert the drug into his veins.

Meanwhile, the addict's health suffers. The drug affects his mouth, stomach and intestines. The vital secretions of the body are decreased through the steady use of narcotics. The teeth rot and fall out. The user loses his natural appetite and becomes sluggish and haggard. The movement of the muscles in his stomach and intestines is reduced; constipation results. 

He loses much of his sense of feeling and becomes subject to colds, flu and pneumonia. As he uses a needle, his veins gradually collapse, leaving purple marks on the skin. Boils and abcesses plague him as the result of the injuries he has inflicted on his skin.

Addicts die young. A prominent authority made a comparative study of death rates among narcotic addicts and those among the general population. His study showed that: "Opium addicts die of tuberculosis at the rate of 4 to 1, compared to nonaddicts; more than 2 to 1 of pneumonia; more than 

5 to 1 of premature old age; 4 to 1 of bronchitis; 3 to 1 of brain hemorrhage; 3 to 1 of cancer and other malignant tumors, and more

than 2 to 1 of a wide variety of other diseases" (Living Death,

U. S. Treasury Department, Bureau of Narcotics). 

And if, by accident, the addict gets some heroin that is of purer grain than he has been accustomed to using, the result of the injection is sudden death.

The habit irresistibly drives the addict into a life of crime. 

A "fix" comes high, costing an average of five dollars. It is not unusual for an addict to have a ten-cap or gram-a-day habit. Narcotics are sold strictly on a cash and carry basis. 

When asked if there was a typical background of addicts, Chiefs of Police all across the United States answered, "Poor," and the Chief of Police of Portland added the grim comment, "Addiction creates poverty."

Addiction thus increases crime. In order to secure the money needed to satisfy his craving the addict turns to crime. Probably, before he was an addict, he was not a criminal. Statistics show that "sixty-seven percent of these young patients [addicts] gave no history of delinquency before they became addicts" (Vogel). "Three-fourths of the addicts admitted to the 

Federal hospital for treatment have no history of a criminal record before they became addicts" (Vogel).

Sometimes the addict steals only for his need, like the bandit who entered a drug store at 1417 N. Vermont, Los 

Angeles, and demanded, "I want junk. Just junk. Morphine, only morphine." Others steal in quantity. Violence, crime, poverty-all stalk in the wake of the little white powder! The use of drugs has been ruled a crime by the laws of the United States, not only because of the harm

done to the body but also because of the effect of drugs on the user's moral character.

The addict, without exception, loses all sense of right and wrong. It has been said that though there may be honor among thieves, there is no honor among addicts. They will betray their dearest and nearest if, by so doing, they can get ahold of the drug. "A starving man will usually steal if need be to secure food mark the qualifying word, which implies that there are normal men who would die rather than steal . . . no such act of self-renunciation as that would be possible for any drug addict" (Drugs against Men).

They will argue that liquor affects people worse than heroin, that drunks are often noisy and argumentative, while all a 'hype' wants is to be left alone. 

They dislike the social scorn, the inconvenience of having to hide their addiction, but they enjoy the effect of the drug, which keeps them from facing reality. 

The juice of the poppy wrecks the body and warps the spirit. The life of the addict is a living death.

The first factor which must be considered is that there is a real use in the world for drugs. The selling of a certain amount of them is legitimate and necessary. To quote an authority: "This all-important fact must be borne constantly in mind. Medical science has no substitute for morphine. Drugs, like many other things on earth, were created for the good of mankind. It is man who abuses them. Man has long faced the problem of how to control the use of drugs. 

They have been known to him for almost six thousand years. 

Opium was used medically by the Assyrians and Sumerians as early as 4,000 B.C. The Sumerians called the opium poppy the "joy plant" which leads one to believe that they knew of the non-medical use of the plant. Homer, who lived sometime between 950 and 850 B.C. spoke of "the intoxicating poppy,

the poppy saturated with lethal slumber."

Early Mohammedan and Arabian physicians prescribed opium for headaches and other ailments. During the ninth and tenth centuries, products prepared from the poppy in Asia Minor were carried to other parts of the world by Arab camel trains. 

In this way, opium reached Persia, India and China. The drug became so widely used in India that the people began to raise the plant themselves. Several hundred years later, the East 

India Company brought opium from India to China. 

So much opium was shipped to China and opium-smoking so debased the people that in 1820 the Chinese government prohibited any ship carrying opium from entering the Canton River. This meant the East India Company lost a great deal of business. The situation eventually led to the Opium War of 

1840, when England fought to force China to open her ports to the opium trade. This action is a great blot upon the name of England.

From China the drug was brought to the United States by the Chinese who came to our country to help construct the transcontinental railroads.

The discovery of morphine, the invention of the hypodermic needle and the rise in drug addiction led to the passing of the Harrison Act in 1914, less than forty years ago. The Harrison 

Act regulated the importation, manufacture, production, compounding and sale of opium, derivatives or preparations. It required all wholesale and retail dealers, doctors, institutions, hospitals and others dealing in or prescribing drugs to obtain licenses and keep accurate records of the drugs bought, used or sold.

Mary Ellen was walking down the street, repeating, “No, no, no." One of her friends noticed and asked, "What are you doing?" She replied, "I am practicing saying 'no."" 

Most of us need practice in saying "no." The normal young person enjoys saying "yes," being a part of everything, having everyone like him. But life is a series of choices and the ability to say "no" without giving offense takes practice. 

Say "no" with a smile. If you are asked to do something which you feel that you should not do, say "no" with your most engaging smile. The person who gave the invitation will be so pleased with the smile that he won't take offense at your answer. 

Say "no" with an added thank you. If you are invited to some place of questionable amusement, say "no" with a thank you added. After all, the person who asked you had the best intentions. So you do thank him for his intentions, but you are not going to go there!

Say "no" with a prepared reply. Often you can see how the conversation is going and can know that you will soon be asked to go somewhere or do something that you can't. So think quick and be able to say, "I can't go with you because I have to do something else which is important to me." This added sentence keeps your reply from being too blunt. 

Say "no" with an honest excuse. Perhaps you have a good reason for not going with the gang. If so, "Be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the 84

85 hope that is in you" (I Peter 3:15). If you have given the matter thought and know your reason, express it kindly but plainly. It may help your friend to know why you aren't doing this certain thing.

Say "no" without an excuse. After all, this is a free country. 

You are privileged to live your own life without explaining to anyone. If you don't want to do what the others are doing, don't do it. Be thankful you can do what you please and say "no," period. 

Say "no" with determination. Many a girl says "no" with her lips and "yes" with her eyes, and the fellow sees only her eyes. He pays no attention to what she is saying because he knows she does not mean it. Mean "no" when you say it and people won't tease you to do what you shouldn't.

Say “no” by avoiding a situation. There are some people that you know will suggest your doing something or going someplace which is against your principles. The easiest way to say "no" to them is by not being around them. 

It will be easy to say "no" if you have many friends to whom you can say "yes." "A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly" (Proverbs 18:24) has been repeated and repeated until it is as tiring as mother telling you to wipe your feet when you come into the house. But it is still true. 

If you are a normal person, you are reasonably shy and would prefer that the other person speak to you first and make the overtures of friendship. But he feels that way also. Therefore, you will be happier and so will your friend, if you are willing to make the first step. 

You smile first. You wave first. You speak first. You invite her into your home, your church, your gang. Soon you will have a crowd of friends, all going your way and you will enjoy life.

These friends whom you cultivate should be church friends. 

Admittedly, all the young men and girls in the church are not saints. But the fact that they attend church proves that they are seeking the finest in life. They are headed in the right

direction. Most of them love the Lord and will achieve the best in life. And that's the kind of friends you want to have. 

You will also be less tempted to smoke, drink or smoke marihuana if you have a vital interest in life. The world is full of a host of interesting things to do-singing in the choir, playing a musical instrument, making ceramics, taking part in sports. All of the young people who began fooling around with drugs lacked a vital interest in life. Time hung heavy on their hands. 

True, you will have problems. Ralph Bunche won the Nobel 

Peace Prize. That is a great honor, but he won it after facing and conquering many problems. He was prepared for them, for his grandmother had warned him, "There are going to be many and great troubles in your path. That is the way of life." 

And "If thou faint in the day of adversity, thy strength is small" (Proverbs 24:10).

Those who turn to narcotics as a way of escape have only proved themselves to be weaklings. Even if you have a weak strain in you, you need not stay weak. As a person grows strong by exercising his body, so you can grow strong by exercising your will.

Accept your problem and face it with God's grace, for in the Lord Jesus Christ there is an answer to all your problems.

I know no better way to prove this than by briefly telling again of my own experience.

I was brought up in an average American home. Probably it was a little better than average from a standpoint of morals. 

My dad was a minister and my mother was a sincere, devout Christian. However, we did not have too much money. The days of my youth were the days of the great depression, and I wanted cars, radios, clothes, things!

I'm not going into detail of how deep I went into sin. That has been told in my story, Why I Quit Syndicated Crime. But it will bear repeating to say that the more things I acquired, the more I wanted. One step into sin only led into another. I became restless and unhappy. I made everyone with whom
