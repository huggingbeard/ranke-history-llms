FOREWORD IN PRESENTING this story of the parkways and the situation that must inevitably follow repeal of the 

Eighteenth Amendment, or modification of the

Volstead Act, I would like to remind my readers that the picture I have drawn is a composite; it is not intended as a criticism of any particular parkway system.

Indeed, rather than criticism, it is my hope that parkway officials and police will find it a true and graphic indictment of the intolerable conditions against which they contend to-day, and which, in that to-morrow of greater freedom, that now seems so near at hand, must become even more intolerable, unless they are given greater authority and the finances with which to perfect a unified police control system such as I describe.

The characters themselves are all fictional and are not presented as thinly-disguised portraits of living persons.

Being under fire was not a new experience for Dan Tobey. He had long since come to recognize that panning the police was a legitimate pastime that made excellent thunder and unfailingly boosted newspaper circulations. This time, however, the barb had bitten deeply into him.

It was absurd, of course; not that policing the traffic was any mean task, what with a great metropolis hurling its thousands of Sunday-drivers at them every week-end and holiday, and the week-day parade of cars so heavy and continuous during the summer months that a man had to look twice at the calendar to be sure every day was not Sunday.

If they had been policing only a landscaped highway, traffic regulation might well have been their major concern. But such was not the case. 

Here was a great system of parkways, of incredible beauty, embracing winding streams, picturesque lakes and leafy woodlands, embellished with rustic bridges and great viaducts of hand-hewn stone, representing millions of dollars of taxpayers' money. Therefore, they found it far more difficult to maintain order and decency off the concrete than on it. On one hand they were dealing with such comparatively simple matters as speeding and reckless driving. On the other, they faced a situation that was of vastly greater danger

to the public welfare, involving in more ways than one, the debauchery of youth, thanks to the dineand-dance joints which lined the parkway, not on county property but as close to the line as money and political drag could put them.

Making beer respectable had not improved matters. Arrests had increased, so had accidents; but order had not returned. MEET Louie De Palma. A bootlegger, but a swell guy with the right people. His Rainbow Gardens was the most pretentious dine-and-dance palace on the parkway. He could do business with the rowdiest element and the best-but not under the same roof. Rainbow Gardens was something in the way of a goal attained in his life. He had set out to make it "swell"; and it had not fallen short of his vision-neither ornately nor at the cash register.

Louie was no altruist, but he had certain standards by which he ordered his business-a very involved rôle that called for a dual personality on his part. Or so he thought. As Louie De Palma, baron of booze, he considered himself a tough guy, with an organization powerful enough to make what he said stick. As the proprietor of Rainbow Gardens, he was a smiling, genial host, as proud of his food and floor show as of the liquor-uncut—which he dispensed there.
