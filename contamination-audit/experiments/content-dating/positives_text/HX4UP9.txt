Foreword 

In writing and compiling the contents of this book, particularly in matters of Religion, it has been my aim to follow closely the principles of doctrine and precept as taught by Jesus Christ. I have searched the good books diligently for the truth, purely for truth's sake, and my fondest hope is that it will do some one some good and no one any harm. 

I have no quarrel with my fellow man. All men are my brothers, regardless of class, sect, creed or nationality. All are children of the self-same God.

TERARY  CHAPTER I. 

Religio-Politico and otherwise (Submitted to Presiding Bishop's Office) 

1. Having been born of goodly parents in the little pioneer village of St. George, Washington County, Utah's Dixie 

Land, on the 23rd day of October, in the year of our Lord, 1863, I make claim on a small portion of the pioneering of this grand and glorious rendezvous of peace and quietude, so graciously hidden away in the midst of these mighty hills, whose scenic wonders marvel the ingenuity of the professional artist. 

2. My Father was a close associate of the Prophet Joseph 

Smith, a member of that gallant band, the Mormon Battalion, and a bodyguard to Brigham Young. My Mother crossed the plains at the age of eighteen and drove two yoke of oxen, arriving in the Salt Lake Valley in 1848. They were married by Brigham Young in the old Endowment House in 1849, Salt Lake City, Utah. 

3. At the age of eight years, I was transfigurated into a prospective Mormon under the hand of Elder Eli Whipple, who without stint of mercy, gave me a baptismal ducking in a big millrace in Pine Valley under a foot of ice through which a large hole had previously been cut for the privilege. And as 

I write on this 8th day of June, 1941, nearly seventy years later, the impress of that memorable event looms as fresh in my mind as the hour it happened.

4. I paid but little attention to my religion, outside the area of that baptismal font, until I settled down in this quiet little haven so appropriately named VIRGIN CITY, some forty odd years ago. It was here I became interested in Church affairs, was ordained an Elder in the Kingdom, and set apart as ward teacher. I filled the position as best I could, and I sometimes think I was the chief beneficiary as the results of my puny efforts. 

5. Later I filled the position of teacher in all the senior classes of the auxiliary organizations; was second counselor to 

Bishop Samuel Bradshaw for two or three years; then Bishop for eleven or twelve years; then ward clerk for twelve or thirteen years. In these forty odd years I cannot recall to mind one instance where I have neglected or refused to respond to a request made of me by any Church authority, and have taken great pleasure in the work. 

6. During all this time, I have been a habitual user of tobacco, and I still partake of the filthy weed. Yet I have done considerable work in the Temple, believe in the genealogy work, am a firm believer in the Prophet Joseph Smith and in the teachings of Jesus. (Just handing you a few details in order that you can better understand what I have in mind for your consideration.) 

7. And then came a time when a new order of things was ushered in, and I was restrained from working in the Temple; I was barred from holding any responsible position in the Church; denied all rights and privileges in the Priesthood except it be to pay tithes and offerings, which I have done until last year when I paid no tithing, but did pay my fast offerings. Now you may tell me I should go before the 

Lord, and in answer I say that is exactly what I do, and my course is being directed by the light which I receive.

Presiding Bishop's Office, 

Salt Lake City, Utah.

Dear Bishop: 

8. If the Word of Wisdom is to be confined to the letter of the Doctrine and Covenants, then we have a very meager conception of the true significance of the revelation to the 

Prophet Joseph Smith on this far-reaching principle of health.

If one be justified, and held in high repute, and privileged to exercise in all the power of the Priesthood and ordinances of the Church who abstains from the use of those things mentioned in the Doctrine and Covenants, while at the same time he is eating and drinking such things which are more injurious to his system, but are not mentioned in the book, and, evidently, this is the principle on which it is being preached and applied, then we must concede there is something radically out of order. 

9. If the use of tobacco is the dividing line between me and my Church, and makes void and null my services to the Church, then it must be admitted that my services in this capacity are of little or no value. And this raises the question as to whether my services to the Church or the Church's services to me are most beneficial by way of my eternal salvation. 

10. No, my Brother, if I must traverse the path mapped out by metes and bounds, longitudes and latitudes, formalities and commandments, according to the wisdom of mortal man; if I must sacrifice my individual initiative and annul my 

God-given prerogative on the altars of religious conventionalities and prescribed rituals, in order to conform to the principles of doctrine and precepts as taught by Jesus the Christ, then I am forced to the conclusion that I am heading for Hell with no Stop-Signals or guide boards on the way. 

11. If what I have said be true, that for forty years I have rendered unto the Church all I had to give, complying

diligently and faithfully to every request made of me, and added to this other services when need or necessity called, and the further fact that the authorities have known all during these years that I used tobacco-I have made no secret of it-and now, at this late hour the Church has abrogated these services and cast me into the discard as an unworthy scavenger, would seem to justify the inference that the Church's service on my behalf is rather abortive and obsolete. 

12. And please do not overlook this all-important fact,

Brother, that my sins, yea, even that all-but unpardonable sin attached to the tobacco habit, is a personal matter which must be settled between my conscience and my God or not at all; and God be merciful to the man or men who take upon themselves the responsibility of judge, prosecution and executioner in the name of religion, to direct the destiny of man under the auspices of authority. 

13. Anyway, I hope my sins will not rest upon the heads of those who have judged me, as I opine they will have sufficient of their own to account for. And this is not to be taken as fault-finding, critizing or casting reflections on the character of authority whatsoever.

Dear Brother Earl: 

As requested by you, I am enclosing herewith your "My

Life's Philosophy," which I have read with much interest. I am very happy to note the useful service you have rendered to the Church. I am very sorry, however, that you should permit the use of tobacco to stand in your way of your con-

tinued activity in the Church, and I have also noted your line of argument as to why you feel that you are justified in using tobacco, and that the leaders of the Church are not justified in interpreting the Word of Wisdom as they do. 

People usually try to find an excuse for doing the thing they want to do. 

You proceed at some length to quote from the Savior that it is not that which is taken into the body that defileth the man, but that which proceedeth out of the mouth. But you apparently fail to keep in mind that the Savior was referring only to the matter of eating with unwashed hands. 

Hence, under your paragraph No. 18, I quote, "These are the things which defile a man, but to eat with unwashed hands defileth not a man." I cannot see what relationship this bears to the use of tobacco or any other poisonous thing that might be taken into one's body. 

Now you say that the Lord in giving the revelation to the Prophet Joseph said that it was not a commandment but that the present leaders of the Church have indicated that it is. All of the presidents from the time of the Prophet Joseph down have given the Saints to understand that they should regard the Word of Wisdom largely in the spirit of a commandment, and I cannot understand why you should take exceptions to this because the Lord in giving the revelation indicates, "That it is the mind and will of the Lord." 

How can anyone love the Lord and have the Lord go to the trouble to indicate what His mind and will is, and then openly oppose it? Certainly, nothing could be more demonstrative of a lack of love of the Lord than to wilfully violate the thing that the Lord had indicated was His mind and will. Then the Lord, in the revelation, proceeds to indicate that tobacco is not good for man, but is to be used for sick cattle; and yet, you try to indicate that the use of tobacco is all right for man, but the Lord said it is not good for man. Now, why do you

not take the Lord at His word? And after the Lord has indicated what is good for us, why should we argue about it as you have done in this "Life's Philosophy" of yours?

Now Brother Earl, much more could be said on this subject, but I shall not take time as you have done to go at length in the matter. 

Accept my very kind regards, Sincerely your brother,

Dear Bishop: 

1. I take this privilege to thank you for your courteous reply to my thesis, My Life's Philosophy as it seems to me an answer is in order, and yet I hesitate lest I should tax your patience and infringe unduly on your valuable time. Were we all to see eye to eye, or attain to a point of common consent, all human progress would come to an end, and confusion worse confounded would cover the earth. 

2. With an apology to Voltaire, "I do not agree with a word you utter, but I will defend, even with my life, if needs be, your right to say it." Jesus, speaking to His Disciples and the multitude, (Matthew 15:7-20 inclusive), mentioned “unwashed hands" merely as a symbol of the very important lesson He was trying to teach them. If we consider carefully verse 17, I believe we will agree that the Savior was speaking from a literal standpoint. 

3. "Do not ye yet undertand, that (WHATSOEVER) entereth in at the mouth goeth into the belly, and is cast out into the draught?" (18) "But those things which proceed out of the mouth cometh from the heart; and they defile a man." President Joseph F. Merrill, in Millennial Star of a

back date, informs us that "what is not written in the Word of Wisdom is not a part of it." Tea and coffee, then, is not a part of the Word of Wisdom, and why have these things been added to the word? 

4. It is claimed by the highest scientific authority that milk is the most perfect food we have, yet there are those who cannot drink milk without it making them sick; and the same may be said of many other foods. Would it be wisdom for such people to use these foods? According to President

Merrill's philosophy it would, for the simple reason they are not mentioned in the Word of Wisdom. 

5. President Heber J. Grant, in the morning session of the General Conference a year or so ago, told the people that he would have to stay in his room in the afternoon, as his doctor told him he should, and he added, "you know we must obey the doctor's orders." My doctor, after giving me an examination, told me to use tobacco, but sparingly; and doctors tell other people to use a little wine, as a stimulant. How about obeying the doctor's orders in such cases? 

6. The Church tells the people there should not be one drop of intoxicating liquor in any Latter-day Saint's home.

How are we to have it for such purposes as are given in the Doctrine and Covenants if we do not have it in the home? 

I accept the Word of Wisdom as given in the Doctrine and Covenants as a basis upon which to build a better standard of physical health, but I do not accept as true gospel doctrine the prescribed edict of modern leaders. 

7. And so I feel like a lost sheep, an outcast, wandering aimlessly in the desolate wastes of intellectual oblivion, without a friendly ray of light to guide me on my way, all of which is the consequence of being a user of tobacco. And here we come face to face with a problem that is quite beyond the range of my comprehension; a problem which involves two. distinct lines of thought, and embraces an apparent conflic-

tion of authority as revealed by the Prophet Joseph Smith on the one hand, and the orthodox version as pronounced by present-day Church authority. 

8. The Word of Wisdom the Prophet Joseph Smith tells us in unmistakeable terms was not given as a commandment, neither as a restraint, but a word of counsel; and I accept it verbatim, and prize it as one of the finest principles ever laid down to man; a principle which, if people will adhere to its precepts, will lift them to a higher sphere of physical purity. 

9. Let us now take a sane and sensible view of this revelation from God to the prophet, and compare with the orthodox version proclaimed by present-day authority, and see if we can harmonize the two; and should we discover a definite confliction between the revealed doctrine through the Prophet Joseph and the concepts of present authority, then we must agree that one or the other is false and should be discarded. 

10. We are all familiar with the law of the Word of 

Wisdom as recorded in the Doctrine and Covenants. We will therefore proceed to consider the present-day set-up from a theological standpoint. In order to make clear my personal conceptions on the subject under discussion, it will be necessary to go into a little detail, and I offer an apology in making the first person the chief object in this respect. 

11. It was during a time when Virgin was a branch of the Hurricane Ward with Samuel Isom, Bishop; James Humphries, Presiding Elder; few in number, owing to a large portion of the members having moved to the new town of Hurricane. The attendance at Sacrament meetings consisted, for the most part, of Brother Humphries and wife, your humble servant and wife, Julia Beames, clerk, and three or four others by chance. But we kept the spark burning for a number of years until other people moved in. 

12. One Sunday Brother Humphries brought the Deseret News containing a sermon by President Joseph F. Smith,

and asked me to read it and comment on it, as he often did. 

The sermon was on the Word of Wisdom, with emphasis on the users of tobacco, and said, in effect, that a man who used tobacco was no good; he is not to be trusted; have no dealings with him; do not associate with him; put him behind you. 

13. A little later when Bishop Isom came to settle tithing, I went to settle mine, and I approached the Bishop on this subject, and asked him, being a tobacco user, and in consideration of what Joseph F. said, "Do you think if I pay tithing it will be acceptable to the Lord?" Bishop Isom never answered my question. He did say that Joseph F. was radical on tobacco, but never did answer my question. 

14. You make use of the word indicate several times in your letter, and I take objections to the use of this word in this particular subject under discussion, on the grounds that God never indicated anything, neither did the Prophet Joseph. The language is too plain and simple to be misunderstood or to be interpreted as meaning anything except what is written there. 

15. Nor did I infer that the present Church leaders have indicated that the Word of Wisdom is a commandment; on the contrary, I stated definitely that the message in the Progress of the Church folder stated that The Church has made it (the Word of Wisdom) a commandment, and that is final. 

And I do not believe that I have expressed myself in any manner that would justify the inference that I justify myself in the use of tobacco. 

16. Neither have I indicated that the Church Leaders are not justified in the course they have taken. I have, however, made it plain that I do not hold the leaders at blame, nor am I finding fault in what they are doing. No, my Brother, I would not dare commit myself in any such manner. My implicit faith in, and my unwavering love for, the principles

of the Gospel of the Son of God would not permit me to such a rash act. 

17. The point which I would like to convey to you is this: that the Word of Wisdom should be considered as an individual proposition, from the simple fact that what is healthful for one is positively injurious to another, therefore the hard-set rules advanced by the Church leaders, and the statement made by Joseph F. Merrill, are not amenable to sound logic or even common sense; besides such a code of conflicting principles would destroy the very fundamentals of the revealed word. 

18. Should another man partake of foods or drinks other than those mentioned in the Word of Wisdom, which are more detrimental to his body than the tobacco which I use is to my body, then he has commited the greater offense, yet, so long as such foods or drinks are not mentioned in the Word of Wisdom, it does not affect his good offices in the Church. 

And why? Simply because, from the viewpoint of Joseph

F. Merrill, as well as the prescribed rules instituted by the leaders, he has not violated the word. 

19. In my humble judgment, if the Church will abandon those iron-clad regulation, and the Church and all the auxiliary organizations, as well as the public schools, will wage a health-promoting campaign, and teach the people, old and young, in the spirit of those fine principles instead of the word of the law; in the spirit that Jesus would teach them were He here, then there might be some progress accomplished. 

20. I would refer you to Doctrine and Covenants, Sec. 

121:35-40, inclusive, and give it your careful and unbiased consideration in connection with my suggestions, also my treatise on this very important subject. 

21. We will now turn to St. Matthew, Chapter 15, and consider this little matter from a Christ-doctrine viewpoint,

and see how the conception of present-day authority measures up with the teachings of Jesus. I am seriously persuaded that we will find a conflict of doctrine on the part of authority, else the inability on my part to harmonize the component parts of the two factors. of

25. And then I fall to pondering on the concepts of present day authority as concerns the Word of Wisdom, and my mind reverts to Isaiah 24:5, "The earth also is defiled under the inhabitants thereof; because they have transgressed the laws, changed the ordinances, broken the everlasting Covenant." 

26. It is possible we are casting into the discard the teachings of Jesus, and making obsolete the revelations of the 

Prophet Joseph Smith? Are we substituting these divine principles with man-made doctrines which are strictly in discord with, and more oppressive to the people than the old orthodox version? I am not offering any advice, merely seeking after truth. 

27. Our present Church authority tells us that the use of tobacco, tea, coffee and intoxicating liquor defiles the body (the man) and that the spirit of God cannot dwell in such unclean tabernacles. Jesus says these things do not defile the man (the body). The question with me is, which shall I believe? That there is a conflict of opinion no one will dispute, and we have for our guidance the teachings of Christ and the revelations of Joseph Smith on the one hand, and the word of mouth of our leaders on the other. 

28. Granting the present leaders have received revelations from God, and we accept the same as being authentic, then the question pops up: "What are we to do with the

teachings of Christ and the revelations handed down to us from our Prophet Joseph Smith? Shall we discard the New 

Testament and annul the Doctrine and Covenants? 

29. And here is the situation as concerns my part in the matter. If my position in the Church is pruned down to merely a member by name, with all the privileges to function in any of the ordinances withheld; barred from holding any office; restrained from doing work in the Temple; unworthy of recognition; not to be trusted; depraved to the degree that my brethren must not associate with me; then may I be justified in the conclusion that my membership in the Church is null and void? 

30. This is, to me at least, a very vital problem, in which 

I am deeply concerned. I am, therefore, trusting the matter in the hands of my good Christian brothers at the top, in the hope that they will give the matter their immediate and conscientious consideration; that they will remember this, that 

I am not criticizing, neither fault-finding. 

31. I am seeking after truth, and nothing but the plain, simple truth will ever satisfy this hungering soul. Trusting the Presiding Bishopric of the Church will accept this in the same good spirit in which it is given, I beg to subscribe myself, most sincerely, June 13, 1941

My dear Bishop: 

S. E. 

Virgin, Utah, June 13, 1941 

I want to thank you again for your friendly reply to my thesis My Life's Philosophy. Pardon me for quoting from your letter on June 9, 1941, paragraph two, beginning on line three; Quote-"But you apparently fail to keep in mind that the Savior was referring only to the matter of eating with unwashed hands." Permit me to call your attention again to 

Matt. 15:17 when Jesus answered Peter's question as to the meaning of the parable. 

He said to Peter: "Do not ye yet understand, that whatsoever entereth in at the mouth goeth into the belly, and is

cast out into the draught" etc. Now the question hinges on the word whatsoever. Webster informs us that it means what ever, any thing so ever every thing that is. Surely, then, the 

Savior was not "referring only to the matter of eating with unwashed hands."

There is but one logical conclusion, and that is that Jesus knew what He was talking about, and that He had in mind foods and beverages of whatsoever kind that mankind partakes of, regardless of its injurious properties. God's spirit doe not dwell in the corruption of the body, but rather in the mind, the heart, the soul.

The body may be full of disease, cancer, and other afflictions, while at the same time that man may be living the righteousness on the Divine law, his soul in tune with the spirit of God, and the mysteries of Heaven will be unfolded to his understanding, even though he is a friend to the sinners, a wine bibbler and takes his food with unwashed hands. 

God never made a temporal law, quite to the contrary all God's laws are spiritual laws, and since the Word of Wisdom is a temporal law, hence it is merly a law of doctrine instituted by man. We humans are too apt to mistake shadow for substance, to accept a whimsical theory as orthodox fact because it is more pleasant to the intellectual palate. We prefer the course that offers the least resistence, hence the Word of Wisdom has been magnified an hundred fold and made a subterfuge by which to escape the weightier matter of the law.

Let us meet the issue squarely, fairly and bravely, that we may not be classed with the "scribes, Pharisees, hypocrites, who Jesus likened unto whitened sepulchers. 

Is Sylvester Earl

Dear Brother Earl: 

If I misunderstood your "My Life's Philosophy" and your letter, from which I assumed that you are using tobacco, I beg your pardon for this misunderstanding. You seem to be quite set in your idea about the right of the Saints to use tobacco and other things forbidden in the Word of Wisdom, and your letter would indicate that President Brigham Young sustained the same ideas as those expressed in your letter. No doubt you have Brigham Young's Discourses, and I quote you from page 283 as follows: "I know that some say the revelations upon these points are not given by way of commandment. Very well, but we are commanded to observe every word that proceeds from the mouth of God. Now I want you should recollect, Bishops,

Elders of Israel, High Priests, Seventies, the Twelve Apostles, the First Presidency, and all the house of Israel, hearken, hearken oh, my people, keep the word of the Lord, observe the Word of Wisdom, sustain one another, sustain the household of faith, and let your enemies alone. Now, Elders of 

Israel, if you have the right to chew tobacco, you have a privilege I have not. If you have the right to drink whiskey, you have a right that I have not. If you have a right to transgress the Word of Wisdom, you have a right that I have not. 

I said to the Saints at our last annual conference, the spirit whispers to me to call upon the Latter-day Saints to observe the Word of Wisdom, to let tea, coffee, and tobacco alone, and to abstain from drinking spirituous drinks. This is what the spirit signifies through me." 

---

If you have one of Brigham Young's Discourses, you will enjoy reading pages 282 to 294, and possibly a little farther. I take the liberty of quoting two more references, the first from page 285: "This Word of Wisdom, which has been supposed to have become stale, and not in force, is like all the counsels of God-as much in force today as it ever was. There is life, everlasting life, in it, the life which now is and the life which is to come."

Then I quote from page 286 as follows: 

"Many of our sisters think they cannot live without tea. 

I will tell you what we can do. I have frequently said it to my brethren and sisters. If they cannot live without tea, coffee, brandy, whiskey, wine, beer, tobacco, etc., they can die without them. This is beyond controversy. If we had the determination we should have, we would live without them or die without them."

CHAPTER II. 

PIONEERING IN UTAH'S DIXIE 

1. My parents were called by Brigham Young to go to

Dixie and help in the settlement of this (then) desolate and forbidden land, so they left their little home in Ogden, and arrived in St. George, Washington County, Territory of Utah, in 1862, where I was born the following year. When I was very young, they moved to Washington, five miles distant, then to Rockville, and from there to Springdale, which is situated near the mouth of Zion Canyon. 

2. My Father made chairs and baskets, and when he had enough to load a wagon he hauled them to the Northern settlements and traded them for products, such as flour, potatoes, meat and other eatables. But he had previously contracted consumption, probably when he used to stand guard all night in water waist deep, and many other exposures. And

Brigham advised him to go to a higher climate, thinking the change might help him. 

3. We moved temporarily to Pine Valley, where my 

Father died that winter, the winter I was eight years old, when, in December I was baptized under the ice as you have previously read. Next summer Mother and we children, three in number, my brother Cyrus, three years my senior, sister 

Rose, the baby, and your humble servant, returned to Springdale. We had a team of horses, "Pick" and "Kate" by name, an old Mormon linchpin wagon, with no brakes nor lock chain, and the harness was minus breechen and poll straps. 

4. I was teamster, and the way we got over the steep, rough roads was a miracle. The horses in holding the wagon from running away down those rocky and precipituous hills, would sometimes be skidding on their haunches, the collars

tight against their heads, the tongue high in the air, and at times the horses' forefeet off the ground, with an elderly lady and three small children under a canvas stretched over three bows made of black willows, would make a picture worthy a place in the world's museum. 

5. Some three years prior to our move to Pine Valley, my brother, who seemed possessed with an inherent desire to learn the art of the violin, often went over to a neighbor, Albert Petty, who played and owned a violin, and would sit and listen intently while Albert played the instrument, and the good neighbor would some times let Cyrus practice on his fiddle and gave him a few lessons. 

6. Brother Cyrus, not having the money to buy a violin decided to manufacture one, so got some pine lumber, and with Father's tools, went to work. He whittled and chiseled, and in time fashioned all the parts, then got the feet of a beef someone had butchered, put them boiling in Mother's big iron kettle, from which he extracted glue, with which he fastened the parts together. All he lacked now was fiddle strings. 

7. He had heard of "catgut" strings, and asked Mother if he could kill the old pussy cat, but Mother turned thumbs down on the proposition. But one day when Mother was off to a public quilting bee, and returned home in the evening, Cyrus had about three hundred yards of catgut strung along the fence, washed clean, and was stripping off small shreds, twisting them between his fingers and testing them. 

8. Mother was angry over Cyrus killing the old cat, and scolded him for his conduct, but Cyrus had the guts, and made strings for his fiddle, and to tell you just how happy that boy was, words fail me. Almost day and night he seesawed on his self-constructed violin. If I had that fiddle and the picture of our old team and wagon with its precious cargo skidding down the Toquer Twist, I would prize it at a half

million with a hat full of Governor Blood's sales tokens thrown in. 

9. The winter we were in Pine Valley, Brigham Nay, the man who married my eldest sister, ran onto an old man who had a violin, a real store fiddle, with two strings on it, and the old fellow was out of tobacco and gave Brigham the fiddle for two chaws of "tabacker" and Brigham gave it to Brother Cyrus. Cyrus was simply elated, and learned to play a number of tunes on the two strings. "Old Dan Tucker," "Yankeedoodle," and "The Girl I Left Behind Me," were his favorites. 

10. As he grew older he took music lessons, later he composed and wrote a number of tunes, sent some of them off for publication, and received some royalties. "Over The 

Waves," which is still played over the world, and which, no doubt, has made someone rich, was one of his compositions. 

But little did he realize out of them. He became a professional violinist, but never traveled; lived out his life a poor man, and died at the age of sixty-four years. 

11. Speaking of hardships incident to settlement of Utah's

Dixie, I can say that I have tasted somewhat of those grand old times. I said "grand old times" and I mean just that; times when the Mormon people were a unity of Brotherhood; time when they shared alike, when there were no rich neither poor, yet all were poor, and all were rich-poor in the things of this world's goods, rich in the spirit of God; times when one housewife would divide the last pan of flour with her neighbor, and gave thanks to God she had it to divide, and the recipient gave thanks to the same God for it. 

12. Times when we gathered pigweeds, red roots and leaves from grease wood, and other vegetation and boiled them for greens, and were thankful for the privilege. Times when we ate wild onions, sego roots, parched crickets, barbecued grasshoppers, and ate them with a relish, and thanked

God for it. I knew one family that lived in Washington, who

lived, or it might be more proper to say existed on boiled lucerne for six weeks, and had neither salt nor pepper nor seasoning. 

13. Later I went to Cedar City to live with one Brother

Morrill, who paid my schooling, gave me my board and keep, for me to do chores and help about the place. Then Joel 

Johnson of Belvue, now Pintura on Highway 91, bargained with Mother to move over there, and he would give us a house to live in and pay us boys $15.00 a month to work for him. That means he gave us $7.50 a month each. And how well I remember when Mother sent for me to come down and go to work, and here it so happened that my schooling ended with the second lesson in the first reader. 

14. Two or three years later Silver Reef mining camp, where silver was first discovered in sandstone, opened up and we moved to Silver Reef. I went to work for Gilbert B. 

Smith, hauling wood and later ore from the mines. The first shipments of ore from these mines were sent to Pioche, Nevada. That was before quartz mills were established in the new camp. 

15. I hauled hundreds of tons of this ore; much of it was fabulously rich in silver content, some worth $2,000 per ton. The stories concerning the original discovery of silver, when and by whom, are somewhat conflicting, on which I shall not so much as offer a comment. However, I will give you a brief account of the first discovery of silver in the Silver Reef section, the findings which have never been made known to the world, so far as I am informed. 

16. In the early settlement by the Mormons in this westland, Brigham Young advised the people not to engage in mining, and wisely, too, for he knew there were great treasures of mineral in the mountains, and that if the people got excited about the matter of getting rich quickly, they would neglect their homes, and great hardships would be the result.

17. It was during this period that an uncle of mine, Wilber Earl who lived in Leeds, a little town situated about one mile from where Silver Reef now stands, located two claims, and worked them secretly, doing his assessments in the night, and was very careful lest some one would become wise to the fact that he had discovered silver in the sandstone reef. Before

Wilber died he disclosed the secret of his findings to his two boys, John and George, and told them to continue on as he had been doing, promising them if they would these prospects would someday make them rich. 

18. But the boys took little stock in the matter, thinking, no doubt, that the old man was visionary, and let the matter rest at that. But in later years they learned to their utter disappointment that their Father had told them the truth, but it now was too late for them to profit by his experience. The boys lived there through the Silver Reef boom days, then moved to Arizona, and I suspect, like most of us, lived out their lives in poverty and hard work. 

19. In one of the mines, several hundred feet below surface, a live toad was blasted out of the rocks; in another, a petrified log was found, from which was taken $60,000 in silver. At least, that was the claim. From Silver Reef I went to Eureka, Nevada, then a booming mining camp, and engaged in burning coal for the big smelters. Fourteen months later I returned to Utah and lived for a year or so in Kingston, where the United Order was flourishing. 

20. I never joined the Order, but two sisters and my brother were full-fledged members, and a very happy community it was. They all took their three meals each day in a large building, where cooks and waitresses prepared meals and served the great congregation. They had their dances, theaters and other amusements, and a more contented and happier people would be hard to fashion even in the mind. If only such a system could be maintained and perpetuated,

even among a tribe of Latter-day Saints, would have set a pattern of economic equality to the world that in time may have revolutionized the human race to a higher plain of temporal perfection. 

21. But the Order was destined to fall, and all because of the greed and selfishness in the hearts of the Leaders. All properties were deeded over to them, which fact in itself, from a mere human viewpoint, would instill into the human soul a desire for selfish gain and the Leaders, as a rule, were humans. The Leaders of the Kingston Order were three brothers named King: William, Edwin and Oliver; and a fourth brother, John who never joined the order, but operated a ranch in Grass Valley and took care of the horses and cattle. 

22. A I remember William King was Bishop and his brothers, Edwin and Oliver, were his counselors, and John a silent partner. Some of the older members of the community became suspicious that "all was not well in Zion" and this suspicion took root in the minds of others, and soon became manifest throughout the community, and a general revolt was in the making. 

23. The Kings of the Kingdom became aware that their little game was up, and, presumably, a message was sent post haste to headquarters-Salt Lake City, Utah-and in due course of time the people were called to go to Arizona and help build up the town of St. Johns. Most of them responded to the call, and the Kings of the Kingdom got off with the swag which in round numbers amounted to thousands of head of sheep, horses and cattle; thousands of acres of land, together with all the buildings, farm equipment and other wealth.

27. Evidently they were planning to steal the sheep from the United Order and selling them to another sheepman who ranged his herd on the opposite side of the valley. The fact that the schemer number two was a brother-in-law of mine, will account for their planning the theft scheme in my presence. A few thoughts involuntarily rambled through my mind and I said, "I can get them for you." Marshall gave me a surprised look and said, "If you can, lad, I'll pay you right handsomely for your trouble." No," I replied, "I don't want any pay, I'll do it just for the fun of it.'

29. And I continued, “See that low summit a quarter mile to the east? I'll drive the herd over the ridge, divide them and give your 500 a run down this side and turn the main herd back. If you hear a gun shot, you will know that the coast is clear; then do your stuff, and I'll take care of my end of the job." Marshall was puzzled as to how I was going to work out the plan, and I informed them that it was all ready worked out and no more explanation was necessary. 

30. At sunset that evening I rode up to the herder's camp and asked him what chance there was to get lodging over night, and he invited me to "climb down," that he was glad to have my company. After supper I asked him where he ranged his sheep, and said, “Up this swale toward the main mountain." I asked him if he cared if I drove them to the west, over the summit, as I believed I could get a buckskin up the swale, providing he would loan me his rifle, to which he readily consented. 

31. At the first light of day next morning I had the sheep moving, and when they lapped over the summit I rode through the herd at the proper time, divided them, gave the 500 a run down the slope and headed the main herd back to the southeast. I saw the two men emerge from their concealment and mount their horses. I fired the rifle and watched until they were around the sheep and hurrying them off to the west. 

32. Then I drove the others back to the swale and arrived at camp for breakfast. I thanked the herder for his hospitality and took my way to town. A week or so later the herder missed some sheep and reported to the Kings who

A week or two later one of the Kings of the Kingdom had a talk with the man who ran a herd of sheep on the opposite side of the valley, and was informed that he had bought some sheep of Jim Marshall, but never suspected them of being stolen property. From this clue the Kings followed up and as a result Jim Marshall was indicted and appeared in court. Several of the supposed stolen sheep were put in a corral near by, and the day of the trial came, and I was among the spectators to witness the proceedings.

The lawyer asked him how he identified the sheep, and he said "by its countenance." "You are excused," said the lawyer, “as a sheep has no countenance." And the witness was excused.

THE VIRGIN OIL FIELD 

1. In 1903, myself with three others, became interested in the prospects of discovering oil in this section. An old resident of Virgin told us of a place about one and one-half

miles west of town, where, he informed us, the early pioneers got oil out of sand shale to grease their wagons; and we were guided to the place by the old settler. 

2. We discovered the live oil in a solid ledge and blasted out a few hundred pounds of the shale, and found it literally full of small pockets of live oil. We went over to old Silver

Reef and obtained the use of a small retort, and distilled a gallon of the crude oil. One of my companions was an analyst, and he refined and separated all the different kinds of oils and lubricants with a small outfit he carried in his buggy. 

3. The test proved the crude oil to contain ninety-seven per cent valuables, and we began locating oil claims. Under the old placer mining law one man could only locate twenty acres, but we learned that by using eight names we could locate 160 acres in one claim; but would have to discard one name and add a new one every eight locations. 

4. We sent one of our men out to see if we could interest someone to come on and investigate the field, with the view of putting up money to bring in an oil rig and drill a well; but Virgin being seventy miles from the nearest railroad station, which was Lund, and a rough wagon road over which to travel, we found it a hard matter to get money interested. The experts told us that without doubt there was a big deposit of oil, but it was too far from transportation. 

5. All three of my partners became discouraged and left, but I was determined to see the thing through, and kept on locating, until in 1907 I induced a party from California to bring a rig in and drill. I gave them five 160 acre claims as an inducement. A few months later they struck oil, which was the first producing oil well in the State of Utah and drilled on my property. 

6. This caused a great excitement and men flocked to 

Virgin by the hundreds. Within one year there were seventeen oil rigs operating in the field. All standard rigs except,

as I remember, two portable ones. It was estimated that one and a quarter millions of dollars was spent here that year. 

Then the money panic came. Most of the drills had struck the hard lime formation which necessitated the buying of new tools, their money was gone, the banks closed, and the bottom of the Virgin oil field dropped out. 

7. Just before the crash two parties came in to buy oil land: Dr. George Middleton, Harry Joseph and two others from Salt Lake City, and a party from Gold Field, Nevada.

They came to me to make the deal, as I was about the only one who had the goods. We closed a deal, the two amounting to $32,000. I was to have the deeds made and the notary's seal added and send them to their respective banks, and they were to put the money in the banks and receive the deeds. 

8. Three days before the parties arrived at their respective banks the banks closed and they could not get a dollar.

RUBBER-NECK 

Speaking of rubber-necks and embarrassing moments, I am reminded of a little incident which happened when I was a young man. It was in the early '70's, as I recollect, when people travelled about with horse teams and dead-ex wagons, and only the high-ups could afford a carriage. 

Good old times, those. When people were content to take more time, and were happy to have even a dead-ex wagon in which to ride. And I am offering no apology in expressing my firm conviction that the people enjoyed themselves to a higher degree, and experienced greater satisfaction in life in those good old days than they are getting in these modern times with the automobile and other conveniences of scientific inventions.

But I started out to relate a little incident in which I figured somewhat conspicuously, at least from a personal standpoint, and which incident had its origin in the little village of Roony, the town I was proud to call my home.

Roony boasted a population of 390 souls, counting the children and not omitting a couple of Irishmen who were making the village their home temporarily.

About the most important thing in and around Roony, aside from the public school and post office, and in passing, I might mention the Co-op store, was its theatrical troupe, whose membership consisted of nine males and four females. 

They were young and full of life and overflowing with zealous anticipation of the coming season, which was now close at hand.

It so happend that at one of our club meetings, which

we held somewhat irregularly on the second Tuesday of each month, that your humble servant was elected by unanimous vote to the exalted position of general manager and stage instructor, all in one. To say that this highly responsible office caused my head to swell would be putting the matter very mildly.

Late in the autumn of '73, having planned a tour of some twelve or fifteen towns to the north, andt equipped with three covered wagons, one of which had a sheep bed—a wagon box 7 x 15 feet-which answered as a sleeping apartment for the ladies, kitchen, dining room and general assembly, we took our departure among shouts of good cheer and acclamatory God-speed from a jolly crowd of good old home-made friends, who had gathered for the express purpose of giving us a rousing send-off.

We were armed with half a dozen good plays, one of which was a wild-and-wooly west sort, with considerable blood and thunder (mostly thunder) mixed in, and a streak of comic as a relief to the more, but I forbear, lest I ever draw the lines, as it is not my inclination to illustrate the characteristic features of the plays nor to follow in detail the course of our travels.

Our outlined course took us to the north, and by the latter part of eDcember we arrived at a town called Sterling, which was situated at the base of the Wasatch range, and seemed to us as being in close proximity to the north pole. 

There was a few inches of snow on the ground, and the thermometer was hovering around zero, which was not particularly comfortable to the blood of a southern clime. 

Sterling was a thrifty little town of some three hundred inhabitants and, to our delight, boasted a fine hewn-log amusement hall, with scenery nicely arranged, and three extra settings. Having located the management we secured the use of the hall and proceeded to send out our hand bills and tack

up our posters for an entertainment which was scheduled to open at 7:30 that evening.

Promptly at the appointed time every arrangement having been artistically completed for the performance, and with spirits overflowing in anticipation of a successful evening, we nervously awaited the arrival of our audience. We waited and waited, with unabated expectations, notwithstanding only fifteen minutes intervened before the time for the curtain to rise, and not a single person had as yet put in an appearance.

Yegles, the comedian, was behind the wings, nervously awaiting the call of the bell; the villian, Christy, decked out in his vicious make-up, and bearing the very semblance of a hungry cannibal, was armed to the teeth and looking for trouble; the scouts were dressed in their buckskin trousers and jackets fringed with gold; the heroine, with crimson cheeks and sparkling eyes was serenely abiding her time in acute anticipation of the beg event when she would be miraculously rescued from the blood-thirsty savages through the daring and heroic efforts of a brave lover.

The clock on the wall pointed to the hour of 7:30, and yet there was no sign of an audience. Suddenly, as if something had dropped from the skies, the whole town was astir. 

The clatter of horses' hoofs upon the frozen ground; the rumbling of wagons and buggies; the jingling and clinking of sleigh bells, and the happy voices in merry song and laughter, floated out upon the frost-bitten atmosphere, and started the stagnated blood coursing through our veins in a manner that fairly lifted us off our feet. Our dropping spirits took on new life with the one and only thought-the good and charitable people of Sterling had carefully planned a surprise, and were making a rush on the show in mass. 

But, oh, Ye Gods and Might Rules of the Dark Ages!

Imagine, if you can the surprise, the consternation, the pangs

of remorse, the vague misgivings of a happy little band of peaceful theatrical performers when, a few minutes later, the rattle of vehicles, the jingle of bells and merry voices passed by and died away in the distance, leaving the town in utter darkness, and death-like silence, save the lonesome howl of a stray dog or the uneasy whinney of a horse that had been separated from its mate.

I was bewildered, amazed, thunder-struck, chagrined. I turned to my companions, who by now had flocked around me, and exclaimed distractedly, "What does all this mean?"

But at that very instant I discovered a look suggestive of the same question evinced on the weird countenance of every member, and for several minutes we all stood staring blankly at each other.

At this juncture we were somewhat relieved by the entering of an urchin, who looked questionably around as if wondering what was going on in the town hall. I rushed at him viciously, and demanded, "What's the matter with the people tonight? Where are they all going? Why don't they came to the show? Speak up, lad; give us a clue; speak, for Heavens sake, why don't you talk? Are you dumb?" 

I stopped for want of breath; I was exhausted; and then

I discerned that the boy was scared. He soon found his tongue, however, and pushing back his broad brimmed slouchy hat, said, drawlingly, "Why, theyre all gone over to

I grabbed the sheet from his hand and glanced at it wildly. It was one of our own. "Sterling!" I shouted, vehemently, "Why, feller, isn't this Stering?" "No, sir," he replied, eying me critically, as though he thought I was going to devour him, "This is Mayfield, and Sterling is 'cross country

CHAPTER III. 

CONTROVERSY PLUS GODLINESS 

1. Controversy is the only means by which the mysteries of Godliness can ever be unfolded to our understanding. 

Brigham Young put it this way: "Now, as I understand it, it is the same with all facts that come to the knowledge of all beings in Heaven or on earth-all facts are proved and made manifest by their opposite." 

2. We are told in the good old Book of Books that "without controversy great is the mystery of Godliness." Yet how few there are who are able to grasp the significance of this simple truth. How can we find our way into realms of thought; discover new evidence of the existence of God; climb to higher realms of wisdom, were it not for controversy? 

3. Mistakes are the stepping stones to eternal progress; failures are but rungs in the ladder of fame; the pathway strewn with thorns and broken stones that gall and bleed the weary feet that traverse its lonely way is the flowery dell of peace and contentment to the courageous soul who dares to pioneer and blaze new trails through unexplored jungles of intellectual obscurities. 

4. Through the eye of the carnal conscience we see and comprehend the material things of life. Through the eye of the spiritual conscience we see and comprehend the glories of Godliness. Were it not so the world would be shrouded in chaos, and the inhabitants thereof would be groping in intellectual oblivion, searching in vain for a light (those opposite evil forces) by which to find their way out. 

5. We will now take up the subject of "law and justice" versus "grace and mercy." Open your Bible and turn to Galations, 3:23, 24, 25. "But before faith came, we were kept

under the law, shut up unto the faith which should be revealed. Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ that we might be justified by faith. But after that faith is come, we are no longer under a schoolmaster." 

6. One of our articles of faith says in part "we believe in honoring, obeying and sustaining the law." In view of the fact that some of the laws are condemned (the repeal law for instance) the question naturally arises, "How can we honor and sustain a law and at the same time condemn it?" This question was put before the Gospel Doctrine Class, but received such a cold reception on the grounds that such deep problems disturbed the tranquility of the class, and create confusion, that the matter was dropped. 

7. The question had previously come to me involuntarily, and impressed me as being vitally important from the viewpoint that it raised a doubt in the wisdom of such doctrine, and further it cast a reflection on the Prophet Joseph 

Smith, the author of the statement. 

8. I became seriously concerned, and determined to find the answer that would justify the Prophet's affirmation. 

When read the above quotation in Galatians, the mystery unfolded to my understanding, and the matter became simple fact. Had I been privileged I would have explained to the class something as follows: 

9. The school master is an indispensible factor in the great plan of life and salvation, for the reason that it is the only source by which we can attain to that faith in Christ, and become a member in His Church. The schoolmaster, in my humble judgment, is the greatest among the evil forces that beset the path of Christian endeavor, while to those who live above this carnal law, it becomes the greater blessing, and for this reason we "believe in honoring, obeying and sustaining the law," as a schoolmaster, and as an essential

factor in the universality of God's Divine plan of life and salvation. I repeat the words of Brigham Young. 

10. Brigham Young informs us that all facts that come to the knowledge of human beings in Heaven or on earth, are proved and made manifest by their opposites. The schoolmaster, then, being the direct opposite of the Divinity of Christ and the Christian faith, proves beyond the shadow of doubt the vital necessity of putting our eternal trust in the great Master and His doctrine which is so far estranged from the jurisprudence of the law. 

11. And thus we arrive at the logical conclusion that of the two great ruling forces of the universe, "good" and "evil" one is just as essential and indispensible as the other in the consummation of the one grand harmonious system of the Divine. We must taste of the bitter in order to appreciate the sweet. Were there no night there could not be a day; without a darkness there could be no light; neither could there be a Heaven were there no Hell. Did you ever get lost in the darkness of the night, and become bewildered as to the right course to follow that you might reach your destination? Suppose there had been no wrong way, in which case anyway would take you home. Think this over carefully, and I am sure you will agree that the story of the schoolmaster contains a world of wisdom that every one professing the Christian faith should thoroughly understand, even at the cost of a little confusion. 

12. For further information on The Law we will consult 

Timothy 1:9. "Knowing this, that the law is not made for a righteous man, but for the lawless and disobedient, for the ungodly and for sinners, for the unholy and profane, for murderers of mothers, for manslayers." Section 10: "For whoremongers, for them that defile themselves with mankind, for manstealers, for liars, for perjured persons, and if there be any other thing that is contrary to sound doctrine.”

13. Here we are given definite understanding as to what the law is made for. There is positively no diversion from these fundamental facts, and that the schoolmaster is the feudal parent of strife and turmoil, and the feculent breeding ground for the deceptive and cunning designers of the kingdom of Satan on earth, is apparent in every walk of life. 

14. And the fact that we Christians are still clinging to the old tradition of "law and justice" is proof positive that we are under the schoolmaster, while we profess our faith in Christ and proclaim to the world our allegiance to His Divine order. And here let me emphasize that the subject under discussion is one of the most significant and far-reaching lessons that can ever be expounded to a Christian people, and the one seldom if ever mentioned and least understood. 

15. Now that we have set the light glimmering on the horizon of a new line of thought, let us follow up by harkening to Paul. "Now we are delivered from the law, that being dead wherein we were held, that we might serve in newness of the spirit and not in the oldness of the letter. Now the righteousness of God without the law is manifested, being witness by the law and the prophets. Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to every one that believeth." 

16. Paul further informs us that "Christ is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law ye are fallen from grace." The foregoing is self-explanatory, and nothing can be more explicit, more simple, more comprehensive to all those who have graduated from the schoolmaster and attained to that faith in Christ. 

17. God's elect people, then, are those who have attained to that faith in Christ; who are keeping themselves unspotted from the world; who are not justified by the law; who are living in that higher sphere of "Grace and Mercy" else how are they to be separated as sheep from the goats? 

18. Upon the principles of "law and justice" on the

one hand, and “grace and mercy" on the other, rests the destiny of Christian civilization. So long as we justify ourselves by the law, just so long will we continue to be slaves under the rule of the schoolmaster. To me there is no solution, for certain it is that we cannot serve two masters. 

19. JOE'S LOVE LETTER 

It was when I was a young man, and that was a long time ago, a dozen or fifteen of us were out in the hills chopping cord wood to furnish fuel for the big quartz mills that were hammering out the gold and silver from the mines in that historical old mining camp, Pioche, Nevada. 

We were a happy-go-lucky masculine population, booming full of jollity with a little innocent deviltry occasionally just to break the monotony of the everyday vocation. One day a newcomer arrived, a young man we learned to call Joe, and I shared my tent camp with him, and Joe and I became good friends. 

One evening Joe came to me and said, “Jack, I just got a letter from my home town, and I believe it is from my best girl over in Utah, and you know I can't read nor write, and thought maybe you might read it for me.” "Why, sure I can," I replied, "let's see it." But Joe with-

held the letter while a troubled expression swept over his countenance. "But," he said, "if you read it you will know what she says, and I don't want any one to know what she says." And

Joe was laboring under a mental strain that hurt. “I thought perhaps you could think of some way you could read it and not know," he concluded, with a spark of hope gleaming in his bright blue eyes.

I pondered for a moment, then a thought came to me and I replied, with wavering confidence, “Sure, Joe, we can fix that; you see I can stuff my ears full of cotton so I can't hear and no one will know anything about it but you." Joe smiled and handed me the letter. "I thought you could think of some way," and his face beamed with delight.

I pulled a handful of cotton out of an old mattress, crammed my ears full, and read Joe's letter. He was tickled plum pink, and then he wanted me to write a letter to his best girl in answer, but again his face clouded, and he wondered how I could turn the trick. You see, he didn't want me to know what the letter said.

But I was in the game now, and the rest was easy. "Easy as fun, Joe," I replied. "You see, I'll stuff my mouth full of cotton so I can't read it," and Joe was laughing all over. I took the cotton from my ears and stuffed my mouth full, then I wrote Joe's love letter, then changed the cotton from my mouth back to my ears and read Joe the letter.

I knew nothing about writing love letters, but consulting my memory I recalled something I had heard some years before, and wrote it to the best of my recollection. 

As I remember it run something like this, "Dear Emly" (Emly was the name of Joe's best girl over in Utah.) "Dear Emly, my love is stronger than the smell of coffee, patent butter or the kick of a young cow; shocks of ex-

quisite joy go through me like coharts of ants through an army cracker, and caper over my heart like young goats on a stable roof or an old stage horse in a green pasture. "As the mean pup hankers after new milk so do I hanker after your presence; as the gosling swimeth in the mud puddle so do I swimeth in a sea of delightfullness when you are near me; my eyes and mouth stand open like cellar doors in a country town, and if my love is not reciprocated I will pine away and die like a poisoned bed bug and you may come and catch cold on my grave. 

Useless to say Joe was delighted to the point of histerics, and he offered to chop me a cord of wood, but I advised him to save his dollars for that future event when he returned home and to remember me kindly to his bride-to-be.

Under the protection of the strong arm of the law (?) the limit to their machinations is measured only by their ability to despoil their fellows.

ALIEN TO ALL THAT IS GOOD 

Moral law and constitutions are but playthings in their lecherous clutches, and gangsters of the underworld are princ-

es of peace by comparison. They are alike destitute of both honor and shame, estranged from any semblance of human justice, and alien to all that is good and true and pure. 

True there are honorable exceptions, but they serve only to emphasize the putrescense of the whole, and if the American people tamely submit to their servile flattery after next November it will be circumstantial evidence that something is radically wrong with their heads, and a mind specialist should be called in to determine the nature of the malady. 

LET US BUILD BETTER 

There is only one sane and logical course to pursue, and that is for the American people to band themselves together in one solid, impregnable unit, demand their inalienable rights under the mandates of the Constitution, and reconstruct the entire political, industrial, social, economic institution from A to Z. And this can be accomplished without the least disturbance in the equilibrium of American citizenship.

Let us not then demolish the old corrupt institutions, but build new and better ones in their stead. I will now make a close examination of the cause of Uncle Sam's afflictions and attempt to infuse into the old poluted structure principles of a more wholesome nature. 

"LOVE OF GOLD THE ROOT OF ALL EVIL" 

The above quotation, is, I believe, accepted by all civilized people as a concrete fact. Gold is that all-alluring agency that deceives, hypnotizes, bewilders and fascinates the mind of man, and leads him captive into the pitfalls of human degeneracy. The love of gold, bright sparkling gold, is, always has been, and will ever continue to be the cause of more human suffering than all other agencies combined, and unless

and until it is stripped of all its unholy power as a medium of exchange, the future is but a sad and forlorn hope. 

That gold is valueless as a medium of exchange except the value put upon it by an Act of Congress and signed by the President of the United States, is a fact too well known to admit of argument. Gold, like paper, would be but a commodity of the market were it not for that Act of Congress that makes it a legal tender; both are governed by the rules of law as distinguished from the rules of equity, and the paper thus declared money would be worth its face value were there not an ounce of gold in existence. 

If gold is as valuable and indispensible as is claimed for it, why, then, is it necessary that Congress exercise its legislative power to make it legal tender? Paper as a useful commodity is many times more valuable than gold, in fact paper is indispensible, whereas if gold were to vanish away it would never be missed.

DISCIPLES OF THE CROSS OF GOLD 

The trouble is we place too much emphasis on mere plausibilities and ignore actualities. We have become absorbed in theory to the extent that we have lost sight of the logical, and the result is those disciples of the "Cross of Gold" who have been soaring to the heights of financial fame are beginning to realize that the royal sceptre which they have been wielding over the heads of the people is but a dark and dismal labyrinth of fantastic delusions into which they have carelessly plunged and from which there is no escape. All medium of exchange, be it gold, silver, paper or what not, is merely a representative of wealth, and of no value beyond its market price as a commodity.

THE GOLDEN IMAGE 

Our big profit takers, politicians and others of their ilk, would have us believe that unless there is gold hoarded up in

government vaults as a surety against the devaluation of our medium of exchange, that we would be financially ruined. But their monetary wisdom is purely imaginary and in no wise amenable to the laws of logic or of common sense. 

Let us, just for the sake of a little friendly discussion, suppose that all the gold in America, coined and uncoined, mined and unmined were piled up in a strong vault in our national capital, and a mighty guard kept continually on patrole to see that no thieves break in and steal. Let us suppose again that green paper with Uncle Sam's dollar mark stamped upon it is issued as legal tender to the exact amount in dollars and cents of the gold in storage, and that the green paper is accepted at its face value because of the gold reserve as guarantee of its money worth. Let us suppose again that some dark night when all the world is slumbering in sweet repose, and not a living thing astir, that the earth, directly beneath the great vault, opens and the last fine ounce of the yellow metal drops to the fathomless depths of the earth, the cavity closes up without a creak or a sound and not a living person is aware of the catastrophe.

A MENTAL HULLUCINATION 

Let us suppose once more, that 100 years have passed, a new generation has grown up; that the guard is still on duty and the green paper doing its money's worth, not because the gold is vaulted against its devaluation, because the yellow met. al is gone for good and for evermore, but the confidence of the people rests entirely on the BELIEF that the gold is still there, which proves beyond the shadow of doubt that our golden image is but a mental hullucination-the fantasy of a deranged intellect.

Were it not for the mischievous calamity-clack of these unhung idiots who pose as the saviors of the nation, all the gold in America could be sunk in the bottom of the ocean

and not one person in 10,000 who could make affidavit of his or her knowledge of there being one ounce of gold in reserve in government vaults. We are simply chasing the shadow in the hope of finding the substance.

THE UNIT OF VALUES-SOMETHING BETTER 

Now that I have proven, as I believe, the sophistry of the claim that there must be a gold reserve to guarantee the standard value of the exchange media, I propose to offer in its stead something better, more substantial, logical and bene ficial. 

It is common knowledge, and all thinking people will readily concede, that LABOR is responsible for all created wealth, that without labor there would be no wealth created? 

In the philosophy of all sound doctrine then, labor should be universally accepted as the unit of values, the balancing medium of all commodities that make up life's necessities and comforts. On an established wage scale all commodities used by man will be valued on the basis of actual cost of production, plus the price of transportation and exchange. Said wage scale will be uniform throughout the country. The man on the plow handle, the pick and shovel, in the mine or in the factory is just as essential and beneficial to the perpetuity of life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness as those acting in official capacities and should receive like consideration. 

RESOURCES BELONG TO PEOPLE 

I believe that all natural resources of the country rightfully and in very truth belong to all the people; that it is not the intent of the Constitution that a privileged few should exercise dominon over and ravage and waste these monuments of wealth for their own selfish purposes; that the Federal Government should put a stop to this outrageous and unrestrained tyranny.

I believe that all manufacturing institutions should operate in a similar manner as at the present time, except that they should be under the watchful supervision of the Federal Government to see that no chiseling or fraudulent tendencies creep in, and that those natural resources be developed economically as the needs of the people justify. Under this plan the man who produces the raw materials and he who turns out the finished goods will receive exactly what he earns, no more, no less, for the very simple reason that the prices of all commodities will depend on the price of labor. There will be no rich, neither poor, but all will enjoy the abundance of the earth, and there will be ushered in that great principle taught by the Master, that, “no man shall possess that which is above another."

THE TAX BURDEN 

Our present method of taxation is one of the most intricate, aggravating and incomprehensible institutions we have to tussel with, and I propose to simplify this part of the people's burden and make of it a plain, simple and understandable institution. It becomes imperative then, to abolish all methods of taxation except one-the income tax. As labor is the producer of all wealth, labor, then, should pay all the revenue, and all industries of whatsoever nature should be operated by the Government on the basis of the wage scale, and the Government should be obligated to furnish employment to all who are able to work.

In the case of unemployment the unemployed should draw their wages just the same, and the revenue should be ample to take care of all the aged, the lame and the blind, the poor and needy, sick and afflicted, and have the best care affordable.

Labor should be taxed a certain per cent, which will be sufficient to defray all expenses. This will simplify the tax problem so that everybody can read and understand.

REVENUE 

Should the revenue at any time become insufficient to carry on Government business, it will be necessary only to increase the wage scale which, in turn, will automatically incease the purchasing power of the people from the fact that prices of all commodities will advance correspondingly and revenue will pour forth abundantly and nobody will feel the pressure.

This method of taxation will not only simplify the tax problem but will also be a big saving factor in the number of officials and in equipment. It will eliminate the necessity of bonding and mortgaging; encourage confidence, instill into the soul of man a higher standard of fellowship and abolish 90 per cent of crime. It will kill the entire banking system, destroy all stock exchange gambling dens, prevent hoarding and profiteering for the very logical reason there will be no need of borrowing money; there will be nothing to gamble on; nothing to hoard and all profits will automatically return to those who honestly earned them.

NO USE FOR THEIR GOLD 

The common people can say to those with their hoarded millions, "Keep your gold and your silver; we have no use for it because we have money of our own, money that never fluctuates in price, and is always worth its face value. If you want our labor or the products of our labor you may have it providing you pay our price and buy with our money; but we have no use for your gold."

This is meant to imply that the privileges that belong to the people under the protecting wing of the Constitution have been wrestled from them by the big bosses under the pretense that "duty of submission to civil Government is expedient in order to promote Democracy." This is fallacy in the last analysis of the conception of the term, from the simple

fact that in their "submission to Civil Government" (?) Government of the big bosses, the people have forfeited their last claim to Democracy, and become mere machines under the will of despots.

Would it not then, be serving the ends of justice for the people to ask for, and if necessary demand, a change in Govemment that will restore their inalienable rights under the constitution?

THE STRENGTH OF GOVERNMENT 

A Democratic form of government is interpreted to mean a Government whose powers rests in the hands of the people, a Government of the people, for the people, by the people. 

Such an institution is founded upon the principles of religion. 

This is in very truth an orthodox Government, the only form of Government established by mortal man that withstand the buffetings of the oppressor, for any Government that reaches beyond the pale of religion is like the house built upon the sands.

Some will likely take objection to this because I have used the word "religion" but keep your seats, gents, until I give you my conception of the meaning of the word. Religion is love for God and one's fellow man; honesty and fairness in all business transactions; equal rights and privileges; moral justice; economic equality; co-operation where all strive for the betterment of the whole; obedience to the laws of right living; fellowship of man; that's religion. Response to "A Toast to the PIONEERS" 

Sylvester Earl

Brothers and Sisters, Friends and Fellow Citizens: 

Let us allow our minds to reflect back even to the times of the Puritans who tore themselves away from their homes and friends, and from every earthly tie that was dear to them; who stemmed the mighty ocean in their little frail ships trusting to the four winds of the earth and to the arm of the almighty God to carry them safely to a place where they might have the privilege to worship that God as their conscience desired.

Again let us reflect for a few brief moments on the lives and works of our progenitors, the MORMON PIONEERS, who after having been persecuted, mobbed, killed, and driven from their homes; who through toil, hardships, danger, hunger and cold, sickness and death, worked and prayed, fought and bled, and forced their way across trackless plains and over almost unsurmountable hills, blazing trails, battling with adverse elements, fighting the savage redman, and finally, after having become exhausted near the point of man's endurance, landed in the Salt Lake Valley on the 24th day of July, 184783 years ago today.

OUR BIRTHRIGHT 

ESAU was the firstborn, and as such had the right to claim the Kingdom, but failing to appreciate that right, sold the privilege for a mess of pottage, and therein lost the greatest blessing that he could ever hope to attain.

Valley, Nevada; and was struck with wonderment at the magnificent and grotesque scene that met my eager gaze.

Here I beheld a panorama of red sandstone cliffs resembling a storm tossed sea of brilliant embers, that lift their craggy heads majestically above the general level, and so strikingly real that one imagines he is surrounded by brilliantly burning hillocks.

The scenic wonder of this picturesque landscape rests primarily in the mental impression of the profound desola-

tion of abomination that meets the view of the spectator on every hand, so weird, fantastic and bewildering are the sights that characterizes the very nature of this forlorn and dreary region.

The sublimity of the somber loneliness is so impressively manifested as one stands in the midst of a vast sahara of low, rugged hills, stretching away in idle monotony until lost from view in oblivious space, and the vision becomes vacant and the mind dumbfounded in a futile attempt to fathom the immensity of its seemingly endless waste. 

Infinitely profounding in its wild, desolate sphere, with neither tree nor shrub nor green spot on which to put one's foot; void of beast or fowl or reptile to break the charm of the death-like stillness, yet so overwhelmingly fascinating that even a miniature oasis would destroy the grandeur of the fantastic scene.

The mule is a quadruped having two hind legs that extend rearwardly in a sinuous course whatever, and when actively engaged are capable of attaining the elevation of timberline, and, metaphorically speaking, the mule can measure to an infinitesimal point of perfection, the object of contact at the farthermost extremity of them legs, and without warning, he plants his mulism characteristics with the utmost precision.

The mule is a freak as well as a natural curiosity; the progeny of one of the lower octogenarian sub-kingdoms, of a melancholy temperment. His only moments of pleasure be-

ing when he is engaged in some atrocious deviltry. He can dissect a respectable dog or kick the breadbasket out of a man with the same comforting cheerfulness as when eating from a stack of fresh hay when he is hungry.

The mule will look you in the eye with the friendliness of a pet polecat and kick a "chaw 'o tabacker" out of your face at the same time, without in the least effecting his equilibrium or changing countenance.

The mule is sort of a musical conglomerate, a degenerate facsimilie of the old martial band as nearly perfect as animal can be, with flutes and bugles at one end and rattling drumsticks at the other. 

He will leave a feed of wholesome oats that has been given him, pick a padlock, walk deliberately into your cabin, rip open sacks, scatter the contents of the premises helterskelter and devour a sack of dried beans and the hind leg of a salted pig just to show his muleship courtesy. 

He moves with the same ease and cheerfulness and gracefulness of a cyclone or a softly purling brook with alacrity and precision to any decimal fraction of the compass without in the least changing his general course of travel. 

The mule is a cross between a jackass rabbit and the running gears of a linchpin wagon. The mule has but two determining faculties-one is persisting in having his own way. The other is to see to it you never have your way.
