PROTECTION OF AMERICAN INTERESTS IN MEXICO 

A 

SPECIAL SESSION of the Mexican Congress will convene at Mexico City on April 11 to prepare the legislative basis for indemnifying American and British petroleum companies for oil properties expropriated by the

Mexican government on March 18. The course of future relations between the United States and Mexico will be largely governed by the success of the Mexican legislature in devising ways and means of assuring adequate compensation to American oil interests. This was made plain by 

Secretary of State Hull in a forceful note delivered to Mexico on March 29 in which he declared that this country expects payment to its nationals of "compensation representing fair, assured, and effective value" for the properties expropriated. 

In expropriating the entire foreign petroleum industry, the government of Mexico took over oil lands, equipment, and concessions valued by the companies at $450,000,000. 

Of the 17 foreign concerns involved, 11 are American, including subsidiaries of the Standard Oil companies of New Jersey and California and of the Sinclair-owned Consolidated Oil Corporation. The largest of the foreign companies involved is the Mexican Eagle Company, a subsidiary of the British-owned Royal Dutch-Shell group. Total Ameri can investments are valued by the companies at about $200,000,000, while British investments are estimated at roughly $250,000,000.

ORIGIN OF DISPUTE LEADING TO EXPROPRIATION DECREE 

Signing of the expropriation decree by President Lazaro

Cardenas on March 18 touched off the latest in a series of controversies over foreign exploitation of Mexico's oil resources which began in 1917 with the promulgation of a new constitution providing for nationalization of the country's petroleum deposits. Past efforts to apply the oil nationalization provision have twice-in 1919 and 1927brought Mexico and the United States to the brink of war. 199

Editorial Research Reports 

The current dispute began nearly a year ago, when the oil workers' union called a strike following refusal of the oil companies to grant their demands for wage increases and other benefits. After examining the companies' books, a government commission ruled in favor of the workers, asking the employers to pay wage increases amounting to about $11,000,000 a year, to establish pension systems, and to give employees limited participation in management. A similar award was subsequently made by a board of conciliation and arbitration to which the dispute had been submitted. Finally, the workers' demands were supported by the Supreme Court of Mexico, in a decision handed down 

March 1. 

Contending that the terms which the Supreme Court ordered them to put into effect were exorbitant and would result in virtual confiscation of their properties, the oil companies refused to obey the order. Accordingly, in a radio speech March 18, charging the companies with "conspiracy" and fomentation of revolutions, President Cardenas announced the signing of the expropriation decree under authority granted to him by a law enacted in 1936. Immediately after the announcement, the companies made lastminute efforts to settle the controversy by offering to pay the wage increases demanded, but they persisted in their refusal to give the workers a voice in management. The offer was refused, and the expropriation decree was promulgated March 19, by which time workers had taken physical possession of the oil plants.

EFFORTS BY UNITED STATES TO ASSURE FAIR COMPENSATION 

Cardenas' drastic action came as a surprise to the United 

States. At Mexico City on March 20, Ambassador Daniels said: "The general impression here was that there would be a government receivership. The expropriation move was a bolt from the blue." Although Secretary Hull at once expressed "serious concern" over the situation, the State Department deferred formal action pending study of a brief submitted by oil interests which charged the Mexican government with "a manifest denial of justice." Meanwhile, Great

Britain informed Mexico that she "reserved full rights in the matter." 

Cessation on April 1 of the Treasury Department's purchases of Mexican silver was announced by Secretary Morgenthau, March 27. "In view of the decision of the Govern- 200

Protection of American Interests in Mexico ment of the United States to re-examine certain of its financial and commercial relationships with Mexico," Morgenthau said, "the Treasury will defer continuation of the monthly silver purchase arrangements with Mexico until further notice." Under an agreement reached with Mexico last December 31, the Treasury had been buying upwards of 5,000,000 ounces of Mexican silver a month, thus contributing to the stability of the Mexican currency. One-cent reductions in the price at which it buys silver on the world market were announced by the Treasury on March 28 and 29.

While cancellation of silver purchases was looked upon in some quarters as a move in retaliation for Mexico's course in the oil controversy, informed observers asserted that domestic political factors were of at least equal importance in determining the Treasury's action.

The views of the United States with respect to the oil controversy were presented to Mexico in a note submitted on March 29, the contents of which were made public by 

Secretary Hull the following day. During the past few years [Secretary Hull stated] the Mexican Government in pursuance of its national policy has expropriated and is continuing to expropriate the properties of citizens of other countries in Mexico and of its own citizens. Among these have been many hundreds of farms and other properties of American citizens. 

Many of our nationals have invested their savings in these properties and have been dependent upon them for their own livelihood.

This government does not undertake to question the right of the Government of Mexico in the exercise of its sovereign power to expropriate properties within its jurisdiction. This government has, however, on numerous occasions and in the most friendly manner pointed out to the Government of Mexico that in accordance with every principle of international law the properties of its national so expropriated are required to be paid for by compensation representing fair, assured, and effective value to the nationals from whom these properties were taken. 

The recent expropriation by the Mexican Government of oil properties belonging to American citizens is therefore but one incident in a long series of incidents of this character and accordingly raises no new question. The subject now under consideration is the matter of compensation for various properties of American citizens expropriated in the past few years. 

It is my very earnest hope that because of the very friendly relations existing between the two governments a fair and equitable solution of this problem may soon be found by the Mexican Government. 

In a note handed to Ambassador Daniels on April 1, President Cardenas replied that the attitude assumed by the United States had "won the esteem of the people of Mexico." 

He gave assurance that "Mexico will know how to honor its obligations of today and its obligations of yesterday." After receiving the Mexican note, Secretary Hull expressed grati- 201

American Investments in Mexico, 1876-1911

AMERICAN and other foreign capital began to flow into Mexico soon after the establishment of the dictatorship of Porfirio Diaz in 1876. Convinced that large-scale foreign investment was the key to speedy development of the country's resources, Diaz made every effort during his long regime to encourage foreign enterprise. After the establishment of transportation and communication facilities in the 1880's, large amounts of foreign capital, mainly American, began to be invested in Mexico's mines, agriculture, petroleum, and manufacturing.

American interests undertook a program of railroad construction in Mexico about 1881, shortly after the Southern Pacific and Santa Fe railroad companies had extended their lines to the Mexican border. By 1911, when the Diaz regime was overthrown, American holdings in Mexican railways were valued at $650,000,000, and Americans had constructed two-thirds of Mexico's 16,000 miles of railroads. During the same period, American capital erected telegraph lines along the railroad routes and laid down cables connecting 

American and Mexican gulf ports. 

Foreign exploitation of Mexico's silver, copper, lead, and gold deposits was begun on a large scale shortly after 1890. 

Among the important American companies to gain an early foothold in the Mexican mining industry were the Greene- Cananea interests, the Anaconda group, and the American Smelter and Refining Company, controlled by the Guggenheim interests. By 1911, American mining and smelter properties in Mexico were valued at $250,000,000. 

An invasion of Mexico by American ranchers, timber companies, planters, land speculators, and small farmers was inaugurated just prior to the turn of the century. Enormous tracts of land, sometimes as large as 1,000,000 acres, were purchased for cattle-raising, lumber production, or the 202

In all, American holdings in farms, ranches, and timberlands were valued at approximately $80,000,000 by the end of the 

Diaz regime. 

After 1900, American capital came to be invested in many new forms of enterprise-public utilities, wholesale and retail trade, banking, insurance, and numerous types of manufacturing. American investments in these fields were estimated at well over $50,000,000 in 1911. 

Development of Mexico's petroleum deposits by American and other foreign interests was not undertaken until after about 1905. A few years earlier, Edward L. Doheny and several associates had sunk some of the first wells in the rich Tampico and Tuxpam oil fields and had created a market for the oil produced by demonstrating its practicability as fuel for steam locomotives. The success of the 

Doheny group attracted nearly 300 other American oil companies to Mexico within the next few years. Among the most important of these were the Standard Oil companies, the 

Gulf Refining Company, the Penn-Mex Fuel Company, the 

Sinclair group, the Waters Pierce Company, and the Panuco- 

Boston Company. In 1906, the Pearson interests of Great

Britain entered the Mexican fields, and somewhat later the Royal Dutch Company staked out claims. Since uses for petroleum had not been extensively developed, however, pre-war investments in the Mexican oil industry were relatively small, American holdings being valued at only about $15,000,000 in 1911.

Estimates of total investments of American capital in Mexico at the end of the Diaz regime ranged from $1,000,- 000,000 to $1,500,000,000. American capital owned 78 per cent of the country's mines, 72 per cent of the smelters, 58 per cent of the oil, and 68 per cent of the rubber business. American interests not only owned more than all other foreign capital combined but more than the Mexicans themselves. 203

AFTER smoldering for more than a decade, opposition to the Diaz regime, largely directed against its policy of encouraging foreign economic exploitation, broke into open revolt in November, 1910. Uniting under the leadership of Francisco Madero, the masses of the population drove Diaz into exile in 1911 and set up a new government with Madero as president. Recognizing the strong popular support behind the revolutionary movement, the United States gave little aid to the Diaz government after the initial outbreak. The friendly attitude adopted by the Taft administration at the beginning of the Madero regime gave way, however, to increasing hostility as attacks on American lives and property continued. Early in 1913, Madero was imprisoned by one of his generals, Victoriano Huerta, who proclaimed himself president of a provisional government. 

Madero was murdered a few days later. Although the 

American ambassador immediately recommended to Washington that the Huerta regime be recognized, Taft left the matter in the hands of the incoming Wilson administration. 

Announcing that the United States had "no sympathy with those who seek to seize the power of government to advance their own personal interests," President Wilson refused to recognize Huerta, and while enunciating a policy of "watchful waiting," took active steps to drive him from power. 

In February, 1914, after he had brought pressure on European diplomats to prevent granting of a loan to the new regime, Wilson lifted an embargo on arms and munitions, thus giving support to the anti-Huerta forces led by Carranza, Villa, and Obregon. In April, following the arrest of American marines at Tampico, he ordered the occupation of Vera Cruz by a naval and military force, and three months later Huerta was forced to flee the country, Venustiano Carranza becoming the new head of the government. American troops were withdrawn from Vera Cruz in November, after Carranza had pledged his administration to respect American lives and property, and recognition was granted to the new government in the following year. 

In retaliation for Wilson's partiality to Carranza, whose authority he disputed, Francisco Villa undertook a series 204

12,000 troops under General Pershing was sent across the border to capture the marauder. Carranza's refusal to suppress Villa led to agitation for stronger intervention to protect American lives and property rights, and the issue was injected into the 1916 presidential campaign. In a speech in the Senate early in June, in which he condemned 

Wilson's policy, Senator Fall (R., N. M.) demanded the complete occupation of Mexico by half a million American troops. Three weeks later, part of the expedition sent against 

Villa was attacked by Carranza's troops, a number of American soldiers being killed and others captured.

When Carranza refused this country's demands for release of the captured troops, the entire United States militia was ordered to the border and war seemed inevitable. Late in 

June, however, after a strong appeal to Carranza by Samuel 

Gompers, president of the American Federation of Labor, the American prisoners were released and the tension eased. 

With the appointment of a commission representing both countries to iron out existing differences, the crisis passed, and after Wilson's victory in the November elections and the entrance of the United States into the World War early in the following year, the Mexican issue receded into the background.

CARRANZA'S EFFORTS TO NATIONALIZE OIL RESOURCES 

The aims of the Mexican revolution of 1911 were codified for the first time in a new constitution adopted in May, 1917. 

Article 27 of that constitution provided for nationalization of the country's mineral resources, declaring that, regardless of the ownership of any given tract of land, the oil and mineral deposits beneath the surface belonged to the nation and could only be worked by concession. This provision was in direct contradiction to laws enacted under the Diaz regime, which gave to the owners of surface land perpetual rights to the subsoil, and which had formed the legal basis for foreign exploitation of Mexico's mineral resources. The provision marked a reversion, however, to the constitution of 1857, which stated that "in the nation is vested direct ownership of all minerals," and the 1857 constitution in turn was based on legal concepts of property ownership going back to the sixteenth century, when land grants from the 205

Editorial Research Reports 

Spanish crown separated surface uses and mining rights, reserving the latter to the emperor.

Early in 1918, while the United States was at war with Germany, the Carranza government announced that Article 27 of the constitution would be applied retroactively. A presidential decree was issued requiring all owners and lessors of petroleum lands to make a declaration within three months that they intended to drill on their property, or else permit the government to grant concessions to other parties, regardless of surface ownership. Protests by 

American oil companies and by the State Department, which refused to accept the constitutional principle of governmental ownership of the subsoil, prevented application of the decree.

During 1918 and 1919, successive increases in taxes on oil properties, coupled with continued public disorders in the border provinces, resulted in an intensification of the campaign conducted by American oil interests for intervention by the United States to replace the hostile Carranza government with one more sympathetic to American capital. 

Such action was averted, in 1920, when a popular revolt against the corruption of the Carranza regime led to assassination of the president and election of Alvaro Obregon to head a new government.

TEMPORARY SETTLEMENT OF OIL DISPUTE UNDER OBREGON 

Recognition of the Obregon administration was delayed pending satisfactory settlement of the oil controversy. 

Early in 1923, a United States-Mexican commission composed of Charles Beecher Warren and John Barton Payne,

American representatives, and Ramon Ross and Fernando Roa, representing Mexico, was appointed to iron out all differences between the two governments. In the negotiations that followed, the United States receded from its position that national dominion over the subsoil would not be tolerated, while Mexico gave definite assurances that legal titles of petroleum properties purchased and developed as such prior to promulgation of the new constitution on May 1, 1917, would not be disturbed. The discussion was thus narrowed down to the question of subsoil rights in lands which the oil companies held as reserves, but which had not been actively exploited before 1917. These lands constituted from 80 to 90 per cent of the companies' total holdings. 

The Carranza decree of 1918, giving retroactive effect to Article 27 of the constitution, had been appealed to the 206

Protection of American Interests in Mexico

Supreme Court of Mexico by several American oil companies, and the Court had ruled in 1921 that rights to petroleum deposits became vested only when some definite step or positive act had been taken to exercise such rights. This "positive-acts" doctrine was reiterated by the Mexican commissioners, who took the position that, while Article 27 should not be applied retroactively against "acquired rights," owners of oil lands who had taken no positive steps to exploit subsoil deposits prior to 1917 had no "acquired rights," but merely interests and expectations, with respect to those deposits. Owners of land on which no positive act had been performed were, however, promised preferential rights over third parties in the granting of new concessions for subsoil exploitation. The United States commissioners refused to sanction this proposal and reserved all rights which had been acquired by their citizens to the subsoil. 

Having reached an agreement with respect to the position of American property owners who had acquired their rights prior to 1917 and duly exercised them, the United States- Mexican Commission submitted its report in August, 1923, and formal recognition was accorded to the Obregon government in September. Shortly afterwards, the United 

States supplied arms and munitions to the Mexican government to aid it in putting down a revolt headed by Adolfo de la Huerta, former Secretary of the Treasury, while at the same time forbidding the export of arms to the rebels. 

Obregon was succeeded by Plutarco Calles in 1924. 

REOPENING OF CONTROVERSY BY CALLES ADMINISTRATION 

Supported by the growing national consciousness of the Mexican people, the Calles administration from the outset adopted a stronger policy in its relations with the United States. This change in attitude was noted by Secretary of 

State Kellogg immediately after he entered the Coolidge cabinet in June, 1925, when he issued a statement declaring that "Mexico is now on trial before the world." Kellogg asserted that, while relations with Mexico were friendly, conditions there were "not entirely satisfactory," and that the United States would continue to support the Mexican government "only so long as it protects American lives and American rights, and complies with its international engagements and obligations."

Coming from a newly appointed Secretary of State, and at a time when internal conditions were more stable and 207

Editorial Research Reports orderly than in many years, Kellogg's statement aroused strong resentment in Mexico. Calles replied through the 

Mexican embassy at Washington that the statement embodied "a threat to the sovereignty of Mexico."

Relations between the United States and Mexico were further strained after the promulgation, December 31, 1925, of a new petroleum law which was to become effective one year later. Like the Carranza decree of 1918, the 1925 law was intended to put into effect Article 27 of the constitution. 

The law declared that petroleum was the property of the nation and that aliens could obtain permission to exploit it only on condition of agreeing to become Mexican citizens and to waive the right to invoke diplomatic protection. The law also confirmed, but only for a limited period, (1) all rights arising from lands on which works for the extraction of petroleum were begun prior to 1917 and (2) all rights arising from contracts made before 1917 by the owner of the surface, or his successor in title, for the express purpose of exploiting petroleum. With reference to all such lands, however, the law required that the companies concerned should apply to the government within 12 months for a 50-year concession, which was to be renewable for an additional 30-year period.

Secretary Kellogg immediately entered vigorous protests against the new law, contending that its effect would be confiscatory. He maintained also that the exchange of a title for a concession having limited duration did not "confirm" the title. The Mexican government took the position that the 50-year concession and the 30-year renewal features of the law amply guaranteed the companies' rights, since no oil well had been known to last 80 years. The United 

States replied that if the companies were obliged to exchange titles for limited concessions, a future government could reduce the concession from 50 to 10 years if it saw fit. 

According to the law, the companies were required to apply for concessions by December 31, 1926, and although there was no provision for action against non-complying companies, tension between the two countries increased as the deadline drew nearer. When the companies failed to apply by the end of the year, a stalemate was reached. 

Diplomatic relations were further aggravated by reason of American opposition to the Calles program for restriction of the influence of the Catholic Church and because Mexico was suspected of having supported a revolutionary group in Nicaragua which was hostile to United States interests. 208

These circumstances brought renewed demands for intervention in Mexico, but the Coolidge administration, remembering the strong public disapproval which had greeted the debarkation of American marines in Nicaragua during the closing days of 1926, refused to take precipitate action. The appointment of Dwight W. Morrow as American ambassador to Mexico, in October, 1927, was interpreted as signifying the adoption of a more conciliatory policy, and this conclusion was soon reinforced by the statement of President Coolidge that "a firm adherence to our rights and a scrupulous respect for the sovereignty of Mexico . . coupled with patience and forbearance, it is hoped, will resolve all our difficulties without interference with the friendly relationship between the two governments." 

COMPROMISE SETTLEMENT OF PETROLEUM DISPUTE IN 1928 

Soon after Ambassador Morrow's arrival in Mexico City in October, 1927, the Mexican Supreme Court handed down a decision-in the first case arising under the 1925 lawin favor of the Mexican Petroleum Company of Californiaa former Doheny concern which had been acquired by the 

Standard Oil Company of Indiana. The decision greatly expedited subsequent negotiations for settlement of the oil dispute.

The question involved was whether the Mexican Department of Industry could properly cancel drilling permits for "tagged" lands-lands with reference to which some positive act looking toward the extraction of petroleum had taken place prior to 1917-because the owner had not applied for a 50-year concession within the time limit fixed by the petroleum law. The Court held that drilling permits could not be cancelled on this ground. It asserted that vested rights could not be subject to partial loss or restriction. 

The owner possessed acquired rights, not mere expectancies, in the oil beneath the lands in question, and a confirmatory concession would be a restriction upon these rights, which were without limitation as to time. 

Following the Court's decision, President Calles called upon the Mexican Congress to modify the petroleum law in conformity with it. Accordingly, in December, 1927, the legislature enacted an amendment requiring that preconstitutional oil rights be confirmed by the issuance of confirmatory concessions "without limitation of time." After negotiations between the oil companies and administrative 209

Editorial Research Reports officials, conducted by Ambassador Morrow, a set of working regulations was agreed upon, and on March 28, 1928, the State Department published a statement declaring: 

These steps voluntarily taken by the Mexican Government would appear to bring to a practical conclusion the discussions which began 10 years ago with reference to the effect of the Mexican constitution and laws upon foreign oil companies. The department feels, as does Ambassador Morrow, that such questions, if any, as may hereafter arise can be settled through the due operation of the 

Mexican administrative departments and the Mexican courts.

As the result of the long controversy, Mexico obtained acceptance of the positive-acts doctrine, and the owners of land on which no action had been taken toward development of oil prior to 1917 were deprived of the right to extract oil from those lands, except on a concession basis. Although likewise required to obtain concessions, the oil companies which had performed positive acts on lands they owned or leased were given exploitation rights in perpetuity. 

Roosevelt Policy Toward Cardenas Regime 

MEXICO'S recent action in expropriating foreign oil properties, as Secretary Hull pointed out in his note of March 29, represents only the most recent step toward fulfillment of a long-term program designed to place control over the nation's natural and industrial resources in the hands of Mexicans. Up to 1934, little progress had been made in putting into effect the economic and social reforms outlined in the 1917 constitution, but since the advent of the Cardenas administration these reforms have been pushed with renewed vigor. Within the last three years, the position of foreign capital has been undermined by three policies of the Cardenas regime-widespread expropriation of agricultural lands, gradual extension of state socialism, and enactment of labor legislation.

Agrarian reform-the return of the land to those who work it is one of the leading features of the Cardenas program. Some 3,000,000 acres of agricultural lands owned by American citizens have been expropriated since inauguration of the land distribution program in 1917, and a large proportion of this total has been taken over within the last three years. During the 1920's, landowners were offered 210

Protection of American Interests in Mexico payment in bonds, but in most cases such compensation was refused. Because service on the bonds issued was not maintained, these securities have recently averaged only about 10 per cent of their face value. The Cardenas government's failure to extend cash compensation for lands expropriated has been the subject of repeated diplomatic representations by the State Department, but no statement showing the value of American lands taken over has been made public. 

Under the terms of the 1936 expropriation law, the Cardenas administration has moved to extend government control over industry. The 1936 law authorizes expropriation of private property to promote "the equal distribution of wealth held and monopolized to the exclusive advantage of a few persons with prejudice to society in general or to any particular social class." Owners of property expropriated were granted recourse only to administrative action, and indemnification was to be based on tax valuation. 

Acting under this law, Cardenas announced in June, 1937, the expropriation of the National Railways of Mexico, one of the country's three principal lines, with some 8,500 miles of track. Fifty-two per cent of the stock in the National 

Railways had been held by the government for several years, and compensation was pledged to other security holders. 

Last December, a new cabinet department was created to direct railroad operations, but preparations are now being made to place management in the hands of the Mexican railroad unions.

Nationalization of the mining industry has proceeded more slowly. Large amounts of mining land, including iron and salt deposits, have recently been added to the national reserves. At the same time, the granting of new mining concessions has been restricted to Mexican citizens. Following expropriation of foreign oil holdings last month, the Mexican mining unions telegraphed President Cardenas that they also faced "a difficult and bitter situation caused by the mining capitalists." Fearing seizure of their properties, American mining interests immediately sought assurance from the Mexican government. Francis H. Brownell, chairman of the board of the American Smelting and Refining Company, told stockholders at the annual meeting in Jersey City, April 5, that, since promulgation of the petroleum expropriation decree, company representatives had "received from government officials statements to the effect that the present administration has no intention of seizing mining properties in Mexico, but on the contrary is desirous 211

Editorial Research Reports that they continue to be operated by those now owning them."

SYMPATHETIC U. S. ATTITUDE TOWARD MEXICAN REFORMS 

In pursuance of its "good neighbor" policy toward Latin America, the Roosevelt administration has taken a sympathetic view of the reforms sought to be achieved by the Mexican government, and the relations between the United States and Mexico since 1933 have been at least as cordial as at any time since the 1911 revolution. Although the policies of the Cardenas regime present a grave threat to foreign capital, Secretary Hull's note of March 29, after the expropriation of petroleum properties, marked the first instance in which the Roosevelt administration had taken a strong stand to protect American investments in Mexico.

During recent months, increasing criticism has been directed against Mexico for its failure to reciprocate the friendship of the United States. While the course pursued by the United States within the last three weeks may be said to reflect this attitude, there is every indication that the Roosevelt administration is anxious to maintain a conciliatory policy. Sources close to the White House said April 1 that the administration believed that oil companies affected by the Mexican expropriation decree should limit their claims to the amount of their actual investment in oil properties, less depreciation. It was pointed out that the administration had no sympathy with claims of certain rich Americans who, through bribery, acquired huge tracts of Mexican land for nothing or as low as 3 cents an acre, and who now claimed sums far in excess of their original investments. 

In no event will the United States support claims based on prospective profits, it was said. 

This country's desire to maintain friendly relations with Mexico is partly dictated by political self-interest, according to Charles A. Thomson. "For the United States, Mexico constitutes, if not the keystone, at least an essential block in the arch of the Roosevelt-Hull Good Neighbor policy. The threatening trend of world affairs has made the political factor of Latin American friendship outweigh at Washington the economic importance of United States investments south of the Rio Grande."
