NAT J. FERBER 

Names which contributed so much to the New York Nat Ferber knows-the New York he has watched grow numerically, expand geographically, and, finally, rise vertically toward the mists that hang over Manhattan. For Nat 

Ferber knows his New York. 

He looked at it first on a dreary morning in 1889—not from the front deck of a Jersey ferry, not from a gate looking into the awesome cavity of the concourse at Grand Central, but through a little window that commanded a view of a narrow, noisy street and of a maze of steel girders. The window was in the room in which he was born; and the room belonged to a tenement house that squatted sulkily beneath Brooklyn Bridge. "Nussen" was the name given to him by his parents, but the pronunciation was so much like "nuisance," that in selfdefense and without permission of the courts, he changed the name to Nat. But that was not a wholly successful change, for there was an elder cousin whose name also was Nat. 

So the younger became "Little Nat." And "Little Nat" he was until, some years later, he became a 168-pound halfback, passed "Big Nat" in height and cubic content, stuck in an extra initial, and became known as Nat J. Ferber. 

Ferber's parents were first cousins, natives of Russia, where the father, Joseph, was a chemist. Arriving on the 

East Side, the father was advised that a man of his scientific attainment must, at the least, become a business man. He became a pail-by-pail coal merchant with headquarters in the basement of a Cherry Street tenement. The business was soon on the rocks, with aggregate liabilities of four and a half dollars: four dollars for a half ton of coal and fifty cents for tools, implements and machinery. The assets were

one zinc pail and two shovels on which the fifty cents were owing. 

In financial circles it was hinted that the firm came to ruin because of its liberal credit policy, one housewife having run up a bill of twenty-five cents for five pails of anthracite. Yes, these were the days when all coal was half slate. 

Those on the inside of things financial held that the firm was reduced to dust and cinders because the ex-chemist squandered his nickel daily on a shoe shine. Intimates of the ruined coal baron insisted that the real cause of the debacle was the hero of this tale. It is declared that the cost attendant on his birth compelled hypothecations far in excess of his value, on the hoof or otherwise. 

After settling with his creditors for thirty-eight cents on the dollar Papa Ferber determined to start life anew, in some far-off community, where none might confront him with his past. The family took a paddle-wheel steamer and emigrated to Brooklyn, where the countless Ferbersthere are hundreds of them-still live and over-multiply. 

Nat was followed by six brothers, and then two sisters. 

He was his mother's birthday gift on her seventeenth birthday and for years, it is told, she tried to exchange him for something that cost as much that was more useful. ... 

Ferber attended public schools—about thirty—in the

Williamsburg district. He made several records that still stand. As an athlete, he won 133 of the 148 points captured by the entire school in a season of track meets. As a truant he survived more lickings than the entire school district. Teachers promoted him in self-defense. Women into whose class he was promoted got rid of him by sending him ahead in mid-term. He might have graduated the eight-year

course at the age of eleven, but just before entering the last class, he decided to become: 

1. A long-distance swimmer. 

2. A newspaper cartoonist. 

3. A poet.

He never became a poet and admits that he never will. As a cartoonist it didn't take him long to discover that the laughter was not induced by his drawings so much as by himself. 

He gave up long-distance swimming when, in a race from the Brooklyn Bridge to Coney Island, a one-legged swimmer beat him by more than a mile.

But this was some time later. After leaving public school 

Ferber worked as an A. D. T. messenger boy, an errand boy on a newspaper, a grocer's boy, a rubber in a Turkish bath, a watchman in the same bath on "ladies" " day, a super in a stock company, the candy butcher in the theatre where the stock company held forth, a contributor to the New 

York Evening Mail, a trainer for prize-fighters—for road work only-a copy boy in a news room, a ringer on the football team of a private school and a rustler of cans of beer for the clients of a beauty parlor. 

Concurrently, Ferber covered a district for each one of the Manhattan dailies and looked after his mother's babies of which there always seemed to be one or more around the house. He also took courses. These covered a wide range, from pinochle to accountancy and philosophy. Strangely, he passed most of his examinations with an H, but never paused long enough with any specified group of subjects to be entitled to a degree. As an accountant, he claims he was passturized but never certified.

He was more successful as an accountant than as a longdistance swimmer, cartoonist or poet, but there still was

no apparent reason for the name of Nat J. Ferber to be of any more importance than the name of John J. Smith. 

But accountancy held no lure for Ferber, and he turned once more to newspaper work—this time as a reporter. If he had shown no especial aptitude for his previous occupations, his career as a reporter and special investigator was a different story. He had-and still has-that peculiar ability to go straight to the heart of a story, to throw aside confusing details and to clarify the important elements. He developed, moreover, a style of writing as sharp and brilliant as his methods for securing the information. He spent hour after hour in libraries-"not," Ferber assures one, "because

I was a bookworm, or had the proverbial thirst for knowledge, but because the assignments given me necessitated specialized reading in preparation for the interviews." 

As a newspaper man, Ferber was able, unaided, to get from the books of financial officers of State and City the material for history-making crusades. Ferber's study of the 

State's method of retiring bonds, and his subsequent articles on the subject, caused the State of New York to make radical changes in its financial system. His study of the machinations of the bankruptcy ring caused him to be invited to address the Rules Committee of Congress which undertook to advocate changes in the law. His articles led the late 

Supreme Court Justice Taft to call into council, in Philadelphia, all the district court judges of the nation with a view to reorganizing the bankruptcy practice in the Federal courts.

Ferber gained access to the books of a hundred brokerage houses and his examination of these books led to the dissolution of the Consolidated Stock Exchange and the jailing of its president.

As the result of a crusade on a dope ring employing boys in the Williamsburg district, Ferber became the social service editor of one of the Hearst newspapers. With the power of the paper behind him, he caused many of the dealers in drugs to be sent to jail. He turned from this crusade to many editorial campaigns for local and city improvement. 

Under his name were written the first articles advocating the Coney Island boardwalk. He won a number of fights for parks and playgrounds in the congested areas, not the least of which was a breathing space adjacent to his old school, in one of the most crowded districts in the world. At the close of the war, he conducted, and obtained the financing for a bureau that found employment for more than three thousand overseas men.

In the course of his newspaper work, he has interviewed almost every important figure to come to these shores, in addition to many famous Americans, in every walk of life: 

Maxim, Edison, Morgan, Schwab, Carnegie, Tagore, Sarah Bernhardt, Balfour, Lord Cecil, Asquith, etc. Ferber knew and was known to the late Presidents Roosevelt, Wilson and Taft. His newspaper work took him to political conventions in San Francisco, to the dedication of the Jewish University in Palestine and to the death house in Sing Sing. He covered the red riots on one day and worked as an unpaid secretary of the Gary Unemployment Committee on the next. Ferber always disliked Gary, but took the work at his side to study him. 

During the unemployment riots of 1914, Ferber met "Sweet Marie" Ganz, firebrand of the radical movement.

He covered the stories in which she figured and, in so far as one could do so in a news story, scolded her continually. 

Later, he was sufficiently intrigued by her activities to sug-

gest that she permit him to write the story of her life. This permission was given and the writing of the story did not enhance their friendship. If anything, it created a breach. 

Soon after the book was published, under the title REBELS, Ferber and Miss Ganz, to the disgust of the publisher, who wanted a romantic ending for his story, decided to and did marry.

For some years Ferber wrote feature stories. These were followed by an achievement of which Ferber has always been more or less proud. He wrote two hundred and one short stories on two hundred and one consecutive days, during thirty-one of which he also wrote his novel SIDEWALKS 

OF NEW YORK. 

Here, for the first time, Ferber brought into play his thorough, sympathetic understanding of East Side New York; the East Side of starvation and privation, the squalor of crowded tenements, of violent loves and tragic deaths. 

No one, perhaps with the exception of Israel Zangwill, has written with so much surety of the old Ghetto. But Ferber saw what Zangwill apparently did not: the comic side of the picture. Those who have read SIDEWALKS OF NEW YORK 

cannot soon forget the old Jewish match-maker and his hysterical connivances to bring about "profitable" marriages.

Subsequently, Ferber became editor of the Book Review department of the New York American, and while engaged in this work he wrote the novel which he titled NEW YORK -wrote it in sixty hours: in twenty three-hour sessions on twenty consecutive days.

As in SIDEWALKS OF NEW YORK, Ferber made adequate use of his knowledge of the people and growth of the city. 

IN NEW YORK, however, Ferber is more concerned with the

physical growth of Greater New York. It is the story of 

Julius Midas whose first earnings come from the sale of a stolen calf. As Midas' financial power grows, one follows with increasing amazement the actual story of the land developments of the city: the coming of the "L's," the building of subways; the reclamation of waste land. 

Both NEW YORK and SIDEWALKS OF NEW YORK were splendidly received by the critics. It is a tribute to Ferber that the intensive training to which he subjected himself put both of his novels high above the level of the average, in spite of the fact that both were written in an incredibly short time.

Despite the praise given his books, Ferber felt that they were not what they might have been had he given more time to them. He resigned from the New York American to write SPAWN. 

Months later I had my first opportunity to read the manuscript of this new Ferber novel. From the first page my amazement grew. This work of Ferber's seemed utterly incredible. I have no intention of attempting to write a critical estimate of SPAWN, but I must say something of the scene against which the theme is developed for it is completely divorced from the ones Ferber has used before. I felt a consuming curiosity to know the source of the author's knowledge.

Ferber explained. . . . 

While doing his column-a-day, which necessitated the reading of at least a book a day, Ferber found himself morally obliged to "ghost" a book for one of the air heroes. To perform the several tasks before him, he sought and, by chance, found a secluded notch in the Catskill mountains. 

He found himself among a people who spoke a language

that was neither that of the New England farmer nor was it quite the dialect of the Southern poor white. But there was something suggestive of each in their speech-something distinctive, though by no means distinguished. Ferber was soon taking down their language phonetically. Soon he was speaking to his neighbors in their own idiom. A native girl, employed in the household, gave Ferber further proof that he had come upon an intriguing bit of history which might prove material for a novel. She confided to him that she "must call" a girl down the road sister; that in still another household there was a boy whom she "must call" brother; that, likewise, there were others whom she "must call" aunt, uncle, cousin, and so on, through the nomenclature of family relationship. The term, Ferber learned, was not imperative, but speculative, conjectural.

While looking into the history of the place Ferber discovered that in the story of a nearby village there was additional material for an unusual novel. There was an unquestioned affinity between the two communities. He realized, then, that he had evolved a story requiring far more care and thought and a greater measure of deliberate writing than had been given to any of his previous work. So 

Ferber resigned his editorship and went into retirement in 

Southern France to write SPAWN. 

Nat J. Ferber, in forty-one years, has accomplished more than a few astonishing feats of news and fiction writing, but in SPAWN he has, I believe, written one of the truly great American novels. It is a book which must be read before one can reach even an approximation of the vast difference between it and Ferber's earliest work.
