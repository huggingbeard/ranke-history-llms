INTRODUCTION 

O HISTORY of eighteenth-century diplomacy can be written without some consideration of Etienne-François, Duke de Choiseul. His life (1719-1785) spanned the greater part of that period, and in many ways he was representative of his time and his class. During the twelve years between 1758 and 1770 when he virtually ruled France as Minister of War, Marine, Foreign Affairs, Posts and Roads, and General of the Army and Navy of the King, he completely altered the historic foreign policy of France, led her through a disastrous war, and, during seven years of peace, entered on an extensive policy of rehabilitation. Raised to power through the favor of one courtesan, he was cut off by the disfavor of another in the midst of his activity and the flowering of his cherished plans. Yet he lived to see his successors carry to fruition some of the policies he had advocated. 

Coming in the germinal period of the French Revolution, his ministry has been overshadowed by the tremendous events it preceded and therefore has not received from historians the attention it merits. The documents necessary for such a study exist in copious numbers in London, Paris, Vienna, and Madrid; yet historians have often contented themselves with a superficial study and appraisal of his career. This has usually resulted in an erroneous conception of Choiseul and his policies. He is generally described, for example, as a talented courtier but a mediocre diplomat, who, after involving France in a disastrous alliance with Spain, planned to bring the two states into a war with England in order to retain his high place in the French government; his base intrigues being discovered, he was dismissed in the interests of peace. This estimate is incorrect, and unfortunately the few monographs and biographies dealing with Choiseul do not alter the impression. Before this important work can be done, however, certain phases of his policy must be carefully investigated. A study of his relations with England and Spain is especially important because historians have usually maintained that his dismissal was brought about by his bellicose attitude toward Britain in the dispute between that country and Spain over the Malouines—a generalization which is not sustained by a study of contemporary documents in the French and English archives. 

It is the purpose of the present study to examine Choiseul's relations with England, to ascertain, if possible, his intentions toward that power, and to determine whether or not his British policy was, as is asserted by some, the real cause of his dismissal and disgrace in 1770. It will be necessary to view Choiseul's British policy as broadly as may be, in all its aspects and ramifications, and to note its influence upon French relations with other states. What, for example, was the real meaning for France of the Pacte de Famille after 1763? How did French relations with England influence 

Choiseul's handling of Spain in the disputes with Great Britain over the Manila ransom and the Malouines? What of Sweden, and of Corsica, where English and French spheres of influence clashed? 

These questions are highly pertinent to an understanding of Anglo- French relations in the period under consideration, and in the discussion of them an attempt will be made to clarify Choiseul's dealings with the British government and to show how these dealings affected French foreign policy from 1763 to 1770. Evidence will then be presented in an attempt to prove that the Duke fell, not because he had failed in his handling of foreign affairs, but because he was a victim of a palace intrigue that drove him from office just as he was on the point of scoring a signal triumph in settling the Malouines dispute between England and Spain. In spite of his dismissal and exile, Choiseul's administration nevertheless remains one of the bright spots in French diplomatic history in the last half of the eighteenth century.

I. THE DUKE DE CHOISEUL 

COMMAND my cousin the Duke de Choiseul," wrote Louis XV, "to place his resignation as secretary of state and superintend- 1* ent of posts, in the hands of the Duke de la Vrillière and to retire to Chanteloup until further orders from me." So closed the political career of a man who seemed to unite in his brilliant person many of the virtues and vices of the eighteenth century. "More than any other, he personified his time and his epoch. . . . Noble, frank, generous, gallant, magnificent, liberal, proud, audacious, volatile and even headstrong, he recalled to his friend, De Gleichen, the old French knights. Of a medium height, with almost red hair and an appearance generally ugly, he made, nevertheless, an excellent impression. His little eyes flashed with intelligence; his turned-up nose gave him a pleasant look and his thick lips, wreathed in smiles, proclaimed his affability. His wit, his sparkling animation, made him irresistible." Not only did he charm those with whom he came in contact, but, with the possible exception of Frederick the Great, he also inspired respect and fear in contemporary statesmen. His great rival, Pitt, once said in Parliament that France had not possessed a minister the equal of Choiseul since Richelieu.* 

The dismissal of this minister, who had enjoyed the confidence of the King and maintained himself against all cabals for twelve years, astonished Europe. To all but a few observers he had seemed as firmly fixed the day preceding his dismissal as the day of his accession to power. Contemporaries were puzzled. "The Choiseuls's indiscretions and impertinences," wrote Maria Theresa, "tolerated for so many years, could not be the real cause of their downfall; there must be some other motive." But before an examination of the matter is undertaken and an attempt made to study his policy, it would be well to know something of his early life and the means by which he rose to power. 

Choiseul was born June 28, 1719, near Lunéville in Lorraine, and was named Etienne-François after the reigning duke. His father, Joseph de Choiseul, Marquis de Stainville, was of a very

old and proud family and was devoted to the ducal house which he had served as minister of Lorraine at the courts of both England and France. The early life and upbringing of Etienne-François remain wrapped in obscurity. Choiseul in his Mémoires wrote very little about it, beyond the vague statement that his infancy and early youth were passed like those of many other young noblemen of the period. In 1738 he entered the army and in the following year became a lieutenant in the Royal-Allemand cavalry in the war between Austria and Turkey. Shortly after this he served as aidede-camp to Prince Charles of Lorraine in the Hungarian campaign against the Turks.

At the outbreak of the War of the Austrian Succession, Choiseul was a lieutenant in the French army and took part in the ill-starred Bohemian enterprise. He apparently gave a good account of himself, as his advance in rank was fairly steady. In May, 1743, he received a regiment; he fought in Provence and Italy in 1744; and in 1745 received the colonelcy of the Regiment of Navarre. This was an excellent command and was well worth the 75,000 livres it cost him,' for it gave him a senior rank among the various colonels. 

He actually paid only 35,000 livres (which he had to borrow at 5 per cent) for the post; the remaining 40,000 livres he promised to remit when he should have obtained his marshal's baton; a lack of confidence was never one of his shortcomings. He was active in the army until the close of the war. 

In 1750 Choiseul married Louise Crozat, daughter of the Marquis de Chatel. It was a good match, for Mademoiselle Crozat was not only a wealthy heiress, but was also, according to the testimony of Horace Walpole and others, a delightful little creature. She adored her husband, and he seemed really fond of her, in spite of his numerous and notorious infidelities. 

In 1752 an event occurred which was to have a decisive influence upon Choiseul's future career. He learned that his cousin, Madame de Choiseul-Romanet, had attracted the attention of Louis XV and was actually intriguing to supplant Madame de Pompadour. Choiseul's part in what followed has been interpreted by some as a shameful betrayal; others have felt that he was simply trying to save the family name from scandal. Whatever may have been his motives, the results of his action guaranteed his career. He promptly informed Madame de Pompadour of his cousin's in-

1939] 

trigue, and Madame de Choiseul-Romanet was soon dismissed from the court. The Pompadour's gratitude was deep and lifelong; henceforth she openly favored him at every opportunity. 

In 1754 Choiseul was appointed ambassador to the Papal court. 

He remained at Rome from 1754 to 1757 and was able to perform a useful service for Louis XV in satisfactorily concluding a longstanding dispute between the King and the Papacy over the Bull Unigenitus; he also became particularly persona grata to the Pope in spite of several violent disputes. In 1756 the Holy Father asked 

Louis XV to make Choiseul a knight of the Saint-Esprit." 

Choiseul returned to Paris in January, 1757, and in August of that year was sent to Vienna as envoy extraordinary and ambassador plenipotentiary to the imperial court. The Viennese embassy, one of the most important diplomatic posts that Louis XV could bestow, was at this time an extraordinarily difficult and compromising situation for any minister. The reason for this lay in the fact that there were two French foreign policies: one, official and at the same time ineffectual, a policy of peace followed by Bernis, the foreign minister; the other, secret but active, a policy of war, which was controlled by Madame de Pompadour and the King. 

Thus, Choiseul often received instructions from the King and Bernis that were contradictory; he could neither openly disregard the one nor recognize the other, but had to give each the impression that he was obeying his instructions to the letter. In general, however, he was able to push the royal policy of continuing the war and maintaining the Austrian alliance, a policy which tallied with his own personal inclinations. This plan finally prevailed officially.10 

In July, 1758, Bernis asked that Choiseul be recalled to Paris to act as his colleague in the department of foreign affairs; shortly after his return, he was created duke and given the portfolios of foreign affairs, war, and marine, while Bernis was made a cardinal and then banished to his estates. 

During the Seven Years' War, Choiseul's problem was to defend, with weak naval resources, a vast and scattered colonial empire against the greatest naval power in the world. At the same time he had to prevent France from becoming isolated in Europe. It was this last danger that held him to a continental policy. His failure to save the French colonies is not to be wondered at: what is remarkable is that he was able to retain any of them.

After the Peace of Paris, Choiseul was faced with two problems: first, internal matters, and second, the task of preventing a renewal of the Anglo-French struggle. Although this study is concerned solely with a question of foreign policy, a word on Choiseul's military and naval reforms may not be out of place here. In 1763, France had no navy really worthy of the name, and the army was badly disorganized. The Duke gave up the nominal control of foreign affairs after 1763, and devoted himself to an intensive reorganization of the army and a rebuilding of the shattered naval forces. 

Though his task was unfinished at the time of his exile, yet so well had he labored that the French navy and several French regiments played a decisive part in the successful American Revolution twelve years later.

Choiseul's foreign policy after 1763 was based on the idea that Pitt would renew the war between England and France at the first favorable opportunity. In 1765, Ossun, French ambassador at Madrid, wrote to Grimaldi, Spanish foreign secretary, that the Duke was persuaded that "Pitt would reënter the ministry during the coming winter, and after that the two crowns could scarcely expect to keep the peace for more than a year.' "The Duke de Choiseul, added Ossun, would not express himself so positively if his opinion were not founded on definite facts. Almost a year later Choiseul expressed the same alarm to Durand, French chargé d'affaires in 

London: 

The only thing which I fear is that this really unpatriotic man [William Pitt] may try to establish himself by a brilliant move and that this would be a war with France and Spain. I would be much less surprised than angry should this misfortune occur, but I hope that we still have the winter before us and I depend a great deal upon your activities. This hypothetical case must be the object of your most attentive observations.12

In order to prepare for this possible malheur, as he called it, Choiseul maintained the "system of the South," which had been set up during the war-the alliances with the Bourbon powers of Spain and Sicily, and with the House of Habsburg. At the same time he strove to prevent England from forming with Russia, Sweden, and Denmark a "system" in the North to counterbalance the one * His cousin, Choiseul-Praslin, became Minister of Foreign Affairs until 1766. Not only does this quotation explain Choiseul's policy, but it also shows clearly his disinclination to engage in a new war. There is no doubt, however, that Choiseul viewed Britain as the principal enemy of Louis XV and France. Stopping short only of a declaration of war, he intrigued and schemed against England in all possible ways. While his ambassador at the Court of St. James's was assuring George III of the pacific intentions of the French government, Vergennes, Choiseul's creature in Constantinople, helped to bring the Porte into a war against Russia, England's friend." 

Under cover of amicable assurances to England, Choiseul attached Corsica to France, and sent secret agents to the English colonies in North America; he strengthened the power of the crown in 

Sweden and drew the Swedish royal family into the orbit of the 

Bourbon powers, while at the same time he tried to strike a blow at the English economic interests in Spain by means of the Pacte de Famille and the commercial convention of 1768. 

To Louis XV he said: "When I saw, Sire, that the system was successful in postponing war, I felt that I could, without danger, profit from the European situation to acquire certain advantages for France.' "15 This declaration aptly characterizes the last seven years of Choiseul's government. But to develop this policy fully, more than seven years were necessary and it was the misfortune of Choiseul to be dismissed with his work only partly completed. 

Nevertheless, his handling of the problems of French foreign policy stands in striking contrast to that of the favored incompetents who had preceded him and who were to follow. Choiseul, in spite of his mistakes, was a man of genuine ability, and sincerely devoted to the state he served. It must have been some appreciation of this which filled the streets of Paris with cheering crowds as he passed, triumphantly, to his exile.

II. ECONOMIC PHASES OF CHOISEUL'S POLICY 

B 

EFORE THE END of the Seven Years' War, Choiseul began to lay the plans for a policy, or system, as the eighteenth century called it, which he hoped would prevent a repetition of the disasters of 1757-1763. In 1761 he had successfully negotiated a remarkable treaty of alliance between the branches of the House of Bourbon reigning in Europe. This union did not come to an end with the Treaty of Paris, 1763, but rather was strengthened by the misfortunes of a peace that was a burden to both France and Spain. The system of Choiseul after 1763 was, as he himself admitted,¹ built on this alliance of 1761, known, since its inception, by contemporaries and present-day historians alike as the Pacte de Famille. In many ways this treaty was the most remarkable document of the eighteenth century, yet its real purpose and place in Choiseul's politique seem to have been neglected by many historians of today.

Some writers have criticized this treaty on the ground that it involved France in the affairs of Spain, a third-rate military power with a truculent foreign policy. These authors have fallen into the same mistake as did the Spaniards who signed the Pacte. They considered it merely a simple military combination between two powers against a stronger third. Had it been that, the critics of the 

French policy would be justified. Spain in the eighteenth century, though gradually recovering from the Habsburg decadence of the seventeenth, was still a weak military power, and certainly was not in any condition to face England. But it can hardly be assumed that a man in Choiseul's position, with his experience and intelligence and all his opportunities for information, could have signed the Pacte de Famille on the sole basis of military considerations. 

Even had he done so, however, Choiseul must certainly have realized by 1763 just how much he might expect from Spain, and his correspondence with Ossun reveals that he did fully comprehend 

Spain's weakness."

Yet, since Choiseul considered the Spanish alliance as one of the foundations of his system, it is necessary to assume that he was [148]

1939] 149. 

influenced by other motives-motives which must have been concealed, for the Spanish government certainly considered the Pacte a military convention. Actually, the real intentions of the Duke toward Spain and the actual meaning of the alliance for him were a profound state secret, and there was ample reason that they should be concealed. In his letter to the Marquis d'Ossun of December 16, 1764, Choiseul clearly showed his hand. 

There are two points of view to be considered in a political system. . . circumstances of peace and those of war. The latter are arranged in time of peace, when there is hope; as for war, the ally you secure will provide money or men which will enable you to fight your enemies with some advantage. This is not true of Spain . . . for that power would only be a dead body in wartime and it would be absurd to count on its forces.*3

Spain, rather, would only be important in the next wars "by reason of her losses," Choiseul went on, just as it had been in the last war.† 

But this usefulness had shown him Spain's weakness. He did not even wish Spain to become involved in a war between England and France, and he was also opposed to France's being involved in a war because of Spain.

The advantage for France is to profit in time of peace from the riches which Spain produces, and to obtain all the favors which she deserves from that power. . . . I know well, that it is difficult for the Spanish government to give to French commerce marked advantages over other nations, but, at the same time, I cannot doubt that it is possible to treat it [French commerce] as favorably as the English (common enemy of both crowns) and more favorably in some circumstances."

Yet even this is only a partial revelation. Choiseul apparently intended to use the Pacte de Famille to obtain favorable commercial concessions for French merchants and to strike a blow at English mercantile interests in Spain. But other parts of the letter are not so clear. The sinister phrase, “Spain will only play a rôle in the next wars because of her losses," becomes pertinent and gives meaning to the rest of the letter only when we read it in connection with another important document. In a mémoire justificatif, presented by Choiseul to the King at the end of 1765, the Minister, in speaking of the negotiations of the Treaty of Paris, said: 

I entangled Spain in the negotiations so that that crown, if we made peace, would be interested to aid us in the negotiation and thus strengthen the treaty. * Italics mine. 

†The Seven Years' War.

If, to the contrary, we failed, my plan was that Spain would be drawn into the war and that France could profit from this event to repair her losses. Finally, if Chance went against us, I had an idea that the losses of Spain would lighten those of France. . . . The attack on Havana averted one from St.-Dominique which would have succeeded much more easily than the one on Havana. And so I have rendered a great service to Your Majesty, who would no longer have possessed any territories in America after that loss." 

The following quotation brings both parts of the system, the economic and the military, into a harmonious whole and is the most complete expression we have of Choiseul's policy. It merits being quoted at some length. ... it seems to me that in the present situation we ought to show much reserve in our relations with and in our attitude toward Spain. We must consider our own interests above everything else and not count on Spain if we have war. . . . 

It is necessary to view Spain as useful to our commerce and for that utility to sacrifice other interests as much as possible, to let the Spanish ministers intrigue, rise, or fall as they wish, and to sustain the idea of the Pacte de Famille in Europe as much as possible and in all circumstances, so that Spain cannot complain of us. We shall handle carefully the susceptibilities of the two Kings. . . . We will make war alone at the beginning, and perhaps to the end if we are successful. If we are beaten, we will try to engage Spain in it, as in the last one, to help us get peace. Such is my system; it will hold us passive except with respect to commerce and friendly relations." 

This was Choiseul's real policy. During the peace, Spain was to provide a fertile market for French commerce and industry. If war should break out and should go badly for France, Spain with her fat colonial empire would certainly attract more attention in a war of conquest than lean France. It was probable that in the flurry and confusion attending a grand attack on the Spanish colonial system, France might hold on to her now meager overseas possessions. And since the Pacte de Famille did not apply to the colonial world, it was scarcely possible, outside Europe, to distinguish the ally, Spain, from the enemy, England. Thus Spain was to play a part in the next wars by reason of her losses.

Such was the general outline of the Duke's Spanish policy. It is no wonder that he concealed it carefully and that it is kept out of most of his correspondence. One can plainly perceive the nervousness and anxiety that lay behind the warning of Ossun, "It is very important that no one here [Madrid] penetrates your system and... it is important to sustain an appearance of complete con-

1939] 

fidence by your proposals and correspondence, especially with the 

Marquis de Grimaldi." 998 However, it is necessary to observe that Choiseul's policy, while hostile to England, was not intended, it appears, to break the peace. 

As will be brought out later on, in all the crises that occurred in the relations between England, France, and Spain, Choiseul's influence was always exerted to avert war. For it was only during peace that Choiseul could successfully pursue his military and economic rehabilitation. It was this factor which led him, peace having been signed, to attempt to exploit the economic implications of Article Twenty-four of the Pacte de Famille, which provided as follows. 

The subjects of the two high contracting parties, with respect to commerce and impositions in each of the kingdoms in Europe, shall enjoy the same treatment as the subjects of the adjoining country, so that in France the Spanish flag will enjoy the same rights and prerogatives as the French flag, and similarly, in Spain, the French flag. The subjects of the two monarchies, in declaring their merchandise, shall pay the same duties that the nationals of the other country pay. Importations and exportations will be as equally free to the nationals of one country as to those of the other. . . . All previous engagements between the two monarchies shall remain abolished; no other power will enjoy in France or Spain more favored treatment than that accorded the two nations. These same rules in France and Spain shall be observed in regard to the flag and subjects of the King of the Two Sicilies . . . [See Appendix, Doc. I.] 

...

This article had been designed, partly, to improve the condition of the French entrepreneurs and workmen in Spain. From the beginning of the eighteenth century there had been a more or less steady penetration of French businessmen and tradesmen into Spain. Although some were only transient and returned to France during certain months of the year, others appear to have made a permanent home within the Spanish kingdom. At first few, their establishments grew and multiplied with a rapidity which attracted the attention of contemporaries. According to one writer of the latter half of the eighteenth century, in any small town in the interior fifteen out-of a possible twenty shops might be French.10 

These numerous establishments, scattered throughout Aragon, Castile, Valencia, and the rest of Spain, in turn attracted to themselves "an infinity of artisans, laborers, domestics and others, escaped or driven from their country.' Some of these formed 9911

12 little businesses of their own, and so the number grew. Although it is undoubtedly absurd to say, as one contemporary writer did, that during this period 25 per cent of the population of Spain was French, no doubt the number was relatively large. The Abbé 

Beliardi, who should be a reliable source (he was French consul general in Spain, 1758–1772), stated that along the coasts of the Mediterranean there could be counted more than 100,000 families of French origin. Many of the French families, by intermarriage with the Spanish, or because of the numerous disabilities which they incurred as a result of their French nationality, gave up this status and became Spanish subjects. "As a result of this," wrote 

Beliardi, "the French so naturalized lose, little by little, their love and devotion for a country which they have given up.... They are a world lost to France and should certainly be repatriated.' 9913 In general, it seems safe to assume that the principal causes of naturalization were the serious handicaps and restrictions placed on French commerciantes by the Spanish, who perceived too late their blind facility in permitting the French economic penetration. "The remedy which they applied was now worse than the disease."" 

This remedy was to subject the French and their trade in Spain to all possible hindrances and limitations, which, not being applied so severely to the English and the Dutch, naturally gave these powers a distinct advantage. It might be well to consider, briefly, some of these difficulties."

In particular, French shipping was severely handled. As soon as a French ship entered a Spanish harbor, it was likely to be visited many times and all or part of its cargo confiscated on a mere technicality or suspicion. The correspondence between France and 

Spain, even as late as 1767, is full of complaints and disputes over the visit of the customs officials to French ships. Vessels were also expected to pay the cost of the health inspection and this often became absurdly exorbitant. Ship captains were forced to land their cargoes only at a certain time and place, according to the pleasure of the harbor master. Sometimes, in order to favor one merchant, the cargo of another would be held on the docks until wind, sun, and rain had completed their work of ruin. Frequently, if a French ship stopped at more than one port, a duty upon the cargo would be charged at each stop. Merchandise was often confiscated on the claim that it was falsely evaluated or declared. Thus cacao of

1939] 

Guyenne was frequently confiscated because the Spanish officials asserted that it was not from Guyenne, but from some Portuguese colony, and so illegal in Spain. 

There were also certain personal restrictions that were very harassing to the French in Spain. Only the tourists, to use a modern term, were counted as foreigners by the government of Charles III. 

All the others, merchants, artists, workers, though not sharing the rights and privileges of the Spanish subjects, were called upon to help bear the burdens of Spanish citizenship. Thus, the French were sometimes required to take part in civil and military service with the native Spaniards, as well as to pay the same taxes, sometimes twice over if they passed from one village to another within a short space of time." Business houses were often visited and ransacked under pretext of looking for contraband, and French families were sometimes forced to lodge Spanish soldiers. In the 

Basque provinces all foreigners, with the special exception of the English, were subjected to the most rigorous restrictions and taxes. 16 The foregoing is far from being a complete list of the difficulties incurred by the French in Spain, but it is enough to help explain the declining condition of French trade there and its inability to compete with the English and Dutch. In 1764, Choiseul stated that "French commerce in Spain, which had greatly declined during the last ten years, would be totally annihilated in ten more.' ,,18 In the words of the Abbé Beliardi,

France, which since the accession of Philip V to the Spanish throne has completely changed the policy followed during two centuries, can no longer impose her will on Spain as the English do, by menaces; all negotiations since that time have been based on the principle of reciprocal interest, of friendship and predilection. But unfortunately, national prejudices have acted against this.10

In short, the French gained nothing from the establishment of their reigning house on the throne of Spain; instead, they lost much because they were unable to overcome the traditional hostility of Spaniards with a background of two hundred years' opposition to France. By virtue of their treaties, the English and the 

Dutch, on the other hand, acquired more commercial privileges than were enjoyed even by the native Spaniards.20 

After 1761, however, a new factor entered the situation. By 

Article Twenty-four of the Pacte de Famille, quoted earlier, the

French when in Spain were to be given in effect the status of Spaniards, with reciprocal privileges for Spaniards when in France. 

The French, it is evident, felt that the inclusion of this article in the treaty would automatically clear up the commercial difficulties between the two powers. In an undated "Mémoire sur les avantages que le Pacte de Famille peut donner...," which undoubtedly is of a period just before or just after the Peace of Paris, the Sieur Barthes wrote: ... this Pacte ... has become a simple treaty of commerce; . . . its perfect execution and its developments can elevate the forces of France and of Spain to the highest degree and render even the peace destructive for their enemies.""

The definitive and final treaty was signed by Ossun and Grimaldi at Madrid, January 2, 1768." It consisted of a preamble and twenty-one articles. In the Preamble it was definitely stated that no privileges enjoyed by either nation were to be considered as altered or abolished by Article Twenty-four of the Pacte de Famille, but were to remain unchanged and in full force and vigor. 

The first article provided that the French should enjoy all the prerogatives and privileges which the English and other nations possessed by virtue of the Treaty of Utrecht, 1713, and the treaty of 1667. A reciprocal provision was inserted to give the same right to the Spaniards in France. The privileges which one of the contracting parties might accord to its subjects in Europe or the near-by islands were assured to the other power by Article Two. 

In the third article, the fishing along the coasts of the two kingdoms was made free to both, provided that the fishermen observed the laws of the place where they were fishing. 

The fourth, fifth, and sixth articles dealt with the touchy question of the visit of customs officials to the ships. Although the convention was designed primarily to improve commercial relations between France and Spain, it was nonetheless indirectly aimed at England, and neither Choiseul nor Grimaldi wished to advertise the fact. The treaty undoubtedly removed many vexatious restrictions from French trade in Spain. The visit of a ship had been defined and regulated; likewise, the powers of the customs officials. French ships of the line, frigates, and other war vessels could now buy freely in Spanish ports without having to pay the usual duties. 

This would be an important concession in the event of war. Even more important was the fact that the Spanish government had failed to sustain its own interpretation of the Pacte de Famille. 

It was definitely established that the action of the Pacte was not retroactive, and the reciprocal features had been accentuated and verified. In general, the signed document bore a striking similarity to the plan of a treaty drawn up by Beliardi in November, 1766. 

But when the treaty is compared with a memoir for such a convention" prepared by the Abbé Beliardi for his government, it can be clearly seen that the work was just beginning. The Abbé wished to have included in the treaty exemption for the French from certain duties and taxes, permission for the French to carry certain amounts of silver out of the country, and the guaranty of certain personal privileges so that they would not be forced into naturali-

35 zation in order to escape persecution. He also desired to place the 

French consulates on a better footing by endowing the consuls with the qualities of public officials rather than merely simple agents of their government. His theory was that the French and Spanish nations both depended on English commerce for many things and this contributed to the strength of their natural and common enemy. They should therefore strive to sell and buy to and from each other and in this way build up their own trade while injuring that of England." This idea was suggested in many of his memoirs and state papers to both France and Spain. He tried to embody it in the convention of 1768, but the two governments decided to treat of so many matters in a series of treaties rather than in one: the convention of 1768 was therefore intended to be followed by others. 

In 1769 there appeared another "Convention to clarify the functions of the consuls and vice-consuls of France and Spain, respectively...." This agreement was of ten articles, which need not be quoted in extenso. In general, it improved the situation and positions of the consuls of both powers. They were allowed certain marks of authority, such as the right to wear a sword, and they also received judicial immunity (Article Two). They were given general authority over the ships of their own nation entering the port where they resided (Article Four) and could cause the arrest and detention of their own nationals (Article Six). The final provision stated that the Spanish King would admit others to the treaty if they would accord the same rights and privileges to the Spanish consuls in the places they inhabited (Article Nine). 

Unfortunately, the ministry of Choiseul came to an end* before the other matters could be adjusted by similar conventions. Had he remained longer in office, there can be little doubt that these other agreements would have been drawn up and signed, to the great benefit of French commerce and trade in Spain. 

Such was the economic development of Choiseul's system, a development which was built up through the Pacte of 1761 and the convention of 1768. Although as a theory it contained little that was new and was a typical expression of the economic ideas of the ancien régime, still it represented one of the most serious attempts during the reign of Louis XV to restrain English commercial activity by other means than force. * December 24, 1770.

III. PLANS AND PREPARATIONS ROM THE SIGNING of the Pacte de Famille (1761) to the end of the ministry of Choiseul (1770), France and Spain were dominated by Choiseul's foreign policy. His influence over the government at Madrid was remarkable. It is not an exaggeration to say that the Duke was foreign minister of the Bourbon powers rather than merely of France. It is also no exaggeration to say that his credit and influence at the Escurial were probably stronger than at Versailles. Choiseul was a warm personal friend of Grimaldi, foreign minister of Charles III and former Spanish ambassador at the court of Louis XV. Charles, himself, placed great confidence in Choiseul and often paid more attention to his advice than to that of his own ministers. Furthermore, the French ambassador at Madrid, Paul Chevalier, Marquis d'Ossun, had long been a friend of the Catholic King; in his youth the Marquis had been stationed at Naples, and when the King of the Two Sicilies became Charles III of Spain, he asked that Ossun be transferred to Madrid. 991 All these factors enabled the Duke de Choiseul to occupy a unique position in the councils of Spain. "My court," once said the 

Spanish ambassador to England, "is very anxious that Monsieur de Choiseul always keep his place, because he made the peace; and in my opinion he is the only minister that will be suitable to us." 

His power in Spain was founded on confidence and respect, and it seems probable that the deference, even the friendship, of the King of Spain for Choiseul was a continual source of strength to the Duke in France."

Because of his favored position, Choiseul did not hesitate to express his ideas and opinions in the freest manner to Grimaldi and the other members of the Spanish ministry. Throughout most of the years of his régime after the Peace of Paris, his Spanish correspondence is full of comment on nearly every subject. More than almost anything else, however, he pleaded with the Spanish ministers, Grimaldi in particular, to see to it that Spain should be in a condition to defend herself if war should break out. This project was a long, rather fantastic, and elaborately worked out scheme of a series of campaigns against England. Only the outlines need be given here. All conflicts on the Continent were to be strictly avoided-advice which the French should have heeded before. France could only hope for success in a war waged outside of Europe. If France should keep forty vessels of the line at Brest and Rochfort, and twenty ships of the line at Toulon, the Duke hoped that England would be forced to keep a large number of ships in European waters to watch the French. Thus, at least thirty English ships would have to be stationed off the coast of Brittany, twenty would probably patrol the Channel, about thirty would remain in the Gulf of Gascony, and at least thirty more would stay in the Mediterranean. From this review of Choiseul's plans and preparations, it is possible to gain some idea of the scope and sweep of his activities. 

His mind was singularly active, fertile in ideas, and receptive to those of others. He was as ready to draw up a plan of campaign for the Portuguese invasion as he was to study the harbors and fortifications of New England. During his administration he succeeded remarkably well in stirring up the slow-moving Spanish bureaucracy into a certain amount of activity. Though he made mistakes, was occasionally attracted by some fantastic scheme such as the proposed Portuguese invasion, and sometimes acted on superficial knowledge, this was almost unavoidable, considering the pressure of business in the various departments under him. This broadness of view, hitherto lacking in either the French or the Spanish government, was the quality most needed in the economic and political rivalry with England. Had he been permitted to carry out his system against England to its fullest development, Choiseul might have changed the history of Western Europe during the latter part of the eighteenth century.

During the last four years of his administration of foreign affairs, he was continually striving to preserve the peace and had little time for theoretical plans and speculations. A study of the policy he pursued in overcoming these difficulties may enable us to discover his real intentions and the objects of his statecraft. 

In the year 1766 occurred the first two of a series of crises which threatened the peace of Europe and involved the Bourbon powers with Great Britain. These crises arose out of the discussions over the ransom of Manila and the further complication of the touchy question of the colonization of the Malouine Islands.* In 1762 the city of Manila was captured by an English expedition. Before following the negotiations relating to these islands, it will first be necessary to recapitulate, briefly, the story of the group. The Malouines, or Falklands, are a group of small islands lying off the southern end of South America, just below the fiftieth parallel of south latitude and almost on the sixtieth degree of longitude. Lying thus about three hundred and fifty miles east of the 

Strait of Magellan, they control the entrance to this passage, which during the eighteenth century was the only gateway to the fabulous South Sea. As a result, the islands were viewed, and rightly, by Spanish and French strategists as the outpost defenses of the viceroyalty of Peru. It is not strange, therefore, that the three great colonial nations of the world considered them to be of strategic importance.

By whom, or exactly when, the islands were discovered is not definitely known. They may have been sighted by Amerigo Vespucci on one of his voyages down the South American coast, but this remains unproved. According to Goebel, who has made the most nearly complete study in English of these islands and their history, they were visited in 1540 by an unnamed vessel belonging to the armada of the bishop of Plasencia.10 

Two English navigators, John Davis and Richard Hawkins, were supposed to have sighted islands, in 1592 and 1594, respectively, which the English later contended were the Malouines, but there were too many errors and uncertainties in their account to give this contention any reliability. The first really definite information came from the voyages of De Weert at the opening of the seventeenth century. Soon the Dutch cartographers began to place the islands, known to them as the Sebaldes, in their correct position. 

But the archipelago was unattractive, windswept, and penguinhaunted, offering little incentive to colonizers. The islands were seldom visited in the seventeenth century, and it was not until the beginning of the eighteenth century, when French commerce began to penetrate the Pacific Ocean, that privateers from Saint- Malo began to stop there for water and supplies. From this fact came the use of the name Malouines, taken over into the Spanish as Malvinas.

In 1740, the English Admiral Anson made a voyage to the South ww

Sea with six ships. Of this little fleet, only one vessel returned to 

Europe. Anson attributed his failure to the lack of a port de relâche in the South Atlantic and near the Strait of Magellan. He strongly recommended that the Malouines be colonized for such a purpose. 

At that time, however, nothing was done." 

It was France which took the initiative. In 1763, Choiseul permitted the Chevalier de Bougainville to establish a French colony on the Malouines at his own expense. The expedition, consisting of the frigate "L'Aigle" and the corvette "Sphinx," actually arrived at the islands in 1764." Bougainville returned to France in the spring and sailed again in 1765 with stores and more colonists to Port Saint-Louis, as the settlement (now known as Berkeley Sound) was called. With a population of one hundred and fifty settlers the little colony was now really established. 

Meanwhile, an expedition in England was being prepared with the same destination in view. On June 21, 1764, two English frigates, the "Dolphin" and the "Tamer," under the command of

Captain John Byron, grandfather of the poet, set sail for the muchdisputed islands." In January of the following year (he did not give the exact date in his account) Byron took possession of the islands, which he called Falkland, in the name of King George the Third. The English colony established there he named Port Egmont, and almost two years were to elapse before the colonists should become aware of the existence of Port Saint-Louis. 

During all this activity, neither of the interested powers looked with any pleasure upon the undertakings of the other. Since it was difficult to gain any definite information, various rumors served instead. In September, 1765, Conway, of the British cabinet, questioned one of the staff of the French embassy on the activities of Bougainville and implied that the islands were a possession of England." On the other hand, Bougainville in a memoir to his government urged that the islands be occupied in force to prevent the English from establishing a firm foothold there."

VI. THE ACQUISITION OF CORSICA HE DUKE DE CHOISEUL is fallen," wrote Horace Walpole to 

Henry Conway. "I feel much for Madame de Choiseul, though nothing for her Corsican husband.... Corsica is engraved in my memory as I believe it is on your heart." Walpole had good reason to grieve. The acquisition of Corsica for France by Choiseul was a bitter pill for British pride to swallow. During most of 1766, the Duke was too much occupied with the Manila and Malouine crises to do aught save mediate between Spain and England. The year 1767 was a brief interlude between these first crises and the tumultuous years that ended Choiseul's ministry. 

However, in 1768 an opportunity presented itself, and Choiseul was able to complete what proved to be the last extension of France under the ancien régime. The island of Corsica occupied a strategic place in the northwestern Mediterranean. About two hundred miles from the coast of France and a scant fifty from the Tuscan coast of Italy, it at once controlled the north coast of the Italian peninsula and afforded an additional protection to the great French naval base of Toulon. In the hands of a strong naval power Corsica would virtually make a mare clausum out of the Gulf of the Lion and the Ligurian Sea. This was all the more reason why France could never permit the establishment of English power in the island. By the capture of Gibraltar in 1704, and of the island of Minorca in the Seven Years' War, Great Britain seemed on the verge of complete control of the western Mediterranean. But under the skillful diplomacy of the Duke de Choiseul, the French were able to check this English advance by the annexation of Corsica in 1768. The crown of France also unknowingly obtained with the island the future services of a young Corsican named Buonaparte, born just after the annexation took place. But long before the young Napoleon interested himself in the cause of Corsican liberty, it became evident that the precarious hold of the Genoese Republic upon the island was weakening. 

During the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the rival cities of Pisa and Genoa had struggled for possession of the Corsican har- [183]

bors. By 1299 the sea power of the Republic had decided the matter. 

In 1348 the first Genoese governor arrived to take over a position which his successors were to find most uncomfortable. In 1553 the 

French appeared on the scene for the first time. Henry II fitted out an expedition to drive the Genoese out of the island, but the treaty of Cateau-Cambrèsis (1559) ended that attempt to gain a stronghold in the Mediterranean.

For almost two hundred years, Corsican opposition to the Genoese merely smoldered. But in 1729 it only needed the imposition of a new tax to fan the discontent into the flame of revolt, and the last phase of Genoese dominance commenced. The revolt was so successful that the Republic called on the European powers for protection against its rebellious subjects. After long negotiations, a bargain was reached with the Emperor Charles VI, who agreed to provide a certain number of mercenary soldiers. Though at first four thousand men were sent, the rebels were able to keep the field until some twelve thousand had been dispatched to the island. 

The Germans patched up a peace between the Genoese and the Corsicans and left in 1732. In 1734 a new revolt broke out. This time, the islanders were assisted by the presence of a king among them. Theodore von Neuhoff, a German adventurer, landed on the island in 1736. He wore Turkish trousers and a Spanish hat; but he brought a cargo of arms and supplies, and promises of European aid. He had no difficulty in winning the rebels to him and was soon crowned King of Corsica. In spite of a liberal distribution of titles and orders, his reign was short. The Corsicans became disgusted with him when the assistance he had promised from Europe failed to materialize. On November 5, 1736, he left the island for good.

Notwithstanding this comic interlude, the Corsicans were, on the whole, fairly successful, and the Genoese saw themselves fast losing their strongholds. The Republic decided to appeal to France for assistance. This power had not ignored the possibilities of annexation. Campredon, French minister at Genoa, had suggested the possibility to his government in 1735. Nothing so definite was done. 

In 1737, Fleury negotiated with Genoa a treaty whereby he agreed to land French troops to help restore order. The following year, they invaded the island, and were successful in forcing comparative calm. In 1740, at the opening of the War of the Austrian Succession, most of them were withdrawn.

VII. INTRIGUE IN SWEDEN 

E STROVE to prevent the Union of the North which the enemies of France conceived because they have been frightened by the alliance of the South.... The southern alliance being solidly established for France, it was necessary, in order to keep the peace, to prevent one favorable to England from being formed in the North." So wrote Choiseul just before his exile, referring to the critical situation in Sweden in 1768. The 

French government suddenly discovered that England seemed on the verge of creating a powerful league of states in the North to oppose the Franco-Austro-Spanish system. This northern union was to be based on the close alliance of England and Russia with Sweden, traditional friend of France. It was to frustrate this plan that the Duke intervened in the affairs of Sweden, in which France had possessed great influence since the days of Gustavus Adolphus. 

After 1760, however, the reputation of the Lilies at the Swedish court began to decline, and by 1765 the two kingdoms had drifted far apart. On February 5, 1766, a treaty of friendship was signed between the government of Sweden and that of Britain. On the twenty-eighth of the month, Grafton, secretary of state, wrote to Goodricke, English ambassador in Sweden, that he was to "assist Mr. Osterman in his endeavors to ally the courts of Russia and 

Sweden." Other letters followed on this subject. In August, Shelburne openly boasted to Durand that a league was being formed in the North to counterbalance the Family Compact."

The most obvious solution of the problem, from the French point of view, was to prevent Sweden from being won over by England and Russia. France had a long tradition of friendship with Sweden, but owing to the peculiar conditions in the northern state this tradition was powerless to improve the situation. The principal complicating factor lay in the extraordinary weakness of the crown of Sweden. After the disastrous Peace of Nystad (1719), which ended the Northern War, the Swedish leaders met to form a new constitution. Because of their experience with Charles XII, most of them felt that absolute monarchy was the bane of the nation. [192]

1939] 

The new constitution, therefore, when finished in 1720, relegated the king to the position of a mere puppet. The real power lay in the four estates-nobles, priests, burgesses, and peasants-which governed the kingdom. Yet, though antimonarchial and democratic in appearance, the complicated system was far from liberal. Each body met separately in the great Diet or Riksdag and had its own speaker. Because of the rigid class system in Sweden, the nobles had an overwhelming preponderance in the government. 

The executive, legislative, and judicial powers during the meetings of the Diet were exercised by a secret committee of fifty nobles, twenty-five clergy, and twenty-five burgesses. This committee controlled the foreign policy of the state, prepared all bills for the Diet, created and deposed the colleges, as the ministries were called, and often acted as a supreme court. During the recesses of the Diet, the executive power was exercised by the Raad or Senate, a conservative body that had existed since the beginning of the monarchy. In this elaborate system the king was of small importance. He could create nobles at his coronation and at no other time. Should he refuse his consent to any bill, his name could be affixed by a rubber stamp and the measure then became a law. 

During the years after the Peace of Nystad, the government was managed by Count Horn with a modicum of success. There was, however, an increasing opposition to his staid and peaceful régime. 

His opponents nicknamed Horn and his party the "Nightcaps," later shortened to "Caps," in derision of their lack of energy. The opposition, mostly made up of younger nobles in the army, were called the "Hats," because of their tricorn headgear.

In 1738 the Hats were successful in gaining control of the government. They immediately returned to the old system of close alliance with France and at the same time embarked on a disastrous war with Russia, chiefly financed by French subsidies. In spite of their unfortunate foreign policy, the Hats were able to retain their power for many years.

The death of the old King Frederick and the accession of his successor, Adolphus Frederick, whose wife was a sister of Frederick of Prussia, took place in 1751. Neither the new King nor his Queen possessed the requisite qualities for leadership in turbulent Sweden. The King was weak and easily swayed. His wife was intelligent and well educated, but grasping and selfish. The only hope

for the country seemed to be in the Crown Prince, Gustavus (born in 1746), who gave promise of ability.

Chiefly as a result of their inept foreign policy, the Hats almost lost power in 1760, but they managed to hang on until 1765. In this year they were completely driven from the government, and the Caps became supreme. Under their control the Diet, known as the "Reduction Riksdag" was held, in 1766. In an effort to remedy the miserable financial and economic status of the kingdom, the Caps committed themselves to a series of reductive and economizing measures that soon made them far more hated than the Hats had been. In their foreign policy the Caps threw themselves blindly into the hands of Catherine of Russia and her subsidies, as their opponents in their turn had reached out for the golden streams that poured from Versailles. They also signed a treaty of friendship, as has been noted above (p. 192), with England. The whole situation was most favorable to the formation of an Anglo-Russo- Swedish alliance. The future of French relations with Sweden was in jeopardy.

In 1766 and early in 1767 two important events occurred. The royal family, hitherto aloof from both parties, made a definite union with the Hats* against the Caps, and the former were thus emboldened to request subsidies from France.* The Hats had asked for and received money from France many times before, but this particular request came at a critical phase in the evolution of French policy toward Sweden. Choiseul had become convinced that France was following the wrong policy toward that state. 

By aiding the Hats against the Caps, the French prolonged the weakness and chaos of the government, which made the Swedish alliance, at all times an expensive commodity, virtually worthless. 

What France should henceforth do was to strengthen in every way the royal power, the crown itself, to the exclusion of any one party, so that the Swedish kingdom might recover from the evils caused by party struggles carried on by a multitude of irresponsible rulers. 

Yet, with his usual insight, Choiseul realized that Sweden was no longer fitted for an absolute government. Rather, he preferred to see a somewhat restricted monarchy of the English type set up in 

Sweden.

On March 7, 1769, he was authorized to pay, as occasion might require, £12,000. Having control of the Diet and government, the Hats showed themselves exceedingly reluctant to turn over their power to the

Subsidies would be paid (1,500,000 francs a year for nine years), and Sweden would agree to give assistance in time of war and to make no other alliances without the consent of France. The offer was, of course, contingent upon Sweden's adopting Choiseul's plan for the new constitution." But as time went on, Choiseul saw that the hoped-for reform was not going to take place. The climax came when the Swedes demanded, in return for certain changes in the government, not only the offered subsidy, but back payments to cover the period since the last subsidies were paid. No more money was to be sent, no matter what might happen. France was not going to become a tributary of Sweden." 

Choiseul's worst fears were justified, for on January 30, 1770, the session of the Diet ended. In the bitterness of his heart Prince 

Gustavus wrote:

No previous Swedish parliament had broken so many solemn pledges and lost so many golden opportunities. That we should have lost the Constitutional battle does not distress me so much; in our present circumstances, a new constitution would have been of little service to us. But what does dismay me is to see my own nation so sunk in corruption as to place its whole felicity in absolute anarchy."

But Choiseul's failure was only partial and apparent rather than real. The reform of the government was only postponed. Two years later it was carried out with striking success by the young Gustavus, following the lines suggested by Choiseul. With reference to the proposed League of the North, Choiseul had definitely attained his object. England's idea of uniting Denmark, Prussia, Russia, and Sweden with her in an alliance hostile to the Bourbon powers had failed. Choiseul's activities in Sweden and Turkey had prevented the British from creating a counterpoise in the North to the Système du Midi.

VIII. THE EXPULSION OF THE ENGLISH FROM THE MALOUINES 

N 1766 AND 1767, Choiseul had striven successfully to prevent a rupture of the peace over the Manila and Malouines affairs. 

When that problem was apparently solved, he was able to pursue profitably his policy against Great Britain. The peaceful acquisition of Corsica and a successful diplomatic intrigue in Sweden against British interests were to be but a prelude to other ideas and wider fields of activity against England.' But before the Duke could carry out these projects, he found himself confronted again with the Malouines quarrel, supposedly quietly allayed in 1767, and he was completely occupied with this problem until his dismissal in December, 1770.

Unfortunately for Choiseul, the dispute over the Malouines between Spain and Great Britain in 1766 closed without alteration of the fundamental factors or solution of the basic problem. Neither Spain nor England would agree to each other's interpretation of the situation, nor of the treaties supposed to regulate it; and both maintained colonies in the disputed possession. Such a state of affairs could only be temporary, but it might have been prolonged far longer than the two years that it lasted had it not been for the impetuosity of Spanish officials and the carelessness of 

Choiseul himself.

In an interview with Fuentes, on February 12, 1768, the Duke showed some apprehension lest Spain should provoke a war by some ill-considered action in the Malouines. If the English establishments there should be destroyed, war would undoubtedly result. 

Choiseul asked Fuentes to inquire from his government if this were likely to happen. When Grimaldi learned of this conversation a fortnight later, he instructed his ambassador to send the Duke a copy of the commands sent to Spanish officials with respect to the English in South America. He could give Choiseul the orders, but not the results. It was possible, Grimaldi wrote, that the commission sent to Bucarelli, governor of Buenos Aires, might have provoked some trouble with England, but he could not be sure. As to [203]

the latest order, he was holding it until Choiseul could express his opinion.❜

To this letter was joined a copy of all the regulations referred to by Grimaldi. On December 23, 1766, and January 4, 1767, the governors of Buenos Aires and the Malouines, and the viceroy of Peru, were informed that Spain suspected the existence of certain English colonies on the Malouines and that they were to investigate. On November 13, 1767, the governors of Buenos Aires and the Malouines were told that the English were at the last-named place and carrying on a forbidden correspondence with agents on the mainland. The Spanish officials were commanded to seize the accomplices of the British, if possible. They were also ordered to fit out ships to capture the settlements and effects of any foreigners who might be carrying on an illicit commerce within the Spanish domains. But when the Spanish government heard a rumor (later discovered to be false) that the English were making a settlement at Tierra del Fuego, Arriaga, minister of colonies, prepared instructions more emphatic and definitive than had hitherto been sent. These instructions were dated February 23, 1768, but were held back until a copy could be sent to Choiseul and an answer received. In view of the important results of these instructions, it is necessary to quote from them at length. Arriaga exhorted the viceroy of 

Peru, Manuel de Amat, to watch carefully for English settlements, and then concluded with the order: "His Majesty charges me to command you, expressly, to use the greatest diligence in not permitting any establishment of the English and to drive them away by force from the places they hold, if warnings are useless. This is to be done in conformity with the regulations prescribed without the need of issuing new orders." Considering the careless way in which Grimaldi spoke of his instructions and the equally careless way in which he treated the possible consequences, it is remarkable that Choiseul did not note the seriousness of affairs and urge the need for caution. The Duke had never been optimistic about the future of the islands. On March 5, he wrote to Châtelet that "sooner or later, the English settlements either on the Malouines or at Port Famine, if they be sustained with the obstinacy and bullheadedness of Milord Egmont, will necessarily cause war between Spain and England, and, by the same necessity, with

France." Shortly afterward, he wrote to Ossun: ". . . inform me with your usual accuracy of the different orders which have been given or may be given by Spain to the governors of that part of the world for the destruction of the English establishments." 

Yet, in spite of the appreciation Choiseul displayed here of the dangers of an ill-considered act of violence by Spain, he read the new regulations over without suggesting any moderations. And shortly after these instructions arrived, he wrote a brief note to Fuentes stating that he approved the commissions which had been sent and which were to be sent to Bucarelli on the subject of the English in the South Sea. As a result of his approval, Grimaldi notified Fuentes on March 28, 1768, that the instructions were to be sent to Bucarelli. There seems no doubt that, if Choiseul had taken a strong stand at this point and insisted on a modification of the orders in the interests of moderation,-the omission, for example, of the phrase "by force” in the instructions to Bucarelli,they would never have been sent. For the subsequent crisis Choiseul was therefore indirectly responsible. 

It seems possible that for once Choiseul was not sure of the proper course to follow. His reasoning shows here an uncertainty usually foreign to him. In a letter written on March 27 to Châtelet, the Duke, after discussing the weakness of the English ministry, commented that, "pour le moment," Spain had adopted a very good policy in planning to destroy the new English establishments. But farther on in the same letter he stated that the English ministers, then in office, would never have the courage to abandon any of the new places settled.

Therefore it is either necessary to suffer the English invasion, or repulse it. . . . Since the negotiation cannot take place and there only remains the middle ground of enduring them until the time when they can be opposed, it remains to be seen, if, in case things remain a long time in the present condition, it will be as easy as at present to destroy the establishments which will later have acquired a sort of consistency.10 

It may be that Choiseul was counting on the weakness of the British cabinet when he sanctioned the orders to Bucarelli. The ministry did not possess the support of the country at large and was vexed with dissension. The internal affairs of the nation, as well as its relations with the American colonies, were unsatisfactory." 

Furthermore, the ministers were continually faced with the pos-

sibility of Lord Chatham's recovery and reëntrance into the cabinet-a possibility which they dreaded." It is also known, from an examination of the correspondence, that even as late as November, 1769, Francès, chargé d'affaires of the embassy in London, was describing the peaceful intentions of the English government to Choiseul. The expulsion of the English thus determined upon, Madariaga was to wait at Buenos Aires with his three vessels for two more which were expected to arrive soon. On the twenty-ninth, Bucarelli ordered Madariaga to leave at once without waiting for his reinforcements, but the following day the tardy ships appeared at Montevideo, and thus Señor Madariaga was able to proceed against the English with five frigates, a very formidable fleet, considering the size of the British forces. 

In view of the fact that the Spanish government later asserted that Bucarelli had exceeded his instructions, it is pertinent to point out here that he believed he was following them to the letter. In his report to Grimaldi and Arriaga informing them of his action, the Governor said: "I have conformed to the literal sense of the order of His Majesty of February 25, 1768, which formally declares his intentions and his unalterable will."" The impartial critic can but agree with him. The meaning of the instructions was clear and definite and could only be understood as Bucarelli understood it. It was only when the Spanish government saw the violent crisis caused by its instructions that it said Bucarelli had exceeded his orders.

IX. THE SECOND MALOUINES CRISIS URING THE MONTHS of June and July, the courts of Madrid, London, and Paris were more occupied with escaping the summer heat than with adjusting their relations with one another. While the European diplomats were idling with the courts in cool retreats, two ships from the southern hemisphere were approaching their destination, bearing the combustibles for another war scare.

Before the news of the expulsion of the English from the Malouine Islands reached London and Madrid, the Spanish government received word, late in July, 1770, of the correspondence between Hunt and Puenta in December of the previous year. After his bellicose remarks in the first Malouines crisis, the Marquis de Grimaldi, ignorant of what had happened in June, felt it necessary to explain why his officers had contented themselves with verbal protests against Captain Hunt. He wrote to Ossun: 

The prohibition of any establishments on the coasts beyond Cape Horn is clearly stipulated in the treaties, in Spain's favor, but the right... to the seas east of it is, perhaps, not so definitely enunciated, and . . . it is claimed by the English. Since a sovereign owes it to God and his subjects... never to undertake a war without just cause... the Catholic King will not precipitate anything in the present circumstances.¹ ...

Choiseul considered that the Spanish had conducted themselves well in this latest episode, but he was surprised at Grimaldi's words about Spain's rights. "This language is quite different from that held formerly by this minister," he wrote to Ambassador Ossun.* 

The Duke hoped nothing would happen, however, for Spain needed two years of peace at least, and he desired the present tranquillity to last as long as possible.

On the seventeenth of August, a short time after this pious wish, the news that Bucarelli had taken Port Egmont burst like a bombshell on the astonished Spaniards. On the twentieth of August, Grimaldi wrote Fuentes, giving him the startling information.

The situation at the close of October was very serious. The English ministry, not actually desirous of a war, according to Choiseul," feared to show any weakness in the face of parliamentary opposition; further, it had a just grievance against Spain for which it demanded reparation. Above everything else, he wished to prevent a conflict, but if it must occur he wished England to be forced to begin it. Therefore, Spain should grant the just demands of England. Charles III, on the other hand, was angry at Masserano for exceeding his instructions and hinting that Spain might agree to the restitution without the setting of any time limit on the English occupancy. The Spaniards felt that if they should disavow Bucarelli they must have assurance, as their price for the disavowal, that the English would retire from the islands at a certain stated time." Of course, the British did not intend to give this assurance. 

The situation was certainly difficult, but Choiseul did not intend to delay. Having suffered a check at London, he turned his attention to Madrid. On October 31, Ossun presented a formal memoir to the Spanish government. After a short prelude on the beauties of peace, it was definitely stated, in accordance with Choiseul's oft-expressed belief, that England had "a just reason for complaint, in reference to the violent proceedings of General Bucarelli.” England wanted a written disavowal of this act. The Prince then brought out his second plan, in which no names were to be mentioned in the disavowal, as suggested by Choiseul. to the islands, whereupon Masserano declared that he could make no further offers and the conference broke up.” 9938 So by the end of November a deadlock had been reached and war appeared to be inevitable. From Madrid, Ossun reported that

England had declared they could go no further in their offers. If war was to be avoided, a third party would have to intervene with speed and decision. The Duke de Choiseul was the only person, by reason of his position, influence, and talents, who could act as a mediator to preserve the peace of Europe in this crisis.

So wrote Walpole in August, 1770; and in order to understand the situation at the French court during the last months of the Malouine crisis, it is necessary to examine the rise of the "new party" referred to by Walpole. From the correspondence of this gentleman, certainly an unprejudiced observer in such matters, a remarkable conflict is revealed between the all-powerful minister and a new upstart from the Parisian gutters, Jeanne, Countess du Barry.

The King became acquainted with her in 1767, and it soon became apparent to the astonished court that she was acquiring a great ascendency over him. This was especially embarrassing to 

Choiseul because he, not foreseeing her power with the King, had made no secret of his scorn for the favorite-a scorn even more warmly and freely expressed by the other members of the Choiseul family.

In 1768, Walpole described the Minister's situation to Weymouth as follows:

The Duke of Choiseul certainly from the beginning of the acquaintance has done everything he could to damp any expectations she might have from his influence . . . and frequently disapproved and discountenanced applications she had made to him within his department. Such was the Duke's position at the opening of December, 1770, when he was confronted with the almost complete breakdown of negotiations between London and Madrid. But although his sympathies were with the British in their demand for reparation, the Duke did not feel that Spain's rights to the islands should be jeopardized or lost. Spain could make the reparation, at the same time asserting her lawful interests to the archipelago. But it was absurd to expect England to evacuate the islands after the reparation, since this would render the satisfaction null and void. Choiseul was afraid the chance for a peaceful settlement was small. His policy was to keep the peace, if possible; if peace should not be possible, England must be made the aggressor. The

Her grievance against the Duke was purely personal, but around her gathered those who were jealous of his power and who stood to gain much if he should fall. It is doubtful if the Du Barry alone could have succeeded in her efforts to be rid of Choiseul. Probably it was the Duke's political enemies, using her against him, who thought of connecting him with the activities of the parlements, so thoroughly detested by the King. He had once demonstrated his feeling for them in the expulsion of their bitter enemies, the Jesuits. He amused himself by various writings and even entered into a minor intrigue against the ministry in 1777. Owing to his extravagant mode of living, he was frequently in financial difficulties. In 1784, his house in Paris had to be sold, and, notwithstanding a loan from the King, even his beloved Chanteloup had to be given up. His grief at this loss was intense and may have hastened his end. In April, 1785, he fell ill, and died of inflammation of the lungs on May 8. "The Duke de Choiseul is dead; it is not a great loss," wrote 

Frederick the Great." But few will agree with that statement. Choiseul, it is true, does not appear as a statesman of the first rank when the last three or four hundred years of French history are considered. But if he be judged in his time, which is the only fair way to appraise a man, he was infinitely superior to his predecessors (except Fleury) and his immediate successors, and was probably only surpassed in his lifetime by William Pitt and Frederick the Great.

Choiseul acquired his power at a time when France was involved in a disastrous war in three widely separated areas. He conducted the nation through the war with a minimum of loss and then began

a program of rehabilitation. Less adept and skillful in handling matters of finance, in his military and naval reforms as well as in his foreign policy he was successful. His problem was to prevent a sudden renewal of the war by Great Britain, and at the same time to strengthen French influence and prestige at the expense of England wherever possible. The Duke succeeded in this difficult task to a marked degree. He gave France a definite policy—a system of insurance-in the Spanish and Austrian alliances, against a sudden attack by England. Furthermore, he was able to acquire 

Corsica and thwart British plans toward Sweden without the firing of a single gun against England. Many of his plans were carried to a successful conclusion after his dismissal. In 1772, the young King of Sweden startled Europe with a brilliant coup d'état through which he overturned the reactionary Senate and greatly strengthened the royal power. Here, Gustavus was merely following a series of suggestions† made to his father and to him by Choiseul some years before. In 1778, Vergennes, Choiseul's pupil in diplomacy, intervened in the quarrel between Great Britain and her North American colonies, and thereby carried to a conclusion a policy anticipated by the Duke when he sent Pont-le-Roy in 1764 and Baron de Kalb in 1768 to the 

English colonies.‡ 

Although Choiseul represented the ancien régime, he typified many of its best qualities in the declining eighteenth century. 

Witty, brilliant, inclined toward skepticism and atheism, he was warmly sympathetic toward the new rationalism without fully appreciating its revolutionary implications. Although he represented an absolute monarch, he was more attracted to the limited monarchy of a government like that enjoyed by the people of England.§ History may not have written the last word on the Duke de 

Choiseul, but it can be definitely said that he led the decrepit French monarchy through seven years of hazardous and critical relations with its dangerous neighbor across the Channel, and as a result, when he handed in his resignation, French power stood high in Europe and was respected as it had not been since the exploits * See Section VI, pages 186 ff., above. † See Section VII, pages 192 ff., above.

ESSAY ON AUTHORITIES 

OST OF THE manuscript sources for this monograph are to be found in the Archives du Ministère des Affaires Etran- gères at Paris. The documents in the archives are divided into two principal classes: the Correspondance Politique, and the Mémoires et Documents. The former, as the name implies, consists, for the most part, of the correspondence between the French foreign office and the various French ambassadors and ministers throughout Europe. It is arranged according to nations. 

The correspondence between France and England, Spain, and Sweden, from 1763 to 1770, is almost complete, with only a few apparent gaps in the numbers of the dispatches. Besides the French documents, the Correspondance Politique includes many copies of letters between the Marquis de Grimaldi and his ambassadors in England and France. These letters are invaluable to a student unable to go to Madrid, as they provide fair evidence of the attitude of the Spanish government during the Choiseul ministry. 

In addition to the regular diplomatic correspondence, this division also contains consular reports, a few memoirs on divers subjects, and occasionally a personal letter between Louis XV and 

Charles III. 

The collection Mémoires et Documents does not easily lend itself to description. It is a heterogeneous collection of badly organized and insufficiently indexed memoirs, fragments of diplomatic correspondence, and special documents between a minister and some particular agent, such as the De Kalb-Choiseul letters. The department is subdivided in the following manner: France, affaires intérieures et extérieures; France et divers Etats; Petits Fonds, Fonds 

Divers, etc. The deposit was of value in preparing this thesis because it included some letters between Choiseul and Ossun that were not found in the Correspondance Politique. 

Although letters of Choiseul appear frequently in the manuscripts, it was sometimes rather difficult to perceive the design of his policy because this minister often hid his intentions from his ambassadors. Only from time to time did the Duke speak with [235]

University of California Publications in History [VOL.17 

frankness, even to those whom he knew well. To complicate the problem, Choiseul's personal papers were destroyed either by him or by his successors, for these desirable documents do not appear in any of the Paris collections. Among the missing letters is the personal correspondence of the Duke and Grimaldi. It is possible, however, to gain some idea of this correspondence, for fragments of the letters are occasionally found in the Correspondance Politique and in the Ossun-Choiseul letters in the Mémoires et Documents, because Choiseul had the habit of summarizing an occasional letter for the benefit of the Ambassador. Most of the documents in the Archives are beautifully legible and in excellent condition. The index, however, often contains errors. 

The Beliardi Papers, found in the Bibliothèque Nationale, were invaluable in the preparation of this dissertation. The Abbé Beliardi was French consul general in Spain from 1758 to 1772 and his papers should not be neglected by students of French or Spanish history. These manuscripts give an interesting and intelligent appraisal of conditions in Spain and her colonies during the first half of the reign of Charles III. They also throw light on the economic aspects of the Pacte de Famille. The Papiers are in ten volumes of manuscript. The first three contain the Abbé's correspondence from 1758 to 1771. There are wide gaps in the years 

1764, 1765, 1769, and a few in 1770. The letters deal principally, but not entirely, with commercial, financial, and còlonial affairs. 

The next four volumes contain the Abbé's memoirs and other state papers written for the French and Spanish governments. The fourth volume contains memoirs on the commercial convention of 1768; the fifth discusses the commerce of France and Spain; the sixth concerns the French and Spanish colonies; and the seventh is filled with memoirs on the marine and war. The remaining three volumes of the Abbé's papers are his diaries; they proved to be quite useless so far as this thesis was concerned. 

The Archives Nationales house a vast amount of documents, badly catalogued. Of value for the purposes of the writer were only a few manuscripts located in the Fonds Marine, B.4, dealing with the missions of Pont-le-Roy and Bougainville. 

The English documents of this period are to be found in the Public Record Office in London, under the heading, State Papers, Foreign. In general, the British diplomatic correspondence is far

less informative than the French, most of the letters consisting of two or three pages-no more. There are also several gaps in the series of letters, which detracted from their utility to the writer. 

Nevertheless, numerous letters were valuable for the view they gave of intrigue and politics at Versailles, as well as of the attitude of the British government. 

Of the numerous published memoirs in which the period abounds, only a few were found useful. The autobiographical works of 

Besenval, Bouillé, Lauzun, and Montbarey were the most important. Unfortunately, Choiseul's memoirs are fragmentary, insufficient, and insipid. Because they often skip important events in 

Choiseul's life, some scholars have deemed them spurious, but most French historians have by this time accepted them. In spite of their genuineness, however, their worth is small. 

The present study has been written primarily from the manuscript sources; still, certain secondary works were of value, and it may be useful to the student of this period to enumerate them. 

Perhaps the best short account in English of Choiseul and his government is The Duke de Choiseul, by Roger H. Soltau, an English scholar. The best general account in any language is the section by 

H. Carré concerning the ministry of the Duke in Lavisse's Histoire de France, Volume VIII, Part Two, pages 262 to 391. Both these studies are superior to the one presented by Choiseul's biographer, Gaston Maugras, in his Le Duc et la Duchesse de Choiseul, leur Vie Intime et leur Temps. Certain phases of French foreign policy in the last years of Louis XV are well covered by a few French monographs. Particularly useful to students are: Blart, Les Rapports de la France et de l'Espagne après le Pacte de Famille jusqu'à la Fin du Ministère du duc de Choiseul; Bourguet, Etudes sur la 

Politique Etrangère du duc de Choiseul. Those who wish to examine more closely the Family Compact will find good studies of it in Le Duc de Choiseul et l'Alliance Espagnole, by Bourguet, and in Sou lange-Bodin's La Diplomatie de Louis XV et le Pacte de Famille. 

Ballesteros y Beretta, Historia de España, gives the Spanish side of the Pacte and the relations of France and Spain during the ministry of Choiseul. H. Hall contributes an interesting article in the Quarterly Review for October, 1899, on the Family Compact and certain other similar treaties of the eighteenth century. 

On the subjects of Corsica and Sweden as treated in this work,

the student can only be referred to the general histories. A biography of Gustavus III, in preparation in 1935 by a Swedish student, should throw new light on that Prince's relations with France which have been indicated in Chapter VII of the present study. Julius 

Goebel, Jr., has published an interesting study of the Malouines episodes, in which he stresses the problems of international law involved, under the title, The Struggle for the Falkland Islands: 

A Study in Legal and Diplomatic History. Two other books nearing completion in 1935 by French scholars should greatly increase our knowledge of the Malouines question. Monsieur Léon Baideff, at the instigation of the Argentine government, has been preparing a complete history of the Falklands, and Monsieur Jean-Etienne Martin has probably completed by now his work on the life of 

Bougainville, French colonizer of the islands.

APPENDIX DOCUMENT I PACTE DE FAMILLE 

Article 24. Les sujets des hautes parties contractantes seront traités relativement au commerce et aux impositions, dans chacun des royaumes en Europe, comme les propres sujets du pays où ils aborderont de sorte que le pavillon espagnol jouira en France des mêmes droits et prérogatives que le pavillon français, et pareillement que le pavillon français sera traité en Espagne avec la même faveur que le pavillon espagnol. Les sujets des deux Monarchies, en déclarant leurs marchandises, paieront les mêmes droits qui seront payés par les nationaux. L'importation et l'exportation leur seront également libres, comme aux sujets naturels, et il n'y aura de droits à payer de part et d'autre, que ceux qui seront perçus sur les propres sujets du Souverain, ni de matières sujettes à confiscation, que celles qui seront prohibées aux nationaux euxmêmes; et pour ce qui regarde ces objets, tous traités, conventions ou engagemens antérieurs entre les deux Monarchies resteront abolis: bien entendu que nulle autre puissance étrangère ne jouira en Espagne, non plus qu'en France, d'aucun privilège plus avantageux que celui des deux nations. On observera les mêmes règles en France et en Espagne à l'égard du pavillon et des sujets du Roi des Deux-Siciles, et S. M. Sicilienne les fera réciproquement observer à l'égard du pavillon et des sujets des Couronnes de France et d'Espagne. 

DOCUMENT II TREATY BETWEEN GREAT BRITAIN AND SPAIN, SIGNED AT UTRECHT, 1713 

Article 8. That there be a free use of navigation and commerce between the subjects of each kingdom, as it was heretofore in time of peace, and before the declaration of this late war, in the reign of Charles the Second of glorious memory, Catholic King of Spain, according to the treaties of friendship, confederation, and commerce, which were formerly made between both nations, according to ancient customs, letters patent, cédulas, and other particular acts; and also according to the treaty or treaties of commerce which are now, or will be made at Madrid. And whereas, among other conditions of the general peace, it is by common consent established as a chief and fundamental rule, that the exercise of navigation and commerce to the Spanish West Indies should remain in the same state as it was in the time of the aforesaid King Charles the Second; that, therefore, this rule may be hereafter observed with inviolable faith, and in a manner never to be broken, and thereby all causes of distrust and suspicion concerning that matter may be prevented and removed, it is especially agreed and concluded, that no license, nor any permission at all, [247]

shall at any time be given, either to the French or to any nation whatever, in any name or under any pretense, directly or indirectly to sail, to traffic in, or introduce negroes, goods, merchandises, or any things whatsoever, into the dominions subject to the crown of Spain in America, except what may be agreed by the treaty or treaties of commerce above said, and the rights and privileges granted in a certain convention, commonly called El Assiento de Negros, whereof mention is made in the twelfth article; except also, whatsoever the said Catholic King, or his heirs or successors, shall promise by any contract or contracts for the introduction of negroes into the Spanish West Indies, to be made after that the Convention, or Assiento de Negros abovementioned, shall be determined. And that more strong and full precautions may be taken on all sides, as above said, concerning the navigation and commerce to the West Indies, it is hereby further agreed and concluded, that neither the Catholic King, nor any of his heirs and successors whatsoever, shall sell, yield, pawn, transfer, or by any means, or under any name, alienate from them and the crown of Spain, to the French, or to any other nations whatever, any lands, dominions, or territories, or any part thereof, belonging to Spain in America. On the contrary, that the Spanish dominions in the West Indies be preserved whole and entire, the Queen of Great Britain engages that she will endeavor and give assistance to the Spaniards, that the ancient limits of their dominions in the West Indies be restored, and settled as they stood in the time of the above said Catholic King Charles the Second, if it shall appear that they have in any manner, or under any pretense, been broken into, and lessened in any part, since the death of the aforesaid Catholic King Charles the Second.

 DOCUMENT V CHOISEUL TO OSSUN, VERSAILLES, JUNE 9, 1766 

“Quoiqu'il en soit, il me semble que dans la situation actuelle, nous devons mettre beaucoup de réserve dans le feu de nos démarches et de notre intérêt pour l'Espagne; il faut songer à la nôtre avant tout, ne pas compter sur l'Espagne si nous avons la guerre, et en vérité, nous n'y comptons pas trop; il faut regarder l'Espagne comme utile à nôtre commerce et pour cette partie, sacrifier dans les autres genres tout ce qui sera possible, laisser les Ministères d'Espagne intriguer, se perdre, ou s'élever comme bon lui semblera, soutenir l'idée du Pacte de Famille en Europe autant que nous pourrons, et voir venir les circonstances; nous ferons en sorte que l'Espagne n'aura pas à se plaindre de nous, nous ménagerons avec soin les sentiments des deux rois, nous commençerons, et quant à la guerre nous la ferons tout seuls dans les commencements, et peut-être jusques à la fin si nous avons du succès. Si nous sommes battus, nous tâcherons d'engager l'Espagne comme à l'autre guerre pour nous procurer la paix. Tel est mon système, il nous rend passif hors sur l'objet du commerce, et sur celui des sentiments: ...”
