THIS WAR: FROM PEARL HARBOR TO TOMORROW 

A day by day story of the first 15 months of America at War 

BY JAMES W. BELL 

Editor, The Evansville, Ind., Sunday Courier and Press 

Fifteen thousand American men have given up their lives more than 32 a day in the first fifteen months that America has been at war. Forty thousand more are missing. 

The first year of the conflict has seen some of the greatest battles in the history of mankind as America and her principal Allies, Great Britain, China and Russia, have fought on land, sea and in the air against the Axis - Germany, Italy and Japan. 

Action has been so fast and deadly on the far-flung fronts all over the globe that it has been hard to get a clear, complete picture of the war. This book is intended to give that picture, tell just what victories the Allies have won, what defeats they have suffered. 

A supplement in the back tells the story briefly of the war before America entered and describes the domestic scene before and after Pearl Harbor.

Sunday, December 7, 1941

The morning haze lifted over the Pacific to reveal the 

American war fleet in Pearl Harbor, Hawaii, as Private 

Joseph Lockhard, 20, of Williamsport, Pa., fiddled with an aircraft detector.

He had asked to stay and practice after the rest of the crew left. 

It was a quiet, peaceful 

Monday dawn on the island naval base of the United 

States, but suddenly Private

Lockhard's calm was shattered his instrument showed a great air fleet approaching! 

re- 

He sent a warning. An inexperienced lieutenant ceived it, "assumed" they were friendly planes and dismissed the matter from his mind. That was 7:02 a.m. 

Fifty-three minutes later (7:55 a.m. Sunday, Hawaiian time, 12:25 p.m. Sunday, Central Standard), 105 Jap

ASSOCIATED PRESS PHOTO 

Private Joseph Lockhard warplanes flashed out of the sky to plunge America into world war in an act of treachery never before equalled in the history of civilized nations. 

They swooped low; crashing bombs sent the 26,100-ton

U.S. Battleship Arizona to the bottom. 

In a few devastating minutes the invaders sank or damaged eight battleships, half the number in our entire fleet and all that were in the Hawaiian area, smashed 10 other warships, killed 2343 Americans, left 960 missing and turned Pearl Harbor into a burning shambles. 

Japan had launched her war to drive the United States and Great Britain out of the Pacific, whip them if she could with the aid of the Axis and deceit. For at the moment she attacked, her special "peace" envoy, Saburo 

Kurusu, and her ambassador, Admiral Kichisaburu Nomura, were holding peace conferences in Washington with 

The Japs had mapped their war plans thoroughly and struck with lightning speed. At the same time as the Pearl 

Harbor attack, they:

Bombed and landed parachute troops on the Philippine Islands, U.S. Far East outpost 6930 miles from San Francisco.

Blasted Singapore by plane and landed sky troops on the Malayan Peninsula 100 miles north of this great British sea base.

Air-raided Hong Kong, English colony in South China. 

Attacked Thailand (Siam).

The greatest tragedy, however, was Pearl Harbor. The 

Americans counted as sunk or badly damaged: the battleships Arizona, Oklahoma, 29,000 tons; California, 29,000 tons; Nevada, 29,000 tons; and West Virginia, 31,800 tons; the destroyers, Shaw, Cassin and Downes; minelayer Oglala; target ship Utah, and a large floating drydock. 

Damaged, but soon to be back in service, were: the battleships Pennsylvania, Maryland and Tennessee; cruisers Honolulu, Helena and Raleigh; seaplane tender Curtiss and repair ship Vestal.

Most of the 475 aircraft on the island were destroyed, few being able to get off the ground. The Navy estimated 

1 the attack force was composed of 21 torpedo planes, 48 dive bombers, and 36 horizontal bombers, aided by twoman submarines. The enemy lost 28 planes, three subs, but the rest escaped, with one plane in pursuit. It lost the trail. 

Caught in the whirlpool of disaster were the two Hawaiian commanders, Admiral Husband Kimmel, and Lieut.

Gen. Walter Short. Both had been warned 10 days before to expect such an attack and had not prepared adequately, an investigating committee later found, in accusing them of "dereliction of duty."

Hawaii, its air defenses riddled, was helpless; the U.S. 

Pacific fleet had no battle line and was greatly outweighed by the Jap fleet. 

Three hours after these stabs in the back, the Japanese cabinet met and blandly declared war on the United States and Great Britain, retroactive to Sunday morning. 

What the assembled Nipponese generals, admirals, and officials did not realize was the extent of the damage they had inflicted upon the United States. Not even Americans could be told the entirety of the tragedy, lest the world and our enemies discover it. Full announcement was to be withheld a year.

Monday, December 8, 1941 

The United States declared war upon Japan at 12:13 p.m., the Senate voting 82 to 0, the House 388 to 1, with 

Congresswoman Jeanette Rankin, Montana Republican who voted against World War I in 1917, voicing the single "No." 

President Roosevelt spoke after the vote, saying: "Always will we remember the character of this onslaught against us . . . . . no matter how long it takes to overcome this premeditated invasion". . . and he cited the 

3700-mile distance from Japan to Pearl Harbor as proof of plotted assault . . . "the American people in their righteous might will win through to complete victory we will gain the inevitable triumph, so help us God." 

In contrast to a wildly cheering Congress, a stolid British Parliament declared war upon Japan, hearing Prime 

Minister Churchill say: 

"It remains for the two great democracies to face the task ahead with whatever strength God has given them." 

This strength grew hourly: Australia, the Dutch, the 

Dutch East Indies, Canada, and Costa Rica declared war on Japan. 

San Francisco had an air-raid alarm, a blackout with radio stations shut off and the military in control. (Not many persons then realized the true danger of this scare, because few knew that Pearl Harbor losses had left our west coast vulnerable to attack.)

The Nipponese smashed on, Thailand in southeast Asia surrendering less than 24 hours after being invaded. 

Tuesday, December 9, 1941

Japan launched an all-out drive on the Philippine island of Luzon, landing parachute troops at Lubang, near Cavite naval base, bombing Clark and Nichols airfields near Manila, the capital city, killing 100. Armed fifth columnists rose to help the Japs as the American army under Gen. 

Douglas MacArthur marched to meet the invaders. 

President Roosevelt spoke to the nation and the world, 3

warning that we were at war with the entire Axis, which he described as "powerful and resourceful international gangsters banded together to make war on the whole human race." Many Americans, he declared, will be killed. 

He closed with a ringing prediction: "We are going to win the war and win the peace that follows." 

New York City had an air-raid fright, and evacuated schools. 

Wednesday, December 10, 1941

Nine Japanese planes at the break of day sighted the great, new 35,000-ton "unsinkable" British battleship

Prince of Wales and 32,000-ton cruiser Repulse steaming proudly off the coast of Malaya, north of Singapore. Single file they stalked the sea dreadnaughts, peeled off one by one and dived fearlessly through fire of antiaircraft guns to loosen bombs and deadly torpedoes at the seacraft. Direct hits set the two warships afire, both sank with the loss of over 500 lives in Britain's greatest maritime disaster.

In a few historic hours the Nipponese flyers had: 

1. Changed sea warfare by proving conclusively air power can destroy battleships that are unprotected by planes. (British Admiral Sir Tom Phillips had seen the 

Jap planes, but had waited four hours to wireless the 

RAF for help. Then it was too late.) 

2. Paved the way for the fall of Singapore.

Two new landings were made on Luzon, one in the north, the other in the west, where the Philippine defenders drove invaders back into the sea with heavy losses. 

The sand-spit U.S. island of Guam, 6326 miles east of 

San Francisco, capitulated to Japs. 555 Americans were taken captive. 

But to raise America's hopes in the midst of these reverses, there emerged the first Yankee hero of the war 

Capt. Colin P. Kelly, of Madison, Fla., sank the 29,000-ton

Jap battleship Haruna in a daring single plane attack. 

Spotting the ship in Lingayen Bay, Philippine Islands, Kelly dived his Flying Fortress through a hail of antiaircraft shells, leveled off just above the battleship, and planted three bombs squarely upon the sea-going fortress. 

As it sank, Kelly sped away, only to be overhauled by a squadron of Jap fighting planes. 

With machine gun and cannon fire, the pursuit ships set Kelly's plane afire. He ordered the crew to bail out and five did safely. Then the 

Flying Fortress exploded. 

Lieut. David Robbin, co-pilot, was miraculously blown to safety. Kelly's body was found beside his plane, which was only two minutes from his base when it went down. 

Capt. Kelly was posthumously awarded the Congressional Medal of Honor and his 18-month-old son, Colin 

Kelly, III, was recommended

Rev. H. R. Latham in memorial services at Kelly's home town of Madison. 

Thursday, December 11, 1941

Germany and Italy declared war upon the United States. 

Hitler blamed U.S. for the outbreak of war with Japan, cried: "I cannot be insulted by Roosevelt." 

Mussolini shouted: ". . . Neither the Axis nor Japan wanted war... U.S. tyranny and treachery brought it." 

Notified at 8:25 a.m. of Germany's declaration and at 

8:50 a.m. of Italy's, U.S. declared war on both nations at 12:04 p.m. The vote: on Germany, Senate, 88 to 0;

House, 393 to 0; on Italy, Senate, 90 to 0; House, 399 to 0. 

Miss Rankin, lone vote against war with Japan, voted "present" on both roll calls. 

Friday, December 12, 1941

First news of the gallant stand of 378 Marines at tiny Wake Island, 5000 miles from U.S. in the Pacific, was announced by Washington. Attacked by superior Japanese forces, the handful of Marines managed to sink a Nipponese cruiser and a destroyer and down two planes. Repulsing four attacks, the embattled Americans held the island.

The American Federation of Labor announced several hundred building trades workers were captured after fighting beside military men at Guam Island. 

Saturday, December 13, 1941

Russia broke the siege of Moscow, handing Hitler his first great defeat of war. A Nazi army of 750,000 started withdrawing, leaving 85,000 dead on the frozen Soviet plains.

The Japs struck at Borneo, Dutch and British island in south Pacific off Singapore whose 151,622 square miles make it the third largest island in the world. The Dutch navy sank two transports but others landed on the island home of fierce Dyak native head hunters, famed as "wild men from Borneo."

Japanese beamed their radio to U.S., blatantly broadcast in English to "good Americans" that their real enemies were not Japan and Germany, but "Roosevelt and his 

Jewish financiers."

The U.S. took crews off French liners and seized the Normandie.

Bulgaria and Hungary declared war on America. The past few days had brought war declarations on the Axis by Panama, Nicaragua, El Salvador, Honduras, Haiti,

Cuba, Guatemala, and the Dominican Republic. 

Sunday, December 14, 1941

Japan threw new troops onto Luzon Island, landing at Aparri on northeast coast, Vigan on upper west coast, and Legaspi in extreme south, but American and Filipino troops were stemming the yellow tide temporarily, inflicting great losses on the enemy in the "battles of the beaches." Fourteen air raids had been made on the Philippines in eight days. 

Monday, December 15, 1941 

War Secretary Frank Knox, returning from an air tour of Hawaii, gave a partial report on Pearl Harbor, announcing six ships were lost, 2729 men killed. (His figures on losses of men and ships were later changed in the full

report on Pearl Harbor on Dec. 7, 1942.) 

He lauded the Navy, declaring: "In the hour of the Navy's greatest peril, the officers and men exhibited magnificent courage. . .The story is not one of individual heroism . . . it lies in the splendid manner in which all hands did their job as long as they were able."

As if writing a climax to Knox's report on Jap treachery, President Roosevelt told Congress that three hours after the Pearl Harbor attack, Nipponese envoys were still talking peace with Secretary of State Hull. He commented: "There is a record for all history to read in amazement, in horror, and in disgust.” 

Japanese troops advanced 50 miles through the steaming Malay jungle toward Singapore. With startling new jungle warfare tactics of infiltering men behind the enemy's lines, and using tanks and mechanized equipment that swept across the flooded rice fields they drove British and Indian troops before them.

At Hong Kong, fighting with isolated formations of ten men in another innovation in warfare, the Japs pushed to the gates of the British city. Others forged rapidly through Thailand, crossed the border into Burma. 

Tuesday, December 16, 1941

Jap warships shelled two small Hawaiian islands, Johnson and Maui, doing little damage.

Fighting with fanatical determination, Nipponese troops captured Kota Bahru airport, the last large field from which Britain could get planes in the air to defend Singapore. Time proved this a forerunner of doom for 

British hopes of holding their great sea base. 

The American public had not yet realized what was in store for the Allies in Pacific. War correspondent Leland 

Stowe predicted the Philippines would have to be surrendered "to save Singapore" - and many read this as a "scare" story. 

Wednesday, December 17, 1941

The War Department removed three top military men at 

Pearl Harbor: Admiral Husband Kimmel; Lieut. Gen. Wal ter Short, Army Commander, and Air Force Commander 

Maj. Gen. Frederick R. Martin. 

Named as replacements were: Rear Admiral Chester

Nimitz as commander of the Pacific fleet; Lieut. Gen. Delos 

C. Emmons, army chief in Hawaii; Brig. Gen. C. L. Tinker, to command air force there.

Congress gave Roosevelt power to set up a censorship and he named Byron Price, Associated Press editor, to do the job. 

Thursday, December 18, 1941

U.S. submarines first appeared in the war picture, as 

Washington reported undersea craft had sunk two troop ships and probably a destroyer off the Philippine Islands. 

Friday, December 19, 1941

Congress voted to register all men from 20 to 44 for military service, and envisioned an army of four million. (This goal was passed a year later and was to grow greater as the war progressed.) Also to register were men 

18 to 64 for civilian war duties.

As the heroic Marines still held on Wake Island, the joke of the hour was that the Navy had wirelessed the Marines and asked if they wanted anything. The Marine reply was: "Send more Japs."

Gen. MacArthur gave an order destined to go down as famous. Asked if the flag in Manila should be taken down because it was a marker for raiding airmen, he replied: "Let's keep the flag flying." 

Saturday, December 20, 1941

A Jap sub rose out of the Pacific, 20 miles off the California coast, torpedoed and sank the big American tanker, Emidio, with loss of five lives. Another U.S. tanker, the 

Agwiworld, escaped with slight damage by zigzagging when a sub popped out of the water and started shelling her.

A fierce land battle developed in Davao, on the southern Philippine island of Mindanao, 800 miles south of Manila, as the Japs landed a big force there. 

Admiral Ernest J. King was appointed commander in chief of the American fleet; Rear Admiral Royal E. Ingersoll succeeded him in charge of the Atlantic fleet. 

Sunday, December 21, 1941 

Hitler answered an "inward call" and took personal command of the German army, dismissing General Field 

Marshal Walther von Brauchitsch.

Calling Russia the "greatest foe of all times," he appealed to his soldiers to "fanatically" wait out the winter for new arms and supplies. Showing his first fear of cracking morale, he emotionally appealed to his army, saying: "I know war from four years of the mighty conflicts from 1914 to 1918. I experienced personally all the horrors of almost all the battles as an ordinary soldier. I was wounded twice, even threatened with blindness. Nothing torments you, weighs upon you, oppresses you that is not known to me."

As der Fuehrer spoke, America became aware of the tremendous blows his submarines were striking in the 

Atlantic against our shipping. Secretary Knox announced

U.S. warships were battling the undersea menace and had sunk 14 U-boats. 

Monday, December 22, 1941

A short, thick figure garbed in a nautical cap and a high buttoned sea jacket got off an airplane in Washington, D.C., to electrify the world - it was Prime Minister 

Churchill, on an unheralded visit to President Roosevelt to map war plans. 

He closeted himself immediately with Mr. Roosevelt at the White House to "plan the defeat of Hitlerism throughout the world." (The heads of the two great democracies made one of the most far-reaching decisions of the war- - to defeat Hitler first. Secrecy surrounded this for months, until events showed that the greater part of American and British power was being poured into the European war theatre.)

Eighty Jap troop ships appeared off the coast of Luzon, in the Philippines, unloaded 100,000 men, tanks, and supplies at the gulf of Lingayen. This army started a 100mile march south to Manila. 

Tuesday, December 23, 1941

The turning point of the war came when Russia repulsed the Nazi hordes and the United States entered the conflict,

Prime Minister Churchill told reporters at the White 

House. 

Standing on top a chair, he declared that for those developments "anyone who had been through the lonely months of 1940 must thank God fervently."

After his press conference, the United States Britain war council was organized. 

Great 

The finish of a heroic saga was written as Jap forces overwhelmed the 378 Marines who had held out on Wake 

Island for 16 days against tremendous odds, sinking two

Jap warships and downing several planes with small field guns. 

Wednesday, December 24, 1941

General MacArthur personally took the field to lead the American-Filipino army in the Philippines, so grave became the threat as Nippon poured more troops onto the island. 

Thursday, December 25, 1941

Hong Kong, the British crown colony with a population of 

1,050,256, in south 

China fell to Nippon.

Jubilant Jap radio broadcasts called it a "fitting Christmas present for the Japanese nation." 

The U.S. Navy announced a Jap sub was sunk off the coast of California. 

Britain, driving westward in the north African desert, captured the important Libyan port of Benghazi from 

German and Italian forces.

Friday, December 26, 1941 Pacific Ocean PHILIPPINES NEA MAP 

Great Britain and the United States will invade the continent of Europe in 1943, Prime Minister Churchill told the U.S. Congress. He was the first British prime minister ever to speak before the American governing body. In a voice tinged with hatred and sarcasm, he referred to the Germans as "those savage Nazis," warned of toil and pain to come but closed: "This day we are masters of our fate, I am sure that the tasks that have been set for us are not beyond our strength. The forces against us are bitter and ruthless, but our resources are greater than theirs." 

Manila was proclaimed an "open city." In face of this declaration that it would not be defended and no troops would be left there, the Japs bombed the defenseless citizens. 

Saturday, December 27, 1941

Nipponese planes continued to rain death on Manila. 

As bombs killed civilians by the hundreds and flames swept large sections of the city, President Roosevelt broadcast to the Philippine capital. He said: "I give to the people of the Philippines my solemn pledge that their freedom will be redeemed and their independence established and protected. The entire resources, in men and material, of the United States stand behind that pledge."

With the scanty resources they had in the Philippines, U.S. fighting men stood behind that pledge. The Navy announced two Motor Torpedo Boats, only 70 feet long and made of plywood, had roared over the waters of Subic Bay, sent hissing torpedoes into two Jap cruisers and sped away as they sank. 

Sunday, December 28, 1941

Japan made its first assault on the Dutch East Indies, bombing and landing paratroops on Sumatra, 735,168square-mile island, just south of Singapore.

Monday, December 29, 1941 

Planes of Nippon raided Corregidor, Manila bay fortress, for three hours. Hundreds were killed and eye-witnesses reported the docks were so covered with blood it was difficult to load wounded on rescue launches.

The Japs made slashing gains on all fronts, seizing Ipoh, valuable tin mine center near Singapore and capturing Kuching, oil city in Borneo. The cost included three warships sunk by Dutch off Borneo.

British commandos raided the Norwegian coast, destroyed a Nazi garrison, sank nine ships. 

Tuesday, December 30, 1941 

six dramatic "Our forces are being pushed back" words from Gen. MacArthur in the Philippines were a prologue to disaster. Jap dive bombers controlled all key roads on Luzon and Nipponese tanks battered back inferiorly equipped Americans. 

Speaking at Ottawa, Canada, Churchill predicted Allied invasions of Europe and Asia will climax the war. 

Wednesday, December 31, 1941

General MacArthur consolidated his forces from scattered Philippine fronts, preparatory to a retreat to a rough, hilly jungle peninsula that he was to make known around the world Bataan. 

Thursday, January 1, 1942

Uncle Sam broke the news to civilians they must walk for war; all manufacture and sales of automobiles were stopped. Tire rationing had previously been decreed. 

A Japanese sub ineffectively shelled the coast of Hawaii. 

Sarawak, north Borneo state, yielded to Japan.

Friday, January 2, 1942 

Manila, capital city of the Philippines which the United States had held since August 13, 1898, when it was captured from Spain by Commodore George Dewey, was taken by the Japanese at 3 p.m. 

There was a strange contrast between the methods by which the city was taken that revealed "progress" in warfare during 43 years.

The Japs marched into a different city, pock-marked from bombs that killed many innocent civilians and gutted by fires set from air explosives. With Manila the Nipponese got Cavite, modern U.S. naval base. General Mac- 

Arthur withdrew his men to wild, tropical Bataan and

In Washington, 26 nations signed a pact to fight with all their might until the Axis is defeated and agreed not to sign a separate peace. To be known from then on as the United Nations, they were: the United States, Great 

Britain, China, Australia, Canada, Greece, Belgium, Luxembourg, Czechoslovakia, India, Netherlands, New Zealand, Poland, Norway, South Africa, Yugoslavia, Cuba,

Costa Rica, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Guatemala, 

Haiti, Honduras, Panama, Nicaragua, and Soviet Russia.

Bardia, Axis port in Libya, North Africa, was taken by the British. 

Saturday, January 3, 1942

British General Sir Archibald Wavell was named to command all Allied troops in the Pacific war zone. 

For five hours, the Japs bombed Corregidor; 13 were killed. 

Sunday, January 4, 1942

The battle of Bataan began with 700 attacking Japs slain and four Nipponese planes shot down. 

Admiral Thomas C. Hart, newly named commander of the United Nations Pacific fleet, arrived in the Netherlands Indies by submarine. 

Monday, January 5, 1942

Americans and Japanese fought fiercely over the southern Philippine island of Davao. A U.S. bomber sank a

Jap destroyer there, and dropped three bombs smack upon a battleship.

While the Far East was occupying war spotlight, Russia continued to rout the German invaders in the Crimea, retaking 572 villages. 

Tuesday, January 6, 1942 

President Roosevelt announced American troops would be rushed to England. (Then it was thought they would be used for defense, but they were actually trained in Ireland, convoyed to North Africa for America's opening offense.) "We will not fight a defensive war," the chief executive told Congress. "We shall carry on an attack against the enemy. We shall hit him again and again wherever and whenever we can reach him. We must keep the war from our shores and carry it to his."

The war will end, the President said, "with the end of militarism in Germany, Japan and Italy. Most assuredly we will not settle for less."

He called for tools with which to attack, setting U.S. production goals in 1942 at: 60,000 planes, 45,000 tanks, 30,000 antiaircraft guns, to be stepped up in 1943 to: 

125,000 planes, 75,000 tanks and 35,000 antiaircraft guns. (Actual production for 1942 was announced on Dec. 7 as: 49,000 planes, 32,000 tanks and self-propelled artillery, 17,000 antiaircraft guns.) 

Wednesday, January 7, 1942 

President Roosevelt asked the largest war budget ever known-58 billion dollars. (This was exceeded in his 

1943 request of 104 billion.) He served notice that American taxes were to rise steeply. 

Thursday, January 8, 1942

Jap armies in the Philippines and Malaya made slow, steady advances. 

Friday, January 9, 1942

Japanese newspapers proclaimed their fleet was the "greatest in the world," told their readers, "the invasion of America is possible," but facts deflated their boasts within the day. A U.S. sub sank the Nipponese freighter 

Unkai Maru No. 1 only 50 miles from Tokyo.

The Japs made it clear to the American man in the street why he was not to get auto tires- they captured Kuala Lumpur, Malaya, the world's crude rubber capital. 

Saturday, January 10, 1942

Germany admitted the plight of her armies in Russia Iwas critical as the Reds drove the Nazis back on the entire front. A Nazi spokesman said: "Hitler will know how to overcome this crisis." 

Sunday, January 11, 1942

Japan invaded the island empire of the Dutch East Indies, richest loot of the south Pacific. Troops landed at 

Celebes and Tarakan, a tiny island reputed to produce some of the finest grade oil in the world. The Jap attack sites were approximately 500 miles south of the Philippines. The Dutch fought back bravely and, aided by Ameri-

can bombers, sank six troop ships and damaged a man-ofwar.

In Bataan the Japs ran into their first stubborn resistance and wheeled up big guns after infantry attacks were repulsed with terrific losses by Americans. The

Yanks were fighting from "foxholes," a word to be added to the dictionaries by this war. (A "foxhole" is a small trench scooped out by the individual soldier to hide and protect him from the enemy. In these small dugouts, so named because they are only a little bigger than holes dug by foxes, many American boys lived and fought for weeks, never leaving them.)

Monday, January 12, 1942 

A large British freighter was sunk by a German sub off the coast of Nova Scotia, 90 losing their lives. 

Tuesday, January 13, 1942

Japan captured Tarakan, Dutch East Indian Isle which produces monthly 80,000 tons of oil from wells so close to the ocean that it can be pumped directly into ship tanks. 

The Dutch resisted desperately and blew up many oil wells. Most of the defenders, however, were slain, since they could not escape from the oil town which was walled in to the sea by impenetrable tropical jungle.

Donald Nelson was named head of the U.S. war production board. 

Wednesday, January 14, 1942

Stopped by the amazing and courageous resistance of 

Gen. MacArthur's American and native soldiers on Bataan, the Japs were pouring reinforcements into Malaya and driving relentlessly toward Singapore. 

A U-boat sank a Panama tanker just 20 miles from 

Long Island, New York. Nineteen seamen perished. 

Thursday, January 15, 1942

The same Nazi sub that sank a tanker off Long Island sent another Allied ship to the bottom just 32 miles from 

New York. 

Friday, January 16, 1942

Britain threw Australian troops into the desperate battle to halt waves of Jap soldiers advancing upon Singapore. 

Saturday, January 17, 1942

Prime Minister Churchill arrived safely back in England, traveling by flying boat from the United States after his historic conference at the White House. 

A U.S. sub penetrated the defenses of Tokyo harbor and sank three Jap merchant ships, the Navy announced. 

Sunday, January 18, 1942 

Skirt-wearing Premier U Saw of Burma was arrested by the English for conspiring to turn over to the Japanese this southeast Asiatic possession of Britain that is known as the "storied" Kipling country. (Burma later fell to the 

Japs as many natives aided the Nipponese.) 

1235 American prisoners from Wake Island were landed in Japan, doomed to long months in prison camps. Most of these were workmen preparing defenses there; some were the remainder of the Marine garrison of 378 that stood off the Japs so valiantly. 

Monday, January 19, 1942

German U-boats, raiding almost within sight of the North Carolina coast, sank three ships. The U.S. merchant vessel, City of Atlanta, was torpedoed, sent to the bottom with 44 dead, and two more were killed when the 

Latvian freighter, Ciltvaria, was sunk. A sub torpedoed the U.S. freighter Malay, then rose to the surface and shelled it, but the ship managed to reach port safely. 

Tuesday, January 20, 1942 

The first 100 U.S. soldiers arrived in England. 

Wednesday, January 21, 1942

Japan was lashing with full fury throughout the Pacific war theatre. Paying a high price in lives, Nipponese soldiers occupied Minnahassa Province, Celebes Island,

Dutch East Indies. Jap fliers bombed New Britain Island in the extreme south Pacific, 800 miles northwest of Australia. So dark was the picture that Prime Minister John

Furious Jap rushes were turned back by Yanks on Bataan. The Nipponese were attacking 24 hours a day, hurling tanks, planes, and men incessantly at U.S. lines in what American veterans of World War I termed the greatest battle for our troops since the Argonne Forest in France.

President Roosevelt revealed that the United States had eight or 10 expeditionary forces on battlefronts throughout the world.

From the fighting front in the Philippines, General

Across the Atlantic, a different type of man spoke- Adolf Hitler. On the ninth anniversary of the rise of the Nazis to power he predicted: "Germany will win the war, but not this year." (British bombings and German losses in Russia caused Hitler to cancel his public appearance a year later on the

Saturday, January 31, 1942

British troops withdrew from Malaya to the city of Singapore, the naval fortress they had built to rule the Orient. They blew up the seven mile causeway connecting the mainland and the island city-a city jam-packed with refugees.

The Nazis installed Vidkun Quisling, whose name had become a synonym for treason to one's country, as premier of Norway. His treachery had paved the way for the

General Rommel drove the British back 65 miles in Libya.

The Japs cut off the supply of water of besieged Singapore.

Amboina Island, second largest Dutch East Indian naval base, fell before Jap land, sea, and air assault.

Air fighting over the south Pacific cost the Japs 20 aircraft, but the Nipponese Zero fighting planes were slowly gaining control of the air. 

President Roosevelt announced that Washington would be military staff headquarters for the United Nations. 

Twenty-six perished as the oil tanker, India Arrow, was sunk off the Jersey coast. 

Saturday, February 7, 1942

The British evacuated the Singapore naval base, their "bastion of empire" in the Orient which was built after 19 years of labor and at a cost of 400 million dollars. 

Workmen dynamited the mammoth floating docks, the only ones in the Far East that could service American and British warships. 

Sunday, February 8, 1942

Troops of Japanese Lieutenant General Tomoyuki Yam ashiti crossed the Strait of Johore, which separates Singapore Island from the Malay mainland, and advanced to within 10 miles of the city of Singapore.

Monday, February 9, 1942 

Japanese tanks were landed on Singapore Island and thrown immediately into battle. The British were frantically removing women and children from Singapore by the sea.

Batavia, Javanese city of 437,000 and capital of the Dutch East Indies, was bombed. Thousands of natives looked in amazement at their first glimpse of modern air warfare. The Dutch commandeered the city's golf courses -the only large flat, open spaces in the hilly, mountainrimmed capital — and practiced tank maneuvers on the fairways and greens. 

A spark flew out of a welder's torch at New York City pier and in a few minutes the $60,000,000 French luxury liner Normandie was a mass of flames. Twenty-two hundred workmen were reconditioning the ship, which had been bought by the U.S. for war use. One of these lost his life and 200 were badly burned. The fire swept the upper decks and firemen poured streams of water on these decks. When the tide came in, the ship, topheavy from this water, rolled over on its side. Sabotage was investigated but no proof was found. 

Tuesday, February 10, 1942 "We are all in the hands of God," said Singapore's governor Sir Thomas Shenton as the Japs closed in on the city.

Savage American resistance caused the Nipponese to pour 50,000 more troops into Bataan. New landings also were made by the Japanese on Celebes, Dutch East Indies, and Australia's island of New Britain. 

Wednesday, February 11, 1942

A lone plane flew over Singapore . . . and out of it fluttered a piece of paper. Picked up in the streets of the beleaguered city, it was found to be a note to the British commanders, demanding immediate surrender. 

When there was no reply, the Japs resumed their ferocious assault and trapped 30,000 Allied troops in the southeastern section of the island. 

Dutch Vice Admiral C. E. L. Helfrich replaced U.S. 

Admiral Thomas Hart as United Nations fleet commander in the Pacific.

The United States rushed troops to the Dutch oil-producing islands of Aruba and Curacao, off the South American coast of Venezuela.

Germany's three mightiest warships, the 26,000 ton battleships, Scharnhorst and Gneisenau, and the 10,000 ton cruiser Prinz Eugen, broke out of the harbor at Brest, France, and made a 700 mile run to Keil, Germany. They were discovered in the English Channel and had to fight their way through 600 attacking English planes in a fivehour battle. Steel nets hung over the sides of the Nazi ships had protected them from bombs. The English lost 42 planes, the Germans 18.

Japan's road to conquest of the South Pacific was cleared as the United Nations admitted the enemy held complete control of Macassar Straits between Celebes and Borneo.

Detroit and New York are not safe from Nazi bombs, nor are our sea coasts secure from attack, President 

Roosevelt warned the nation.

The Japs prepared to attack Java and war experts warned that if it fell the war would last 15 years. (It is interesting to compare this prediction with those the "experts" are now making, which forecast, in most cases, a much shorter conflict.)

Singapore was renamed Shonan (Light of the South), by the Japs. 

Thursday, February 19, 1942

General Douglas MacArthur, fighting desperately with his small band of Americans and Filipinos in the "foxholes" of Bataan, hurling back charge after charge of the larger Jap forces, was told by the War Department that he would be transferred to Australia to head the Allied defense there. Enemy shells fell upon United States soil for the first time in the war. A submarine slipped in from the Pacific, fired wildly at an oil refinery in the small California coastal town of Golata. Slight damage was done and no one was hurt.

President Roosevelt declare:

Soon, we, and not our enemies, will have the offensive; we, not they, will win the final battles; and we, not they, will make the final peace."

The Japs assembled troop ships in the Java Sea for invasion of the Island of Java, richest of the Dutch East

Friday, February 27, 1942

The Allied fleet was outnumbered and faced ships with bigger guns and heavier armor. But Admiral Deoorman's orders were to attack. He did . . . and in a three-day battle the United Nations' East Indian fleet was wiped out and the fate of Java sealed.

Deoorman to a Dutch hero's death. The Houston and Perth were separated from the rest of the fleet and never heard of again.

Admiral E. J. King was named commander in chief of the U.S. fleet. 

Tuesday, March 10, 1942 

Japan was nearing the end of her quick conquests in the South Pacific though little did she know it. In three months the Nipponese had taken Singapore, Borneo, the rich Dutch East Indies and was on the verge of victory in the Philippines.

The Nipponese began landing at the northern tip of the 1500-mile long island and American fliers gave them a taste of what was to come by sinking three transports. (The United Nations on March 6, 1943, still held the lower half of New Guinea).

Lieut. Commander 

Robert Bulkley, in charge of the small fleet, well knew the superhuman task that confronted him. The boats were no longer fast. Once they could match the speed of the fastest auto on a prewar highway. But constant fighting for 13

months without overhauling or repair had cut their speed to less than that of the slowest of Jap war craft. Carbon so choked their valves that they had to be babied to turn up any performance at all.

Before Bulkley lay a vast ocean controlled and patrolled by the enemy. Jap subs and destroyers lurked everywhere.

He had to travel at night, hide by day. The first night he had to still his engines and float for hours when a Jap cruiser loomed on the horizon. 

His only navigation instruments were of the crudest sort. Rough seas washed over the tiny craft and tossed them around at the mercy of the sea.

But Bulkley had to get through, for he carried a precious cargo for the United Nations - Gen. MacArthur, Mrs.

MacArthur, their four-year-old son, and the general's military staff.

Commander Bulkley did get through, after some adventures that, though amusing now, were tragic then. 

The pilots couldn't control the tired engines of their boats.

The last boat saw a vessel looming behind it. It opened up full speed. The other craft gained. Desperately, the crew threw overboard everything, to lighten the boat. 

They tossed over all their fuel. The "pursuing" ship gained... finally caught up.

The boat behind was Bulkley's. The other MPT boat had passed it unbeknownst to either. 

Because its fuel was gone the one craft had to turn back. It was never heard of again.

The seas were so rough the men running the boats had to clamber over sea-sick army men.

During the day they hid. Friendly natives protected them. They had several close shaves, almost being found by Japanese.

Finally they made it to a rendezvous with a Flying Fortress on Mindoro in the South Philippines. A plane took 

MacArthur to Australia where he traveled by rail to Melbourne to be met by 4,000 cheering Australians. 

Lieut. Commander Bulkley was to get a Congressional 

Medal of Honor for his achievement. 

Succeeding MacArthur as commander in the Philippines was Maj. Gen. Jonathan "Skinny" Wainwright, a cavalry officer.

Wednesday, April 1, 1942 The tide was beginning to turn in the Atlantic in favor of the United States. The Navy announced 28 Axis subs had been sunk off the east coast. 

Thursday, April 2, 1942

The Japs drove the Americans back on Bataan. 

In Burma the Nipponese seized Akyab, the last seaport the Allies held in that country. 

Friday, April 3, 1942

Malta, the island where the fabled Ulysses found the one-eyed giant Cyclops, where the crusaders fought for Christendom during the Middle Ages, was attacked determinedly by Axis planes. The Nazis weren't interested in the 20 by 15 mile isle for its history. They wanted it because it blocked their sea routes, prevented them from controlling the Mediterranean Sea. Daily, squadrons from 

20 to 80 bombers attacked the British garrison there. (Malta survived to become the most bombed piece of land in the world, but still an Allied fortress.) 

Saturday, April 4, 1942 

Japan wheeled up big guns to batter Corregidor. Nipponese assault troops broke the Yank line and made small gains. 

Sunday, April 5, 1942

Air disaster hit the Axis as 212 planes were shot down; the Japanese lost 57 when their air fleet attempted a sneak raid on Ceylon, 240 by 170 mile island off the south tip of India in the Indian ocean, and 35 in dog fights over 

Australia; Germany lost 107 on the Russian battle front and 13 in a raid on Malta. 

Monday, April 6, 1942 

Three hundred bombers, the largest British raiding fleet yet to take to the air, dealt death and destruction in the German Ruhr and the industrial suburbs of Paris. 

Tuesday, April 7, 1942 "Superior enemy forces, supported by tanks and artillery savagely attacked the center of our lines" . . . it was the Bataan war communique, signalling the beginning of the end for the Yank defenders in the Philippines. 

The Japs attacked India. Planes dive-bombed Vizgapatam and Cocanada, cities of 40,000 on the northeast coast. 

Wednesday, April 8, 1942

Wave upon wave of Jap assault troops were hurled at the Yank lines in Bataan. The troops of Gen. Yamashiti had been attacking continuously for 48 hours when . . . 

President Roosevelt wired General Wainwright, giving him absolute authority to continue the fight or make terms as he might see fit. 

There was little choice for Wainwright. 

Food for his soldiers was giving out. 

But even as the

Americans were on the verge of defeat on one side of the world, they made plans to attack on the other. Gen. Mar shall, U.S. Army chief of staff, and Harry 

Hopkins, head of the 

British American Munitions Assignment 

Board, arrived in Britain by plane. (They conferred on plans for the British-American invasion of North Africa, to occur Nov. 7, 1942, it is now generally agreed.)

On the other side of the globe, the RAF rained bombs and death over a 500-mile front in Axis-held Europe. 

Tuesday, April 14, 1942

Russia made it clear to the world for the first time that though she was fighting with the Allies against Hitler, she was not going to war against Japan. The Reds warned 

Japan to "strictly observe" the neutrality pact between the two nations and quiet "irresponsible" Jap generals from "blabbering" about attacking Siberia. 

Pierre Laval, an ardent follower of Hitler, was restored to power as premier of France.

Destruction of 322 German planes in a week brought the total destroyed since March 11, 1942, to 1103, Britain announced. 

Wednesday, April 15, 1942

America warned all her citizens to get out of unoccupied France quickly. 

Thursday, April 16, 1942

New Jap troops were hurled into the battle of Burma and British lines were breaking. Soldiers and civilians, many of them women, were fleeing into the mountain jungles, trying to escape to India. 

Friday, April 17, 1942

A sheet of flame miles long flared over West Burma on the banks of the Irawaddy River - the retreating British had dynamited 6000 oil wells. 

Saturday, April 18, 1942

Mid-day in the third largest city in the world found its seven million inhabitants engaged in typical Saturday activities. Some were working in war plants, others watching a baseball game, many just strolling down the boulevards.

There was no cause for worry. True, the nation was at - but war had proved only a series of rapid impreswar sive victories. The enemy at the closest striking point was no nearer than 3000 miles. 

If a citizen was nervous, he could stroll to the waterfront and look at the line of big ships anchored there. 

But few were nervous that day in the capital of the 

Rising Sun - Tokyo. 

Not even when they looked up at a squadron of low flying planes.

But suddenly, over the industrial heart of the city, these planes began to drop bombs. Great fires blossomed up în war plants. Air-raid sirens screamed. Antiaircraft guns started firing . ...

The Americans were bombing Tokyo. 

The factual story of what happened is best told by 

Brigadier General James Doolittle, who, with 79 other

American fliers, made the raid on Japan in Army B-25's. 

Doolittle's story is an aviator's account based upon what he actually saw from the air. Only the Japs know the extent of the damage and the rest of the world will find out only when Allied armies march into Tokyo. Many Japs are believed to have been killed.

Said Doolittle: 

"The success of the raid . . . exceeded our most optimistic expectations. Each plane was assigned specific targets and the bombardiers carried out their duties with remarkable precision . . . ... "Apparently there was no advance warning of the raid as we experienced little hostile action. Not more than 30 active pursuit planes were observed . . . and these were completely ineffective. Several, we know, were shot down... "We approached our objectives just over the housetops, but bombed at 1500 feet. "The target of one plane was a portion of the navy yard south of Tokyo . . ... "One salvo made a direct hit on a new cruiser or battleship under construction. They left it in flames. "After releasing our bombs we dived again to the treetops. to avoid antiaircraft fire. ... "Along the coastline we observed several squadrons of cruisers, destroyers and battleships. "One of our rear gunners reported as we were 25 miles out at sea that he saw columns of smoke rising thousands of feet in the air. (This indicated American bombs had hit their mark on the Jap navy.) "One of our bombardiers strewed incendiary bombs along a quarter of a mile of aircraft factory in Nagoya. (Besides Tokyo, the cities of Nagoya, 1,249,100 population about 150 miles southeast of Tokyo; Kobe, 1,006,100, about 250 miles southeast of Tokyo, and Yokohama, 866,- 200, seaport just a few miles from Tokyo, were bombed. 

All are on Honschiu, the main island of Japan.) "Another bombardier illuminated an oil tank farm "One of our party observed a ball game in progress. 

The players and spectators did not start their run for cover until just as the field passed out of sight . . . "It appeared to us practically every bomb reached the target for which it was intended. 

"We would like to have tarried and watched the later developments of fire and explosion, but, even so, we were fortunate to receive a fairly detailed report from the excited Japanese radio broadcasts. 16

"It took them several hours to calm down from deception and accusation."

Doolittle, when deco rated by the President on May 19 for his feat, said that he had express orders not to bomb the imperial pal ace of Emperor Hiro hito. 

Fires that burned for two days were known to have been started in the flimsily constructed residential sector around military objectives.

Roosevelt:

"They must have taken off from Shangri-La." (Shangri-La is a fictitious mountain Utopia described by novelist James Hilton in one of his books.) 

Months afterward this "Shangri-La" was revealed as the aircraft carrier Hornet. (Later sunk off the Solomons, Oct. 26, 1942.)

Carrying the 16 Mitchell medium bombers, the ship set out for a designated spot 400 miles from Japan, but 800 miles from Tokyo ran into a Jap warship. The Hornet sank the Nippon craft, but fearful the ship had radioed a warning, the attack had to be moved up 10 hours. 

Doolittle led the take-off at 8:20 a.m. and after the bombings the planes flew to China; they hit a storm, and ran out of gas before they reached landing fields in China. 

Doolittle bailed out of his plane at 9:20 p.m. It and 14 other craft crashed after the aviators parachuted out.

Eight Americans landed in Jap-occupied China and were captured; one plane carrying five landed in Russia and the men were interned; one flier was killed and two are missing. The other 64 worked their way back through China to America.

The fliers used a 20 cent bomb-sight so the Japs could not learn the secrets of the prized U.S. Norden bomb-sight. 

Japan shocked the civilized world by executing some of the eight captured American fliers. The others were treated as criminals, not as prisoners of war. Announcing this, President Roosevelt said: 

"This recourse to frightfulness by our enemies is barbarous. The efforts of the Japanese warlords to intimidate us will fail utterly. It will make the American people more determined than ever to blot out the shameless militarism of Japan."

The executions were justified, replied the Tokyo radio, saying: "Rest assured, Americans, that every flier that comes here has a strictly one-way ticket to hell." 

Lost in the day's big news was the fact that Admiral Jean Darlan was retained in power in France by Premier Laval and was placed in charge of all the French armed forces. (Darlan, fated to become big news in November in 

North Africa when he went over to the Allies, was yet a dutiful stooge of Hitler.) 

Sunday, April 19, 1942

Shells were bursting on Corregidor . . . and one hit the 

American flag staff, sending Old Glory to the ground. 

Three American soldiers risked their lives in the Jap barrage to put it up again.

A submarine again shelled the Dutch Island of Curacao off the coast of Venezuela. Oil refineries were the target. 

Monday, April 20, 1942

All resistance to the Japs ceased on Cebu, Philippine island. Slowly but surely the Japs were mopping up on small Allied-held islands of the Pacific. 

Tuesday, April 21, 1942

The jittery Japs had another air raid scare and blacked out Tokyo. It was a false alarm.

The execution of 85 French hostages in a Nazi reprisal for a British commando raid ushered in the regime of 

Premier Laval. 

Wednesday, April 22, 1942

With their faces blackened so they could not be seen at night, British commandos made hit and run raids at Boulougne, on the French coast; St. Nazaire, France, where naval locks were wrecked; at Vaagasoe, Norway; Spitzenberg, Norway, and the Norse islands of Lofoten. 

Thursday, April 23, 1942

Russia interned an American bombing crew that was forced down in Soviet territory after the raid on Tokyo, Moscow announced. 

Friday, April 24, 1942 

British bombers flew 950 miles to raid Rostock, German

Saturday, April 25, 1942 

French General Henri Giraud, 63, slid down a rope in the dark of night to escape from a German prison camp.

He had been captured in May, 1940, while riding in a tank. 

At that time he was commander of the northern army of France. But German prison camps were not for Gen. Giraud. He proved that by escaping from one in the first 

World War and now he had duplicated his feat. The Nazis offered 100,000 marks reward for his capture as he fled to Switzerland, disguised as a peasant.

British bombers struck at French towns and paid with the loss of 15 planes; six waves of RAF fliers in 11 hours, however, did great damage to German operated plants and military centers. 

Sunday, April 26, 1942 "Germany will go on hammering the Bolshevist giant until he is smashed to pieces". . . declared Hitler in addressing the Reichstag.

In a strange, ranting speech, der Fuehrer demanded "life and death power" over all Germans. He said all offensives in the Soviet must be postponed until spring and revealed the Red Army and bitter cold brought the Nazis "near catastrophy" in the winter's war. 

He threatened retaliation for Allied air raids . . . but as he spoke British bombers smashed at Skoda, vast Nazi arms works in subject Czechoslovakia.

The British bombed Trondheim, Nazi base in Norway where a fleet was concentrated to prey upon the American supply line to Russia. 

Wednesday, April 29, 1942

Hitler offered peace feelers through Switzerland. He proposed Germany rule Europe and the Dutch, French and Belgian colonies; the British empire was to be left intact, the United States to control Latin America, with trade relations to be set up between the three imperial empires. 

In captured Singapore, Emperor Hirohito's birthday was celebrated in a great public ceremony. Thirty-six traitors to the Allies were publicly decorated. Schools were reopened and ordered to teach only the Japanese language. 

Thursday, April 30, 1942

The Japs swarmed over the Burma road, as the last Allied defense line, manned mainly by the Chinese collapsed.

Corregidor fortress, tiny rock island that controls Manila

Seized by the Nipponese were 11,574 prisoners of war.

Friday, May 8, 1942

America won its first great naval battle of the war-in the Coral Sea. 

A huge Jap invasion fleet, headed for Australia, was 18

smashed and fled under heavy plane bombardment after a five day fight.

American planes, patrolling the sun-bathed Coral Sea spotted the invader Monday, May 3, 1000 miles off Australia.

U.S. dive bombers attacked first, hitting several warships. U.S. destroyers, meanwhile, laid a smoke screen around the Jap ships. Through this they dashed in and out to attack. It was hit and run sea warfare. 

The Americans withdrew and while the invaders licked their wounds, struck again, this time with a heavy aerial assault. 

The final crushing blow was dealt the Japs this day, when U.S. planes and ships attacked together. The American fliers had to battle off hundreds of Nipponese planes, but they broke through the protecting sky umbrella to rain explosives on the Oriental warships and Japan's fleet turned and ran. 

Losses inflicted upon the Japanese were: sunk: an aircraft carrier, two cruisers, two destroyers, four gunboats and a supply ship; believed sunk: an aircraft carrier; damaged: two cruisers, a seaplane tender, two other small war craft.

The Japs lashed back by sinking the U.S. destroyer 

Sims and the supply ship Neosho by dive bombing. 

The knockout blow was delivered to the Japs on Friday. Pouring everything they had into the battle, the 

Americans set the aircraft carrier Shokaku afire, planted bombs on three cruisers, three destroyers and a number of smaller vessels.

With almost half their ships afire . . . the Japs turned and fled. 

But the victory was not without its price. The aircraft carrier Lexington, old but one of the largest in the world, was hit by Jap torpedo planes. It exploded and sank several hours later.

The score sheet of the battle read: Japan, 15 ships sunk, including one aircraft carrier, three heavy cruisers, one light cruiser, and two destroyers; 20 ships damaged; 100 planes destroyed. 

The United States: three ships lost, the Lexington, Sims and Neosho; no report on aircraft losses.

Sunday, May 10, 1942

In a speech of amazingly accurate prophecy, Prime Minister Churchill told the Nazis the Americans and British would bomb German cities into rubble and emerge vic torious. Said Churchill: 

... "We know our Hun . . . We are on his tracks . . . "The British and presently the American bombing offensive against Germany will be one of the principal features of this year's World War. (The Americans first joined in the bombing of Europe on July 4.) "Now while the German armies will be bleeding and burning up their strength against a 2000-mile Russian front and when the news of the casualties by the hundreds of thousands will be streaming back into the German Reich, now is the time to bring home to the German people the wickedness of their rulers by destroying under their very eyes the factories and seaports on which their war efforts depend . . . 

"We have only to endure and persevere to conquer there can be but one end." ...

It was a "message of good cheer," that he brought to the civilized world and showed clearly how the cause of the Allies had advanced in two years. For it was only 24 months since the Germans had stormed Dunkerque, just 20 miles from England . . . and the English were hearing 

Churchill tell them bluntly, that he promised nothing but "blood, sweat and tears."

The last 5000 British soldiers, remnants of a once proud colonial army, fled Burma, crossing the frontier into China.

Sunday, May 31, 1942

ASSOCIATED PRESS MAP 

Great Britain made a mass raid with 1130 planes on Cologne, a Rhineland city of 800,000. The raids were so elaborately timed that planes arrived over the city on a schedule of every six seconds to drop their cargo of bombs some of them "block-busters" weighing two tons. A total of 3000 tons of explosives rained on the city. 

The burning of Cologne could be seen for hundreds of miles and it was not until six days later when the fires and smoke had disappeared that RAF planes were able to take photographs of the devastated city.

The pictures showed that eight square miles of the city had been razed.

German papers said every block in the city had been hit. 

The dead were reported at 20,000, with 54,000 wounded. 

Hitler asserted 366 were killed and 419 wounded.

The raid cost Britain 44 planes. Monday, June 1, 1942 In what they called a "reprisal raid for the terror attack of the British on Cologne," the Germans bombed the cathedral town of Canterbury. Damage was slight. 

Swarms of British bombers raided France and Belgium in daylight.

The American air arm struck at the Jap bases of Lae, New Guinea, and Tulagi, Solomon islands, starting fires that could be seen for 80 miles. 

Tuesday, June 2, 1942 

Britain made another mass "terror" raid, hurling 1036 aircraft over Essen, home of the Krupp arms works; Duisberg and Oberhausen in west Germany. The Nazis admitted 

100 were killed and 250 wounded.

At 6 a.m. a wave of Nipponese planes raided Dutch Harbor, U.S. navy and air base in the Aleutian islands, Unalaska.

At noon, a heavier assault force returned and set warehouses afire with incendiary bombs. 

The Aleutian islands drop southwest like a curved blade off the tip of Alaska and have been called "an American dagger pointed at the heart of Japan." They are 2345 miles from San Francisco. As they lie 2835 miles from Tokyo, it was evident the attack was made from aircraft carriers. 

Late in the afternoon, the patrolling Flying Fortresses, whose beat was off Midway Island in the Pacific, spotted several Jap warships. They dumped their bombs and returned to their base.

Little did they know that these bombs were the first punches thrown in one of the greatest of U.S. naval battles. 

Thursday, June 4, 1942

A squadron of naval flying boats took off on dawn patrol from Midway, a sand and coral island about one and onehalf miles square that juts out of the Pacific. 

Daily they made their patrol, because Midway was just what the name sounds like-midway, or about 2600 miles from both Tokyo and San Francisco and the California coast and for this reason an important U.S. outpost. 

Dawn patrol was a chore most days - but not this onefor a few hundred miles to the east these aviators sighted a fleet of Jap warships bearing down on Midway. 

There were over 50 ships in two columns, spearheaded by battleships and heavy cruisers. Protectively flanking these were destroyers and strung out behind were first troop transports, then aircraft carriers.

The Americans radioed the alarm, dove in to attack. 

At this very minute Jap Zero planes were attacking Midway Island and the American fleet stationed there. 

But this time it was the Japs who were surprised because the Americans were ready.

Admiral King in Washington had been apprised by his reconnaisance planes that Japan was massing a great fleet in the Pacific; so had U.S. Air Chief Arnold. 

When the Japs had made a feint at Dutch Harbor they had not let the U.S. sea and air power be pulled out of position they had waited at Midway and . . . 

In one of the fiercest and most decisive battles of the war, they drove off the Jap fleet. 

It was a new kind of ocean warfare; the big ships of the two fleets never approached within two days' sailing of each other. They fought with their air arms. 

Land based airplanes rose from Midway's tiny field to help; long range bombers raced 1300 miles from Hawaii.

The U.S. planes singled out the Jap aircraft carriers, far in the rear of the fleet. 

Navy Squadron No. 8 dived low over, suicidally low, over the Jap cruisers.

Direct hits set one carrier afire and the airmen saw its crew leaping overboard.

Another carrier was struck from torpedoes from a plane and when it reached huge fuel supplies a Jap cruiser pumped shells into it to keep it from exploding and damaging other ships in the fleet. 21

Four Jap carriers were sunk and their short-range planes left with no place to land. Many were shot down, others plunged into the sea when their fuel ran out. 

The price? Nineteen of the 20 pilots of bombing squadron No. 8 never returned. None of the gunners were ever seen again.

But it was a great American victory after three days fighting the Jap fleet fled in disorderly retreat. 

The U.S. Navy announced 13 to 15 Nipponese ships were sunk, including the four carriers, three battleships, and five cruisers; three destroyers and four troop transports were damaged. In addition the enemy lost 275 planes. 

Dramatic highpoint of the conflict was the U.S. Carrier Yorktown.

Shortly after the battle began, some 20 planes appeared above her. They started to land on the American ship, when some sharp-eyed sailor noted they were Japanese. 

The Yorktown batteries began to fire, but on down came the Japs. Many crashed burning into the sea. One just barely cleared her decks.

But finally several planes scored. 

The Yorktown crew fought the fierce fires, tried to limp to port, but a Jap sub on June 6 sent two torpedoes into the crippled craft. The crew of 300 slid down ropes over the sides of the stricken ship-most of them to safetyand she went down. 

Besides the Yorktown and destroyer Hammann sunk, 307 Americans were killed in the battle. 

Admiral Chester Nimitz, U.S. naval commander, who in his June 4 communique punned: "We are about Midway in our objectives," later reported: "The enemy is withdrawing. Contact was lost Saturday night." (That was June 6.)

Destination of the Japanese apparently was Hawaii and a land attack was indicated by the large number of troop transports.

In an attempt to save their face, the Japanese announced the defeated fleet was "only a diversional" one to allow them to land troops on the Aleutian Islands. (The U.S. 

Navy said on June 7 that small landings were made on the barren Aleutians.)

Sunday, June 21, 1942

Erwin Rommel fought for the Kaiser in World War I as a private. ...

Erwin Rommel fought for Hitler in World War II. .. 

as a general.

The gap between the two positions is seldom ever bridged in the caste-ridden armies of Europe. Rommel did it in two ways: first by becoming a Nazi through and through; secondly by mastering the strategy and operation of tank warfare.

These two virtues suited Hitler to a T. He could play off Rommel's successes against the oligarchy of generals in the German reichswehr . . . point out how a Hitlerite commoner had bettered the achievements of the old school of Junker commanders.

Today the legend of the hard-faced, hard-driving Rommel reached its apex. 

It was a bubble soon to be deflated by the Allies, but today was his day... for the Axis armies under his command seized Tobruk in a stunningly swift blow. 

Britons had told the world confidently that the fortified garrison would hold out for a long time, but at dawn Nazi 

armored cars breached the outer defense lines and dashed into the city.

They sped through the town, pulled big guns up and attacked ships in the Tobruk harbor. Their shelling sunk, set afire or drove away in pandemonium the Allied vessels there.

Now no escape, even by sea, was available for the Tobruk garrison.

It surrendered 33,000 men, including several generals, and great stores of supplies.

The impressive victory set off bitter recriminations in the British Parliament. Targets were Churchill and Lieut. 

Gen. Neil M. Ritchie, who headed the British armies in 23

Egypt. (One member even charged a story bandied about

Hitler capitalized upon all this- he named Rommel a field marshal and let him speak to the Reich by radio from his African headquarters. 

Rommel said simply: "Germany will win the war."

The day was also one that residents of Seaside, Oregon, a hamlet on the Pacific coast, will never forget.

Explosions shook them from their beds at 11:30 p.m. and they ran out to see a Jap sub shelling the coastline. 

Nine times it fired and nine times the shells fell harmlessly into a sandy waste. 

No one knows what the intended object of the attacker was. The sub had travelled 5000 miles from Japan . . . to fail in its mission.

Saturday, June 27, 1942

Eight Nazi spies, landed upon the U.S. coast by submarine, had been captured, J. Edgar Hoover, Federal Bureau of Investigation head, announced.

It was one of the most fantastic tales of the war that 

Hoover told. 

John C. Cullen, 21, a coast guardsman, was walking his lonely six-mile beat on July 13 at Amagansett Beach,

Long Island. Suddenly he came upon three men, one in civilian clothes standing on the beach, two others in bathing suits, knee-deep in water. 

Coast Guardsman Cullen had no gun. The men took a threatening attitude to the young guardsman. Then they talked things over, privately, and came back. 

They offered Cullen $300 to forget the incident. He agreed, took the bribe and hurried back to the coast guard station to report the matter. 

Other coast guardsmen were rushed to the scene and found German cigarets on the beach. (Just how they captured the four who landed there was not made public. 

Neither were details on the capture of the other four saboteurs who landed four days later in the lonely sand dunes on Ponta Verda Beach near Jacksonville, Fla. It is known that some of the saboteurs got as far as Chicago before they were caught.)

All eight had spent a great part of their lives in the United States and spoke English fluently. They had returned to Germany, where they were trained at a special sabotage school near Berlin. The subs bringing them here had taken off from France.

They had a minutely worked out plan for two years' sabotage, including orders to blow up waterways, railroads and war industries and even to set off bombs in public places to create panics. 

They had $149,748.61, explosives, maps, social security and draft registration cards.

The cry for a second front was rising around the world . . . and the 

Allies answered with the great Dieppe commando raid.

Striking just before the break of day, the Allies poured wave after wave of land troops Canadians, Americans,

Free French, British and Poles - onto the continent of Europe. 

They struck at one of the heaviest Nazi fortified towns on the French invasion coast - Dieppe, just 100 miles from Paris.

Land barges dropped the commandos ashore at six points. Tanks, armored cars and small artillery were landed to back up the troops. Overhead boomed a barrage from assembled warships in the English channel and higher yet was an umbrella of thousands of protecting Allied planes, as far as the eye could see. 28

It was a raid along the lines of those of Civil War General Sherman-to burn, to smash and to destroy as deep as possible into enemy territory. 

But, more important, said military observers, it was a test case for invasion to find just what was the price of a second front.

And the answer was tragic for the Allies. 

The Canadians bore the brunt of the attack, and of the 5000 men she sent ashore, there were 3350 casualties (65 per cent). Of these, 170 were killed, 633 wounded and 2547 missing.

Chance had smiled on Hitler to account for this in part. 

A small Nazi tanker was chugging up the English channel, protected by a half dozen gun boats.

Suddenly a large warship loomed before the tiny convoy. 

The gunboats opened fire and the flash of their guns and the green and red lights of their tracer bullets outlined a fleet of 300 to 400 ships before them.

By accident, they had stumbled into the Dieppe raiders. 

The Nazi boats radioed a warning to their shore batteries and the Allies timing was thrown off. They had to attack at once, several hours before they planned.

As the landing barges bearing the first shock troops skimmed to the shore they were met by murderous fire of machine guns, rifles, and mortars.

It was a hail of death. Many brave Canadians never reached the shore alive. One of the six landing parties was driven off. Another was able to establish a beachhead for only a few hours.

When the main body landed in the daylight, big guns, that these early landing parties were supposed to have silenced, poured shells into their ranks. 

But the landings were made literally in Nazi cannon's mouth and the Allied troops fought for nine hours on French soil, drove far back from the coast to capture and destroy big gun emplacements, kill 400 German soldiers, and wreck harbor facilities.

It was a battle high in drama-because the Nazis were forced to tell the peoples of France and the conquered countries of it.

It was into one of the wierdest battles of all the history of warfare that he was sending his men. Fighting raged atop the Owen Stanley mountains, 6000 feet above the sea, in the great equatorial rain forest that girdles the globe.

The Allied soldier first faced a killing, near-perpendicular climb. For each soldier there had to be a native bearer of supplies. No white man could be a bearer for another, because his larger body required so much food that by the time he had reached the battle ground in the sky he had consumed a poundage of food equal to what he carried up. 

Once atop the mountains, the Allies fought in a land of perpetual mist and rain, where the sun scarcely ever shown.

Great tree ferns, bright with moisture, and towering slate crags were backdrops for battle. The soldiers' hands on clammy cold rifles would grow damp with chill. 

The Americans had to fight for breath in the thin mountain air and also fight a foe camouflaged with turquoise paint, wearing bullet proof vests, carrying ancient swords (knives kill quietly while gunfire gives away the position of the assailant) and using small caliber rifles that allowed them to carry twice the ammunition of an Allied soldier. 

It was Indian style fighting, each man stalking his foe, lying in wait for him. The old Wild West adage prevailed, that there were two kinds of men - the quick and the dead. Seldom did enemies spot each other until they were within about 20 yards and the man who was first on the draw survived.

Saturday, October 24, 1942

Gen. Rommel, the "desert fox," was to turn and run

Sunday, October 25, 1942

Landing 11,000 more troops, the Japs unleashed their full-scale attack upon Guadalcanal. 34

The Marines were encamped on the five-mile square plateau between the high mountain and the sand dune beach of the island.

In this meadow of chest-high grass was fought one of the most sanguinary contests of the south Pacific. 

Again it was a battleground bizarre. 

Bright-plumed red, yellow and green tropical birds overhead chattered as loudly as the machine guns of the contending armies. Fruit bats, so large they are called "flying foxes," swooped between the battle lines.

The Jap attack varied. First it was in mass. Then in pairs or singly. Some Japs carried machine guns strapped to their back and bent over to form human gun carriages for their comrades.

Both armies wore green uniforms so they could not be picked out against the verdant background. 

Jap warships shelled marine positions from the sea and U.S. planes fought to keep off attacking Nipponese divebombers. 

But the slam-bang tactics of the marines won out. . . . Five tank attacks were beaten off and in three days the Japs had retreated and the beach was so cluttered with the invaders' dead that the marines reported "disposal of bodies has become a problem."

On the sands across the globe another battle raged this day - in 

Egypt the British tanks slit the defense lines of Gen. Rommel's Axis African army. 

American-built General Grant tanks were spearheading the attack that took 1450 Axis prisoners, destroyed much mechanized equipment.

Wednesday, November 4, 1942

The Axis armies were routed in Egypt in the most stunning defeat for Hitler thus far in the war.

These men were hustling to Libya, with their rear guard fighting off advance units of the British.

Sunday, November 8, 1942

The city of Algiers, capital of Algeria, surrendered to the Yanks after 17 hours of fighting. American shock troops had seized two of its main airports and the U.S. planes were bombing the city from its own runways when the French garrison capitulated. Street fighting had raged for several hours in the seaport of 252,321 persons.

Rabat, capital of French Morocco, collapsed before the greatest of all American invasions. 

For 24 hours, a never-ending stream of Americans had spurted ashore- and more were to come.

Monday, November 9, 1942 

Gen. Henri Giraud, French hero of two wars, broadcast an appeal for "all true Frenchmen" to help the invasion.

They had entered the agreed French port at night, on Nov. 7 but had almost been caught when Giraud fell into the sea boarding the sub. He was fished out and arrived safely in Africa.

Giraud also announced he was forming a French army to fight in Africa with the Allies.

Free French followers of Gen. Charles De Gaulle, rioted in Casablanca and were fighting in the city for the Allies.

Hitler scrapped his armistice and seized all France. (When France fell June 22, 1940, under an “armistice,”

Thursday, November 12, 1942

All French West Africa surrendered to the Americans after a four-day gun and political blitzkrieg.

The Yanks had won a territory as vast as the continental United States, the nearly 2,800,000 square miles of 

Algiers, French Morocco and French West Africa which supported 30 million people. 

It was a land the like of which few American soldiers had ever seen before. For, as part of a new AEF, the doughboy marched across the burning sands of Oriental mystery.

The nations he was occupying was 10 per cent old world, with its French and Italian residents, and 90 per cent Older World, with its turbaned Moslems who looked as if they had stepped out of Arabian Nights and its millions of Negroes who still practice voodoo.

Algiers, French since 1881, had once been ruled by ancient Rome and parts of roadways built by the Caesars still were visible through the shifting sands where the caissons were now rolling along. 

Though they skirt the far-reaching Sahara desert, the North African territories are not hot, parched lands. Their nights are cool, rainfall is frequent and they raise great quantities of food.

This was one reason Hitler fought to take them back, and landed air-borne reinforcement troops at Bizerte in 

Tunisia, goal of the next Allied thrust.

President Roosevelt had asked the Bey of Tunis for passage through his country, but before he could reply, Hitler had filled it so full of Axis soldiers that a battle was inevitable.

Sergt. LeMaster was on K. P. and in a jeep jounced along in the dead of night at the tail of an armored column, his mind on what's cookin' for the next day's breakfast.

At a turn in the road, LeMaster and his command — the jeep, one water wagon and two trucks of groceries - went the wrong way. Pretty soon one of the men remarked the trucks all around them looked strange. "They're British," replied LeMaster curtly.

A little piece further on one of the men leaned out and asked directions. He was answered in a foreign tongue. 

But Sergt. LeMasters wasn't to be fooled. "Them French," he muttered, "I can't understand a thing they say". . . and on he went.

About dawn one of the men got out and read a sign. 

It said: Tunis, three miles. The K. P. convoy was 10 miles behind the German lines. "We turned around and went for home as fast as we could," said LeMasters. "The Germans took a couple of shots at us, but we outran 'em."

BACKGROUND AND SUMMARY AND SUMMARY OF WORLD OF WORLD WAR II 

At daybreak Sept. 1, 1939, the German army of Adolf

Hitler swept across the Polish frontier and set off a world war that within three bloody years engulfed every major nation on the globe.

Hitler's excuse for invasion was a demand that Germany be given the Polish corridor, created in 1919 by the Treaty of Versailles to provide Poland an outlet to the Baltic Sea.

This thin strip of land cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany. 

The Nazis issued an ultimatum to Poland but invaded without a declaration of war before the Poles had time to reply. Britain demanded the German army withdraw and offered concessions to the Reich if she complied. The 

English request went unanswered.

On Sept. 3, a British statesman stained with the stigma of appeasement, stepped to a microphone and declared war on Germany.

He was Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain who had stood by in the name of peace when Germany seized Austria in March, 1938, and had been humiliated by Hitler at the Munich conference that allowed Hitler to take over 

Czechoslovakia in March, 1939.

France declared war a few hours later. 

Blitzkrieg, the lightning war of Germany, hit Poland.

The Poles fought bravely, but were no match for the 

Nazis. Russia joined the Germans Sept. 16 and invaded 

Poland from the east. The German and Russian armies met Sept. 27 at Brest-Litovsk. Warsaw fell the same day. 

Poland had been crushed in four weeks.

The blitzkrieg was over, the sitzkrieg was here. 

After World War I France built the Maginot line on the German border. Parallel to it the Nazis constructed the 

Siegfried line. Behind these big gun-studded fortifications the rival armies sat for months. 

On April 9, 1940, Germany seized two neutral countries,

Denmark and Norway. Denmark could not resist. Britain rushed aid to Norway but the Nazis won quickly with the help of a well-organized Norwegian fifth column. 

Battle Of France 

Then on May 10, the Germans struck at the Maginot line, first invading neutral Belgium and Holland. Dutch opposition was wiped out in five days. 

The Belgians fought back, but May 15 the Nazis broke through the Maginot line at Sedan. Bombing civilians and forcing them to flee and jam roads, the Allies needed to move supplies over, the Nazis slashed deep into Belgium and France. Belgian King Leopold surrendered May 28.

The Nazi tanks cut toward the English channel and trapped one great Allied army at Dunkerque. Winston 

Churchill, who May 10 had replaced Neville Chamberlain as British prime minister, rounded up all the small boats in England and with plane and warship protection they evacuated 320,000 British and French from Dunkerque. 

But the fate of France was sealed.

On June 10, Italy, which had previously stayed out of the war, jumped in for the kill by declaring war on France. 

On June 14 Paris was abandoned to the Nazis and June 16, 83-year-old Marshal Henri Petain, who had been called in as premier, surrendered France. An armistice was signed 

June 22 at Compiegne on Hitler's terms. Germany occupied the northern part of France, left the south with a semblance of sovereignty under Petain at Vichy.

Battle of Britain 

Britain stood alone, Germany attacked Aug. 8 by air.

Hundreds of bombers daily roared over London and other big cities, blasting buildings to ruins and killing thousands.

British airmen vastly outnumbered fought back and by late September had so decimated the German attackers that the great raids stopped. 

Mussolini made the next move, Oct. 28, attacking Greece.

The Greeks fought back valiantly and stopped the Italians until April 6, 1941, when Hitler blitzkrieged the Balkans.

The Slavs and Greeks resisted but Yugoslavia was conquered by April 18 and Greece fell April 23. Air-borne Nazi troops then grabbed Crete.

The British, meantime, had cleaned out the Italian African empire except Libya, where a see-saw battle raged. 

Hitler, intoxicated with his quick successes, then turned on Russia. His armies attacked June 22, 1941, sweeping across the Polish border. The Nazis hoped to subdue Russia before winter set in, but they ran into fierce Red resistance. At a heavy cost in lives, the Germans fought their way to the gates of Leningrad and Moscow, both over 1000 miles from Berlin, and were laying siege to these cities when Pearl Harbor was attacked.

A United States Divided 

Before the Japanese attack upon Hawaii, the United

States was a nation divided on the war. 

A strong isolationist faction was led by Senator Burton 

Wheeler, Montana Democrat; Charles A. Lindbergh, the first man to fly the Atlantic Ocean, and Senator Gerald

Nye, North Dakota Republican. 

This group argued the war was an age-old European quarrel into which the U.S. should not enter. They held that the oceans protected America from attack, pointing out that Hitler had not been able to cross the 20 miles of water of the English channel and attack Britain by land. 

They had a large following particularly in the Middle West.

Lindbergh had visited Germany and was impressed by the Nazi military might. In 1941 he was cheered by 20,000 in the Hollywood bowl when he urged a negotiated peace with Germany.

Another national leadership group opposed this view, believing the Axis was pushing the whole world into war and America was sure to be enmeshed. President Roosevelt was the main spokesman for this view, although the group was bipartisan and included such Republicans as Wendell 

Willkie. 

On Jan. 3, 1943, Roosevelt told the people that if the 

Axis was not defeated "the Americas would be living at the point of a gun" and added that we "must become the arsenal of democracy." 

Factories were being converted from peace goods to armament making and Congress had passed a bill authorizing the President to "sell for cash" or to "lease, lend or otherwise dispose of" defense articles of a value of $1,300,- 000,000 with approval of the army or navy. This was allout aid to Britain, Russia, China, and other nations fighting the Axis. 

Roosevelt and Churchill met in the Atlantic Ocean on a warship in August, 1941, and signed the Atlantic Charter, a basis for a postwar world based on the Four Freedoms: 

1. Freedom of speech; 2. Freedom of worship; 3. Freedom from want; 4. Freedom from fear.

Japan had been waging war on China since 1937 and we had been selling her oil and scrap metal to use in making munitions. On Aug. 1 the United States stopped these exports to Nippon by presidential order.

The first peacetime draft in U.S. history was voted Sept. 

14, 1940. Registration Day was Oct. 16 and by Jan. 1, 1941, men between the ages of 21 and 36 were being selected for army service.

A number of our ships were being sunk in the Atlantic, presumably by German submarines. We had provided 

Britain with destroyers and Russia and China with munitions through lend-lease, were turning out arms at a great pace, but the isolationist sentiment was still strong and Congress had refused to vote funds to fortify the 

Philippines, Wake and Guam Island. Then ... 

Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. 

Overnight the country was united. 

America was at war, wholeheartedly to change civilian life vastly.

Arrival Of Rationing 

a war that was

The Americans are a roving race, and here they felt war's pinch first. Auto manufacture was stopped and as 

Japan cut off our rubber supplies, tires were first rationed 48

according to need, then gasoline. 

U-boat sinking throttled supplies of oil to the east and they felt gas restrictions the most. A cross-country pipeline was built from Texas to Norris City, Ill., to help remedy this. Fuel oil also was rationed and many easterners shivered through winter.

The draft was stepped up and the army passed seven million, headed for 10 million. Married men without children were being drafted with fathers next. 

Taxes rose steeply and on March 15, 1943, Americans paid their greatest income taxes in history. 

Food production became a problem as it was necessary to supply our armed forces and our Allies to a great extent besides home consumption. After many farm youths were drafted, exemption was voted farm workers to help meet the food crisis. Coffee and sugar, mostly imported, were rationed first. Then most canned goods, meat and butter were rationed. Aside from food, shoes were rationed. 

Women At War Plants 

Industry quickly changed to war goods manufacture. 

Making of mechanical refrigerators, radios, washing machines and like products were stopped. 

Many new plants sprung up and industry shifted mainly to the Mid-West. In these defense centers, new problems arose resulting in government orders in many cities for a 48-hour week and the freezing of rents, salaries and wages. 

These latter were part of an effort to prevent inflation and sky-high prices.

The draft thinned the ranks of workmen and thousands of women took their places at factory benches. 

In spite of these changes, management and labor were pitching in and, in most cases, setting new production goals each month.

America's War In Review 

On the fighting fronts, the first 15 months of America at war might best be summed up with the phrase: From 

Pearl Harbor To Tunisia. 

The early days of the war brought the Allies bitter reverses but as their power grew in men, planes, ships and munitions they turned the tide and the Americans, British and Russians seized the offensive from the Axis. 

IN THE PACIFIC, the Japs had their own way for three months. The U.S. fleet had been crippled at Pearl Harbor and the British fleet was busy in the Atlantic. The Allied 

East Indian fleet was wiped out Feb. 27 at the battle of the 

Java Sea. Distances were short for the Japs, long for the Allies to send men and planes into battle. 

By May 5, when Corregidor surrendered, the Japs had taken Hong Kong, Singapore, Malaya, the Philippines, Thailand, the Dutch East Indies, and small islands too many to mention. In the next few weeks they swept on to conquer Burma, seize the Solomons and northern New Guinea.

But America was giving evidence she was soon to strike back. First indication was the April air raid on Tokyo.

On land the Yanks hit the Japs first Aug. 7, when they invaded Guadalcanal in the Solomons. In six months they drove out the Japanese and smashed a great portion of the Jap fleet in a series of ocean battles off Guadalcanal. 

The Yanks and Australians, meanwhile, had repelled the Japs in southern New Guinea, driven them off the lower portion of the island.

Japan's most ambitious attempt on the United States had resulted in a disasterous sea defeat for her at Midway Island in June. A great invasion force headed apparently for Hawaii had been routed by U.S. air and sea forces. 

Most of the U.S. victories, except Guadalcanal, were purely defensive. It was like a football team being driven back over the vast Pacific, then rallying on their goal line, which would be Australia, and taking over the ball for their own offensive.

IN AFRICA, the United States made her first big invasion of the war Nov. 7. They seized Algeria, French North 

Afrca and Morocco, an area almost as big as the continental United States. The British had routed Rommel, driven him almost 1500 miles from Egypt into Tunisia. 

In bitter fighting in Tunisia the Yanks and English were slowly pushing the Axis in the sea. Their victory would clear North Africa of the Axis.

IN RUSSIA, the Red army in its stupendous winter offensive had freed Stalingrad and Leningrad from siege. 

The Reds killed and captured thousands of Germans, a serious blow to the power of the Nazi army. But the Reds had advanced only a few hundred miles and new German troops poured into the Soviet were again seizing the offensive. 

IN THE AIR, Germany was taking a terrific battering.

Her great cities were bombed daily and Hitler reportedly had appealed to the Pope in an effort to get an air truce. 

His planes were tied up on the Russian and African fronts and he could not hit back at England. 

UNDERSEA the Nazis were scoring their greatest successes. U-boats sunk 498 Allied ships according to Associated Press during the first 10 months of 1942 and the toll mounted higher weekly. The Allies were working out a secret plan to end the sub menace, Churchill and Roosevelt announced in March. 49
