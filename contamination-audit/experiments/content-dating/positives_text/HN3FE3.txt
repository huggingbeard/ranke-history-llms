JOHN S. KYSER 

John Schnebly Kyser was born on September 18, 1900, in El Paso, Illinois. After attending the public elementary and high schools in 

El Paso, he entered the University of Michigan, where he received his A.B. degree. HISTORY OF THE COLLEGE 

Northwestern State College of Louisiana is located in the historic and picturesque little city of Natchitoches, the oldest settlement in the 

Louisiana Purchase. 

The site of the present campus was the property of Judge Henry Adams Bullard until purchased by the Ladies of the Sacred Heart, who established a convent school here in 1856. Three white stuccoed columns, once supporting the east gable of the imposing Bullard Mansion, remain as symbols of this early chapter of the college history. 

The convent closed its doors in 1875. 

In 1884 the property was purchased by the town and parish of Natchitoches, and was donated to the State of Louisiana for the establishment of the Louisiana State Normal School. From 1885 until 1918, the Normal offered two years of work, for the training of teachers; in 

1918 the school became Louisiana State Normal College, offering four years of work culminating in an academic degree. 

The functions and curricula of the College having greatly expanded by 1944, the State Normal College took on a new name-Northwestern State College of Louisiana-which was more indicative of its new and broader role. In 1947-48 the administration of the College was reorganized and three undergraduate Schools were created-Applied Arts and Sciences, Arts and Sciences, and Education. To this was added, in 1949-50, the undergraduate School of Nursing. 

In 1954 authorization was granted the College to offer graduate instruction for teachers. The first graduate courses were offered during the 1955 spring semester. 

Northwestern State College of Louisiana holds membership in the

Association of American Colleges, American Association of Colleges for 

Teacher Education, American Council on Education, Association of 

Teacher Education Institutions, and the National Commission on Accrediting. The College is fully accredited by the Southern Association of Colleges and Secondary Schools; and the teacher-training program is accredited by the National Council for Accreditation of Teacher 

Education.

TEACHER EDUCATION 

AT 

NORTHWESTERN 

By 

JOHN A. JONES Dean, School of Education 

The inauguration of a graduate program of education at Northwestern State College marks the attainment of another significant milestone in the seventy years of its illustrious history. This happy occasion seems an appropriate time for reviewing briefly some of the contributions to teacher education which this great institution has made since it opened its doors to receive its first class in 1885. 

Although there were strong pleas as far back as 1870 for the establishment of a state institution for the training of teachers, it was not until 1884 that the Louisiana Legislature finally took a necessary step, by passing Act 51 establishing the Louisiana State Normal School. 

Reference is made to this specific Act because of the tremendous effect that its passage has had upon teacher education. The Act contained eight sections, two of which seem worth noting: Section Five designated the president as ex-officio member of the Board of Administrators, gave a brief general listing of the course of studies, which required two sessions for completion, and, most important, empowered the institution to grant diplomas that were to rank as teaching certificates, valid for three years. Section Seven required that the insignificant sum of $6,000 be appropriated annually from the general fund to maintain the school and to conduct three teachers' institutes. 

Despite the fact that there was great interest in the establishment of a state institution for the education of teachers, the very inauspicious beginning of the Normal School could hardly presage the notable achievements and significant contributions that it has made in the ensuing years. Perhaps this progress can best be accounted for by the fact that its administrators, faculties, and other friends have ever been alert to meet new demands or needs brought about by the passage of time. Such is the case in the inauguration of the graduate program. 

New demands have stemmed largely from an ever increasing population and from the amount and kind of education provided in 10

our elementary and secondary schools, these demands resulting in changes in certification regulations and teacher-training programs. The brief review which follows reflects these relationships. 

The first faculty (session of 1885-86) consisted of a president and two other teachers. Sixty students-forty-five women and fifteen menwere enrolled. The curriculum consisted of the following: 

Reviews and methods of teaching arithmetic, geography, grammar, composition, reading, phonetics, history, drawing, spelling, penmanship, calisthenics and vocal music.

Literature, natural science, hygiene, physiology and civil government. 

Philosophy of education, history of education, science and art of teaching psychology, ethics, discipline and school management are the studies prescribed. To this is added daily exercises in teaching in the "Practice School."

By 1890 the program had been expanded to include four years of work, much of it on the secondary school level. The addition of some subjects, such as government, zoology and botany, and general history, broadened the offerings considerably. 

Very few changes were made in the offerings during the next several years, but steady growth was shown in other ways. At the end of the first twenty years of its existence (1905-06) it had a faculty of twenty-four, a graduating class of eighty-eight, an enrollment of six hundred, and an annual appropriation of $29,000. In those twenty years, the institution had graduated eight hundred twenty-four students, and had spent for all purposes less than a half-million dollars. 

Most of the eight hundred twenty-four graduates went out into the public schools of the state to teach. The comparatively excellent quality of work which they did had two significant results: it convinced the people of the state that teachers did need special college training, and it changed their attitude towards the public schools from one of indifference or disapproval to one of approval and support. The first created a demand for better-educated teachers, and the second a demand for more of them.

These demands were reflected in actions at the state level. State control of teacher certification was begun in 1906 with the passage of Act 55, which made provisions for a "State Teachers Certificate"; and such control was completed by passage of Act 214 of 1912, which, among other things, gave the State Board of Education the right to set up college training as a part of the requirements for certification. This 11

right the Board began to exercise immediately; and the Normal School began expanding its offerings to meet these requirements and to satisfy the demand for more teachers. 

Time does not permit a detailed account of all the changes that were made. Suffice it to say that by 1918 a four-year professional curriculum leading to the bachelor's degree was established; and the 

Louisiana State Normal School became the Louisiana State Normal College, a notable milestone in its development, similar to the one we are celebrating today.

By 1924, the year in which the State Board placed all certification on the basis of college education, the Normal College was offering a program of studies which provided for electives in both the two-year and the four-year curricula as follows: 

Two-Year Curricula For Primary Teachers For Intermediate Teachers For Teachers of Grammar Grades Four-Year Curricula English-Social Science English-Foreign Language Mathematics-Physical Science Mathematics-Natural Science Home Economics Rural Education Music and Art 

It can readily be seen that such a program offered a relatively wide range of specialization, which has been one of the outstanding characteristics of teacher education for the last thirty years. However, it should not be inferred that this was the first of such offerings. The initial step in specialized training was designed for those who planned to teach in rural schools, a logical beginning for a state that was primarily rural. As far back as 1914, a special curriculum was offered for grade teachers, another for high school teachers, and another for supervisors and specialists.

This modest beginning in specialized offerings was made because of a recognized need. The expansion of such offerings to more than twenty has been the result of both recognized need and certification requirements. In this day and age, specialization in teacher education is no different from specialization in other professions. It is made necessary by the tremendous expansion of knowledge in the various fields. 

As a matter of fact, since the beginning of World War II, the colleges and universities of the country have not been able to fill the demands made upon them for specially educated people. This situation was 12

partly responsible for the Normal College entering other fields of training and in having its name changed to Northwestern State College of Louisiana. However, it is still primarily an institution for the education of teachers, as the launching of a graduate program in teacher education here today so well indicates. 

Further understanding of the work and growth of the institution may be had by giving some thought to the numbers of its graduates through the years. The two-year program which was set up at the opening of the institution was continued until the end of 1937. During the fifty-three years of its existence, this program was completed by 7,196 students, all of whom were eligible for certificates to teach. 

Two or three interesting sidelights concerning this program seem worth noting. The number of graduates in any one year exceeded the one-hundred mark in 1902, the two-hundred mark in 1912, the three-hundred mark in 1923, and the four-hundred mark only one year later, 1924, the year in which the greatest number of students, 442, completed the program. Perhaps one of the most revealing facts concerning this program is that of the 2,771 students who completed it during the last thirteen years of its existence, only 100 were men. 

The four-year program, leading to the bachelor's degree, had its first graduating class in 1921, a class of four. The number of graduates in any one year in this program passed the one-hundred mark in 1927, the two hundred in 1938, and the three hundred in 1939. The Second 

World War has doubtless been the cause of the four-hundred mark not having been passed. Perhaps there is no better evidence of the terrible impact and awful effect of war upon a society than that which is evidenced in the reduction of graduates in teacher education at this institution, during each of two world wars. In 1917, the number of graduates was 244. Three years later it was 163. In 1940, the number of graduates was 362. Six years later the number was 100. 

Despite these and other set-backs, this great institution has made steady progress and outstanding accomplishments in teacher education. Since its founding seventy years ago, 7,196 students completed its two-year program and 5,302 have completed its four-year program and received the bachelor's degree. There can be no adequate measurement of the great contributions made by this grand total of 12,498 qualified teachers toward the education of children and youth-not only of Louisiana but of this nation and of the world. And here the word "world" is used advisedly, for many of the graduates are at this moment practicing their profession in the "four corners of the world," from Alaska to Brazil and Germany to Japan. Contemplation of the tremendous achievements of the past certainly should fill the friends of this great College with the brightest of hopes for its future. 13

THE WIDENING ROAD By T. M. STINNETT 

Executive Secretary

National Commission on Teacher Education and Professional Standards 

AHEAD The most fascinating thing in all the world to the spirit of man is the lure of the unknown-of the widening road ahead. 

The present is but a fleeting moment linking the past to the future. 

As such, its perfect use is to serve as a platform of pause, to evaluate what has gone before, that we may prove adequate to that which is to come.

In front of the Archives Building on Constitution Avenue, in Washington, there is a famous statue. In cold marble, a woman is reading a book. On the base is the inscription, taken from Shakespeare's The 

Tempest, "What is past is prologue."

I suspect that neither in higher education nor in the complexity of life ahead have we "seen anything yet." And it seems to me that such a convocation as this provides an appropriate platform-a platform of pause-on which to ask ourselves: "Where have we been? 

Where are we now? Where are we going?”

And may I digress at this point to observe how fortunate is the college which can celebrate simultaneously the inauguration of a new president and a program of graduate study. I surmise there must be some causal relationship between the two. If my surmise is correct, I should like to congratulate the College upon the vision and sensitive leadership of its new administration; for, as I shall later demonstrate, the field of graduate education for truly competent teachers is "already white unto the harvest, but the laborers are all too few." 

And, at this point, I shall digress to admonish, out of my somewhat extensive and intimate knowledge of college and university faculties, gently but with an appropriate realism, both President Kyser and Dean Allbritten that the husband speaking in his own household does not necessarily reflect the views of the management. 15

L 

The Teaching Profession Comes of Age 

What of the past? What of the fleeting moment we call the present? 

And what do they presage for the future?

It would appear evident that you have already assayed your history; and judged that, in the logical evolution of that history and the purposes for which the College was founded, the compulsion for providing advanced study for teachers is a pressing one. I would certainly agree. But I would ask you to examine these questions: 

Graduate study for what? For whom? And to what ends? 

Why do I say that I agree? Because, as I understand it, this

College was founded-and continues as such-to serve primarily as an instrument of the Commonwealth in meeting its needs for teachers. 

The need for advanced study for teachers is a compelling one from many standpoints. I shall mention two: (1) The rapidly rising levels of education of the general population demand yet greater levels of competence for those who would presume to teach the people. (2) The rapidly increasing complexity of our society demands new emphases.

May I undertake to illustrate the first by describing what is happening within the ranks of teachers to achieve that level of competence for themselves that will earn the designation of a profession. 

The teaching profession in the United States has come of age. 

The road has been a long one, hard, beset with many and diverse obstacles. But we have arrived at our majority. 

What is meant by "coming of age"? According to the dictionary, it is that time of life at which certain powers, certain qualifications, capacities, arise or vest. 

There is a beautiful analogy in Alexis Carrel's Man the Unknown which is apropos at this point. In paraphrase, he says that time and life may be likened to a moving stream and a running figure. In the early morning the stream is a swiftly moving current beside which a boy is running. The exuberant, steadily swelling energy of the boy drives him always ahead of the current. Impatiently he motions to it to come on, to catch up. This is youth, when days seem to move upon leaden feet. Eventually, the running boy begins to tire, and the 16

inexorable pace of the current begins to shorten the gap and finally to come abreast. This is mid-day and mid-life, when days and time move evenly but resolutely forward. As the day wears on, the running figure becomes more and more weary, his pace decelerates. Try as best he may, he cannot keep up with the moving stream. It forges ahead, steadily widening the gap as the afternoon wears on. This is old age, when days fly away upon winged feet, when we would cup our hands in an effort to hold back the stream and time, but we cannot. 

Finally, twilight approaches, and the now scarcely moving figure eventually comes to a stop, lies down to rest, and the stream moves on unmindful of the sleeping figure. That is death.

I take it that one might appropriately depict the coming of age of an institution or an association as the running boy in mid-morning, still with abundant energy, still able to out-distance the moving current of life, that period when reluctant days and events have to be dragged abreast by sheer force of will and of energy. 

This analogy I apply to the teaching profession and to teacher education. The teaching profession has arrived at that point where certain powers, qualifications, capacities are vested in it. And this implies not full stature, nor arrival at that plateau where physical and mental powers have reached full expression, which is the inevitable prelude to the descent into senility. It implies, rather, that these powers are beginning to flow with full vigor, in readiness for the climb toward maturity. And, in this figure of speech, the teaching profession and teacher education, as is true of the individual life at the point of majority, are on the threshold of their greatest accomplishments. The maturing potentials of their services to society yet await fullest expression. The great powers which predecessors have placed upon them yet in their youth bespeak magnificent vistas for the future. 

These assertions are made with deliberation. I am quite aware, painfully so, of the current and often scathing criticisms which have been and are being directed at both the teaching profession and teacher education. Some of the criticisms are deserved; some are not. But the important thing is that out of the criticisms will come progress; for paradoxical as it may seem, in a free society controversy is the wellspring of progress.

You must recall that Machiavelli somewhere in his Discourses said, "Had Rome removed the causes of her tumults, she must have surely interfered with the causes of her progress." 

In the face of these criticisms, I am proud to assert that, in my opinion, the best teaching in the history of the world is going on in 17

the United States of America today. I am proud to assert that, in my opinion, the quality of that teaching shall grow in consonance with the new imperatives of the widening road ahead. 

Now, what evidence is there that teaching has come of age in the United States?

Teachers are taking charge of their own profession, as has been the case of every other profession worthy of the name, and are insisting upon the enforcement of standards which will guarantee a professionally competent teacher in every classroom. When the NCTEPS was organized in 1946, only fifteen states thought enough of elementary teachers to require degree preparation for them. Today, thirty states are enforcing the degree requirement, and six additional states have set legal deadlines for this requirement to become operative. 

And it is high time. The meager preparation which satisfied the needs of teachers of a generation ago no longer will suffice for the complex age in which we live, or the much more complex one we are coming to. Yet, the traditional lag has persisted with irritating doggedness. Still, in one state, the post-secondary preparation required for barbers is thirty weeks; for elementary teachers, eight weeks. 

In another state, at least this was true a year or so ago, two years of post-secondary work is required for morticians; for elementary teachers, eight weeks. In other words, it must be assumed that the people of that state deemed that more education was needed for those who would bury a man than for those who would teach him. 

In the November issue of Harper's Magazine appeared an article under the title "Can Our Teachers Read and Write." This was written by an English instructor in Kansas State College, and pictured with great effectiveness the ignorance of syntax of a group of elementary teachers enrolled in the author's extension class. This article really doomed all teachers, because its title and its content implied, or left the impression with the reader, that no teachers can read or write. 

The author neglected to point out that the teachers in his class were people who had obtained emergency permits only, issued in response to the demands of local school boards at a time when the state was still issuing regular certificates to elementary teachers upon completion of a mere eight weeks of college work. 

So the teaching profession is determined to raise its standards. It has set as goals-and is rapidly moving toward those goals, despite the most serious teacher shortage in our history-minimum preparation 18

of the Bachelor's degree for any beginning teacher at whatever level; for full professional qualifications for teachers at any level, five years of college preparation; and for administrative, supervisory, and special school service positions, six college years of preparation. 

And, you know, every day my commission has to fight someone in education-not the public-who will say, "This is silly; you cannot raise standards during a period of teacher shortage." Ladies and gentlemen, that is the only way the American people will ever solve the teacher shortage, to raise these standards to a point where you make teaching a profession that attracts bright young people to go into it. Whenever you start lowering your standards, you drive them out. 

And we have clear evidence of that. 

What has been the effect of these increasing standards? Have the dire predictions of antagonists to the movement been fulfilled, such as making the shortage worse? They have not. On the contrary, every evidence is a good one. The number of degreed elementary teachers prepared annually has trebled since 1948.

The preparation of employed elementary teachers has steadily risen since 1949 when only 45% had degrees. This year 68% have degrees. The median preparation of all public school teachers now is about 4.5 college years; of high school teachers, 5 years; and of elementary teachers, 4.3 years. 

These increasing standards are having profound influence upon the number and calibre of young people who are choosing to prepare for teaching. The increase in teacher education enrollments this year outstripped all other areas of senior college enrollments. And reports that reach us are categoric in their assertion that the calibre of teacher education students today far outstrips that of the past. 

That is enough comment on progress of this movement for me to make application to the occasion we are celebrating here. 

II. 

Graduate Education for What? 

Thus, if this college intends to keep abreast of the moving current of life, if it intends to serve adequately the purpose for which it was founded, it would appear to me that it could not have long avoided or long delayed the decision to establish a graduate school. 19

The pressure of salary schedules, the complexity of life, the advancing educational level of the general population are going to make this move necessary all over the United States. 

But the establishment, per se, of a graduate school is not necessarily significant. What kind of a graduate school? For what purposes? 

For what clientele? These are the significant questions-and the answers to them which this college is prepared to reflect in its graduate program-these are the significant things. 

First, there is the matter of clientele. I presume that your clientele is predominantly teachers. Should you attempt to operate graduate programs for every area of teaching-administrators, supervisors, special school service personnel, and classroom teachers? I would not presume to suggest an answer for you. But I will presume to say that, in my judgment, the pressing need for graduate work in education across the country is graduate education for classroom teachers, designed specifically to increase their competence-first as persons and second as practitioners.

I will presume to say that, in my judgment, too many newly inaugurated graduate programs set out first of all to serve administrators -because, I suppose, this field is judged to have more status. But I suspect that such institutions also have an eye on the ready placement of their teacher graduates. I have an idea that a good college, like this one, could carve out a niche for itself, could earn a unique national reputation were it to boldly take the position that its graduate school would serve, primarily, if not exclusively, the needs of classroom teachers, and then proceed to build a real program to this end. 

You see, the first of our graduate programs of education were developed for school superintendents and administrators; and that has been perpetuated, so a third grade teacher who takes a graduate degree gets 90% of it in school administration. And the public is beginning to ask itself-is this sort of thing enhancing the ability of that third grade teacher to do the job that she is hired to do, or is it just an idea of accumulating some credits to go up another step on the salary schedule? We have had all too much of that. 

You see, we are still, I fear, in that period when such graduate programs as do exist for the classroom teacher are but pale imitations or even near duplicates of ones really designed for administrators. 

Moreover, it is my belief that the field of graduate study for administrators is seriously over-supplied. Of this, I am quite certain: the great critical need for graduate study in education is that for the practicing teacher; and that need is now at a critical point, and it will become 20

more so in the next decade. Because you see, in the next decade in this country we will have to add 600,000 new teaching positions at the elementary, secondary, and college levels. Where we have 38,000,000 youngsters in elementary and secondary schools and in colleges, we will have 50,000,000 in the next decade-1965. Every seven and onefourth seconds another child is born in the United States; we are the fastest growing nation on earth. By 1975, our population will number 

220,000,000. We ought to throw our hats in the air and shout "Glory,

Hallelujah" because it means we are still a live, vigorous, growing young nation with our future still ahead of us. But many of us are scared to death-"How are we going to build the classrooms and hire the teachers?" Why, this is the greatest thing that ever happened in the history of the world-certainly for Americal The need for teachers: 600,000 new positions; 150,000 new teachers a year; teacher education enrollments to increase from 10 - 20% each year in each institution.

And the need is for graduate programs for teachers which are intended to broaden their perspectives and their ability to function as liberally educated persons, to broaden and deepen the mastering of their respective teaching fields, as well as to enhance their ability to function as professional persons.

The National Commission on Teacher Education and Professional 

Standards believes that there are already too many advanced programs in education, especially for teachers, which are but diluted extensions of undergraduate preparation in professional courses. Let me give two illustrations.

In one state the Legislative Council published a survey of higher education. This Council found that in one teachers college, one professor, while carrying a full-time teaching load, had supervised the writing of one hundred ninety-one (or some approximately horrible number) of master's theses in one calendar year. 

The second illustration: In a conversation with the Dean of the 

Graduate School of one of America's largest state universities, he told me that, ten years or so ago, his university employed some fifteen persons in a certain academic field to teach in the summer school. 

Now, he said, we employ only one, because teachers have found that they can take a master's degree in education and avoid any of the relatively difficult graduate content in their teaching fields. 

Now I would be the first to defend the appropriateness of graduate study in education for teachers. But can we defend graduate programs 21

for teachers which consist wholly of such work? I think not; and I am glad to see the trend in such programs toward restricting concentration in professional phases of the advanced degree to nine to twelve hours, specifying the remainder to be completed in the teaching field of the candidate.

I am told that this college was the first, and for many years the exclusive, single-purpose teacher education institution in Louisiana. 

It had such a monopoly of, enjoyed such a high reputation for, the preparation of teachers in this State that the annual convention of the Louisiana Education Association was really the annual meeting of the Alumni Association of the Louisiana Normal College. 

The days of monopoly in teacher education are gone everywhere. 

But the preferred status of a given institution can be maintained, or can be recaptured, only by earning a reputation for scholarly programs in a chosen field. I do not assert, I ask: "Does not this prospect hold great promise for the Northwestern State College of Louisiana to occupy again a unique position in higher education in this State?" 

At the beginning of this century, your position was unique because you were meeting the needs of teachers, at a lower level of preparation. May not the second half of the century see this college in relatively the same position because of the quality of its program at an advanced level?

If so, you must ask some searching questions about the quality of your staff, both academic and professional, as well as the quality of the content of your graduate program. Out of the growing participation of the profession in the improvement of teacher education across the country, one thing is crystal clear to me: the day is rapidly drawing to a close when faculty members in education can continue to offer, year after year, repetitious and routinized courses, unconcerned and unrelated to what is taking place in the schools. If there be such left on college campuses-and I am told there are some-the road ahead for them, until they can be retired, is going to be an uncomfortable, if not an unhappy, one.

And, certainly, the day is past when the failure in public school teaching can find nice, comfortable, untroubled spots on teacher education faculties. What I am saying at this point is that the quality of your graduate school, or any graduate school, is a function first of the kind of people who make up its staff, in all teaching fields; and second, of the kind of program of studies which the school offers for its peculiar clientele. 22

I am certain, too, that graduate education for teachers in the future must be for a purpose. The time is drawing to a close when an accumulation of credits in discrete courses, gathered over a period of several years until a prescribed number satisfies the requirements for an advanced degree, will suffice. 

IIL Implications for General Education 

First, let me say that the demands of some vocal critics for a return to the so-called eternal verities in education, an exclusive obsession with the classical disciplines of the past, is not substantiated by the realities of the future. The kind of general education for the road ahead is one which shall differ radically with the needs of the past. 

The plain truth is that the kind of general (or liberal) education our people must have for the future is, I am afraid, not the kind of general education most of the critics of education—at least most of those I read or hear-are talking about.

I cannot escape the impression that they are talking of the restrictive, classical curriculum of old Europe. Such a concept, if they are talking of this as exclusive, as designed for the few, does not and cannot fit into the American system of education for the single reason that ours is an entirely different kind of a society, with a different history, with an economic and social mobility unknown to Europe. 

Here, we began one hundred years ago to adapt the European system to our conditions. And two basic and different viewpoints-I may call them principles-emerged from this adaptation. These two viewpoints or principles are (1) the equality of opportunity for all youth, and (2) the equality of respect for all honest fields of endeavor. 

These, I am convinced, the American people will not abandon. 

They will not abandon them because, despite admitted weaknesses at many points, of the demonstrated powers and promise of such a system. "Education for all is education for none." This is nonsense. 

But the critics are certainly on sound ground in advocating the need for a new emphasis upon general education. Only, I repeat, the new emphases which we must have are not the ones they are talking about. 23

Almost over-night, conditions have so changed that the indicated needs in this area, even ten years ago, have changed radically. Today's conditions are as different from ten years ago as were those of our grandparents as compared to Europe. In their times, they could struggle through their problems alone. Now new power realities and new power potentials, plus the airplane, have caused the shrivelled fringes of our frontiers to press in upon us. And, whether we like it or not, we are one of two nations which will determine the future of the world. Moreover, whether we like it or not, we must find the means of living with and getting along with all kinds of people. Whether we like it or not, despite all the tough talk of some of our political leaders, we are faced with two realistic, harsh but irrevocable, alternatives: co-existence or co-extinction. 

If the latest bomb were to be dropped on Manhattan, in ten seconds an area one and one-half miles in diameter would reach a temperature of 2700° F. In seventy-two seconds within that tremendous circle there is no human life. Brick and stone and mortar exist no more. There is not a great mass of debris and rubble-nothing is there; everything has become radioactive dust. Now, we can pound our breasts and talk tough; but we are just silly if we do not realize that the alternative in the world today is to co-exist or co-extinct. It is just as simple as that. 

What are the implications for the kind of general education for the future? They are, as I see them, as follows: 

1. Every American must be equipped to understand realistically other peoples. Stevenson, in his "Godkin Lectures" at Harvard, classified the world's peoples into three categories—the democrats, the communists, the uncommitted. We must learn to know the uncommitted and to work with them. "The Revolution of Rising Expectations" demands this. 

2. Every American must have a deep understanding of the history of his own country-American history-but American history that gives a new emphasis to our relationships with the rest of the world, not America alone in splendid isolation. 

3. Every American will have need in the future for a functional use of two or three foreign languages. You see, the airplane has done for us what has been true of Europeans for centuries. It has placed us in daily and hourly contact with peoples who speak another language. 

It is just a matter of a few years when no place on earth will be more than an hour or so removed from any of us, wherever we are. 24

4. The future will demand, also, a specialized education for many built upon sophistication with the problems and the conditions of other peoples. This implies wisdom and human understanding and relationships. We Americans must train the specialists who will work and live in other lands. 

5. There is the need for American institutions to prepare future teachers among the youth of the uncommitted nations. There is also the need for American teacher education institutions to assist in the development of teacher education programs in these countries. 

IV. 

Education for Freedom 

There is another basic need to be served by graduate programs for teachers. How shall I state it?

I have spoken of the necessity of the broadly, liberally educated teacher. That to which I now refer is in that area, if properly conceived. 

But we have come to use these phrases so glibly, these phrases about liberal education, that they have come to connote any liberal arts education; and just any liberal arts education will not suffice for teachers, nor will it necessarily suffice for the thing I am now discussing. 

I am talking about education for freedom. We have had, beginning with Plato if not earlier, many definitions of the aims of liberal education. At the risk of over-simplification, I choose the one from which the term "liberal education" arises. This purpose is to give men an irrevocable commitment to the values of freedom and the ability to use it fruitfully.

It takes no very wise observer to discern past shortcomings of education in America in this respect. 

The period from 1950-1955-and let us hope that the period abruptly ends here has been a strange interlude in our national life; from my viewpoint, an inexplicable one.

I cannot somehow escape the notion that historians a generation hence may identify this period as America's Gethsemane, surpassing in some respects the Civil War period. How else can one explain this baffling, this incomprehensible period, except that Americans, by and large, have shown an alarming misunderstanding of the meaning and uses of freedom. Which is to say that somewhere, somehow, education in America has failed, to a degree, in its one really basic purpose. 25

Take the meaning of the value of freedom, for example. The English have one concept; apparently we have another. The English apply the concept with dogged, unremitting determination to the inviolability of the individual, his person, his home, his right under law. Here we seem to confuse the term with the rights of the group as a whole, or of separate groups as a whole, or the right to vote, feeling that a finer individual can be trampled underfoot and that all individuals are not thereby harmed.

This concept has produced, in the last five years, some weirdthey would be ludicrous were their implications not so tragic-situations. 

We have seen the McCarthy technique ride rampant over individual integrity, violate ancient safeguards and make those safeguards a thing of public scorn. We have seen distinguished Americans denied the right to speak because they had become "controversial" figures; that is, because they had expounded viewpoints at variance with the tide of public opinion, or what has been whipped up as public opinion as a political gimmick. We have seen widespread use of wire tapping, the dismissal of respected public employees-including teachersbecause they expressed an opinion which some Little Caesar in a community did not like. And to top it all, we have read a report from a Congressional investigating committee which says that foundations and associational groups like the NEA should be chary of new ideas and should sponsor only ideas which the people condone or like. 

And who protested these assults upon our freedom? A few brave, far-seeing individuals who paid a rather heavy price. 

Time and time again I have seen in the public prints such comments as "what freedom is violated? The very fact that you are reading about these cases is evidence that freedom of the press is not being denied."

Time and again I have seen statements in the public press: "Name one person whose rights have been violated or hurt by McCarthyism." 

The answer is, of course, that the rights of all of us have been violated. 

The whole of the American people have been hurt. 

As Mark Etheridge has said, "You might as well talk about surrendering a little of your virtue as surrendering a little of your freedom." 

But the most disturbing thing about this whole matter is the way the individual American is giving way to the mass mind, to mob psychology. 26

You will recall Socrates' exchanges with Adementes in Plato's 

Republic: 

Said Socrates: "Do you hold the popular belief that certain young men are demoralized by the private instruction of some sophist?" 

Socrates' answer: "Whenever the populace crowds together at any public gathering, in the Assembly, the law courts, the theater, the camps, and sits there clamoring approval or disapproval, both alike excessive, of whatever is being said or done, booing or clapping until the rocks ring and the whole place redoubles the noise of cheer, applause, and outcries. In such a scene, what do you suppose will be the young man's state of mind? What sort of private instruction will have given him the strength to hold out against the force of such a torrent or will save him from being swept away down the stream until he accepts all their notions of right and wrong, does as they do, and comes to be just as they are?"

Again, it takes no very wise observer to point out that here is the self-same problem 2400 years after these words were spoken-2400 years in which liberal education ought to have made this sort of thing impossible.

Even today, our standards are mass-mind standards; we are still creatures of our environment. And the craven way our educational processes have given way to mass media is the most disgraceful surrender of all. The moral influence of the McGuffey readers has given way to TV advertising.

The problem of education here is not a technical problem. If it were, then we could be concerned only with means, with gimmicks, not ends. I am happy to report to you that, in my opinion, we are now devoting an ever-increasing emphasis to the proper ends of educationto education for freedom; and this, I take it, is the greatest of all the trends which an individual, however thoughtful, may now discern. V.

Conclusion 

Two additional things I would yet say here. 

I would hope that this college, or any college inaugurating a graduate school for teachers or for any other segment of our people, would stand apart for a moment from our gadget-filled, technologicallydominated, cash-register society, and spend that moment in contemplating the proposition that, in the final analysis, the proper end of education is man himself; not man adjusted to the machines which he has created as implied as that is in our culture; not man as the potential creator of still greater constellations of power-but man as man, the creature of and made in the image of God; thinking, feeling, sensitive, 27

virtuous man, whose greatest glory is yet to be true to himself and to the divinity within him. 

Someone has said, "Man has learned to fly through the air like birds; he has learned to swim under the sea like fish; but he has not, as yet, learned to walk the earth uprightly as man."

A poignant, penetrating comment by William Faulkner illustrates the point and the task of education. In a letter to the New York Times he says:

This is about the Italian airliner which undershot the runway and crashed at Idlewilde after failing three times to hold the instrument glide-path which would have brought it down to the runway. It is written on the idea that the instruments or instrument — altimeter-cumdrift-indicator - failed or had failed, was already out of order or incorrect. It is written in grief-not just for the sorrow of the bereaved ones of those who died in the crash, and for the airline, but for the pilot himself, who, along with his unaware passengers, was victim of that mystical, unquestioning, almost religious awe and veneration in which our culture has trained us to hold gadgets any gadget, if it is only complex enough and cryptic enough and costs enough.

I imagine that even after the first failure to hold the glidepath, certainly after the second one, his instinct-the seat of his pants, call it what you will-after that many hours in the air, told him that something was wrong. And his seniority as a four-engine over-water captain probably told him where the trouble was. But he dared not accept that knowledge and act on it. He dared not flout and affront, even with his own life, too, at stake, our cultural postulate of the infallibility of machines, instruments, gadgets. I grieve for him, for that moment's victims. We all had better grieve for all people beneath a culture which holds any mechanism superior to any man simply because the one, being mechanical, is infallible, while the other, being nothing but man, is not just subject to failure but doomed to it.

I should like to embellish that point with a reference to George Santayana. In his heyday at Harvard, he used to say that the essence of religion is the tender concern of God that nothing be lost. I submit to you that universal education, as we know it in America, is a tender concern of society that no talent be lost, no matter how small, however meager, however great.

The remaining thing I want to say here is that I hope this, or any college inaugurating a graduate school for teachers, will stand apart 28

for a moment from the bitter clamor and vitriolic name-calling among groups in higher education, separate the wheat from the chaff, evaluate motives, and seek to identify the widening road ahead in terms of what kind of teachers we must have in the years ahead to teach not the select alone, not the potential poet and philosophers, not the writers and the critics alone, not the potential technologists and scientists alone, but to teach forty million Americans of every race and creed and tongue and economic and social and cultural background. In short, I would hope that this college, or any college inaugurating a graduate school for teachers, would stand aside for a moment and take a real look at the American teacher's job as it is—not what some would have it be, not what some believe it to be, but what it is, and seek to equip teachers for this job. 

An analysis published in 1887 is still germane today: 

Teachers are employed for purposes vastly great. They must teach the science of health with all the learning but without the pay of the doctor; they must inculcate the principles of morality with all the impressive sincerity but without the sectarianism of the minister; they must be altogether more patient and discrete than God Almighty himself, for He was "wroth" when He punished the wicked, whereas, if a teacher punishes in anger, he is guilty of an assault and battery; they must invent schemes to invert human nature, and make every good thing and thought enticing and every bad thing and thought abominably disgusting . . . They must tenderly moderate the zeal of the too ambitious, and inspire the dullest blockhead with a manly thirst for fame and knowledge; the incorrigibly uncouth and vicious, they must endow with the tastes, the instincts, and the manners of the refined and virtuous. And in short, they must turn all from the thousand paths that lead to indolence, ignorance, and folly; and prepare them to find infallibly all the ways of pleasantness and all the paths of peace. 

And for this new graduate school-for this college-for the teachers of America, I would suggest a credo for the difficult years they are coming to. It is found in Tawney's, The Rise of Religion and Capitalism: 

There is a magic mirror in which each organ and order of society, as the consciousness of its character and destiny dawns upon it, looks for a moment before the dust of conflict or the glamour of success obscures its vision. In that glass it sees its own lineament reflected with ravishing allurements; for what it sees is not what it is, but what in the eyes of mankind and its own heart it would be. 29

SOME TRENDS 

IN 

GRADUATE EDUCATION 

By 

ROGER P. MCCUTCHEON Director, Program for the Preparation of College Teachers 

Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee

The opening of a new graduate program is a fitting occasion on which to review the place which the graduate school has come to occupy, to remind ourselves of some of the basic requirements now needed for a good graduate program, and to speculate a little about what might be the future of our graduate schools. It will help to understand the present conditions in graduate work if we remind ourselves that within the last century our college population has increased thirty-five times as fast as our total population. Much of this increase has come within the last fifty years. Fifty years ago about one person out of twenty-five of the college-age population went to college, perhaps 4%. Today our educational statisticians tell us that at least one out of every four of our college-age population is in college, a shift from 4% to 25%. Approximately one-half of the people who graduate from high school enter college. The college graduate of fifty years ago was a marked person in the community; employment came much more easily to him because there were so few of him. College graduates are not yet a dime a dozen in the labor market, but they are by no means in short supply. Today it is the holder of a graduate school degree who enjoys the preferential position of the bachelor's degree holder of half a century ago. Just how long the holder of a graduate degree will continue to enjoy this preferential position will depend on the graduate schools themselves. If these go into the assembly-line type of graduate education, in which the number of their product is more important than the quality, one does not have to be the seventh son of a seventh son to prophesy the result. 

How shall a graduate school protect its standards of excellence? 

The question, surely a basic one, may be asked in another way: What does it take to make a good graduate school? For one thing, those in charge must know the difference between education on the college level and education in a graduate school. One of the greatest of our 31

American familiar philosophers, or to use an endearing term, one of our crackerbox philosophers, a generation ago described a college president welcoming a newly arrived Freshman. As Mr. Dooley put it, the president takes the Freshman into his private office, sits him down in an armchair, offers him a five-cent seegar, and says to him, "Now, my fine young gentleman, just what courses do you want our learned professors to study for ye this year?" Perhaps it is not unfair to say that education on the college level still consists to a large degree in having the learned professors study the courses for the student. Some of this spoon-feeding still occurs in the graduate school, but at its best the graduate school insists that the graduate student learn how to ask his own questions and how to find his own answers. 

In the nature of things, this is what we have a right to expect a good graduate school to do. Not many colleges can do this. Most of them must content themselves with transmitting to their students a part of our inherited culture. The good graduate school can justify its existence only if it is in some way pushing back the frontiers of our knowledge. And this it will do through its research programs. 

It follows that the professor in a graduate school must have somewhat different qualities from those which make a good professor in a college. Adequate knowledge of his subject should be the possession of every college professor. A man who holds a professorship in a graduate school should not only have this knowledge but through his own research he should be increasing this knowledge. A faculty trained in research, interested in research, and active in research: this is the ideal. Without a nucleus of research workers in its faculty, a graduate school can offer to its students nothing better than a fifth year of undergraduate work.

We are all agreed, I think, that it would be extremely difficult to turn even a very good high school into a good college by using only the high school faculty. They are not specialists. To turn the average college into a good graduate school without renovating or replacing the faculty is certainly a problem. Perhaps in every college faculty there are several who can regain their interest in research if all the factors are favorable. These factors will include an administration which understands the importance of research. This administrative sympathy must be expressed practically and not merely piously. A faculty member who is giving a graduate course will need to have his teaching load lightened. If he is directing several students in their research, his load should be adjusted. He must have the tools and the funds with which to do his research. In the field I know best these tools would include adequate library facilities, and more often than 32

not these facilities must be files of learned journals, which cannot be improvised overnight. There must be travel funds available to send our man to the meetings of his learned societies. An occasional summer grant should be available to him, so that he can spend two or three months in a really great library, or work in some of the superbly equipped laboratories in our greatest universities. 

Our professor who is to teach graduate courses will do a better job if other members in his department or in allied departments are also well qualified and eager. There should be more than one graduate instructor per department. I have heard experienced graduate deans set six as the minimum number they would require-six active and research-minded professors-before a department could offer the Ph.D. degree. Perhaps by this time you are understanding why in the graduate standards of the Southern Association of Colleges and Secondary Schools, institutions are warned that a good graduate program will also be a very expensive program, and that the costs will be progressively cumulative.

But let us assume that a sufficient nucleus of a graduate faculty is ready. Let us assume also that the necessary tools for research are available. Neither the faculty nor the research equipment can be improvised. By the time the students arrive it will be too late. And this leads us to a discussion of the third ingredient in this ideal graduate school-the student body.

The officer charged with the responsibility of admitting students to a graduate school will need to apply a different set of standards from those used for admission to the college. Many of our state colleges are required by law to admit all graduates of state high schools. No such convenient rule of thumb exists for admission to graduate schools. 

The college from which the applicant comes, the concentration of courses in the major field, and the grades made on the undergraduate courses all have to be checked. Most graduate schools prefer to encourage only students who have made a "B" average in undergraduate courses in their major field. This, too, is a rule which cannot be applied blindly. For a student with a "C" average from a quality college may actually be better prepared for graduate study than a student with an "A" record from some diploma mill. Those of us who are old in the game have come to know what colleges can be counted on to provide good graduate material. We have learned also, by the hard way, how very few professors can be relied on to write an honest and revealing recommendation.

Some graduate schools are impertinent enough to inquire into the age of the applicants. Perhaps age is not too serious a factor, if the 33

applicant is interested only in graduate work leading to a master's degree. The investment of faculty time and graduate school resources in applicants for the doctor's degree must be watched with particular care. As between two applicants with substantially equivalent academic records, the one being twenty-five years old, the other being forty-five years old, a university may quite well be warranted in selecting the younger person, since the long and expensive training will pay more dividends in the younger man. At precisely what age hardening of the intellectual arteries starts is, of course, a question the answer to which will have many individual differences. In regard to the Ph.D. degree, however, it can safely be said that applicants over forty years old should be discouraged.

It is known that most graduate schools require a "B" average out of their student body. I have sometimes thought that this "B" average requirement has had a softening effect upon the faculties of most of our graduate schools, since very few graduate students in my experience ever receive anything less than a "B". This has made me wonder whether or not the mere fact of gaining admission into a graduate school transforms a "C" student into a "B" student. This is a somewhat cynical observation; but there is merit in it if it will alert admissions officers to some of the occupational hazards of a graduate school. 

What size shall our graduate school student body be? Obviously, we shall do our graduate students no lasting favor by expanding the enrollment of the graduate school to the point where sound graduate education becomes impossible. You can always improvise a Freshman 

English teaching staff for all the students the registrar will admit. At least, so many college presidents operate on this belief that to challenge it seriously would be heresy. But you cannot improvise a graduate faculty, and the ratio of faculty to students in a good graduate school must never threaten the ideal relationship between graduate teacher and graduate student. I have visited graduate schools in which a single professor was "directing" as many as fifty students. This is a dishonest intellectual procedure and cannot be condoned. 

But on this matter of size there is another observation to be made. 

If classes get too small, the intellectual rewards of a seminar may be imperiled. Furthermore, the graduate student should have the advantage of the education which comes from other good graduate students. This type of education does not take place in the classroom, but in the laboratory and even in the library, in spite of the rules against conversation in the stacks. It can take place naturally and pleasantly in the lounges in the dormitories and around a table at the coffee hour, or even in the college cafeteria. The main thing is that it should take place. 34

There have been two revolutions on the part of graduate students within the last ten years. These have to be reckoned with in any plans for a graduate school today. The first is that a graduate student now expects to get paid for attending a graduate school. The best prospects for graduate training, especially those in the sciences, can expect that some graduate school will offer them a subsidy. The market price on a first-year graduate student is not yet as high as that on a good halfback, but the price levels are rapidly rising. 

The second revolution is more social, or sociological at least, than economic. Most graduate students these days are already married when they start their graduate study, a state of derring-do unheard of in my own graduate days. Not only are they married, but they have children and will continue to have children throughout their graduate school careers. This revolution adds to the housing problems at all universities and is second only to that perennial and ever acute problem-where shall the students park their cars? Most of the faculty will continue to walk, I suspect.

Now that we have discussed the faculty and the students, some of you may be thinking that it is time to stop, at least if you belong to the group of educators who answer all complaints with the bland statement, "We teach the students." Somehow this statement does not carry as much weight as it used to, even with parent-teacher audiences. 

Underlying all the recent attacks which have been made against our public schools and those who train teachers for our public schools is the conviction that not enough attention has been paid to subject matter. After all, can you teach students without teaching them something? I find myself obliged, therefore, to discuss for a little the question of what our faculty is to teach these selected students. 

In this discussion I do not propose to repeat in detail the several criticisms which have been voiced. Some of them have been illinformed and ill-tempered. I suggest that they will not be answered by name-calling. Too many professional educators have tried to evade all criticism by calling their critics enemies of public education. Nor will all the critics be hushed by the time-honored device of sending the high school band down Main Street, no matter how many shapely and prancing drum majorettes may lead the procession. When the defenders as well as the critics indulge in heated and intemperate argument, the smoke and dust raised is likely to convince the public that there must be a little fire.

One of the most useful studies of the conflict between the liberal arts and the schools of education is found in the "Newsletter" of the 35

American Council of Learned Societies, Volume 5, No. 2, 1954. I refer to it because it deserves to be much more widely known. The article points out particularly that the great change in function of public education needs to be more clearly understood. From serving largely as preparatory schools for those who wish to go to college, which was the main function half a century ago, the schools have broadened their function very greatly. They have properly become much more vocational. As the "Newsletter" article puts it, "Many students remain in high school not because they like it and want to learn, but because they need a parking place between adolescence and the time when modern mechanized industry finally agrees to employ them." Then, too, the confusion between democracy and conformity has led too many of us to regard the gifted pupil as socially or mentally abnormal. 

We have gone far in creating special classes for defective children but have been embarrassed to know what to do for the highly talented children.

Sometimes the subject matter fields have made it difficult for the high school teacher to get satisfactory graduate work. The plight of the science teacher in many of our high schools is a reminder of this. 

He is likely to be a person drafted into teaching high school science because his college transcript shows that he once had a course in elementary biology and perhaps one year of chemistry. His concentration in college may often have been in physical education. When he presents himself to the graduate school for courses which will help him teach scientific subjects, he will find that he does not have the necessary prerequisites to enter graduate work. He needs four years of college chemistry to take graduate work in chemistry, and he must also have had mathematics through calculus. So, instead of devoting a number of summers to his undergraduate preparation, he will almost certainly take his master's degree in education. Our science departments could make a much more impressive contribution to the training of high school teachers by providing suitable courses, even if they have to defy the edicts of the American Chemical Society. 

The sciences represent an extreme case, to be sure. Yet the other subject-matter fields have also been reluctant to treat realistically the problem of educating high school teachers. As Northwestern State 

College develops its graduate program, it has a magnificent opportunity to do very important pioneering in devising a curriculum more suitable to the needs of the practicing teachers. Some of us believe that this can be done without sacrificing the academic respectability of the subject-matter courses and at the same time with practical results that could be revolutionary. 36

Here I shall be as specific as possible, and shall discuss the field I know best-English. Fifty years ago our graduate departments of 

English were almost all under the control of professors who had been trained primarily in the discipline of philology. Their approach to 

English was primarily linguistic. Linguistics, they believed, was "scientific." A student working out a thesis in linguistics could reach conclusions which would be scientifically as correct as the conclusions reached by a graduate student in chemistry or mathematics. It was a long time before students and faculty members as well began to doubt the validity of approaching literature scientifically. The emphasis today has shifted from philology to criticism. Even though the approach and the techniques have changed, the emphasis may still be at fault if the practitioners of literary criticism insist on forgetting that literature and even criticism is, or should be, an art and not a science. 

There is also the wish to turn out students as completely equipped as possible in all fields of literature. Physically, it is impossible to require a sound reading knowledge of all the beloved classics in English literature. We must begin to select more carefully the masterpieces we wish to make the objects of our study. 

This job of selection is further complicated by the growing realization that the teacher of English should know something of other literatures. Practically, he will have to acquaint himself with other literatures by using translations. This is regarded by members of the old school as a very inadequate means. But most of the graduate students in 

English nowadays have no acquaintance with Greek and Latin, and whatever skill they present in modern languages is likely to be in Spanish rather than in French or German. For the study of literary history, Spanish is not nearly so rich a language as French or German. 

Consequently, if our prospective English teacher is to know something about other literatures he will have to get this knowledge through translations. But how many graduate departments of English are willing to provide graduate courses in world literature in translation? 

One of the most fascinating developments in modern literary scholarship has been in the history of ideas. This discipline has developed its own techniques and supports the Journal of the History of Ideas. The discipline at its best requires a much broader training than the old philological courses provided. Our historian of ideas must know a good deal of philosophy, and he must know social and political history, if he is to be effective. 

The development of this type of study in ideas suggests that our training in the past may have been too narrow. To be sure, the frontiers 37

of knowledge will be pushed back most successfully by specialists, by research workers who have been well trained in their basic discipline. Our graduate schools in the future must not forget this point. 

At the same time, it is important to note that there is a distinct trend toward general studies today. One school of thought insists on regarding all knowledge as a unity. This school would study literature as only a part of a broad cultural development. This school insists that an individual can know very little about literature unless he also informs himself in such related fields as philosophy, religion, music, and painting. The trend has been recognized in some graduate schools, and I recently read a prediction by a graduate dean that within fifteen or twenty years there will no longer be separate departments of specialized learning in the graduate schools. This is a trend to be aware of, even though we may remain skeptical about the time schedule.

In the broad field of graduate education of teachers, several programs are now in operation which contain experimental approaches. 

There is the state-wide program in Arkansas; there are interesting developments at Stanford, at Yale, and at Harvard. We have two at 

Vanderbilt. The one I am directing leads to the Ph.D. degree and is called the Program for the Preparation of College Teachers. It endeavors to provide the students with a small common body of knowledge, through a seminar in basic issues. The topic for this year has been "Freedom and Authority." This program also requires a voice test, a course in the history and organization of the American College, and teaching under supervision.

Our second program, for the secondary school teacher, is a joint program with Peabody College, and leads to the M.A. in Teaching. 

It is open to graduates of liberal arts colleges, and stresses subject matter, though the students can satisfy the certification requirements of most states. This program is designed for students who want to teach but who have not been in education courses in college. Completion of the program requires an academic year and a summer. The forty-five students accepted in this program receive fellowships, must do practice teaching, and must be qualified for graduate study in one of the following subjects: English, Foreign Languages, Mathematics,

Natural Sciences, Social Sciences, or History. 

Finally, I wish to say something about the requirement of a thesis. 

I happen to believe firmly that every student who receives a graduate degree should write a thesis. If the subject is properly selected, if the significant source materials are available, and if the director is competent, the preparation of a thesis becomes a highly educational enterprise. Through this discipline, the graduate student gets needed training 38

in organization of materials. He also becomes aware of the difficulty of establishing a fact. This in turn adds to his humility, a characteristic nowadays too easily associated with an inferiority complex, but a characteristic which is an extremely desirable quality for a man to possess if he would become a good citizen of the republic of letters. 

As Northwestern State College enters upon its new program of graduate studies, its hopes and purposes are, I am sure, both appropriately high. I have tried to suggest that there are some basic precautions to be taken and that these concern the college administration and its attitude, the faculty, its qualifications and its resources, and the student body and the selection of the student body. Some of us would like to see Northwestern State College develop a program of graduate study which, within certain limits, might be described as experimental. It is my happy opportunity to welcome you in your new function and to assure you of the sympathetic interest of all those concerned with higher education. The new program can mean much to Louisiana and to our region. 39

Tallahassee The occasion that brings us together today is the formal inauguration of John S. Kyser as President of the Northwestern State College of

I say to you, Mr. President, as one who has a right to speak, that your time will never more be your own.

If he has undergone trials in the past they have been but the means of preparation of the stamina to undergo greater trials in the future; if he has experienced the exuberance that attends intellectual 43

achievement it has been but a pattern of the great co-operative intellectual adventure that lies ahead; the spiritual experiences that have been his with high-minded men and with Almighty God are but an earnest of the spiritual power requisite to the life and the personality of a great institution over which he has the honor to preside. 

And so, as we recognize in this inaugural ceremony, being conducted in this quiet and beautiful place, henceforth man and institution are one body, one mind, and one spirit; and we dare to look forward with them toward academic horizons which they must inevitably approach. We speak, therefore, with admitted temerity, about some of the responsibilities of higher education in the decades that lie immediately ahead.

All of us are poignantly aware of the great expansion of higher education in this nation during the first half of this century. To meet the demands of increasing hordes of students who apply for admission, higher institutions have multiplied their physical facilities, increased their faculties, and broadened the scope of their curriculum. In order to maintain publicly supported higher institutions the taxpayers have provided financial support in ever-increasing amounts. And the end of this growth and expansion does not yet appear to be in sight. 

On every hand, in the press, over radio and television, in public meetings and forums, we hear discussions of problems that relate to education. Such discussions, in large measure, have to do with the urgencies that are produced by the increasing numbers of young people to be served by our schools. Population studies indicate that the steady increase of population will continue with substantial acceleration. Higher percentages of those who graduate from the high schools will apply for admission to colleges and universities. How far a state may go in providing post-high school education for those who desire it has not yet been determined. Apparently, the limit of our ability and our desire to make such provision has not yet been reached. 

The great and increasing influx of students calls for some fundamental changes and improvements in the curriculum as well as in the organization and administration of higher education. These masses of students present a wider variety of native capability, of educational achievement and of interests and attitudes than ever before. The possibility of success of many of them in the traditional academic program is not too promising. They are strongly actuated, for the most part, by vocational or other practical considerations. Not all of the students who seek admission have well defined purposes. 44

In the face of these conditions it is the obligation of the higher institution to discover as early as possible the capabilities as well as the intentions of its students. It must then guide them into such courses of study as will provide the maximum opportunity for each individual according to his capabilities and the firmness of his purpose. This means that not all who present themselves are potential scholars in the strict sense of that term. However, all of them are potential American citizens with the responsibility for carrying each his own share of maintaining a sound government and a sound economy for our people. 

We are under the obligation to provide each young person with education that will develop the use of the fundamental processes in human development. Each must learn the elemental processes of securing information, of maturing judgments, of drawing conclusions upon what he reads and hears and sees on television. In other words, we must provide the means of learning how to think; we must provide all students, whatever their capabilities, with as much understanding as is possible of the world in which they live and of the responsibilities that are theirs. The mastery of well-selected subject matter in a wide variety of fields is the best known method that has yet been devised to bring about this desired result. 

No university or college can perform its function without providing in the field of the liberal arts and sciences a program of instruction of the highest quality. It is by no accident or academic whim that such courses have long been referred to as the "disciplines." We hear a great deal on every hand about freedom, and well we may. But we see all about us too many who enjoy freedom without accepting its responsibilities. Every educational institution must give major attention to things that constitute real freedom. I remind you, therefore, of the fact that a liberal education is defined properly as "an education that befits a free man." It must concern itself not only with the acquisition of facts in the broad fields of the world's knowledge but it must go far deeper than that. It must be concerned with those elements of human character that make for the solidarity of our people. It must be concerned with those high moral values such as honesty, integrity, dependability, exactness, self control; for without these qualities no person may really be said to be educated. 

Does this mean that we must forsake our time-honored loyalties to high scholarship? Must we so dilute our scholarship that it becomes insipid to the real scholar and at the same time ineffectual for those of lesser minds? Such insecure logic is hoary with age, and we often indulge the faint suspicion that it may be a shield to cover ineffective 45

teaching when the going gets hard. Certainly, there is no hint that any standards of scholarship must be abandoned. The challenge today is that we discover ways to deal with the condition. That, either because of the new condition or in spite of it, we find better ways to enhance scholarship.

The old order and the old concept are exemplified in the custom, at some institutions, of admitting more students than the institution can properly teach, and then, at the first examination, disposing of a predetermined number so that the normal process of scholarship can move forward with accustomed serenity. The social wastage and the human tragedy of this continuing process amazes us. The complacency with which such treatment has been accepted by the laity attests mainly a sort of blind faith in the infallibility of the academic leadership. 

Our American democracy, if it means anything, recognizes the individual as the beginning and the end of our government and our society. To provide for his needs, to develop his capacities, to make of him a competent individual-this lies at the very core of our society. 

It is still true that an intelligent, informed electorate is indeed our chief bulwark. We have professed this as our cardinal philosophy through the years. We must now objectify it in our treatment of the multitudes who look to us for guidance and education. 

The issues which confront the world today are grave indeed. Men in high places recognize that the scientific and technological developments that have provided so many blessings for humankind may at the same time be the means of our destruction. It seems clear that the attitude of our people toward high moral values as controls over the powers we possess will, in the long run, determine the destiny of our nation and of the world. It is our obligation, therefore, to instill into all of our young people, insofar as it is possible, the full consciousness of the fact that the freedom we cherish can be sustained only by people who sense the real values of life; people who are willing to subordinate their own personal desires and ambitions to the higher good; people who understand that the only ultimate control is selfcontrol, and that self-control inevitably involves sacrifice. We must conclude that the only free man is one who voluntarily assumes the responsibilities of moral conduct. Are we concerning ourselves with morals in our educational system? I think we are; but I think we must do vastly more. 46

Ruskin was right when he said these charming words:

Education does not mean teaching people what they do not know. It means teaching them to behave as they do not behave. It is not teaching the youth the shapes of letters and the tricks of numbers, and then leaving them to turn their arithmetic to roguery and their literature to lust. It means, on the contrary, training them into the perfect exercise and kingly continence of their bodies and souls. 

It is extremely difficult, however, in a world that is as largely actuated and dominated by materialism as is ours to instill into a generation of students those deep and abiding truths of which I speak. 

For some three hundred fifty years the Western World has moved away from the concepts of higher moral values, dominated as it is by a philosophy of humanism-or, if you please, naturalism—or, if you wish to be naughty, animism. This movement makes certain fundamental assumptions that appeal to the popular mind for one reason and to the scholarly mind for another. Some of these assumptions are: the entire universe operates in all of its parts in accordance with certain universal laws; the nature and the operation of these laws can be discovered by the process of accurate observation and correct thinking; these laws are universal, not only in time but also in space, and they are unchangeable. 

For the popular mind this concept is convenient; its acceptance relieves one of all responsibility for one's own acts. For the scholar it is in a degree satisfying in that it provides him with an orderly means to apply his scholarship to the pursuit of knowledge. To the Christian or other person who believes in the Providence of God, it is troublesome philosophy which negates the very basis for any values other than material.

For more than half a century we have been under the domination of a prevailing psychology that is materialistic in the extreme—perhaps we should term it mechanistic. Its roots go back much further than the so-called new psychology, which we have seen flowering in the last quarter-century. Do you remember the poem by Browning entitled "Tray," in which the vivisectionists would examine the brain of the dog to ascertain what inspired him to save the child's doll, which had fallen into the pond?

At its height, the mechanistic school of thought took the position that "the only thing one inherits is a complex mechanism. This mechanism can do only one thing-respond to stimuli." The will accordingly is nothing more than a "drive" which is the result of the interaction of 47

certain stimuli. Ergo, we behave as we behave because we cannot help ourselves. Out of such a concept has come a Freudian philosophy which at once rules out all possibility of sin and righteousness, and of responsibility for conduct.

It is in the midst of such widely prevalent and powerful influences that the college student of today finds himself trying to pursue truth. 

How can one accept the postulates and discoveries of science and the conclusions of philosophers, while at the same time satisfying those inner urges that are a part of every human being. Our task of interpretation on such matters is as grave as it is difficult. 

There is hope that the age of materialism is showing signs of breaking. It has brought us two world wars in this century. Its effect upon the destinies of the world is gradually becoming known for what it really is. One writer gives us hope when he tells us that the most significant clue to the gravity of the present situation does not lie in the controlling ideology around which modernity has attempted to build a culture, self-defeating as this is; that clue, rather, is to be found in the fact that despite these convictions, the mind of the moderns was surprised when we were precipitated into the debacle of 19411945. The troubled world is becoming convinced that materialism leads but to confusion and ultimate destruction. Men in increasing numbers, men in high places, are saying "God save us, for we cannot save ourselves."

It is no small task to provide young people, through their college experiences, with the ability to find equilibrium in the midst of the confusions of the world about them. However difficult it may be, the college must provide that opportunity. 

Looking toward the horizon ahead, we have dealt briefly with only two of the great problems that confront higher education, the one quantitative and immediate, the other qualitative. For the solution of such major problems, along with the day-to-day demands of the multitude of conditions that must arise in a complex institution, we must look to the man who, today, is being inaugurated as the directing head of this college. Whatever else may lie upon the horizon which we view today, certain we are that the greatest challenge to administrators, scholars, and pupils alike lies in the direction of the full activation of the cherished principles of our beloved Republic-not merely the talking about them, but the actual living of these ideals by our faculty and students. 48

One of the satisfying wonders of higher education is that there is no geographic limitation upon scholarship. The smallest and most impecunious college may provide the scholar who discovers the farthest star. This noble college which today officially yields its direction to a new president is as free as is any institution in the world to contribute greatly to the next epoch in the illustrious history of American higher education. And so, Mr. President, we salute you. I am sure that I speak for students, faculty, alumni, and the citizens of a great commonwealth when I pledge to you their enthusiastic support. 49

NORTHWAS JUSTICE STATE COLLEGE LOUISIANA TOCHES LOUISIANA The Inaugural Address

INAUGURAL ADDRESS By PRESIDENT JOHN S. KYSER Northwestern State College of Louisiana 

Before launching the ship that carries the principal burden of my declared thoughts on this occasion, I wish to say a few words of recognition and appreciation. Reduced to a minimum, these constitute something of a torrent of praise for the spirit that prompts your presence here today to take part in these exercises that honor our college. The force of this manifestation of community and loyalty will carry over long after these few moments of exchange and fellowship shall have been concluded.

It is certain that your good judgment lets you know that the totality of what has been planned and effected is in celebration of the accomplishments of, and the outlook for, this institution of higher education service to the people of Louisiana and of our country. Anything that may seem to be personalized is but an incident in the larger reality. Anyone who may be singled out for a fleeting moment of especial recognition is aught but a child if he is unmindful of diminutive size in the larger pattern about us. 

I can not emphasize too strongly the point that the direction and administration of this college involve activities and encompass responsibilities far beyond the capabilities of any single individual. 

During the ten and one-half months of my incumbency, we have had spirited teamwork that has exceeded my liveliest anticipation. I wish first of all to pay public tribute to my colleagues, our students, and loyal alumni. Beyond the campus community, the cooperation of the 

State Board of Education, the State Superintendent of Education, school men and laymen, legislators, the press, and the public at large has been spontaneous and full. The reservoir of good will for this college is indeed broad and deep. 

Our supreme purpose is to pay richly-deserved honor to the college that has made pioneer, and even heroic, contributions in the past; and 61

which, by virtue of this varied and profound experience, is amply and uniquely fortified to engage in a wider pattern of service in the future. 

We who have the obligations and privileges of the direction of our college community at this time are immeasurably determined to be in full harmony with the truly great accomplishments of yesterday, and to deserve your confidence tomorrow. 

You who will evaluate what I shall say this morning may be disposed to grant that each season of life has its advantages. Perhaps it is the unique privilege of middle age to be able to have a median look both forward and backward, to be capable of striking a relatively favorable balance between what has been and what may be, and, above all, to have the mood to avoid excessive laudation of the "good old days" on the one hand, and on the other to escape both undue apprehension and expectation of what lies ahead. 

It has often seemed to me that the life pattern of individuals may be applied symbolically to colleges and universities and to the regions which they serve. An institution may embrace a span of time and circumstance that fosters a degree of freedom from both immaturity and the incipient tyranny which is always the threat of old age. In a very real sense, an institution is but the lengthened shadow of a particular area or region with its pattern and flux of behavior and epoch. 

Each of the corners of our habitable planet has its elements of distinction-indeed a few have that richness of mien and of mood that transcends the inanimate and non-human and partakes of the nature of personality. Like any human personality, a region has its unique combination of elements of strength and of need. Today, I wish to place especial emphasis on the regional complex of our people and college. 

We who work at Northwestern State College, on the old meeting ground of the proud New World empires of France and Spain of nearly two and one-half centuries ago, are still intrigued by this background. 

In reminding you that within yards of where you sit this morning there was built the citadel of this oldest town in an area so vast that it embraces nearly a third of our republic, we are not guilty of a gross flaunting of self; rather we are merging ourselves with our corner of 

Earth in a way that is above pettiness. In fitting ourselves into the perspective of many generations, no miasmic mist arises from this ground to stifle initiative even while we remember that any praise of the contemporary is but a ground swell in the great sea of the past and even less in the boundless ocean of the future. 

We hold that an awareness of a spirited past is an element of strength because it is rich with the lessons of accomplishment in the 62

face of difficulties. Are the difficulties of the mid-twentieth century more numerous and complex than those of yesterday? In any event, what are one or two difficulties of American higher education, and how do they apply to Northwestern State College?

The remaking of the life of our planet by forces of unprecedented violence in both the physical and technical worlds is such that no habited spot escapes implications of infinite disaster. Shocked by this knowledge, the peoples of the literate world reveal reactions that are as varied as the combinations of intellect, courage, and spiritual resources of individuals. When we look about us, we find those who quail at the very mention of activities that are impregnated with the possibility of terror, and who, as a result, seek refuge in ways and places hitherto unwonted; we discover others who respond with the grimness of the stoics, while yet others are indifferent. To prescribe adequately for all is indeed the work of deity; to act with courage and whatever wisdom is available is the profoundest obligation of humans. 

The students, faculty, and friends of Northwestern State College respond to this and other facets of the problems of modern society as do other Americans. We share most of the doubts and fears and hopes of our countrymen. We have very much in common, but we do have a few problems that differ, at least in degree, from those found in many other places.

What must we keep in mind if we, at this college, are to give the best service to higher education, here, in our time? We need not expect a chorus of hallelujas for any one recommendation. I do not give mine on any authoritarian basis; but in the American tradition that if a man keeps at least the semblance of a civil tongue in his head, he has a right to be heard.

For this college to render the maximum service to our people at this stage of our development, a major need is the redefinition of "college" and, following this, a re-examination of the "higher" in higher education. It should not be forgotten that different periods yield varied meanings to a given word; and even in the same period, peoples in different areas impose seemingly diametrically opposed ideational content to the same word—if in doubt of at least the latter just consult Izvestia and Pravda for their notions of Kremlin democracy as opposed to those of America.

If we would follow the urgings of many traditionalists, "college" and "higher" education would be confined to a pattern that would seriously compromise many of the urgent requirements of our evolving 63

society; they might even threaten our very survival. To cite a condition in point, does anyone imagine that, with the Russians currently turning out double or treble the number of engineers who graduate from American colleges and universities, we must not be concerned with balancing this threat? If we do not produce as many engineers, then certainly we must have better ones. Who in his right mind will harbor a spurious and disdainful attitude toward engineers as participants in and practitioners of higher education? 

Suppose, to make the issue even more forceful, it were proposed to turn back to the pattern of higher education as defined in colonial New England or Virginia, or in the monasteries of Europe even more centuries ago. Grateful as we are for the labors of the devoted monks as they engaged in their charities, speculated on the nature of man, and painfully produced their magnificent manuscripts, we but do the obvious in demanding a broader definition of the scope of higher education today.

Next, and more limitedly, what is the meaning of "higher" in higher education, when many of the learning activities involved in the college community are seemingly those of the limited professional vocationalist or even of the craft skills acquired by the apprentice of just last century? Experienced educators are quite aware that the answers fall into several patterns, but most of these are but degree variations of two dominant classifications, each with its own implied prescription for action The first involves summary dismissal of all vocationalism as unworthy of the label higher education; the second, blind acceptance of vocationalism as an end-in-itself. 

Quite apart from the validity of the acceptance of either of these views, higher education in America is evolving in a way that exhibits the best social tradition of the western World-Compromise! Neither of the contending parties of advocates has been able to attain complete acceptance of the concepts it espouses. The compromises that have already been worked out are but forerunners of others to follow. 

It must be admitted that the dangers inherent in compromise are serious and that it is easy to confuse compromise with surrender. 

There are examples of compromise so one-sided that the result is only little removed from utter capitulation. Academic coexistence requires a good look.

Compromise in higher education does not mean the reduction of the democratic process to uncritical egalitarianism. This mistaken dead-level equality of the French revolutionaries did not work then and is truly unembraceable now. Somehow, those who direct the fate 64

of colleges and universities must recognize the need for hierarchy in higher education; but this pattern of differentiation must be characterized by a rich appreciation of the worth and dignity of the great majority of human activities. Further, there must be the recapture of an unhesitating belief in the glory of excellence, no matter what the field of endeavor.

To illustrate the evaluation contended for, just who shall say that the song of a sweetly running mechanism, the fruit of centuries of effort in the experimental and theoretical sciences, may not fill the soul of one man with something that is at least comparable in "magnificence" to the higher art of surrealist paintings as expressed in the crumpled and amorphous timepiece of Salvador Dali? Who shall have the right to restrict "culture" to the realm of the fine arts and to consign advocates of any other view to the catacombs of the illiterate? The answer unquestionably is that the protector of higher education, in terms of capital C culture, is himself the victim of insufficient elevationand if not a matter of height, then certainly one of inadequate breadth. 

Thus far it might seem that these remarks are but a brief for something approaching raw vocationalism. Nothing could be further from the reality of what is envisioned and embraced as the right educational and life philosophy. Actually it is held that there is abundant evidence to show that unabridged technicalism in higher education produces varying degrees of intellectual sterility. The world is full of well-trained but poorly educated people. 

The neglect of the subjects that impart an understanding of human values is not uniformly harmful in any starry-eyed sense; this avoidance is tragic because the effects reveal the inability of so-called practical men to solve their larger human problems even in the realm of business. When one of the world's greatest corporations sets up a program that requires many of its administrative and technical executives to take "time out" to study such seemingly impractical subjects as comparative religion, classical literature, and biography, it is high time that the extreme vocationalism of some curricula of higher education be called into question. 

The matter of a corrective for this condition in American education involves an evaluation that must be cast in the mold of our American outlook, especially in its great tenet of "equality." There is nothing wrong with "I am as good as you are"-if this statement does not stand alone. To this affirmation should be added a corollary that is really much more important, to wit, "In some way, or combination of ways, I am not only as good, but actually better than you are." A facing up of many "betters" in a great variety of occupations and 65

realms of achievement provides the great essentials for the highest type of equality.

The dignity of the individual personality can be adequate only when it is recognized that, in the eyes of our Creator, each person may have the potential for infinite worth. After that, it is up to the individual to establish his hierarchical rating. On the college campus, the key to it all is mutual respect for different kinds of specialized accomplishment when built upon a foundation in which the curricular bricks have a mortar of common interest. A college curriculum that does not provide youth with at least exposure to the intellectual attainments and enduring values of our human kind does not deserve the label of higher education. 

If college is to be defined to include instruction in the theory and practice that prepares us to serve the work-a-day and other needs of our epoch, it is imperative that means be devised to meet the principal demands of the American prestige mechanism. The existing pattern is not inviolate. But change must proceed at measured pace and with judicious use of the brake of insight that is the outgrowth of full knowledge.

Because we can not meaningfully give a higher academic degree at birth, and since we can find adequate reason to devise programs that impart excellence in realms as varied as household management and classical languages, it is imperative that we devise insignia which, with reasonable accuracy, describe different types of accomplishment. Logically, this calls for a range of degrees and certificates that would shatter tradition at many points. We should generate that degree of courage which will comport with our logic. 

When I think of all of this in relationship to our college, I go back over the curricular changes of the last thirty years. With others, I share pride in the old Normal School background of Northwestern State College. Never did an institution perform more yeoman service in a day when need was so widespread and so deep. With its limited resources, the old Normal prepared a balanced academic diet, even if not at the level of the research laboratory. 

Later, in a swirl of misapprehension that was virtually nationwide, there came a time when the art of pedagogy suffered grave injury. In part, the excesses of selected theorists and practitioners in this important realm brought low esteem upon themselves and their disciples. Happily, we, along with other colleges with strong backgrounds in teacher education, have moved out-perhaps some choose to hold that we are still moving out-of that period of unfortunate 66

evaluation of a basic need. Today, there is a resurgence of vital interest in the art of harnessing sound pedagogy to full knowledge. 

In the realm of higher education, it is not difficult to find proof of this interest. At the Tenth Annual Conference on Higher Education in 

Chicago a month ago, the sections most widely attended by the nearly eight hundred college and university administrators and faculty members from all of the states of our union were those that were concerned with (1) "How can institutions evaluate the effectiveness of teaching and other faculty services?" (2) "What programs have proved helpful in the improvement of college teaching?" (3) "How can conflicting points of view of teacher education be resolved?” Even the section that tried to answer the question of "Who should go to college?" trailed behind these three basic interests in the great problem of successful marriage of method and knowledge. 

The evidence is, then, that we have a new lease on the old reality of good and even great teaching. Looked at in retrospect, how childish to have assumed that the embryo Ph.D., steeped in seminars, would by some process of academic osmosis take on the capacity to give good instruction! Many tens of thousands of college and university freshmen and others have suffered injury from those youthful tyros of the college classroom who might even brag of the fact that they had never been exposed to a single day of study of how to "put over their stuff." If this is true for college and university instruction, how very great is the carry-over to the levels of the elementary and secondary schools? Can there be any doubt that among the many objectives of Northwestern State College, none is as important as the making of important contributions to richer, fuller-bodied classroom teachers?

Now, it will be most unfortunate if these remarks are to be interpreted as some joyous latterday beheading of selected professors in particular academic matter fields. In review, I believe it will be granted that an effort has been made to point out the faults of both camps of partisans. Further, it should be clear that, having gone through extremes, we are now in a favorable position to achieve a new balance that will produce results superior to any we have ever known. 

We are certain that a teacher without rich and abiding learning represents a total contradiction in terms. We know equally well that great teaching is indeed tinged with the earmarkings of scientific method and, above all, that it is one of the very finest of the fine arts. 

The judgment of the ages proves to us that superb teachers stand at the apex of the pantheon of the great and noble of our species. Granted 67

that only a few ever reach this high stature, and that the seeds of genius escape analysis, it is yet true that untold good can be accomplished by programs of higher education that are dedicated to the proposition that men and women can be improved by exposure and instruction.

In the new level of opportunity, we here at Northwestern State College will do our utmost to achieve a vital blending of learning and method. An outstanding goal of our college is the training of the truly professional teacher whose ideals range far beyond material comfort and the cheers and popularity of the moment. This ideal can be closely approached only with the whole-hearted backing of an informed and well disposed public. Our perpetual effort must be to demonstrate that by the very excellence of our performance and service we deserve the support that we require. 

In the breadth of the past of our college, we are aware that what we enjoy today is the result of the combined efforts of those splendid men and women who possessed vision that was grounded in ability and fortified by unceasing effort. As we look over our world and contemplate the mold of the future, we know that both the scholar and the unlearned are agape with awe at the uncertain commotion of indefinable portent. In the midst of developments that point the way to some of the innermost secrets of, at least, the physical universe, humility before our Creator and our fellows is so obviously compelling that only the arrogant will challenge the necessity for this attitude which is the very essence of the requirement for yet greater human achievement.

With this thought that inevitably impells us toward greater faith and tolerance, I trust that you who visit this campus may sense something of the fine relationships that exist between faculty and students, and of the magnitude of the effort that is being put forth by able and loyal colleagues, alumni, and other friends. If this cooperation is continued, this college can truly be a leader in devising ways and means of meeting the expanding needs of our youth and of our entire citizenry.

If we but realize that confidence in the future is the indispensable core of achievement, and if we are seized of a determination to strike a decent balance between the needs and obligations of individuals and groups, we shall find that inspiration which is indispensable to our high purpose. If we insist upon an unremitting stream of intelligent and sincere effort, this college and our region will attain a yet higher place of service and respect in our state, our country, and beyond. 68
