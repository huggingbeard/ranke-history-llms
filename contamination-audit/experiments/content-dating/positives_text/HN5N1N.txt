THE CORPSE AT THE QUILL CLUB 

The original mistake of the founders of the 

Quill Club, an amateur writers' group, was advertising for members. Because if they hadn't they would never have gotten into a mess of murder.

The Quillians began receiving poison pen letters, and the unpopular Marguerite English, one of the "ad" members, was suspected. Everyone regretted that she had been invited to spend a week-end in the country with the rest. But it froze their spirits when she was found dead and the group realized one of them was a murderer. 

The popular Miss Long introduces a new heroine, the author and mystery expert "Peter" Piper.

Chapter One  

EVER since I promised Margaret and Althea that 

I'd try to write this story, I've been wondering where I ought to start-whether it should be with that ominous green door at the turn of the stairs at Arlea, which always gave Althea and me a queer, tingly sensation along our scalps and spines every time we passed it, and the "White Lady" that Alice, the colored maid-of-all-work, claimed she saw walking up and down those same stairs; or whether it ought to be with Tim Kent's suicide two years before the events at Arlea took place erratic, bril- 11

liant Tim, the only really good writer the Quill Club has ever had, or is ever likely to have. But the first seems a little late and the second a little early for a proper beginning; so perhaps I'd better discard them both, and begin with that Thursday morning last July when Margaret Hale came into my garden, looking as though tragedy had just struck. "Pete, are you busy?" she asked.

I was cutting withered flowers from the larkspur on the bank at the time, which necessitated my standing with one foot headed north and the other south; and since the larkspur was of the annual variety, which grows only three feet high, I was bent over nearly double besides. However, I understood that the question was merely rhetorical. "No," I answered, looking at her from under my left elbow. "I'll be with you in a minute." "For Heaven's sake, be careful!" she jittered as

I went through a few more contortions on my way down the bank. "You'll break something!" "I can't any more," I told her. "Both shoulder straps went long ago; and it's sheer defiance of the law of gravity that's holding my slip up now. But come over and sit on the bench, and tell me what's on your mind." 

Before going further, perhaps I had better clear

up any possible ambiguity about my sex. My given name is Katherine, not Pete. But when your last name is Piper, you're bound to be called Peter, which is eventually streamlined into Pete. Well, so much for that.

Margaret came over and sat down beside me on the rustic bench. She has about the most expressive eyes of anyone I know; and just then they were expressing outrage, injury, and indignation, and doing a thorough job of it. "Pete," she began, "you write detective stories. 

What do you know about handwriting?" "Nothing," I answered truthfully. "It's one of the things I've got a literary license for bluffing about. Why?"

She took an envelope from her bag, and handed it to me. "Look at that," she directed.

I looked. It was an ordinary enough envelopethe kind you get a dozen of for a nickel at the ten cent store. It was addressed to Miss Margaret Hale in a bold, but what looked like a disguised, handwriting. The postmark was local. "Do I look inside?" I inquired after I had examined both front and back of the outside.

She nodded, and I drew out a single sheet of typewriter paper. It was covered with about twenty or so disconnected sentences-not handwritten like

the address on the envelope, but done in a queer, purplish sort of type. There was neither salutation nor signature.

I'll not repeat what was on that paper, but it was one of the meanest, cruelest things I've ever read.

It took all of Margaret's little weaknesses and shortcomings, and presented them as they'd appear if looked at through the new Mt. Palomar telescope; and it added a few innuendoes for which there was absolutely no foundation. It made fun of her writing-she's a newspaper columnist, and a good one-and it ended up by calling her— But I think I've said enough. "The cat that wrote that," I declared, "ought to be poisoned. Have you any idea who it was?"

She looked off across the garden for a minute before replying; then: "I hate to say this, Pete," she began, "but I'm afraid it was someone in the Club. Those nasty jabs about my writing make it look that way. There are one or two people, you know, who think that newspaper work shouldn't be counted in the Quill Club."

I knew who she meant. Althea Raeburn, who organized the Quill Club a little over five years ago, had high ideals about the part it should play in elevating the standard of American literature, and about the type of belles lettres-and only belles

lettres that should be written by its members. (God alone knows how I got in!) And George Raeburn, Althea's husband, whose theme song is "I 

Married An Angel," always thinks what Althea thinks. But while Althea has done some fantastic things in her time, upholding her ideals by means of poison pen letters has never been one of them. "If you mean who I think you mean," I said to 

Margaret, "my opinion is no. What's more, I believe you're wrong about its having been anyone in the Club. We may get on one another's nerves at times, and we've all had our nasty moments; but when you get down to brass tacks, we're all too loyal to one another to do any stabbing in the back. Besides," I went on, examining the sheet more closely, "this wasn't typed; it was done on a hektograph machine. You can tell by the peculiar color of the purple ink, and by the absence of punctuation marks showing through on the back that a typewriter always leaves. And so far as I know, nobody in the Club owns a hektograph machine."

She looked relieved for the fraction of a second; then a look of absolute horror leaped into her eyes. "Hektograph!" she exclaimed. "Pete, do you realize what that means? It means that a lot of copies must have been made. And Heaven alone knows to whom they've been sent!"

I had realized that, but I'd been hoping she wouldn't. "Yes, I suppose so," I said. "But your friends won't believe any of that rubbish, and your enemies don't matter. So don't worry so much about it." 

She didn't look particularly comforted. "That sounds all right when you say it," she told me, "but think how you'd feel in my place, having all your friends get copies of that outrageous thing." "I'd be fighting mad, just as you must be," I admitted. "But as it is, I can look at it from the viewpoint of an outsider; and since it's outside reactions you're worried about, you can tell from mine that they'll be against the writer of this affair rather than against you."

I handed the letter back to her, and she returned it absently to her bag. "I've got to find out some way who wrote it," she declared. "For until I do, I'll be suspecting everybody; I simply won't be able to help it. Why, it makes me feel like not going along to Arlea tomorrow. I'd hate to go and imagine that every time anyone looked at me, they'd be thinking aboutthis."

I managed to talk her out of that idea; and by the time she left, she was feeling, if not exactly lighthearted about the whole thing, at least a little

better after the relief that one female always gets from talking over the thing that's troubling her with another. 

But while I had tried to make light of the letter to Margaret, I wasn't quite so easy about it in my own mind. Her suggestion that it might have been written by a member of our writers' club worried me. Our little organization is pretty much of an all-for-one-and-one-for-all affair, and it was preposterous that any member could be guilty of such treachery. Yet once the idea had been suggested, I found that I was unable to get rid of it. Which I suppose just goes to show how depraved the human mind can be upon occasion. 

But the episode of Margaret and her letter wasn't the only disturbing element that day was to contain. That afternoon, Judith Norton dropped around. 

Judith is the Quill Club's unpredictable quantity, and I suspect that she secretly revels in the reputation. She's the type of person that you either like intensely or violently dislike; and if she suspects that she can shock you, she'll go out of her way to do it. 

I'll never forget, for example, the evening she read a story in the course of which the heroine had a baby-without benefit of wedlock-when the club

was meeting at the home of little Mr. Simms, our 

Sunday school superintendent member. The poor man was so embarrassed he didn't attend a meeting for the next six months, a circumstance which, I've got to admit, was something of a relief, since his own contributions were never exactly what could be called literary gems. But with all her foolishness,

Judith is a good sport and intensely loyal to the rest of us. "Have you heard the latest, Pietro?" she inquired now, dropping into one porch chair and crossing her feet upon another. "Althea's invited 

Marguerite English along to Arlea." "But I thought only club members were being invited," I protested. "And Mrs. English isn't a member any more." 

Judith shrugged. "That's what we all thought,' she remarked. "But it seems she's got different ideas. Anyway, she calmly announced to Althea that she'd decided to come back to the Club for the social angle, even if she doesn't think much of us as writers. Then she proceeded to ask rather pointedly about this week-end she'd heard we're all to spend at the Raeburns' country place. "Who told you all this?" I inquired. "Fran Beresford," she answered. “She says Althea is no more keen about having Sister English up

Chapter Two  

UPON reading over what I have written so far, it seems to me that I've made a great many references that need explaining; so perhaps I'd better take a chapter out for background. I'll begin with the Quill 

Club itself.

It's just a small, more or less professional writers' club, that was organized about five years ago by 

Althea and George Raeburn and Margaret Hale, the other charter members having been rounded up by means of an advertisement inserted by Althea in the local newspaper. Glaring examples of the fallacy of this plan-although for radically different reasons-had been Mrs. Marguerite English, about whom enough, if not too much, has been said already, and Mr. Theophilus Simms. After that, 24

Althea grew more careful, and established reading requirements.

Mr. Simms was a quiet, well-meaning little soul who ran a grocery store six days of the week, and a 

Sunday school the seventh. He produced some of the weirdest story plots that it's ever been my amazement to listen to; but he couched them in equally weird grammar. The result was that whenever he got up to read, the rest of us all took a deep breath, then held on to our chairs and our facial expressions.

Other charter members were Mr. and Mrs. Theodore Tuttle. Mr. Tuttle didn't write, but he was made an honorary member because of Mrs. Tuttle -a small, bird-like person who looks as though her whole world would be bounded by the four walls of her house, but who writes some of the loveliest nature poetry I have ever read.

I came in during the second year of the club's existence, following my graduation from college. I write detective stories, and don't give a damn who knows it. 

Also in the second year came Mrs. Louise Kearns, writer of children's stories and editor of a small children's magazine. With the exception of Mr. and 

Mrs. Tuttle, she was considerably older than the rest of us, but such an intensely vital person that

difference in age seemed not to matter. Also came 

Judith Norton, who claimed to write anything from college humor up or down-and two or three others who have since dropped out.

By this time, the membership of the club had become a good seventy-five per cent feminine, although, as Mr. Simms said, it really didn't matter, since we were all "non-sectarian." (What exactly he meant by that, we never did learn.) However, with the beginning of the third year, Althea began to feel that we needed more of what she called "the masculine viewpoint"; so what came to be known as "Althea's Man Campaign" got under way. 

We collared two: George's law partner, Warren

Dane, a somewhat shy, serious-minded young man, and his best friend since their college days, Tim Kent.

It was Tim alone, of all of us, who came up to Althea's original ideals for the Quill Club. A strange mixture of reserve and outspokenness, he was one of those rare personalities who seem to do everything well, and to imbue whatever they touch with an air of glamour. He had been everywhere, seen everything; and there was nothing in life that did not interest him, from the ocean seething off Cape

Cod to a row of old whiskey barrels converted, during bygone Prohibition days, into containers for soft soap. He wrote poetry about both of them—

strong, virile poetry, that would have done credit to Masefield in his earlier days. 

But with all his swashbuckling romanticism, he possessed a streak of hard-headed Yankee realism that hated pretense and meaningless ostentation. I remember the evening when one of us, in an illadvised moment, got to talking about ancestry; and before we knew it, over half of us had begun to throw our weight around on the subject. Tim listened for a while in silence; then he looked up with a sardonic gleam in his grey eyes, but with his lean, hawk-like face otherwise expressionless. "I never talk about my ancestors," he remarked offhandedly. "Back before the Revolution, one of them got hanged in Connecticut for a horse thief."

Nobody in the room knew whether he was telling the truth, or merely bringing the rest of us down a peg in his own way; but the conversation changed abruptly.

As the club year drew toward its close in the spring, some of us thought we detected a change in 

Tim. He became more reserved; talked less; and his wit, when it did flash forth, seemed to possess a new quality that was almost too dangerously brilliant. At our frequent requests, he continued to read to us from the familiar brown book in which he kept his manuscripts, bits of his own verse or some of the amusing, modern translations he had made from

the Greek and Latin classics; but we soon noticed that he was writing nothing new, and it puzzled us. 

In a person like Tim, a dearth of new ideas seemed impossible.

During the winter, he had developed a cough, for which he had been receiving treatment from Dr. 

English-husband of the celebrated Margueritebut it had not appeared to be serious enough to account for the change that was taking place in him. 

Besides, he was not the type to become morbid over a question of health.

As a matter of fact, Warren took that cough much more seriously than Tim did; and on at least one occasion that I know of, he went to consult Dr. 

English in private concerning his friend's condition. 

But apparently the doctor was reassuring; for after that Warren showed less alarm whenever Tim was seized with one of his occasional coughing fits.

The year ended with its customary outdoor meeting, at which Tim seemed more like his old self. Indeed, he was gayer and more talkative than we had ever known him to be before-exchanging light badinage with Judith, entering into all the special events that are a part of such meetings, and even teasing Warren a little on his seriousness. I noticed, too, that he had his pipe with him, although he had given up smoking entirely over the winter. All in

And then the very next day, it happened.

Warren, returning a little later than usual from his office, found him lying upon his bed in the apartment that they shared-apparently asleep, except for the little, powder-rimmed hole in his temple and the revolver that had fallen from his relaxed hand. 

We were all shocked and horrified when we heard about it; but even through our grief at the loss of our friend, one question persisted in pushing itself to the fore: Why had he done it?

The answer was not so easy to find. Financial difficulties? It wasn't likely. Tim had been a more than ordinarily successful architect, with no family or other dependents to drain upon him. An illstarred love affair? But so far as any of us knew, he had never shown any sentimental interest in women. 

His health? But that had been improving during the past few months. In the end, we were obliged to let the question go unanswered.

Warren, of course, was inconsolable. While it may sound overly sentimental, those two had been more to each other than brothers. Theirs had been one of of those rare, Damon-and-Pythias sort of friendships that are more often read about than actually encountered in real life.

At first he had avoided the rest of us; had avoided, in fact, everyone and everything that reminded him of Tim. But gradually he had drifted back to us, more shy and serious than ever. 

It may seem a little odd that I should go to such length to describe a man who, for only one brief year, blazed like a meteor across our little sky, only to be extinguished forever in the black sea of eternity. But it was Tim Kent who was actually the motivating force behind what is to follow, even as the shadow of the murdered Julius Caesar lies across the pages of the play that bears his name. 

But I see I'm getting a little reckless with my rhetoric.

In the fourth year of our club's existence, we acquired one new member, Frances Beresford. Fran is British, and has all of the British strength of mind, although without any of the smugness that so often accompanies it. In addition to having a good, sound literary style, she is a natural leader and champion of causes; and so at the beginning of our fifth year, she was elected president of the Quill 

Club by a unanimous vote.

It was at the end of the fifth year that Althea conceived her idea of inviting the whole club up to the Raeburns' country place, Arlea, for a long weekend to begin Friday and extend through the following Monday. It was to be a week-end devoted to

intensive writing, during which we would endeavor to get off our chests all the things we never seemed to find time to get onto paper amid the bustle and distraction of city life. The plan had appealed to all of us, and we had accepted one hundred per cent, even including little Mr. Simms. 

Our physical well-being was to be looked after by Alice, the colored girl who does the laundry for several of our families. Alice's full name is Alice 

Mississippi Jackson; and she had been chosen to go along to lend color-in more senses of the word than one-to the party. It was Alice whom I had first heard speak of "The League of Notions" long before the phrase occurred to any of the radio commentators; and she didn't do it with her tongue in her cheek, either. Also, she pronounces Mussolini's name as though it were spelled, "Muscle-inny," which I contend is descriptively correct, at that. 

And so, with our week-end bags in one hand and our portable typewriters in the other, we set merrily off on a party whose uninvited guest was to be murder.

Chapter Three  

ARLEA is the old Raeburn homestead, and has belonged to George's family for generations. It was originally an old Pennsylvania farmhouse; but when George decided to go in for corporation law rather than farming, he had sold most of the land, retaining only the house itself and a part of the meadow adjoining it. Shortly after he and Althea were married, they had converted the place into a summer home and given it its present name, the two first letters being Althea's initials, and the "lea" part being self-explanatory.

The house itself is a huge, rambling affair that Althea, with all her enthusiasm for modern improvements, has had the good taste to keep as close to its original tradition as is in keeping with modern comfort. Therefore, although it has indoor plumb- 32

ing installed, it is still lighted with the picturesque old oil lamps that George's grandfather knew. Like so many early Pennsylvania houses of its type, its floors were not built all on one level, so that it is necessary to descend a few steps in going from the front part of it to the back. On the second floor, this is taken care of by a landing about two thirds of the way up the stairs, from which a green painted door gives upon a single large room over the kitchen and laundry, while five additional steps lead to a cross hall from which the rooms at the front of the house open. "I suppose you'll all want to go up to your rooms first thing, and change into sport clothes," Althea began as soon as we had all arrived and were assembled in the big, old-fashioned living room with its needle-point chair-backs and hand-hooked rugs, "so I'll tell you where you're to be. Pete, you and

Judith are to have the room at the head of the stairs. Margaret and Mrs. Kearns, you're to be in the one next to it. Maude," to Mrs. Tuttle, "I'm putting you and Teddy in the middle room on the cross hall. Mrs. English, you'll be in the first room on the side hall, Fran in the second, and Mr. Simms and Warren in the third. Is that all right with everybody?"

It was so far as nine tenths of us were concerned. 

Mrs. English was the other tenth.

"What's the matter with me?" she demanded, looking down her nose at the rest of us. "Am I supposed to have a contagious disease or something, that no one is willing to room with me?" 

Althea looked uncomfortable. "I-I didn't put you in with any of the others," she explained, "because I thought that perhaps Dr. English might care to stay over until tomorrow morning before driving back to the city." "Well, he won't," Mrs. English answered for him. "But, Marguerite dear," poor Dr. English remonstrated mildly, "you know you never want to share a room with anyone when you go away anywhere."

She gave him a look that withered him. "Wanting to and being obliged to are different, Harry," she pronounced.

I saw from that that our week-end was getting off to a good start. "Poor Dr. English!" Judith exclaimed sympathetically when we were in our room changing into slacks. "I don't see how he stands it." "It's one of two things," I said, trying to brush my boyish bob into something that looked less like a dish-mop. "Either "To the pure all things are pure,' or 'Love is blind.' 999 "But she's got a face that only a mother could

love," Judith remarked. "And he's not her mother. 

Seriously, though, Pietro, do you think he honestly believes she's as perfect as he pretends?" "Seriously, I don't," I replied. "But I imagine he's got himself hypnotized into believing that he does. That's one way of making the best of a bad bargain, you know-simply closing your eyes to it."

I was ready before Judith was, and so I started downstairs ahead of her. As I came to that green door on the landing, I experienced an unaccountable tingling sensation over my scalp, while little mice-feet seemed to chase one another up and down my spine. It made me shiver, although the time was near noon on a July day.

Althea, who was standing at the foot of the stairs in the lower hall, looked up and saw me. "What's the matter, Pete?" she asked. "Nothing," I answered with a laugh, trying to shake off the feeling. "I just felt for a minute as though someone were walking over my grave, that's all."

An odd expression came into Althea's face. "So you notice it, too!" she half whispered in that awed tone that people so often use when speaking of something they can't explain. "Pete, I always get the queerest sensation every time I pass that green door; something-creepy."

"Perhaps a murder was once committed behind it," I suggested. "It looks as though it would be a good place for one."

It was after luncheon, and just before he started on his drive back to the city, that Dr. English made his unexpected presentation. "I have a little something here that Marguerite and I thought might serve as a sort of souvenir of what we feel is going to be a very pleasant weekend for her," he began, lifting a briefcase from the back of his car as he spoke. "During the past few weeks, I've been collecting-with the aid of your secretary, Mrs. Tuttle-a number of this club's unpublished manuscripts. A few days ago, I had my secretary run off copies of them on my office hektograph machine, and bind them into booklets. I believe I've included in the collection something representative of each one of you."

He opened the briefcase and brought out a bundle of very respectable-looking brochures, each stapled together between bindings of bright red cover stock. As he distributed them there were exclamations of surprise on all sides—including one from Mrs. English, who it was quite evident had known absolutely nothing about them until that very moment.

It was a most gracious gesture on the doctor's part, collecting all those things that most of us had

written just for the fun of it, and putting them together in a kind of Quill Club fifth anniversary volume. But it told us something that he had probably never intended: Mrs. English had been contemplating a return to our circle for some time; and he, either knowing or suspecting something of the conditions under which she had left us a year before, had done this as a sort of peace-offering to smooth the way for her.

It was an hour or so later when Althea maneuvered me away from the others on the pretext of showing me her study.

THE arrest of Warren, even though it had been more or less foreshadowed practically from the beginning, left all of us stunned. Judith, of course, was inconsolable, and blamed herself for having first turned the Sergeant's suspicions in his direction by her statement that he had been moving about the house around two o'clock in the morning -the approximate time when Mrs. English must have taken or been given the fatal dose of caffeine. 

After trying vainly to persuade her that he would have been arrested in any case, once his fingerprints had been found on those two lamps, I finally induced her to take some nerve medicine and to lie down for an hour or so.
