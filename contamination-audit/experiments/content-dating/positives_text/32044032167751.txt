FOREWORD 

This is the third Bulletin issued by this League dealing with the work of the Senate Committee investigating REA. It is based upon an 

Interim Report issued June 23rd. It is unfortunate that Part 4 of the 

Hearings is not yet published since important evidence relating to the Committee's findings will appear in that volume. Parts I to III are available and references are made to them herein. 

The Committee has not concluded its inquiry. Further hearings will be held and further reports made. 

This Interim Report sustains Mr. Slattery in his resistance to the methods employed to force his resignation; it fixes responsibility for the "mess" in which REA now finds itself and emphatically recommends immediate restoration of its independence. The Chairman of the 

Committee has introduced a bill to that end, the text of which will be found on pages 23 and 24 of this Bulletin. 

The Popular Government League will actively and unitedly support the bill to free REA. Our interest lies in the fact that since 1921 we have carried on educational work for rural electrification, first in connection with advocacy of legislation resulting in the TVA Act of 1933, and since 1935 to promote and protect REA itself. 

We invite the cooperation of all persons and organizations interested in the permanent welfare of the Rural Electrification Administration.

J. K.

Set REA Free! 

I. INTRODUCTION Why Administrator Carmody Resigned When in June, 1939, the Rural Electrification Administration was deprived of its independence and placed under the domain of the Secretary of Agriculture, Administrator John M. Carmody promptly resigned. He made no public statement but privately told his friends that under any such control efficient management would be impossible. Further, that the agency would inevitably be turned into a political machine and he would have none of it. 

On June 23rd last, the Senate Subcommittee of the Committee on Agriculture, which has been investigating the fate of REA under Secretary Wickard, made a unanimous interim report. Referring to Mr. Carmody's testimony as "very impressive," the members signing it, Senators Smith (D., S. C.), Chairman; Shipstead, (R., Minn.); Gillette, (D., Iowa), and Aiken, (R., Vt.), stated: "There can be no question as to why he retired. He made his reasons known in the testimony before the Committee. He feared that the success of the REA would be impaired and the REA would be handicapped when it became subordinate to the Department of Agriculture. He anticipated the menace of ‘layers of authority' over ŘEA. His anticipations and fears were fully justified." -Report, p. 4; Hearings, Part I, pp. 43-47.

WHY SENATOR NORRIS OBJECTED 

Now we learn that the father of TVA and REA was likewise disturbed. In the record of the hearings will be found a letter to the 

President from Senator George W. Norris dated June 5, 1939, from which the following sentences are taken: "I am very much worried about the REA. The recent order transferring this organization to the Department of Agriculture, I fear, is going to result in great damage to that Administration.... Mr. Carmody has kept the REA entirely out of politics. . . . He has been in a bitter fight with the private power companies and I doubt very much whether you are acquainted except in a general way with the details of the fight he has been compelled to carry on. 

Mr. Carmody has built up a wonderful organization. It commands the respect of the farmers of the 

Nation.... It is a great thing where the farmers of America are brought into perfect accord in their fight for common justice against the private power monopoly. And it would be a serious, even disastrous situation if this fight should be hampered or even temporarily suspended on its onward and upward march." 

-Hearings, Part II, p. 488. 

The prophetic insight of Administrator Carmody and of Senator Norris is emphasized in the introduction to this bulletin because they anticipated before the event exactly what this bi-partisan Committee discovered to have occurred after the event. It is now clear that the root cause of the "REA mess," as it is popularly designated, lies in the usurpation and abuse by Secretary Wickard, in his grab for political power, of management functions, the exer- 3

cise of which was fixed by law-even in the Reorganization Actin the Administrator of REA, and not in himself.

That basic conclusion must be held firmly in mind in the interest of an accurate diagnosis of this case. The cooperative and public power world has been confused and disturbed over conflicting statements as to the facts in the controversy, now over a year old. They differ as widely in sworn statements before the Committee as in the public prints and speeches and rumors before the investigation started. The conclusion goes straight to the heart of the matter and is as fundamental as the remedy suggested to cure the trouble in REA-restore its independence.

The controversy began with the efforts of the Department of Agriculture, the NRECA, and White House assistants to oust Mr. 

Slattery as head of REA. All of them based their action on the grounds that he was "inefficient," sidestepping their real objectives. 

The Committee rejected this explanation. It finds, in effect, that the chief trouble with Mr. Slattery from the viewpoints of Mr. 

Wickard and of the officers of NRECA was his obstinate refusal to join their cabal, put his o.K. on their respective schemes, and thus betray his public trust. A withering rebuke is conveyed in the observation that Mr. Slattery's alleged "incompetency" was "suddenly discovered."

The testimony makes it clear that any Administrator deprived of his authority as was Mr. Slattery could not have been efficient if his policies and selection of personnel to carry them out ran counter to the political objectives of Mr. Wickard and the racketeering insurance and other plans of the leaders of NRECA. Anyone reading the testimony must conclude that if Mr. Carmody, whose efficiency is unquestioned, had remained as Administrator he, too, would have met the same difficulties, since he also would have fought political scheming and private racketeering. 

THE COMMITTEE WAS FAIR 

The opponents of this investigation have made the widespread claim that it was initiated and conducted by anti-Roosevelt Senators to embarrass the President in this campaign. The conduct of the 

Committee does not justify any such charge. To be sure, Senator

Smith has been an outspoken opponent of the New Deal, but he has also been an outspoken friend of rural electrification and of REA. It is true that Senator Gillette did not always agree with 

New Deal policies and practices and the President tried to “purge" him in 1936. Likewise it is true that Senator Gillette has given faithful support to REA as a matter of earnest conviction. It is true that Senators Shipstead and Aiken are Republicans, but certain it is that they have fought for rural electrification from the beginning and are both public power men. Senator Shipstead supported Senator Norris throughout the fight for TVA and started a rural electric cooperative in his county a year before REA was authorized. Senator Aiken is the author of a bill now pending for public development of the St. Lawrence.

In fact Mr. Slattery and his supporters were cross questioned just as sternly as Mr. Craig, Secretary Wickard and their pro-

ponents.

Counsel Beedy was unbiased in his efforts to get at the truth. 

Rural Electrification is a non-partisan movement and this trouble in REA should be a non-partisan issue. No man who attended the hearings or who reads the printed reports can truthfully assert that Mr. Wickard or Mr. Daniels, or Mr. Hamilton was not treated with the utmost fairness when on the stand. Hence the candid inquirer will discard these rumors and look at the evidence which is too amazing in its character to sustain such mis representations. 

Again, it is claimed that Senator Shipstead's purpose in introducing the resolution was to whitewash Mr. Slattery. That, too, is dissipated by the evidence. If the opponents of this investigation have clear consciences why did they move heaven and earth to smother or divert it? Several administration men were active in that effort which failed. Why did Senator Lucas, chairman of the Senate Committee on Contingent Expenses, prevent the subcommittee from getting adequate funds to employ a sufficient staff and so speed up and make more effective this probe? 

There are a good many things those attending the hearings would like to have explained. For example, at every hearing of the 

Committee Mr. Maurice Mahoney, attorney for the Copperweld

Steel Company, was in active attendance as attorney for the former deputy administrator, Robert B. Craig, now an employee of the company. Why this concern over Mr. Craig's career while deputy administrator? Why also the constant attendance of Mr. Carlin 

Gray, Washington representative of the Copperwire Engineering 

Association? Further, why did Mr. Avery C. Moore who succeeded 

Clyde Ellis as executive manager of NRECA constantly sit with and fraternize with these gentlemen from the copper interests?

Everybody has been disappointed that this investigation has been so long drawn out. It is unfortunate and due, as the Committee explains, to two reasons; limitation of funds and their preoccupation with other great issues in war time. As they announced, their labors are not completed. They say they have requested information from Secretary Wickard which is yet to come and it is my prediction that other matters will see the light as startling as those already unearthed.

Nevertheless, ample evidence has been developed to support the Committee's findings that there is "an emergency situation within REA" which demands early relief; and to support its recommendation that "REA be, by law, returned to the status first assigned to it by the Congress, and this Committee recommends that that be done at the earliest possible date." Very well, just what was that "status" as fixed in the Norris-Rayburn Act of 1936, and how was it changed by the Reorganization Act of 1939? 

III. WHY AND HOW REA WAS MADE INDEPENDENT 

"It was hoped that the record of freedom from political activity and the efficiency of management and operation of this agency would compare favorably with the record of TVA which was also made an independent administrative agency not subject to interference in its program and management."-Report, p. 1. 765487

To avoid the notorious evils of political party patronage, red tape and to secure efficient management, many cities and states in creating new economic or social undertakings now place them under the control of separate boards or executives divorced, as much as possible, from politics and given the same freedom of action in business management as private corporations with which they are frequently in conflict. 

That was why TVA and REA were both made independent as the Committee notes. The first section of the Norris-Rayburn Act, drafted by Senator Norris, directs that "All of the powers (of REA) shall be exercised by an Administrator. The Reorganization 

Act of 1939 directs that the "functions and activities of REA shall be administered in that department by the Administrator of REA under the general direction and supervision of the Secretary of Agriculture." 

Just how Mr. Wickard, or the Comptroller General, can pick out of this language authority for what has been done to REA is beyond this layman's intelligence, or that of several high legal authorities he has consulted. A court decision on this is in order. 

III. HOW SECRETARY WALLACE EXERCISED "GENERAL SUPERVISION” 

"During the early years of the Administration it was administered in accord with the spirit of the Act... as an independent agency . REA gained the respect and commendation of Congress as well as of the people of the United States and was singularly free from adverse criticism."-Report, p. 2. 

Hon. Henry A. Wallace was Secretary of Agriculture when

REA came under him in July, 1939, and until he resigned on Sept. 

5, 1940. When Mr. Slattery was appointed Administrator Mr. Wallace assured him he had no intention to change the organization set-up of REA or to interfere with the day to day management of its work. Execution would be left to Mr. Slattery and his staff and the Secretary would hold him responsible. About once a week the two conferred over general policies. This was Mr. Wallace's conception of the meaning of "general supervision" and who it was to be "exercised by."

And incidentally, after nearly a year of this sort of relationship, on August 15, 1940, Mr. Wallace over a nation-wide radio hookup spoke of the manner in which management had been "exercised by" 

Mr. Slattery as follows: 

66 The Rural Electrification Administration, under the dynamic guidance of Harry Slattery, one of the most experienced, keen and respected men in public service today, will prompt and foster the continued development of rural electric distribution systems, engineered and managed for the benefit of the farm family.” -H. S. Reply to Wickard's Testimony, p. 1.

IV. WICKARD STARTS TO "INTEGRATE"-1940 

“Centralization of authority and responsibility placed by the Congress in the person of the Administrator became dissipated among the various agencies of the Agricultural Department."-Report, p. 1.

As students of language and applied democracy, Messrs. Wallace and Wickard were evidently trained in different schools of

thought. In November, 1940, shortly after he became Secretary, Mr. Wickard appointed an "Integration Committee" to take over

REA. It consisted of Under-Secretary Paul Appleby, chairman; 

Solicitor White, Budget Director Jump, Information Chief Eisen hower, Chief of Plant and Operations Thatcher, and others.

The job handed to these gentlemen was an imposition, whether or not they embraced it with joy and pride. They were expected in a few days of actual working time, presumably without harm to the patient, to dismember a highly specialized organism about which they knew nothing, either by training or practical experience. It was, as former Administrator Carmody, its chief architect, told the Committee, "a tight little organization," which had been built up over five years to accomplish a job-brand new in the history of either the utilities or the Government. Its dissection was a managerial crime. It was in fact a dis-integration Committee. 

Mr. Slattery made a member of this committee soon saw the dangers from the operation and declined to cooperate. Hence the next move of the determined Mr. Wickard was to eliminate Mr. 

Slattery starting in January, 1941. Mr. Slattery swore that Senator

Norris told him that Secretary Wickard had come to see him and stated that he desired to remove Slattery as Administrator and integrate REA into Agriculture. He said there was chaos in REA and that Slattery was "a very adamant man" who did not bend easily to administration policies.-Hearings, Part II, pp. 502-04. 

This testimony is based upon a memorandum Mr. Slattery dictated soon after his interview with the Senator.

The Senator turned thumbs down to all this, as did Vice President Wallace and Congressman Rankin, and the scheme had to wait for a more convenient season. Mr. Slattery in his reply to Wickard before the Committee notes that Wickard asked Norris to agree to "a coordinated administration in which we retain Mr. Slattery and place an additional Administrator." Norris replied that this was ridiculous, and that there was no provision for it in the law. 

However, the integration went forward in part. Agriculture took over personnel, and Slattery had no power to hire or fire. It also absorbed the Legal and Information Divisions, disbanded the Utilization Division, and so on, as the Report indicates. Crucial information was kept from the Administrator. Wickard had begun to take over.

V. MR. CRAIG PLANS AN ASSOCIATION TO "RUN" REA-1940 

"An intensive campaign was started by the officers of this organization 

(National Rural Electric Cooperative Association) and various employees of REA to induce the more than 800 local cooperatives to become members. Deputy Administrator Robert B. Craig under oath testified he was willing to accept the responsibility of parentage of this scheme the importance of such an organization for political purposes was estimated and appraised . . . after some time the true purpose of this organization became apparent. ."-Report, pp. 2-3. 

The Committee thus settles, for those in doubt, the long dispute over the origin of NRECA. It was not a "grass roots" organization inspired by the co-op farmers themselves, as Vice President Stoneman and ex-Executive Manager Clyde T. Ellis testified. It was admittedly the brain child of Mr. Craig who planned it, hand-picked

the ten men who incorporated it on March 20, 1942, worked out its insurance scheme, promoted its convention at St. Louis in January, 1943, and was the chief factor throughout in guiding its destinies up to, and perhaps even after, his resignation as deputy administrator of REA on May 5, 1943, ten days before he went to work for the Copperweld Steel Company. 

Driven into a corner and compelled to admit the truth Craig testified: 

Mr. Craig: "Mr. Beedy, let's straighten the record. I love it; it is my brain child." 

Mr. Beedy: "That is right. I don't want you to accept the paternity of anything that isn't yours.' -Part II, p. 320. 

Mr. John A. Becker of Wisconsin swore that Craig revealed the scheme to him in December, 1940, and affirmed his determination to "run REA" either from the inside or outside. The plan included the use of the association in politics and the organization of appliance and insurance mutual corporations, and a pretentious monthly magazine, all so set up as to give control and yield profits to the insiders. Becker was shocked and foresaw danger ahead. His memorandum written right after this interview is one of the most sensational documents in evidence for the reason that abundant testimony proves that the NRECA as organized and conducted was of the same pattern as Craig outlined to Becker. -See testimony of Carson and Carmody, Part I, pp. 6-57 and of 

Becker, Part III, pp. 801-46.

It is then of dramatic interest to note that in the winter of 

1940-41 Secretary Wickard and his subordinates, and Mr. Craig and his intimates, both were hatching plans to take over administrative control of the Rural Electrification Administration. It is of importance to observe that each concealed his real purpose. 

Wickard denied to the Committee that he had usurped administrative authority in practice. Stoneman today is running NRECA, as did Craig and Ellis at the start, and shaping its main policies without authorization by the membership. Both now want to forget Craig and let bygones be bygones.

THE WICKARD AND CRAIG FORCES JOIN UP 

But in 1940-41 Wickard had to wait for an opportune moment and Craig had to wait until his national association was in operation. In the spring of 1943 the time for action arrived and they joined forces to eliminate the chief stumbling block on their respective paths to power. That was the stubborn opposition of Harry 

Slattery to the dismemberment of REA, its uses for political purposes or to unsecured loans of the funds of the cooperatives to finance insurance companies.

Such a partnership is not surprising. Although it is not in the evidence it is well known to insiders in Washington and out that when Mr. Wickard was engaged in a despreate effort to get the appointment as Secretary of Agriculture in 1940, Mr. Craig, who at that time was highly regarded among the farm cooperatives, suddenly swung to his support. That, plus the supposed influence

of Craig's NRECA machine in the political field may help explain several mysteries necessary to examine at this point, not for the purpose of raking up scandal, but to understand the situation REA is now in and the alliance that is fighting against its independence. 

In promoting his schemes Craig kept in the background and pulled the strings. In the attempt to oust Mr. Slattery he worked through and with his co-partners in REA and the top officers of NRECA. That a cabinet officer should team up with such forces for such a purpose is amazing in itself. It is all the more astonishing in the light of Craig's history in REA, well known to the Secretary. 

Let us see.

The Copperweld-Aluminum Controversy. The Committee report states: 

"The evidence also indicates that one source of disorganization and trouble was the almost constant effort of some of the employees and engi neers of the REA to force upon the farm cooperatives a higher cost of conductor than was desired by some of the farm cooperatives. There was considerable evidence to indicate that the products of the Copperweld Steel Company were favored over aluminum by some of the engineers having to do with the approval of contracts. The testimony in regard to this controversy was conflicting, but the committee is of the opinion that there was more than a legitimate and ethical connection between some of the engineers and other personnel employed by the REA in relation to the selection of conductor and other construction materials. "The_testimony before the committee indicates that after the local co-ops had let contracts favoring aluminum as a lower cost conductor, pressure was brought to bear by employees of the REA to have the contracts cancelled and new contracts made permitting the use of a higher cost conductor."-Report, p. 2.

The evidence shows that the engineers and employees referred to were working directly under Mr. Craig when Acting Administrator from July to September, 1939, and later as Deputy Administrator. Through more than 100 pages of testimony the details of this sordid business were revealed by former staff men of REA- 

Mr. Freeman, Coordinator of Engineering; Messrs. Richter, Creim and Thomas, engineers, and Mr. Mercer Johnston, division operations and engineering. The high points of their testimony were promptly denied by Mr. Craig.-Hearings, Part I, pp. 57-121; Part 

II, pp. 123-214 and pp. 215-365.

The reader will note the significant conclusion of the Senators that there was something more than was "legitimate and ethical" at the bottom of this copper affair. I take it that this is one of the things that "deserve future study"-and perhaps action.

It is clear that the danger to REA in this situation was pointed out to Wickard shortly after he became secretary and that a Department investigation was in progress; that though prolonged many months, a report was made by J. K. Smith promptly suppressed and not even given to Administrator Slattery. Mr. Craig and Mr. Wickard on the stand had a difficult time in explaining these matters to the Committee.

FALSE REPORTS TO CONGRESS 

In 1941 serious inaccuracies and omissions were discovered by 

Congressman Dirksen in reports of Deputy Administrator Craig to the House Appropriations Committee, on promotions of REA employees, giving them higher ratings and salaries. Mr. Dirksen

was charged on the House floor by Congressman Rankin with attempting to smear REA and oppose public power. Mr. Dirksen hotly affirmed his interest in REA, and demanded honest management. He asked Wickard to investigate. It was done, the charges were found true and so reported by Wickard to the Appropriations 

Subcommittee on November 15, 1941. As Dirksen put it, Craig had "passed the buck" to subordinates who had prepared the data in the reports he had signed but which were prepared under his instructions. The subordinates were fired and Craig got off with a reprimand from Wickard. It is dated November 6, 1941, and con cluded as follows: 

"The reports in question contained false information with respect to promotions which you and your secretary had received during the fiscal year 1939, and, according to your testimony, you were not sufficiently concerned to examine the reports for the purpose of ascertaining what information had been included therein with respect to these promotions. . . . It has been decided, therefore, to issue this letter of reprimand, a copy of which will be filed in your service record, and to warn you that upon receipt of evidence of further lax administration or other improper actions on your part consideration will be given to the imposition of a more drastic penalty."-Hearings, Part II, pp. 353-4. See 

Cong. Rec., March 18, 1942, pp. 2449-50.

PROMOTING NRECA AGAINST ORDERS 

Again, when Secretary Wickard on July 22, 1942, warned all

REA employees not to take part in promoting the National's membership drive or otherwise, the order was ignored by Craig. He admitted that he had continued his actions in its behalf. Asked by 

Counsel Beedy if this did not violate the order, Craig blandly replied "It evidently was not assumed so by the Secretary," and promptly offered for the record the letter from Wickard accepting his resignation. It reads: "DEAR MR. CRAIG: 

“DEPARTMENT OF AGRICULTURE, 

Washington, May 6, 1943. "Assuming that you have fully considered your decision, I accept your resignation as Deputy Administrator of the Rural Electrification Administration, to become effective June 1, 1943. "In accepting your decision I am aware that it terminates a period of public service marked by unusual energy and dilligence, as well as a zealous devotion to the cause of electrifying rural America. You have played an important part in one of the most spectacular social pioneering activities which has been undertaken in the last decade. 

Sincerely yours,

CLAUDE R. WICKARD, Secretary. 

-Hearings, Part II, p. 307. 

At the time Mr. Wickard signed this cum laude his own investigators were looking into the record of Mr. Craig, and Craig himself was cooperating with the Tate-Ellis-Stoneman crowd in their attack upon Slattery. Craig testified that on June 7 he was helping 

Ellis at the NRECA headquarters in Washington prepare a "blockbuster" letter to President Roosevelt demanding Slattery's removal for incompetency, requesting an interview for Mr. Tate, and asserting that the Administrator was "wrecking REA." -For testimony and text of letters, see Hearings, Part II, pp. 

360, 320. 10

Should the reader ask, "In the light of all this, how could a cabinet officer protect and praise Mr. Craig? The answer is the old saw-that politics makes strange bed fellows. 

Before the Committee Mr. Wickard admitted he knew "Mr. 

Craig had certain charges pending against him." Asked if the thought had occurred to him that before accepting Craig's resignation he should have waited until the investigation was completed, the Secretary replied "No sir, I never gave it any thought at all." 

-Hearing, Part II, p. 799. 

VI. SLATTERY “ADAMANT” AGAINST POLITICS AND RACKETEERING 

IN REA "When these facts came to the knowledge of the Administrator, Mr. 

Slattery, he wrote a letter of warning to the cooperatives, telling them to 'stop, look, and listen.' He also wrote a letter to the Secretary of 

Agriculture, Mr. Wickard, to obtain a legal opinion as to the legality of the scheme permitting the local cooperatives to use their funds for such purpose, as of course their funds were under lien to the Government of the United States for loans."-Report, p. 3. 

We have seen that in 1941 Mr. Wickard failed in his initial ef-. fort to remove Mr. Slattery as Administrator. Relating Senator

Norris' account of the interview, Mr. Slattery testified Wickard thought him "a very adamant man"-who did not easily bend to administration policies. That quality has always surprised new acquaintances of this modest, pleasant mannered South Carolinian who dislikes and even shrinks from personal quarreling and maneuvering, but unexpectedly becomes an obstinate, fighting Irishman when his conservation principles, his public duty, or his personal honor are at stake. 

Messrs. Craig, Tate, Ellis, Stoneman, and others who have been running NRECA were next to discover the iron in Slattery's makeup. He had spoken at their St. Louis convention and favored their association, even the insurance proposal, so long as he thought it on the level and genuinely cooperative. But when they suddenly, under 

Craig's leadership, switched to a scheme to borrow over $1,000,000 from the cooperatives on unsecured notes to finance this insurance venture and demanded of him a letter to the Co-ops endorsing it, he flatly refused. Having been deprived of his legal staff, he in February requested Secretary Wickard to get from the Department

Solicitor an opinion on the legal right of the cooperatives to make such loans, as the Committee points out. 

That put Mr. Wickard on the spot. Here was the National association of his friends, the political availability of which had been “estimated and appraised" as the Committee found, asking a favor he hesitated to deny. So he procrastinated. Solicitor Shields' opinion questioning such loans was not handed out until May 6th and then, for some reason never explained, was promptly suppressed and withheld from the newspapers, the REA staff and the cooperatives.

Meantime, Mr. Slattery, desperate over a flood of requests from the cooperatives asking his advice had, on April 19th, issued the "stop, look and listen" letter noted in the Committee's report which, 11

as a hostile co-op superintendent remarked, "wrecked the gravy train.” That letter brought a prompt "cat thrashing" threat from

President Tate, speaking for the National Board, to drive the 

Administrator out of office and public life. Clever Mr. Wickard had sidestepped and allowed the Administrator to take the rap of angered frustration from the NRECA crowd. It is difficult not to conclude that he thought he had found in these NRECA officials an agency to accomplish the elimination of Mr. Slattery by abuse and slander which he had been unable to accomplish legally. 

VII. TEAM PLAY IN SLANDER 

On April 24th, five days after Slattery spiked their scheme, the 

NRECA leaders interviewed Wickard in his office and demanded his immediate removal. We must be specific at this point. As the hearings, at this date unprinted, will show there were present 

President Tate, Vice President Stoneman, Executive Manager Ellis,

Insurance Expert Moore, and board members Edmunds of Minnesota, Herrald of Indiana and Farrow of Texas. They then presented to Mr. Wickard what has since become known as the "floating memorandum" containing charges against the Administrator which, with like later documents, constitute the "sudden discovery" of evidence of his "incompetency" referred to in the Committee report. Up to that date he had been "Dear Harry" whom they professed to admire and love. 

On May 8th Ellis addressed a letter to President Roosevelt urging the "immediate" dismissal of Slattery. On June 7th he sent a "confidential memorandum" to REA cooperatives and all members of Congress seeking their support in his efforts for immediate removal. Both were filled with reckless falsehoods as the investigation discloses. Counsel Beedy described the "floating memorandum” as containing "very, very rotten, dirty, insinuating charges. . . involving Mr. Slattery's turpitude.' 99 

THE "FLOATING MEMORANDUM" 

And no wonder. It was an unsigned, undated, 4-page typewritten screed, carbon copies of which were circulated from the NRECA office. It listed and attacked the records of five members of the 

Administrator's staff described as "pensioners" who did nothing. 

I have a copy which named and in accompanying comment reflected upon the moral characters of seven young women of unquestioned reputation and ability who had long served REA, some of them in important posts. The late William Allen White called it "the scandal sheet."

Every person in REA or out acquainted with these men and women denounced the screed as wholesale slander, but noted that each person named had declined to join with Craig's machine in 

REA but had remained loyal to the Administrator. Ellis and his crowd were playing Craig's old game of slander with a vengeance.

The mention of such slime in this Bulletin dealing with important issues and policies would be unpardonable but for the astounding fact that the Secretary of Agriculture of the United States made this document the chief basis of and excuse for an 12

investigation of REA by the Division of Investigation of his department. It was even included in the report of the investigators sent to the President by Wickard requesting Slattery's removal. 

Counsel Beedy made heroic efforts to discover the authorship of this "floating memorandum" but failed. All living persons deny it. 

But on the stand Ellis swore it was handed to him in February or March, 1943, by Charles A. Falkenwald, then Chief of the Applications and Loans Division of REA who died on March 24, 1943. 

Friends of Falkenwald who know the whole inside story resent this testimony as a new low in the conduct of an ex-congressman and former executive head of a national association of honest, decent farmers and rural dwellers. They point out that prior to January 

24, 1943, "Charley" as he was affectionately known throughout the cooperative world, had had a violent quarrel with Craig over the new insurance loan plan and quit him; that he had revealed all this to the Administrator, as Mr. Slattery testified, and was hostile to Craig, Ellis, and the whole scheme. I personally got the same story from Charley himself a few days before his death and denounce Ellis' sworn testimony as slander of the dead.

THERE WERE NO “PENSIONERS” 

One climax came-and there are others to come-when the Committee put in the record the entire report of Wickard's investigators headed by Mr. V. A. Young. Here was discovered that the investigators found untrue the allegations against the so-called "pensioners" or "deadwood," the inactivity of whom Ellis had written threatened a scandal in Congress which he was trying to avoid, and who have been maligned, nationwide, by the NRECA officials. Mr. Young's report states: "In the investigator's opinion, the persons named in the complaint of the National Association cannot be described properly as 'pensioners,' and if it had not been for opposition to the National Association and/or Mr. Craig it is unlikely that such a charge would have been made against them. These workers are well qualified and appear to be conscientious workers. IX. TEAM PLAY IN POLITICS 

"The Congress desired to protect the administration of the REA program from all kinds of political influences, and desired to establish the REA as an agency of Government entirely free and independent of any and every influence other than that of the Act itself, and the officials and employees appointed to administer the Act."-Report, p. 5. 

On the stand witnesses from the Agriculture Department, from the National Association, and from the White House, assured the Committee, in substance, that in all their dealings the utilization of REA or the cooperatives for partisan political purposes was the furthest thing from their minds and hearts. They were concerned only in helping the farmers get electricity regardless of votes. Ex- 

Executive Manager Clyde Ellis, now a lieutenant in the Navy on leave from NRECA, and who announced in his farewell letter that he would return to his post after the war, was especially insistent on that point. But there stands the record of deeds, and what are we to do with that record and the record still being made before our eyes?

Take Craig's revelations to Becker and his later tie-in with 

Casey at the White House, Wickard and Hamilton of Agriculture. 

Take Wickard's early desire to acquire control over the appointment of all local REA cooperative managers. Take Wickard's refusal to allow Slattery to investigate Ellis' campaign for the Senate. Take 

Hamilton's introduction of Neal. Take Casey's speeches at the St.

Louis and Chicago NRECA conventions and at statewide meetings for the past year. Take Ellis' career as executive manager. Take 

Stoneman's latest deal with Wickard. Take the proposed regionalization of REA on a basis favorable to political manipulation. Such things spell something besides electric rural self-service! And there is much more to tell.

Mr. Craig told a House Committee he had done nothing to “guide the destinies" of the National. Mr. Wickard attempted to demonstrate at length to the Senate Committee that he had kept hands off this Association, and remained neutral. There was, however, a whole string of events which he overlooked, that indicate anything but neutrality. 

The Senate Resolution of July 1st last year pointed a finger straight at the NRECA and asked what efforts it had made "to influence the administration of the REA Act." It had already brought upon this fine agency its first shadow of public disgrace, and had disgusted scores of cooperatives. Many of them withdrew from membership, and others were refusing to join. Mr. Ellis and his board were under severe criticism, for reasons already stated.

Rebellion at the Chicago Convention. Despite this Administration help to the original NRECA promoters, all was not well at the 

Chicago convention. President Steve Tate, the businessman and

The Senate Committee in a questionnaire to the co-ops, posed this as one quetsion. The reply of the Pennsylvania Statewide Association is typical of the general sentiment. Its resolution unamimously adopted on March 17, 1944, furnished me by its President, reads: 

Resolved that the Pennsylvania Association of Rural Electric Cooperatives representing 28,000 members, favors the removal of the Rural Electrification Administration from the jurisdiction of the Department of Agriculture of the United States Federal Government and the reestablishment of said Rural Electrification Administration as an independent government agency; and

Be it further resolved that a certified copy of this resolution be transmitted immediately to the Committee of the United States Senate now investigating the affairs of the Rural Electrification Administration. 

A special convention of the National to ratify the by-law changes was held in St. Louis on May 23rd, where the question of independence was again by-passed. 

THE PAYOFF 

By this time two things were morally certain to happen, and soon-that the Senate Committee would make some unpleasant findings, and recommend independence for REA; second, that the 

Secretary of Agriculture, Mr. Wickard, would fight independence.

This prospect put the new and assumedly reform president of NRECA on the spot, along with his old Board of Directors. They were forced to choose between a politically-minded administration of REA, to which they were all heavily indebted, and the Cooperatives whose opinions they well knew and whom they represented. 

Mr. Stoneman did not hesitate any more than Tate, Ellis, or Craig would have done. He came to Washington right after the St. Louis convention, had a long conference with Mr. Wickard, and spent several days interviewing Senators and Representatives. On June 8th he attended a meeting of the Wisconsin cooperative managers at 20

Menomonie, and to the astonishment of his audience, made a speech advocating the retention of REA in Agriculture. He said that he was stating his own opinion and not the stand of the National. He holds that if REA could be made independent and remain so he would agree, but that it was impossible; that REA would need the help of Agriculture in the post-war era; that as a separate organization it would not have the strength to combat the private utiltiies; that it would not have the support of Congress, and hence it should be left under the protection of Agriculture.

The new Executive Manager, Mr. Moore, entertains these views. 

What the National Board thinks, assuming Stoneman is not proceeding under its direction, remains to be seen. At any rate, Stoneman has been making trips to other states and is busy trying to convert the co-ops to his position of standing by Mr. Wickard. 

Another opinion of Mr. Stoneman's is of significance. He holds that REA men have been too hostile to the private utilities and have too often voiced bitter accusations against them. He believes more could be gained by a friendly attitude and the spirit of cooperation with the utilities. This he thinks would result in more favorable wholesale rates. Informed listeners will hear this in an echo from the Department of Agriculture.

X. THE TASK AHEAD: FREE REA 

Andrew Carnegie once said that the worst thing that can happen to an industrial organization is the disbandonment and loss of its experienced, trained, and loyal employees.

That is what is happening in REA today, and is one factor making up the "emergency situation" which the Committee states requires prompt action.

Another is the shift of viewpoint and practice from business efficiency to political expediency, which is bound to influence harmfully the REA staff and the cooperative managers, directors, and membership, if they succumb to it.

The American people who lend the money to the cooperatives and the entire agricultural world are vitally interested in this issue. 

It is of concern to all consumer-producer cooperatives as a threat to the success of the cooperative principle operating on a large scale. 

It is of interest to labor, to manufacturers, and to business men for obvious reasons. It is of importance to students of government and business management.

The Committee's recommendation that the solution of the trouble lies in the return to independence as first established by Congress will be approved by all disinterested parties. We invite all organizations and individuals believing in this principle to aid in the passage of a bill for restoration "at the earliest possible date." 22

In the introduction to this Bulletin the apprehensions of Senator 

Norris and Administrator Carmody over the transfer of REA in 1939 were quoted. Let the conclusion show what they think today. 

States Mr. Carmody: "In spite of this forthright policy of independence that laid the foundation that not even maladministration has been able completely to destroy, REA during its period of independence never once caused the Administration a moment's embarrassment. It was a clean show. We took care of our own business. We told the selfish power operators to stay on their own side of the fence and we would stay on our side precisely as the Congress provided in the REA Act. We worked for the farm people and they worked with us. We had no subtle plans; our cards were on top of the table. We went on the theory that good administration will make plenty of friends for any administration and let it go at that. 

What a pity the tinkers tinkered in this case."

Writes Senator Norris from his home in McCook, Nebr., on 

August 5th: "You ask if I favor setting REA free. I answer emphatically 'Yes.' I am in favor of any bill that Congress can enact into law that will restore the REA to its independent organization and you can say so in the 

Bulletin. 

"I think the transfer of REA to the Agricultural Department in 1939 was a mistake. I said so then. I told the President it was. I regretted very much that this transfer took place. I have been opposed to this transfer to Agriculture or any other department ever since because the truth is that it will not fit in with any department. I have not changed my mind. REA ought to be an independent, nonpartisan organization and it must be if it is going to succeed. If it becomes a political football it is bound to fail."
