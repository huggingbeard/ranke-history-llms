REPORT TO THE PRESIDENT United States Tariff Commission July 24, 1959. 

To the President: 

On January 10, 1958, the United States Tariff Commission submitted to the President a report on its findings and recommendations in its "escape clause" investigation (No. 61) of stainless-steel table flatware, made under section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended. This report, supplemental to that of January 10, 1958, is made in response to the 

President's request of March 7, 1958. 

In its report of January 10, 1958, the Commission unanimously found that, as a result, in part, of concessions granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, stainless-steel table flatware measuring 10 inches or less in overall length, classifiable under paragraphs 339 and 355 of the Tariff Act of 1930, were being imported into the United States in such increased quantities, both actual and relative, as to cause serious injury to the domestic industry producing like products. 

In order to remedy such serious injury, Commissioners Brossard, Schreiber, and Sutton recommended that stainless-steel table flatware measuring 10 inches or less in overall length and valued under $3 per dozen pieces be subject to the rates of duty originally established therefor by the Tariff 

Act of 1930. 

Commissioners Talbot, Jones, and Dowling recommended that stainless-steel table flatware measuring 10. inches or less in overall length, regardless of value, be subject to the rates originally established in the Tariff Act of 1930.

On March 7, 1958, the President announced that action on the Commission's recommendation was inadvisable at that time and that he had decided that a full evaluation of Japan's voluntary limitation of shipments to the United States was necessary since this voluntary limitation signified an important

2 reduction of the volume of imports and thus held considerable promise of relieving the situation of domestic producers. The President, therefore, requested the Tariff Commission to keep this matter under review and to report to him as soon as practicable after December 31, 1958, with particular reference to the experience of the domestic industry in 1958, during which the Japanese limitation on exports to the United States would have been in effect. The President set forth his action and his reasons for it in identical letters to the chairmen of the House Ways and Means Committee and the Senate Finance Committee. The text of these letters is as follows: 

Under Section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended, the United States Tariff Commission reported to me on January 10, 1957 its finding that the domestic producers of stainless steel table flatware were experiencing serious injury as a result of increased imports. 

I have carefully studied the facts of this case, and I have had the benefit of the advice of the Trade Policy Committee and various departments and agencies of the Executive Branch. 

Although entirely satisfactory information is not available, especially for the year 1957, the Tariff Commission's report demonstrates a striking upward trend in imports with important consequences for domestic producers. Bearing on this situation, however, are two significant developments that the Commission has not had an opportunity to appraise fully. Japan, which accounted for more than ninety per cent of our imports in 1956, has limited its flatware exports to the United States. The first action in this regard set a limit of 5.9 million dozen for the year beginning last October first.

The Government of Japan has now informed this Government that it has decided to limit Japanese shipments to the United States to 5.5 million dozen for the current calendar year. 

These developments signify an important reduction in the volume of imports and thus hold considerable promise of relieving the situation of domestic producers. Because of this, I have concluded, after a thorough examination of the facts of this case, that a full evaluation of these developments is required and that action at this time on the Commission's recommendations is inadvisable. 

In order that the necessary evaluation might be as precise as possible, I have asked the Secretary of Commerce to see that appropriate information on flatware imports is officially collected and tabulated.

I am, moreover, requesting the Tariff Commission to keep this matter under review and to report to me as soon as practicable after December thirty-first with particular reference to the experience of the domestic industry in 1958 during which the Japanese limitation on exports to the United States will have been in effect. In the event that unusual circumstances require, I shall call upon the Commission for a report at an earlier date. 

The text of the President's letter of March 7, 1958, requesting the Tariff Commission to keep the matter under review and to report to him is as follows: 

For the reasons set forth in the enclosed copy of my letter of today to the Chairmen of the Senate Finance and the House Ways and Means Committees, I have concluded that action at this time is inadvisable on the Tariff Commission's recommendation of 

January 10, 1958 concerning stainless steel table flatware.

I request the Commission, however, to keep this matter under review and to report to me as soon as practicable after December thirty-first with particular reference to the experience of the domestic industry in 1958 during which the Japanese limitations on exports to the United States will have been in effect. 

In the event that unusual circumstances require, I shall call upon the Commission for a report on an earlier date. 

Accordingly, the Commission instituted an investigation supplemental to the above-mentioned investigation No. 61 under section 7 of the Trade Agreements Extension Act of 1951, as amended. 

Announcement of the supplemental investigation was published in the 

Federal Register on March 25, 1958 (23 F.R. 1976), and in the March 

27, 1958, issue of Treasury Decisions. A public hearing in connection with this supplemental investigation was held on April 21, 1959. The hearing was originally scheduled for March 17, 1959, but was ordered postponed by the Commission at the request of the applicants, the Stainless 

Steel Flatware Manufacturers Association. Public notice of the postponed hearing was given by publication of the notice in the Federal Register on

March 11, 1959 (24 F.R. 1763), and in the March 12, 1959, issue of Treasury Decisions.

This supplemental report is based not only on the information assembled by the Commission in connection with the original investigation, including the public hearing, but also by new information obtained by the Commission through questionnaires, fieldwork, the second public hearing, and data obtained from other Government agencies, especially data on imports for 1958 compiled by the U.S. Department of Commerce. 

This report, therefore, summarizes the information previously obtained for the years ending with 1956 and provides additional information for 1957 and 1958. As requested, special attention is devoted to imports of stainless-steel table flatware in 1958 and the experience of the domestic industry since 1956.

Description of Articles Investigated General description 

The articles covered by this investigation are table knives, forks, and spoons, wholly of metal and in chief value of stainless steel, hereinafter referred to collectively as stainless-steel table flatware. generally refers to knives, forks, and spoons commonly used for eating purposes in the home, as well as at tables or lunch counters in restaurants, hotels, and eating places of other establishments. Most table flatware is 10 inches or less in overall length. Many of the larger pieces consist of kitchen ware rather than table flatware.

All table flatware wholly of metal and in chief value of stainless steel, that is produced in the United States or imported into the United States is in fact wholly of stainless steel except that some hollow-handled 

Table flatware

knives contain a little solder or brazing material of other metal in the handles. The type of stainless steel used is corrosion resistant under the ordinary uses to which the flatware is put. This quality in steel is brought about by additions of chromium in amounts exceeding 10 percent Stainless steel containing only chromium and no nickel is nonaustenitic, and it is known in the trade as chrome steel. Stainless steel containing 

6 to 22 percent nickel in addition to 16 to 26 percent chromium is austenitic and is known in the trade as nickel steel. 1/ Nickel steel is more expensive than chrome steel. Also, it has a finer grain structure and it is somewhat easier to work if properly annealed. (Generally, flatware made of chrome steel is attracted by a magnet, whereas that made of nickel steel is not.) Flatware made of nickel steel can often be distinguished from that made of chrome steel by its "warmer," less bluish, appearance. 

By special polishing and buffing, however, chrome steel flatware can be made to resemble nickel steel flatware. 

Generally, three types of chrome steel are used by domestic manufacturers in making stainles-steel table flatware; these are designated by the American Iron and Steel Institute by type numbers 410, 420, and 430.

Types 410 and 420, used in knife blades only, contain 11.5 to 14 percent chromium with the rest principally iron; type 430, by far the most common type used in the United States in making stainless-steel knife handles, forks, and spoons, contains 14 to 18 percent chromium. At least one manufacturer also uses a type 440B chrome steel for knife blades which contains 16 to 18 percent chromium and which also differs from the other types of chrome steel in its higher carbon content; the higher carbon content permits making knife blades harder without making them brittle, and hence such In this report austenitic stainless steel is hereafter referred to as nickel steel and sonaustenitic stainless steel 53 chrome

knife blades retain their sharp cutting edges longer without resharpening. Type 301 nickel steel, used in the United States mostly in making the better grades of stainless-steel table flatware, generally contains 17 percent chromium and 7 percent nickel (with the rest principally iron). 

About one-tenth of the stainless-steel table flatware produced in the United States is made of stainless-steel scrap, including both the nickel and chrome varieties; generally, this flatware is ungraded, light in weight, and low priced.

The design, quality, and price of stainless-steel table flatware, whether produced in the United States or imported, vary widely. The quality of stainless-steel flatware is determined by its design or pattern, by the weight and type of stainless steel of which it is made, by grading, by the amount of finishing, and by the type of knife. Grading refers to the operation of varying the thickness from the end of the handles to the tip of the bowl of spoons or tines of forks, in order to obtain a proper balance of the piece and to leave strength where needed in the handle. 

Finishing involves the removal of rough edges and burrs from the sides of the pieces and from between the tines of forks, as well as the buffing and polishing of the surfaces. Four types of knives are produced (ranged in order of their cost of production in the United States from lowest to highest): Two-piece with blade not forged, one-piece forged, hollowhandled with blade not forged, and hollow-handled with forged blade. 

Comparability of domestic and imported flatware 

Stainless-steel table flatware is produced in many patterns and qualities in the United States, Japan, and European countries. Generally, the flatware imported from Japan is produced only after orders have been received,

and in accordance with the designs fumished by importers; some of the designs have proved to be copies of those developed by United States Some of the patterns imported from European countries are distinctive, especially the so-called modern designs, and have great appeal for certain buyers. At least a third of the European flatware also has an established prestige value because of its superior workmanship and origin; therefore, it is sold in the United States at prices above those for the most nearly comparable domestically produced flatware. manufacturers.

The following comparison of significant features of the stainlesssteel table flatware produced in the United States with those of flatware imported from Japan and Europe is derived from information submitted to the Tariff Commission by domestic manufacturers and importers on their best selling patterns in 1956. The total dollar volume of s ales in 1956 of the patterns for which such information was supplied amounted to about 50 percent of total sales by domestic manufacturers, 38 percent of the estimated total sales in the United States of flatware from Japan, and about 57 percent of the estimated sales in the United 

States of flatware from Europe.

In 1956 about 46 percent (based on total value of sales) of the stainless-steel table flatware produced in the United States was made of chrome steel, 13 percent was made of nickel steel, and the remaining 41 percent was comprised of patterns containing flatware made of both chrome steel and nickel steel or for which the type of stainless steel was not specified. In the same year about 41 percent (based on value of sales) of the stainlesssteel table flatware imported from Europe was made of chrome steel, and the

remaining 59 percent was made of nickel steel. flatware imported from Japan is made almost exclusively of chrome steel containing 13 percent chromium. The Japanese flatware is made generally from hot-rolled sheet, whereas the flatware produced in the United States is made from cold-rolled strip, which has a better surface quality. 

There is no information on the extent to which scrap stainless steel is now used for making flatware in foreign countries, although trade reports indicate it might still be used to some extent in Japan. 

About two-thirds of the domestic flatware and almost all of the European flatware sold in the United States in 1956 was graded, whereas only about half of the Japanese flatware sold in the United States was graded.

About half the domestic flatware sold in 1956 was lightweight and half 1/ was medium to heavy in weight; almost all of the European flatware sold was medium to heavy in weight, and about two-thirds of the Japanese flatware sold was lightweight. 

Stainless-steel table 1/ Lightweight refers to flatware for which the combined weight of a dozen teaspoons, a dozen soup spoons, and a dozen dinner forks is 40 ounces or less; medium to heavy weight refers to flatware for which the combined weight of these teaspoons, soup spoons, and dinner forks is more than 40 ounces.

United States Customs Treatment Changes in rates of duty 

Table knives and forks, wholly of metal and in chief value of stainless steel, are dutiable under paragraph 355 of the Tariff Act of 1930 (see table 1 in the appendix). They were originally dutiable under the Tariff Act of 1930 at either 2 cents each and 45 percent ad valorem or 8 cents each and 45 percent ad valorem, depending upon whether the length exclusive of handle was less than 4 inches or was 4 inches or over. These statutory rates were reduced pursuant to a concession granted in the bilateral trade agreement with the United Kingdom, effective January 1, 1939, and these reduced rates were subsequently bound against increase or further reduced pursuant to concessions granted in the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT). 

The current (July 1959) rate of duty on these knives and forks that are inches long or over, exclusive of handle, is 4 cents each and 17-1/2 percent ad valorem. The current rate of duty on such knives and forks that are less than 4 inches long, exclusive of handle, is 1 cent each and 17-1/2 percent ad valorem, if the handles are of austenitic steel and 1 cent each and 12-1/2 percent ad valorem, if the handles are of steel other than austenitic. These rates reflect the concessions under GATT that are currently in force. 

Table spoons (spoons for table use), wholly of metal and in chief value of stainless steel, were originally dutiable at 40 percent ad valorem under paragraph 339 of the Tariff Act of 1930. This rate was reduced pursuant to concessions granted in GATT, the latest being

a three-stage reduction from 20 percent to 17 percent ad valorem. The last stage of the reduction, to 17 percent ad valorem, became effective June 30, 1958. The rate of 17 percent therefore reflects the concession under GATT that is currently in force. 

Average ad valorem equivalents of current rates of duty The rates of duty currently in effect (July 1959) and their average ad valorem equivalents based on imports in 1958 are shown in table 2. 

The rate of duty applicable to spoons, 17 percent ad valorem, is lower than the average ad valorem equivalent of the compound rates applicable to knives (21 percent) and to forks (27.8 percent).

The rates of duty applicable to knives and forks with blades less than 4 inches long are lower than the rates applicable to knives and forks with blades 4 inches long or over. As a consequence, imports (unlike domestic production) consist principally of flatware with blades less than 4 inches long, which results in a lower average ad valorem equivalent of the rates applicable to imports with blades less than ↳ inches long (23.2 percent) than for imports with blades 4 inches or more in length (29.3 percent).

Average ad valorem equivalents of current duties on stainless-steel table knives, forks, and spoons based on data on imports in 1958 are somewhat lower than similar averages computed on the basis of data on imports in 1956 (shown in table 2 of the Commission's report of January 1958).

The average ad valorem equivalents of the compound rates of duty on imports of knives and forks from Japan are substantially higher than those on imports of knives and forks from Europe, inasmuch as imports from Japan have substantially lower unit values than imports from Europe (table 2).

General United States Consumption 

The use of stainless-steel table flatware has increased rapidly since World War II. In 1953, the first year for which complete data are available, apparent consumption in the United States amounted to about 11,563,000 dozen pieces, computed as follows: Sales by domestic manufacturers of 

10,827,000 dozen plus imports of 883,000 dozen minus exports of 147,000 dozen (table 3). Apparent consumption in 1954 increased to 12,050,000 dozen, principally as a result of increased imports, since manufacturers' sales remained virtually unchanged and exports were small. In 1955, apparent consumption rose sharply to about 18,081,000 dozen, reflecting both a substantial increase in sales by domestic manufacturers and a large increase in imports. In 1956, apparent consumption rose to 22,309,000 dozen, despite a small decrease in sales by domestic manufacturers, because imports more than doubled the previous year's volume. The above data were included in the Commission's original report to the President in January 1958. 

In 1957 (according to data assembled in the Commission's supplemental investigation), apparent consumption of stainless-steel table flatware in the United States reached a record peak estimated at 

23,725,000 dozen pieces. While sales of domestically produced flatware declined from 14,392,000 dozen in 1956 to 13,207,000 dozen in 1957, imports rose to a record level estimated at 10,600,000 dozen. 

In 1958, apparent consumption declined to 22,775,000 dozen pieces, probably partly reflecting the business recession in the United States which began toward the end of 1957. Imports declined in 1958 to 

9,180,000 dozen, a level higher than that of any previous year except 1957. Sales of domestically produced flatware rose to a record peak of

1,887,000 dozen in 1958, most of the increase over the previous year 

Exports, amounting to 1,292,000 dozen in 1958, were being exported. many times higher than in any previous year. 

The growth in the postwar consumption of stainless-steel table flatware is attributable partly to the trend toward modern house furnishings and decor, to which the simpler designs of stainless-steel table flatware are suitable. It is also attributable to the availability of this flatware at moderate prices, to its novelty, durability (silver-plated ware, for example, is easily scratched and its plate eventually wears off), and to the preference of many housewives for a type of tableware which requires little or no polishing. The trend toward increased domestic production of stainless-steel flatware was accelerated by wartime shortages of silver, and of copper and nickel (the metals used in making nickel silver, which is the base metal for silverplating) and by the resulting efforts to use less critical metals. 

Also, during World War II and later during the Korean emergency, Government contracts for durable yet inexpensive stainless-steel flatware for mess kits for the armed forces gave manufacturers the opportunity to develop techniques and gain experience for making chrome-type stainlesssteel flatware.

Ratio of imports to consumption and to production In the period 1953-57, a steadily increasing proportion of total apparent consumption of stainless-steel table flatware in the United States was supplied by imports. The ratio of imports to consumption increased from 7.6 percent in 1953 to 11.2 percent in 1954, to 19.8 percent in 1955, to 35.9 percent in 1956, and to 44.7 percent (estimated)

in 1957. In 1958, however, the share of the United States market supplied by imports declined to 40.3 percent. 

United States imports of stainless-steel table flatware increased, both actually and relative to total domestic production, from 1953 to 1957, and then declined in 1958 (table 4). The ratio of imports to domestic production increased from 8.0 percent in 1953 to 24.1 percent in 1955. Imports in the next 3 years were equivalent to more than half of the domestic production in those years, amounting to 54.4 percent of production in 1956, to 81 percent (estimated) in 1957, and to 61.1 percent in 1958. The reduction in the ratio from about 81 percent in 1957 to 

61.1 percent in 1958 reflects an actual decline in imports, as well as a decline relative to production.

United States Industry Description of the industry 

Stainless-steel table flatware was produced in the United States by 26 different companies at one time or another during 1951-58. There were 

18 producers in each of the years 1951 and 1952, 19 in 1953, 23 in 1954, 22 in 1955, 21 in each of the years 1956 and 1957, and 20 in 1958. Eight companies, mostly small producers, did not begin producing stainless-steel table flatware until sometime after 1951 (two in 1953, four in 1954, one in 1956, and one in 1958). Seven companies, including one very small producer, discontinued the production of stainless-steel table flatware after 1951 (one in 1953, one in 1954, two in 1955, two in 1957, and one at the end of 1958); of these seven former producers, three are now importing this type of flatware.

The four largest domestic manufacturers, each of which produced more than 1 million dozen pieces a year during 1954-58, accounted for 62-63 percent of the total quantity, and 68-71 percent of the total value of stainless-steel table flatware sold by domestic producers during 1956-58.

Stainless-steel table flatware was produced in the last 3 years, 1956-58, in nine States (table 5). Of the total quantity of domestically produced flatware sold in the United States during these 3 years, 60 percent was produced in Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Massachusetts;

Virginia, Ohio, Indiana, and California. In terms of total factory value of sales, the three groups of States accounted for 63 percent, 29 percent, and 8 percent of the total, respectively.

Description of flatware manufacturing processes 

Stainless steel strip of requisite gage and width is fed to machines which punch out blanks for spoons, forks, and for flat knife blades. The three spaces between the fork tines are punched out of the blanks either one at a time, or first two, then one; a piece of metal is left across the ends of the four tines for convenience in further forming, after which it is cut off. From 30 to 50 percent of the stainless steel in the original strip used in making flatware becomes plant scrap. This scrap is sold because it cannot be reused at the plants; prices realized on sales of scrap are very low--generally amounting to less than 2 percent of the prices paid for the original metal.

In making the lower priced, ungraded flatware from lightweight stock, the blanks are fed to drophammers or presses for forming and 1/ for imparting (embossing) the pattern on the handles. 

After it is formed, the cheaper flatware is tumbled (revolved in wood-lined vessels containing shot) and cleaned without any edge or surface trimming or finishing. The fair to good quality flatware is at least lightly edged and surface buffed or polished. In the highest quality flatware the outside edges and the edges between the fork tines are trimmed and the surfaces are fully polished and buffed to either a mirror or a satin finish. These trimming and finishing operations constitute a large part of the cost of making the higher quality flatware.

As the dies wear out, new ones are made from the master die. The making of these dies requires highly skilled labor, and the stock of dies maintained for the various patterns of flatware represents a sizable investmen

Semigraded flatware (in which the bowls of the spoons and the tines of the forks are made thinner than the thickness of the original blanks) and fully graded flatware (in which the ends of the handles are also made thinner) require additional processing. The blanks must be partly rolled to achieve the desired variation in thickness. With heavy or repeated rolling the materials become hard to work and must be annealed to relieve the stresses developed in the rolling, that is, the rolled blanks are heated to a high temperature before further forming.. 

Knives are more costly to manufacture than spoons or forks, and they require special equipment. The least expensive type of knife is made of two pieces. The handle is made of rod stock which must be slotted or drilled st one end to receive the tang of the blade. A one-piece knife is made of bar or rod stock, and, because of the thickness of the material, it must be forged into shape after the stock has been properly heated. The most expensive type of knife has a hollow handle and is made of three pieces. 

The handle of this type of knife is made of two formed pieces that are brazed together and trimmed, and a blade, either flat or forged, that is inserted and soldered into the handle.

The manufacturing processes and types of machinery used in the manufacture of flatware vary among the producers, depending upon the type and volume of flatware being produced and the ingenuity and capital resources of the manufacturers. The largest manufacturers, who regularly process many pieces of one pattern in a single run, have introduced numerous labor-saving devices, especially to increase the speed of the machines and to eliminate some of the hand-feeding and hand-trimming or polishing operations.

Trend of production, manufacturers' inventories, and sales After declining from 1951 to 1952 owing to raw material shortages, production and sales of stainless-steel table flatware by domestic producers increased almost continuously to 1955 (table 8). They rose moderately in 1953 and 1954, and they rose sharply in 1955. In that year the quantity of production reached a postwar peak of 14,848,000 dozen pieces, and the quantity of sales, 14,654,000 dozen, was higher than in any previous year. In 

1956 the quantity of production was 1.0 percent (14,695,000 dozen) and the quantity of sales (14,392,000 dozen) was 1.8 percent, below that of the previous year; however, because of an increase in unit values, the value of sales in 1956 was 10.3 percent above the value of sales in 1955.

Production and sales declined further in 1957. The quantity produced, amounting to 13,079,000 dozen pieces, was 11.0 percent below that of 1956, while the quantity sold was 8.2 percent below and the value of sales was 5.2 percent below the 1956 level. These reductions reflect the effect of import competition and, toward the end of 1957, the business recession.

Production and sales (quantity and value) reached record levels in 1958, however, despite the continuation of the business recession through the first half of that year. The production of 15,030,000 dozen pieces in 1958, was 14.9 percent higher than in 1957, when imports were at a record level. The quantity of domestic flatware sold in 1958 (14,887,000 dozen pieces), was 12.7 percent above the 1957 level; and the value of sales in 1958 ($31,554,000) was 6.5 percent above that of the previous year.

Employment, wages, and labor productivity

The average number of all employees in the stainless-steel table flatware industry in the United States ranged between about 2,000 and 2,400 durin the years 1951-54 and increased to close to 3,000 employees during 1955-58 (table 12). The number of all employees in 1958 was 3,057, compared with 

2,888 in 1957 and the postwar peak of 3,080 in 1955. The number of production and related workers employed during 1951-58 averaged about four-fifths of the average number of all employees; the number of such employees in 1958 averaged 2,326 compared with 2,248 in 1957 and 2,501 in 1955.

During the period covered by the investigation, the changes in the man-hours (paid for) of production and related workers corresponded generally to the changes in the number of workers employed. However, the number of production and related workers and the number of man-hours of such workers increased less rapidly over the 7-year period 1951-58 than production because of an increase in labor productivity.

The output of stainless-steel table flatware per man-hour of production and related workers for the industry as a whole rose continuously from 

2.6 dozen pieces in 1952 to 2.9 dozen in 1956, declined to 2.8 dozen in 

1957 (when production also declined), and increased to 3.2 dozen in 1958 (when production also increased). The change in the number of pieces of flatware produced per man-hour, however, understates the actual increase in labor productivity, for the general quality of the flatware produced by the industry as a whole also improved, as evidenced by the fact that the increase in the average unit value of sales from 1953 to 1958 was substantially greater than the increase in the selling prices of the flatware produced. 1

Total wages paid production and related workers in the stainless- I steel table flatware industry increased from $6,822,000 in 1951 to a record peak of $10,034,000 in 1955, representing an increase of 47 percent. 

Therefore, as production declined, wage payments declined to $9,820,000 in 1956, and to $9,225,000 in 1957. However, with higher output in 1958, = total wage payments rose to $9,895,000. Total wages paid to production and related workers for the industry as a whole are equal to almost onethird of the sales value of the flatware produced. 

Except for a slight decline in 1954, average hourly wage payments to production and related workers engaged in making stainless-steel table flatware increased continuously for the industry as a whole from an average of $1.60 per man-hour in 1951 to $2.08 in 1958, representing an increase of 

30 percent.

The wages paid to production and related workers as given above do not include all of the "fringe benefits" received by the workers. Although they include such benefits as pay for sick leave, holidays, and vacations taken, they do not include cash payments for vacations not taken, retroactive pay not earned during the period reported, value of payments in kind, company contributions to welfare funds and insurance or pension plans, or commissions and bonuses unless earned and paid regularly each pay period. On the basis of information reported to the Tariff Commission by 17 companies (which accounted for 94 percent of total wages paid in the industry) total wages paid-including all fringe benefits reported--would be higher than the reported wages by about 7 percent in 1951 and 1952; 8 percent in 1953, 1954, and 1955; and about 9 percent in 1956, 1957, and 1958. Including all fringe benefits reported to the Commission by the producers, total wage payments probably averaged $1.72

in 1951, $1.81 in 1952, $1.88 in 1953, $1.85 in 1954, $2.03 in 1955, $2.12 in 1956, $2.16 in 1957, and $2.26 in 1958; the reported average hourly payments in 1958 were about 31 percent above the industry average for 1951.

Prices paid for stainless steel

The cost of stainless steel strip or rod is equivalent to about onethird of the sales value of stainless-steel table flatware produced in the United States. This cost is approximately equal to wages paid to production and related workers. The relative importance of labor and stainless steel in the total cost of manufacturing stainless-steel table flatware varies, however, with the quality of the flatware produced; the labor cost is relatively more important in the production of high-quality flatware, which requires much polishing or buffing and finishing. Data supplied to the 

Tariff Commission by three representative manufacturers indicate that the prices paid for chrome stainless steel rose about 15 to 17 percent between mid-1953 and mid-1957, and that the prices paid for nickel stainless steel increased by about 25 percent over the same interval of time. At the public hearing in April 1959, the Commission received information from manufacturers that after July 1, 1957, there was a further increase of about 5 percent in the price of stainless-steel strip and rod.

Number and size of importing firms Stainless-steel table flatware was imported into the United States by about 50 importers in 1953, by at least 148 in 1956, by about 180 in 1957, and by about 217 in 1958; reports received by the Tariff Commission from 202 importers accounted for 78 percent of the total quantity imported in 1958.

A large percentage of total imports is accounted for by a small number of large importers. In 1956 the 6 largest importers that reported to the Commission, each importing more than 300,000 dozen pieces, accounted for 53 percent of the quantity and 46 percent of the entered value of all imports; the 18 largest importers, each importing more than 100,000 dozen, accounted for 79 percent of the quantity and 67 percent of the entered value of all imports. Japan's voluntary export quota

In the fall of 1957, when the Tariff Commission's escape-clause investigation (No. 61) on stainless-steel table flatware was underway, the Japanese Government announced that it would limit its exports of stainless-steel table flatware to the United States to 5.9 million dozen pieces for the year beginning on October 1, 1957.

In March 1958 Japan announced that it had postponed the initial date for beginning its voluntary limitation of exports to the United States to January 1, 1958, and that the quota for the calendar year 1958 would be limited to 5.5 million dozen pieces of stainless-steel table flatware 26 centimeters or less in overall length. This length limit, equivalent to 10.2 inches, was apparently adopted in view of the 

Tariff Commission's recommendations in its report to the President in January 1958 that the increased duties recommended be limited to stainlesssteel table flatware 10 inches or less in overall length.

La 

In administering the quota, the Japanese Government through the 

Ministry of International. Trade and Industry (MITI) issued export licenses during 1958 authorizing exports totaling 5,457,554 dozen pieces (table 17), a quantity slightly smaller than the 1958 quota. Actual licensed exports to the United States in 1958 totaled 5,378,604 dozen.

However, export licenses were usable within 3 months after the date of issuance, so that some additional flatware under the 1958 export quota was exported during January, February, or March of 1959.

Imports by types of flatware 

The United States Department of Commerce has compiled detailed data on United States imports of stainless-steel table flatware in 1958, segregating data for knives, forks, and spoons and dividing each of these types into items measuring 10.2 inches or less in overall length and those measuring more than 10.2 inches in length. Knives and forks were further classified according to whether or not the blades were less than 4 inches in length; and for those which were less than 4 inches in length according to whether or not the handles were of chrome steel (nonaustenitic) or of nickel steel (austenitic). These classifications of knives and forks are subject to different rates of duty (table 1). In addition to the above classifications based on physical characteristics, imports were classified according to country of origin and whether or not their entered value averaged less than $3.00 per dozen. Each import entry was separately analyzed; the items were first classified according to their physical characteristics and country of origin and then further classified according to average unit value per dozen. These data are summarized in tables 19 and 20 in the appendix.

The great bulk of all stainless steel flatware imported in 1958, about 99 percent of the total quantity, measured 10.2 inches or less in overall length. About 98 percent of the total quantity imported from 

Japan had an average entered value of less than $3.00 per dozen. Of the total quantity imported from Europe, slightly less than half was valued at less than $3.00 per dozen and slightly more than half was valued at $3.00 or more per dozen.

Importers' costs

Data on cost of imports of stainless-steel table flatware were reported to the Tariff Commission by 121 importers in 1956 and by 

198 importers in 1958 (table 21). The importers reporting costs in 

1956 accounted for 89 percent of the total quantity of imports, and those reporting in 1958 accounted for 78 percent. The average foreign value of imports from all countries was $1.10 per dozen pieces in 1956, compared with $0.98 per dozen in 1958. The average cost of delivery to the United States port of entry (including foreign handling charges, consular fees, commissions, and the like costs incident to placing the shipment on board the vessel in the foreign port, ocean freight, and other transportation costs to the United States port of entry) was 8 cents per dozen pieces in 1956 and 6 cents per dozen in 1958. The average duty paid in 1956 was 24 cents per dozen, or 21 percent of foreign value; in 1958 it was 20 cents per dozen, or 21 percent of foreign value. Additional charges in the United States incident to delivery of the flatware from the port of entry to the point from which shipments are made to customers (such as wharfage, cartage, and customs brokerage) averaged 3 cents per dozen pieces in 1956 and 4 cents per dozen in 1958. Thus, the average landed duty-paid cost was $1.45 per dozen in 1956 (31.9 percent above the average foreign value), and in 1958 it was $1.28 (30.7 percent above the average foreign value).

All of the elements of cost enumerated above were substantially higher for importers that imported exclusively or predominantly (90 percent or more in terms of quantity) from Europe than for those that imported exclusively or predominantly from Japan. .

Information received by the Commission indicates that several steps recently taken in Japan have placed Japanese manufacturers in a more favorable bargaining position vis-a-vis Japanese exporters, United States buyers, and importers; as a corollary,

Japanese manufacturers' selling prices have increased. 

January 1, 1959, quota allocations were transferred from exporters to manufacturers. This enabled the Japanese manufacturers to pick and choose the highest bidder from among the exporters rather than bid against each other. Another measure strengthening the financial position of Japanese manufacturers was supplied when the Central Cooperative Bank of Commerce and Industry in Japan agreed to underwrite loans to the flatware manufacturers, thereby reducing their need of relying on exporters for financing.
