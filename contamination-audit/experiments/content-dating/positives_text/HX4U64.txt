The Missouri 

Still Runs Wild by 

R. Lester Mondale 

The Missouri is now a Ghost River... fearful most of all to the river people. 

Onto this treacherous stream, in an eighteen-foot canvas canoe, venture three men, sickened of alarm clocks, time clocks and telephones. They discover a wilderness stretching four hundred miles from Kansas City to 

St. Louis... a wilderness into which civilization cannot and will not enter. 

Losing themselves in this wilderness they find the Great Blue Heron, the 

Giant Bull Frog, "River Rats", a

Witches' Sabbath, The Queen of the 

Hoboes, storms and laughter. and they find themselves.

THE MISSOURI STILL RUNS WILD By R. LESTER MONDALE 

I go with my friend to the shore of our little river, and with one stroke of the paddle, I leave the village politics and personalities, yes, and the world of villages and personalities behind, and pass into a delicate realm of sunset and moonlight We penetrate bodily this incredible beauty; we dip our hands in this painted element: our eyes are bathed in these lights and forms am taught the poorness of our invention, the ugliness of towns and palaces. Art and luxury have early learned that they must work as enhancement and sequel to this orginal beauty. I am overinstructed for my return. 

Henceforth I shall be hard to please. I cannot go back to toys. I am grown expensive and sophisticated."-Emerson.

Jo Abe & Al Fellow Paddlers

The peculiar expressions we saw on the faces of our wives as we parted to step into the canoe, settled heavily in the turbid Missouri below, were as much of a study as the river racing 'round the bend at the foot of Grand Avenue in Kansas City. They had watched us slide and stumble mightily down the gullied and rocky embankment and carefully drop eighteen feet of old orange canoe into the grayish-brown silt-saturated current. They had watched us lug the three army cots, two hard-packed canvas duffel-bags, an orange crate of provisions, gallon water cans (formerly for turpentine), small gasoline stove, paddles, cushion and lazy back out of the car and then pass them hand to hand and fit them like pieces of a puzzle into the open boat. As they stood there, some fifteen feet above us, sharply silhouetted against the hot July 11 sky, we saw a thinish young man come up to them and point toward the massive steel bridge below us and down the river. He shook his head, and then went back to the power plant across the street. He had warned the women. He had done his duty. It was "risky and foolish to think of getting to St. Louis in that mantrap." He

This was merely the finale of the disturbing Cassandra chorus we had been hearing. "My father spent all his life on the banks of the Missouri. thought he knew his way around until one day he swam into a whirlpool. It sucked him down and when he woke up he was lying washed onto a sand bank and our old shepherd dog was licking his face." And other 1

themes of the chorus: The terrifying quicksands: "An uncle of mine lost a herd of cows in them once." The treacherous bottom: "Why, one step and you'll go from water only up to your knees into a hole way over your head." The powerful eddys, the boils, the shores caving in on boats, the racehorse speed and irresistible grip of the current in spots. And then the literature of the Missouri: The first white man's account, Father 

Marquette's notation of July 4, 1673—“As we were sailing down a beautiful, still, clear water we heard the noise of a rapid... I have seen nothing more frightful; a mass of large trees, entire with branches, real floating islands, came rushing from the mouth of the river Pekitanouii (probably the Illinois Indians' name for the Missouri) so impetuously that we could not without great danger expose ourselves to pass across. The agitation was so great that the water was all muddy and could not get clear. Pekitanouii is a considerable river which, coming from very far in the northwest, empties into the Mississippi. Many Indian towns are ranged along the river. I hope by its means to make the discovery of the Red or California Sea." From the log of the Lewis and Clark expedition in 1804 come similarly vivid paragraphs. It tells of sticking on snags, of running into shallows where the charging stream and suddenly shifting bottom sands would have flooded if not actually overturned their fifty-foot keel boat had not all extra hands quickly jumped overboard and thrown their strength on the side of the others sweating at the oarsweeps. Lewis also wrote of the unpredictable squalls, sweeping up and down and across the wideshouldered Missouri valley, that so frequently held them paralyzed in midstream. And from the steamboat's halcyon era, the innumerable accounts, each of which invariably included "snags," "bars," "wrecks."

Each of us, however, regarded himself as a competent swimmer. The duffel-bags and the canoe would float. The camera we sealed in a molasses can tied to a cross bar. The three of us, moreover, were not entirely unacquainted with the river's personality. Both Ivan 

Dillee (a lay minister in the Church of the Latter Day Saints, and in the enameling business) and I (minister of All Souls Unitarian Church of Kansas City) had 2

been with Kelley (head machinist of Linotype maintenance of the K. C. Star) for all-day canoe trips above and below the city. Thus, regardless of any lack of confidence Dillee and I may have felt in ourselves, and the Cassandra chorus had not been entirely ineffective, we had learned to rely on the river experience gathered by Kelley, "Cap'n," on his several trips in this same brilliant orange canoe (he bought it in 1914, second hand) down the river from Fort Benton, Montana, down the Mississippi to New Orleans, and, once before, to St. Louis. He knew enough about tawney "Old 

Woman River" to warn us solemnly that "You can't get very far with her on a swagger." However much fun the trip might be it was also a serious business. 

The wives on the shore, only one of whom could boast of "river legs," betrayed in their faces that they felt the full brunt of the serious side. Dominant in their peculiar farewell expressions, however, was a look of puzzlement. Just why were these men, “men, mind you," so avid to subject themselves to a week's broiling and sweating and the misery of showers by day and by night? This question when put to Dillee several hundred miles down the river brought the answer: "New experience; something to talk about." 

Then he began to qualify. "That's what I had in mind when we slid off Saturday; but I felt at the time that that was merely an excuse, a rationalization for needs that are now being taken care of, but which I don't believe I could put into words even now." Kelley, much the same: "I used to think I had to paddle for my health. But I don't know now. Health is such a doggone complicated thing. My shouting for the pure joy of it when this seahorse clears a big wave is part of health... and so is a sunset across the river." But why a trip on the muddy Missouri-such a sewery contrast (we happened to be tied up between vile and belching six-foot sewer vents) to a clear water trip in the Ozarks or a clean, safe jaunt in the Rockies; or even a week on a 

Minnesota lake? Romance in this? Taking off from a slummy Kansas City backyard, and going down a mudbanked river decent people aren't supposed to swim in, and along which no one but a "river rat" or a duck hunter would throw up a shelter?

Even in the W. P. A. Writers Handbook of Mis souri, in which are listed numerous scenic and historic tours of the State, there is not as much as a word about the possibilities of the river itself being a tour trail. The use of the river as a trail ended with the coming of the railroad and the Civil War. Thus the last seventy-five years have witnessed a steady decrease in the number of those who know something of this, the once much-traveled thoroughfare into the west, the route over which passed pioneers by the hundreds of thousands in their bloody race for Kansas and the California gold fields. In the words of an old river man, written more than a generation ago: "The glory of the past is gone. The evolution is complete. The Indian canoe, the perogue, the batteau, the keelboat, the mackinaw boat, the steamboat have all passed away. and there now remains, on what was once the great communal thoroughfare of the West, only the original navigator-the little blue-wing teal duck. The recollection of steamboating on the Missouri River is, to the old steamboatman, but a pleasant dream of the past." (P. E. Chappell). Today the river is more of a memory than a river. Only one in a hundred thousand swims in it. Almost no one, except a few professionals, fishes it. When a government-subsidized freighter arrives in Kansas City it brings newspaper headlines. 

The canoes and pleasure boats used locally on the river can be counted on the fingers of two hands. Once each spring the populace awakens to the river's existence when it rises from the dead and floods its banks. 

The remainder of the year it is a ghost river, fearful to children who for generations have been frightened away from its real perils by a cumulatively terrorizing folklore, but fearful most of all to the river people themselves, who, according to Cap'n Kelley, are always the most skeptical of the river-worthiness of a canoe. 

What compulsion, what inner hunger lay behind this long-planned expedition? The desire to acquaint ourselves at first hand with a great fact: that the Missouri river is still the greatest fact in Missouri? -the carver of the roadbeds for the interminable trains of the Katy line to the north or the Missouri Pacific stream-

liners to the south, both of which rumble through the valley with a sound that makes the hearer think of the reverberations of the lonesome drums of the Fingerlake country of New York State?—the landscaper of the hills and bluffs of beautiful Kansas City and Jefferson

City? the creator, with the Mississippi, of rich bottom lands on which two-thirds of the millions of Missouri live? —the State's last and uncivilizeable frontier and wilderness, as we were to realize so poignantly in but a few hours? Why our zeal to explore 387 miles of, the reaches and bends lying between Kansas City and the mouth, sixteen miles above St. Louis? Perhaps the best answer comes from the words of a chief of the Osage Indians who with their hollow-log canoes are supposed, according to one of several conflicting traditions, to have given the Missouri its name, "hollow-log-people." The chief, Has-hak-da-Tangar (Big Soldier) declared to a white friend: "You are surrounded by slaves. Everything about you is in chains, and you are in chains yourselves. I fear if I should change my pursuit for yours, I too, should become a slave."

Whatever the cravings, hidden or otherwise, there was no mistaking the utter boyishness of the cry that went up from the canoe as we pulled the new twentyfive-foot clothes-line anchor rope down the bank and I, with one foot in the boat and the other on a rock, nosed her into the river: “All aboard for St. Louis. . 

New Orleans. . . Trinidad. . . Cape of Good Hope... and points south!" The boyhood dreams in which we had lived in imagination with Huckleberry Finn and with 

Marquette and Joliet, De Soto, and young Lincoln on the Mississippi were now a reality. And boys we truly were again as we laughingly pulled out of the sewer fumes and with strong, clean strokes of our paddles swung around in a semi-circle and into the torrential current. "Hey! Wait a second!" Paddling ceased. A bellow to the shore. "Did you remember to take the butter jar out of the refrigerator and pack it?" A frantic and helpless waving of the arms of one of the wives and a disconcerted cry, "Oh heavens. No!" First casualty, no butter. Thereupon, a parting shout from Dillee,

directed to another of the group bidding us bon voyage: "See you and a jar of butter in Cement City in two hours."

Cement City, according to Cap'n Kelley's "Table of Distances, Missouri River" (published by the U. S. Engineers Office), lay approximately ten miles below us. 

That meant a speed of five miles an hour. The "river within the river," as Cap'n Kelley calls it, swept the southern bank from which we had taken off. In less than five minutes it had carried us past the fortresslike piles of the iron bridge below, out of sight of waving handkerchiefs, and brought us alongside a line of rusty government barges loaded with willow mats and rocks. "There's danger number one," announced the Captain, "if ever a canoeist is foolish enough to come up to a barge head-on he'll probably get such a big drink that he'll never be able to come back for another. Always up to the side, and best of all, up to the stern and from below-then you won't be pulled under."

More barges, extensive two-storied white dormitories or "quarter boats" for government river-workers, a flat-bottomed two-stacked, black and brown sternwheeler with the two-turreted fortress insignia of the U. S. Engineers in red and white on its funnels-all of these were moored against the flood-soaked mud banks. 

Gargantuan cement elevators, blocks of smoking and ramshackle factories, acres of slummy subsistence hovels soon only an occasional shack.

The canoe was carrying its heavy load lightly and confidently. Our work as roustabouts had been well executed as was evident from the ease with which she responded to the feathering of the rear paddle and her steadiness of course. The dampness and then the distinct dribble of river water between the midribs that had us not a little on edge as we were about to put off, Dillee took care of easily with the large red rubber sponge brought along for this and similar purposes. A little dampening had stopped the leak, wherever it was. 

To our right a clump of cottonwoods rushed towards us. It came so near that we could pick up the hard rustling of the leaves with their sound so like the patter of gentle rain. A red cow, its ribs visible from

the water's edge, munched at something green on a bush. But greenery except in trees was for the most part nonexistent. At this spot we had seen, two weeks before, what Meriwether Lewis described during the annual flood season 138 years before with the statement that "water was everywhere." Now the "river rat" was paying his rent. Laboriously going in and out of his erstwhile shanty home he lugged shovels of mud to a mound already the size of a two-ton dump. Had the renter been a poet and not a depression refugee he might have found food for thought at least in the fact that he was carrying out of his kitchen and living room tops of the Rockies washed into the Missouri by its three mountain-stream tributaries, the Jefferson, the Madison and the Gallatin. That which was mud and probably only mud to the returned refugee presented itself to us in the canoe in the brownish veil-like whorls we saw in the water alongside-sediment that settled immediately upon the rushing flood's relaxing over a Nile-like flatland beyond its ever-crumbling banks. 

Always seeking quiet and peace-even if it be on the inner areas of bends or the bottoms of wide reaches -always dropping mountain-top silt inches (even a foot, or two feet) at a time, the Missouri builds up the bottom lands between rocky bluffs, bends and twists. 

Then in flood stages it often cuts through the necks of the bends and uproots trees left undisturbed for twenty or, it may be fifty years. The result: the fantastic bars, the crescent lakes teeming with minnows, "crawdads" and channel cat, but soon choked and filled by sediment. The Missouri with her annual backbreaking alluvial load of 500,000,000 tons which she drops into the lap of Old Man River is thus always working against herself, creating her own obstacles; always the tempestuous and unpredictable neurotic-her own self her worst enemy. 

A fleeting glance at the "river rat's" establishment; then, in response to Kelley's feathering, a sharp veering into midstream, and with the prow pointed northward we made for a white structure half a mile diagonally downstream, a pole with an imposing X of white one-by-six boards. It was in the direction of this pilot's marker that we had to steer if we were to keep

on the river within the river. Along this course the water is always deepest and therefore swiftest, and here naturally is the must-route for all power boats whether going with or bucking the current. Nailed to the pole below the cross was a small rectangular sign with a number in black. This was the mileage marker we were to find at intervals of approximately a mile stating the distance to the confluence of the Missouri and the Mississippi. On the channel marker was also the lantern whose intermittent flash we were to camp so near night after night. Up to the marker in a few minutes, and then another sight we were to see so frequently-the crumbling and caving of banks. So swift was the current at this point that it washed away the soft underpinnings with such rapidity that the dropping with loud splashing of blocks of mud as big as a man went on continuously. Here the river in the form of a whirlpool had swirled in during the recent flood, had gouged a perfect semicircle a hundred feet into the bank, and was now engaged in a race against the U. S. Engineers to wash out the shoulders of the newly carved basin. Against the shore, however, were moored several much-battered steel and wooden U. S. Engineer barges of some seventy-five feet in length loaded with huge mattings made of willow trees, cut from the myriad thickets facing the length and breadth of the Missouri and woven into squares. These mats crews of laborers, brown and stripped to the waist, painstakingly slid off the barges and against the dissolving walls of the stream. A few willow mats dipping at a thirty-degree angle into the water and on them a layer of rocks placed by a swarm of laborers, and wild Old Woman River would probably have to contain herself at this point until the rages of another June or July. In the meantime the very opposition to the widening of the course would compel the river to scoop sediment from its own bed and thus make a channel deep enough to cope with flood waters which otherwise would sweep with fury and destruction over bottomlands where farmers now find fertilizing by crop rotation, composting and the adding of nitrates preferable to fertilizing by floods. That the Engineers thus maintain a ten-

foot channel to enable government scows and tugs and pile drivers to keep the channel open for more scows and tugs and pile drivers to keep the channel open, is largely incidental. 

Two miles below Kansas City-a marker read 376the unpainted scrap tin and tar paper shacks and houseboats of the river people became infrequent. Another mile and willows and cottonwoods, with occasional soft maples and python-stemmed sycamores repossessed banks they have claimed since the glacial ages. Sometimes, but only occasionally, the trees thinly screened a railroad embankment or grazing cattle. Steadily the screen became thicker so thick and impenetrable, particularly where willows commanded the verges, that getting through them would have called for a machete. 

Such was the wilderness we had traded for streetcars and insistent telephones and time clocks and glorious retreats on the war fronts. Here the blackbird on the reed in the bayou and here the little grey-backed and reddish-bottomed squirrel that raced us along the river's shores, here, under the arm of an endlessly defiant river, each lived on in its own Eden. Here, as we were to learn from the "river rats" at whom urban respectables look down their noses with such ill-advised contempt, men also, men for whom the West was now as closed as the East, a land of vigilantes and Grapes of Wrath, found for themselves a sort of game refuge from landlord and tax collector and advertising man.

Here, in a wilderness kept for them by Old Woman River, they could "go West" again. In the Old Woman they had someone to whom, as will be described presently, they could come back again and again. She has wood for warm fires, ground for a house, even food for the larder. Here also men like us could come and immerse themselves in the elemental realities of nature. 

Into this realm civilization cannot and will not come; only so far-pilings here and there, ripraps-but no farther. Here Old Woman River rules over areas of dozens of square miles of nothing but bars and wastelands. She rules defiant of roads and land hunger and "No Trespassing" signs. Purified by her friend and 

God the sun, she moves along unspoiled by the sewage of a score of Yahoo cities.

Another bend. The paddlers dipped the light yellowpine fins almost noiselessly into the water, pulling slowly and rhythmically with long strokes which left miniature whirlpools gurgling in the wake of the paddles. Back of us above the undulant green loomed a dim, dream-like skyscraper eminence or two. Ahead appeared a little dent in the now almost continuously wooded bottomlands and bluffs. This was Sugar Creek, once in steamboat days a river landing, but now only a valley filled with the shameless and malodorous gastronomy of an oil refinery. The town has long since abandoned the river for the road and forgotten the liquid rungs by which it ascended to its present dignity as roadside purveyor of ice-cream cones and hot dogs to relieve the tedium of motorists bored and suffering from eyestrain. But for us this dent in the wilderness and the greenery that framed it moved by with the calm and soothing stateliness that appears only to those who choose slower means of locomotion.

Not far ahead around a bend or two lay another dent, Cement City-already suggested by the sulphrous haze lingering over immense chimneys that topped our horizon of green. Behind the bald grey bluff overlooking the watercourse there now are some forty miles of man-made limestone caves. Here was Dillee's boyhood tramping ground. Entertained by his yarns of eluding the guards on Sunday afternoons, and of half-closed openings into labyrinths, of times when candles burned low or blew out, of icy waters and Tom Sawyer escapades, we found minutes slipping by in quarter-hour lumps. Presently Cap'n nosed the ship into a huge raft at the foot of the dusty sheet-metal cement plant. We were well ahead of time. But had the friends taken Dillee seriously? Cement City was merely a cement plant and no place for buying butter. Anyhow, rest was already warmly welcome to muscles now in the first throes of a grueling that was to go on for ten to fourteen hours a day for at least a week. Without steady paddling, the indefatigable Kelley repeatedly assured us, the trip would take at least three weeks. “You have to paddle to take it easy." His meaning was that keeping 10

on the river within the river called for continuous alertness and effort. Without paddling we would literally find ourselves, as was repeatedly demonstrated as we fought our way out of tenacious eddies, traveling back to Kansas City. I, moreover, was scheduled to meet wife and children in St. Louis in nine days and to go on with them-reservations all made to the minutest detail-to 

Washington D. C.

With much prying into one of the duffel-bags and not a little muttering Dillee finally extracted his twentytwo revolver, and until six-thirty (we had taken off at 4:30 p. m.) he generously passed it from one to another to devastate corncobs, mayonnaise jars and ink bottles beachcombed from the crevices of the raft and tossed into the current. 6:32 p. m.; two minutes over two hours. An "All aboard” from Cap'n, the wild braying of an automobile horn, and triumphantly raised aloft, a jar full of butter! We were saved from bacon grease. 

Farewells again, and on into the lengthening shadows. Our first night's bar we had spotted on an exploratory trip the preceding Sunday. We now had exactly fourteen miles to go-then, under the experienced guidance of the Captain, the making of the fires, the beds, the meal. . . all at the same time. But how about the chigger, the excruciating red mite that raises such hob with all Missouri nature lovers? "You can forget all about the chigger on the river," Kelley had assured us. He was right about the chigger. But the mosquito? "Same holds for the skeeter. I've never met one but once and that was when we had to pull to the banks and sleep under the trees." 

Seven o'clock; the rapidly descending sun hung over low cloud ramparts rimmed with fire and bastioned with towers more solid than we found it comfortable to behold. Already we had broken into a package of fig newtons. The better part of one of the two gallons of water we had sweated away. Cap'n and Dillee at the paddles paused to strip to the waist and then, their wet and glistening skins taking on the bronzed aspect of everything struck by the sun, worked on as tirelessly and silently as if they had suddenly become reincarnations of Osage braves. Half a mile of reddish pil- 11

ings rose ahead-huge telephone poles driven deep and lashed in pairs or trios down a line, four or five feet apart. This was the pile-driver's answer to the Missouri's jab at the neck of a peninsula. There must be no dividing and shallowing of channels at this point. 

But the river, never willing to take No for an answer, had responded, as every piling and retard vividly showed, with a determined battering at the stockades. 

The battering rams were cottonwood trunks sometimes three or four feet in diameter and twenty to thirty feet long. Up and down the stockade lay the wreckage of the most recent struggle. Cottonwood trunks, long since stripped of their coarse bark, white and bone-like, lay wildly jammed against the poles in conglomerations sometimes as big as a house. Occasionally a giant trunk was lodged high on a piling, its roots flaying the air and its tentacle branches projecting out into the racehorse current roaring down the line of the fortification. Once in this stream we flew along at twice our normal speed . . . threatened always with running into some half-submerged cottonwood monster lodged between the poles and projecting into the river. A rip in the canvas, water pouring into the hold, and then the lateral current rushing through the stockade debris would crash the canoe into the wall and suck us into the entangled branches. . . but more of this later.

Ahead was the dance macabre we had seen previously this short afternoon in dozens of these wooden walls; two poles, their footing clawed away by the nervous fingers of Old Woman River. Lashed within two feet of each other by a dozen or more turns of heavy steel wire they were literally dancing: forward and backward, swiftly, jerkily, their surface given the color of flesh in the twilight sun. Over them and a few yards farther on lay the thousand writhing arms of a medusa-headed jam. Were we still on earth or had we sailed off among the clouds we saw reflected in the water surrounding us and into an inter-planetary realm of nightmares? 

Another hour. Cap'n thinking aloud: "Now that looks like the bar we saw Sunday. But I don't know.. you fellows like its looks?" We didn't. "Gotta land soon. 12

Certainly aren't organized for a 9:30 bar yet." By the term "9:30 bar" he meant to describe about the worst possible landing place-not one so heavenly a camper would claim it at 6:30 when his schedule called for bars only after 7:00 p. m., and not one he would take at 8:00 (an 8:00 o'clock bar). Thus we graded bars by an o'clock rating. A good bar is an island, barren and lifeless; and the farther removed it is from greenery the happier. It was already verging perilously on nine. A black and treeless stretch off to the south and right, a point. "Guess this has got to be it.”

The crew gladly nodded assent. "Alright, mates." The prow grated into the sand. "Out with the cargo. Ivan to the beds and bedding. Rev to the driftwood and fire. 

I'll operate this stove. O. K.?" These directions were delivered not as commands but in the tone of offering suggestions. Our point was a strip or bank of sand lying along the line taken by the current at flood stage when driven offshore by a retard. The river was now at 

13.5 feet about ten feet below the high of a fortnight before. To the north and east of us, dark, unfriendly, and no more than a hundred yards away, stretched a mass of low willow thicket. Between our settlement on the ridge and the willows lay a basin moist with the last vestiges of the flood and reticulated generally by the cracks of the drying mud which divided itself into sections each comparable in size and thickness to the bottom layer of a hefty wedding cake. 

Sheltered by an army cot windshield the gasoline two-burner blazed under a pot of spaghetti and meatballs. Soon cook Kelley dumped liberal portions onto the tin pie plates laid out on a rubberized poncho. This was spread on the sand and littered with such articles as bread, butter jar, a bar of semi-sweet chocolate, fig newtons and potato chips. That meal, so satisfying to weary muscles, called for stretching out... but there was no time to be lost. We could see our way about only with the help of Kelley's electric lantern. How about the blankets disgorged from duffel-bags and piled crazily on one of the cots? Whose was whose? A small patchwork quilt, a thin woolen blanket and a cotton blanket were mine. Warm nights called for using the quilt as mattress; cold, for the quilt 13

as cover. A turkish towel rolled in a shirt made a splendid pillow.

We were to sleep in the open and under the stars. 

Against the possibility of rain we had a pup tent, and under it three could get "some kind of shelter," Kelley had assured us. "But you needn't worry about the weather; in the middle of July it's ordinarily well settled. Never had any trouble with storms." But how about the flashes-soundless, admittedly; but flashes nevertheless-from that bank in the west and south? "Oh that's just heat lightning. Heat lightning doesn't mean a thing."

Food packed into the orange crate and covered with the poncho; eating utensils cleaned by a process of squatting at the water's edge and scouring each item with sand and then wiping with sheets of paper toweling; clothes stripped off and chucked into rain-proof duffel-bags, and three tired men found themseves at liberty literally to flop. First, however, Kelley had to take a plunge. He was hot, too hot to sleep. Emerging from river and darkness and standing before the fire he gave his shoulder a sharp slap. "Well, what do you know about that?" Another slap from Dillee's direction where our eyes made out a pajama-clad figure bent over his leather zipper bag. Nevertheless no mosquito comments from anyone except Kelley, a muttered, "I didn't like the looks of that mud basin alongside. It's got the looks of a mosquito breeder. Anyhow it's practically dry."

A water bird's squawk, the faint rumble of a train following the ancient river bed, an unceasing roaring and rushing of water against the long retard above us, the soft golden flash of the beacon on the bend above the stockade-such was the spectacle that held the three of us occupied, each with his own plenitude of thought. A mosquito whined overhead, then nosedived at my shoulder. The faint tickle of hair-like feet. Then the alert attention focused on getting in the fatal slap during the split second between his settling comfortably in secure toe-holds and the pricking insertion of his well-digger snout. My timing was perfect. Another steel-guitar voice. Blood sucking sleep-thief! But he 14

was a thief also of something more than sleep-peace, quiet, of appreciation and enjoyment of the stellar scenery. Another thought: How about salvaging the hours ahead with the attitude that for once I should be able to watch, the night through, the revolution of the galaxies. . . and with the help of these erstwhile pests. They'd keep me awake! Tomorrow there'd be no lecture appointment with questions demanding clear-headedness; and tomorrow night's bar would surely measure up to the Captain's expectations. Let the thoughts come as they would. No counting of sheep to get in the tyrannous eight-hour minimum. What were eight hours against the light years overhead?

There shone the Milky Way system, our super-galaxy; and beyond, other such universes, one to three hundred million light years away. The Milky Way, our Milky

Way was called by the ancient Chinese, "The River of 

Heaven." A star of this River of Heaven, the sun, drawing moisture from the land and sea, the moisture dropping on the Rockies, on Montana, Dakota, and now roaring past the pilings above us-the Missouri was a part and a continuation of the River of Heaven. But where was I? Where did I fit into this scene? A man of thirty-eight, noticeably underweight after a year of struggling to make real such abstractions as friendship, truth, love, liberty, democracy, God-hands fidgety from fumbling pens, papers, typewriter keys-cheated muscles aching to wrestle with the concrete things for which they were designed. Now I was face to face with the river and the heavy luggage, the dragging and cracking of firewood, the exhausting liquid resistance to the paddle's stroke, the burning sun. In these concrete realities I was becoming immersed. I was in the swim and in living contact with that elemental stability in things which gives rise eventually to all abstractions and their power over man. Something like the fins of a fish were now taking hold of something like the water for which the fins were designed, and I felt unaccountably steadied.

Slowly a thin haze spread over the skies, and, with the help of a mosquito shield in the form of the cotton blanket thrown, except for a blow-hole, shiek-like over my head, a similar haze gradually overcast the 15

universe of thought. Night descended from within, and mind and body abandoned themselves to the subconscious but terribly wise currents of another stream, the psychic river whose ultimate and proterozoic source is also the River of Heaven.

II 

Turning over, in what I knew full well was a vain attempt to fit the interstices of my torso to the unyielding hickory framework of my army cot, I glanced upwards. Gone were the Milky Way, the Pleiades, and all the beasts and heroes of the Grecian skies. In their stead, an impenetrable murkiness-air sticky and motionless. Was that a drop? Another, which confirmed the substantiality of drop number one. "Hey gang! 

Rain! At your service, Cap'n." Warm drops coming thicker every second did to the camp what in an instant a boy's prodding stick does to an anthill. Kelley's electric lantern blazed into the mouth of a duffel-bag after the pup tent. "Push the cots together," he shouted. "Push them side by side, and get the paddles." This was directed at Dillee. Flash in hand I leaped to the orange crate and bags, covered the crate with an old raincoat and then pulled, dragged each item to the cots and shoved them as far back under as strength and the intricate leg-works of the cots permitted. Meanwhile Kelley covered only by his shirt and Dillee who was stripped lashed and strapped paddles fore and aft to the middle cot. These, handles aloft, were our poles. Then the wind, and with it lightning so continuous as to make our flashlights all but unnecessary.

The raindrops had become a veritable firehose discharge. But the blankets, beneath the quickly spread tent canvas, were comparatively dry. Corners of this canvas we strapped to the edges of the three-cot platform. In less than five minutes our shelter was ready for occupancy. In the downpour it reminded one of a tropical palm leaf house built on stilts. 

The last one in, hair soaked and streaming, I found myself under a booming canvas ceiling that continuously bellied out and then slapped down at me. The same was taking place over on Dillee's side. Where the fabric hit shoulders and buttocks, there, experience 16

had taught me, the water would surely come through.

My muddy feet I let hang out in the rain. The downpour scrubbed and washed determinedly, but this, combined with the streams that presently descended through the ceiling, soon had me shivering from stem to stern. Feeling the shaking, Kelley moved over, flapped a portion of his dry blanket in my direction and called through the rumbling and sputtering and snapping to "come into my apartment." Soon Dillee moved middleward, lying front down on the double ridge made by the cot frames. "Lucky we're still friends," shouted Kelley. Then a laughing explosion from Dillee, "We aren't. Not your elbow and my ribs."

Overhead the sturdy paddles, their blades thrust deep into the sand, bent to what we feared at times was the breaking point. To avoid catastrophe one or another regularly threw up his arm as a flying buttress. Then on my left arm, draped over Kelley's side, a chill trickle. 

The upper lashings were evidently not tight enough to keep the roof from sliding, inch by inch, down the rear pole; and from a sag forming a canvas basin in the center came the leak. I moved my arm, eased back a little. How about letting friend Kelley get a little wetting too! However, instead of striking him on the sitting end of his anatomy it trickled down and by some freak of hydraulics flowed over the canvas of the cot with which my skin was in direct contact. Thereupon we alternated in raising our arms as a center tent pole-this in addition to their use as a flying buttress.

Half an hour, and no let-up in the wind and rain and thunder. Here we were on the most exposed spot in this flatland neighborhood, many rods distant from shielding such as is afforded by tall trees. Our camp, so I found myself thinking, made a splendid target for one of the bolts flashing and sizzling on every hand. "You know mates"-this from Dillee-"I just love displays of lightning." An instantaneous roar of laughter from his hearers. Yes, there was a funny side to all this compounded misery; and luckily we were able not only to see it but also to enjoy it heartily. "You know fellows;" words from Dillee again, inserted between crashes, "I'm enjoying this." But the storm 17

gods also seemed to be enjoying their riot. They carried on for a full hour of fury, with only the briefest of lulls. Turning a flash on his wristwatch Dillee lay back and began booming in his accurate baritone voice "Three O'Clock in the Morning. . . dah da, da dah, da dah." Another half hour, and the worst was over.

The canvas above could cope with the persisting drizzle. Deep breathing from Dillee; a snore from Cap'n. 

But there was to be no dozing for me.

I was miserable, but not from the cramping or the drenching or lack of sleep. It came from the right side from within-that burning sensation which had appeared several weeks before and which had refused to respond markedly to rest or diet. Was this confounded nuisance going to put me in the hospital for an appendectomy; was this to be my summer's outing?

Such were the thoughts which had popped into my mind whenever the question of the river trip had arisen. And yet was this symptom anything serious? Was fatigue a more correct diagnosis? Was it merely nervous tension-something one ought to get rid of by working it off? However, even without this, I was physically hardly a fit canoe companion, Kelley, unaware of the whole story, had sensed nevertheless, that all was not well, and on the Friday preceding the day of our takeoff (it seemed months away now), when we were lashing the canoe onto his car, he had expressed his doubts. 

To my remark: "There is only one thing I really fear about this trip; it's that I may not be physically strong enough to hold up my end." His anything but lighthearted answer: "Frankly, if you and I were to go alone, I'm afraid you couldn't." Throughout the afternoon and evening both companions, each in splendid trim and well endowed with muscles and endurance, had seen to it that I did not keep at paddling to the point of limpness. I could tell stories, I could keep the log, I could scurry about and do little extras about the camp to try to make up for my lack of power in the pull. But this burning down deep in the gut? Against danger of acute attack I had secretly chucked a hotwater bottle into my canvas bag. This would help matters some, in case. And maps had shown towns at regular intervals. I was eating sparingly and carefully. 18

So far paddling had caused no inner discomfort. in fact, it seemed to help matters, as I had hoped. Today would tell. But how about the effect on the others? This, after all, was only the first night of the first day; anyone can put up with miseries and irritations for a few hours. 

But a week of burning sun and rain and mosquitoes perhaps, and plain slogging hard work-such gruelling tries the goodwill of the best of men. And to have one man pulling one or two hours to two and three for each of the others. . . ! ?

Warm again from Kelley's body and his woolen blanket, I felt better, and was awakened-it seemed in but a matter of minutes-by his mighty heave to get out from under, "you damned corpses." It was dimly light, 5:00 a. m., and he was all for getting under way despite the unrelenting drizzle. Dragging from under the cots the duffel-bag into which we had tossed our trousers, shirts, etc., he found it half filled with water. 

Someone had forgotten to tie it shut and had laid its open mouth directly in the bed of a Missouri tributary. 

From a second bag he drew some of the wraps such as he had warned us to be sure to pack-an old woolen bathing suit belonging to the shoulder-strap era, a cotton sweatshirt. This was his grooming for the day. 

For me there was another wool swim suit, a strip of canvas for the shoulders and my sodden farmer-jake straw hat. Trunks and a leather jacket lastly were Dillee's lot. "Alright now, Rev, what ya got for breakfast?"

These words followed Kelley's shoving the orange crate into the house and under my nose. I had been in charge of buying and packing provisions. There were bacon, a cellophane bag of sweetrolls and cans of powdered coffee. Coffee called for water, water for the can Kelley had ingeniously devised for cooking purposes and which he now held before us. This was an ordinary quart tin can with holes punched diametrically across from each other near the rim. Through these holes he could thrust an iron rod, thus providing us with a compact, packable, and long-handled pot for stove or grate. But the water; where was that other water can, the one with the canvas wrapper? It was still in the boat, floating. . . . and empty. Someone had slipped. 19

Kelley smiled."I've been without water before." He looked at the river. "Little gritty, maybe, but if you boil it it's as good as any water." Dillee, standing over the canoe half filled with rain water, looking down into its clear depths, pointed at the night's accumulation and protested: "I can take river water alright, but I wonder if foot wash might not go down a wee bit easier than just plain hogwash?" By some miracle Kelley presently had his pressure cooker roaring under the coffee pot and curling bacon-all this despite the unceasing downfall. Soon spoons of coffee powder went into three tin cups, followed by steaming water, making, together with bacon and rolls, a meal which Dillee protested was in step with a Haydn minuet or a Strauss waltz.

Turning the water out of the canoe, stuffing soggy blankets into duffel-bags and fitting everything into our ship took but a few minutes. Our interest now was in getting under way and paddling sluggish blood into circulation. Off we slid for a watery take-off-water below us as usual, but water also coming down generously from above, water soaking our clothes, and a grey, watery haze ahead of us. Dillee took the stern, I the prow, Kelley, blue lipped, his feet straddling the orange crate and each leg resting on a collapsed cot (or two) alongside, lay against the ribs of the lazyback propped against a hard duffel-bag. Over the whole body of the load (except for Kelley's head) extended the pup tent and black pancho. Such was our leavetaking, and our second day's beginning.

In yesterday's gallon can was water enough for several light rounds. This reserve, with the numerous cups of breakfast coffee, we hoped would get us without discomfort the sixteen miles we had to make before reaching Napoleon. Complicating the water situation was the thinning of the sky after a little more than an hour. In another hour the sun broke through with such brilliance and directness that socks, shoes, trousers, and shirts spread out one by one became wearably dry. If there was not to be burning we had to be fully clothed. 

The heat wave, with the numerous prostrations and heart failures we were to read about later, was beginning its first and powerful chapter. In a little more 20

than two hours Napoleon, with its several dozen old white houses on the crest of a distant bluff, and her little white church with its picket-fence graveyard and stones, appeared around a bend-another gash in the wilderness of willow and cottonwood flatlands and bluffs. Here, the destination of two previous Sunday's trial runs, was cool water. Cans were filled within a few minutes after 'landing at the nearby gasoline station, and presently, refreshed with pineapple sodas, we were paddling on into a country that was new and at the same time merely an endless repetition of the same wilderness, the same pilings, retards, willow and stone ripraps, crumbling banks. At every yard of the river's course, roots continued crazily and frantically to interlace the banks and grab desperately at the soft black mainland. Had we seen it all?

Truth was we cared but little for scenery now. Far more beautiful were the naps we were able to enjoy between turns at the paddles. A little later hunger rose to first importance. Where to land? Our load called for a spot favorable to the spreading of blankets and towels. 

Ahead, a graceful stone-faced curve presented itself.

Here in a little more than an hour, during which time we feasted on the remains of the spaghetti and meat balls, a can of soup, some oranges and chocolate, the blankets became thoroughly dry--such was the intensity of the sun under which we were traveling. Off again. Round after round went one drinking can and then the other in a vain attempt to keep up with streams seeping from millions of pores. Unless we were to find replenishment for our rapidly lightening supply we would find ourselves having to wait for the settling of boiled river water. In Kelley's book of mileages, moreover, there was nothing but river for many an hour. Two-thirty, and off to the northeast in one of those infrequent breaks in the foliage walls, half a mile inland, loomed the hip-roof of a barn. 

The nearest landing place turned out to be a bank belted by a series of muddy steps, the terraces left by successive lowerings of the river level. Six or eight feet above the water, and protecting the shoreline like a net, stretched a characteristic interminable tangle of willow shoots and cottonwood sap- 21

lings. Through this net, however, sinking at least half a foot into the ooze of the first two steps, Dillee and I plunged to the flood-swept levels above. Willow trunks we climbed by had thrown out roots into the high water, roots that grew directly through the rough bark. And roots here and there, still thick with tendrils, were already sprouting leaves. The willow and the Missouri are truly symbiotic.

With the canoe tied to a convenient trunk Kelley stretched out in the shade. A ten minute walk, past a large fishing scow (which indicated how the farmer supplemented his income) and between lush cornfields -but not lush enough as yet for roasting ears!brought us to a farm yard and a pump pointed out to us by a comely farmerette. She took time out only for a simple inclination of an index finger and an indifferent "O. K." The object of her preoccupation lolled on the screened back porch, an adolescent swain with whom she was enjoying the flushing thrills of the push- 'n'-pullem stage of love making. Water, in quick response to Dillee's vigorous sweeping of a heavy iron handle, poured freely back into the well through wide cracks between its top boards. Over this well top, nearby chickens, cats, even pigs had wandered as unrestrained as if the Bill of Rights had been enacted especially for them. But the water was refrigeratorcold, it was clear, and the healthy lovers nearby and the half-a-dozen small children making mud pies decorated with vari-colored corn silks betrayed neither symptoms nor after effects of typhoid. Their plumpness and rosy complexions suggested, in fact, that their father probably kept a goodly proportion of the products of his soil for his own family table instead of trading them, as has become the custom, for silk stockings, oleomargarine and chain-store bread.

Then came the splashing of the cool water over greasyhot foreheads, over swelling noses and lips and cheeks rapidly turning the tell-tale lobster red; cool water upon wrists, wetting them again and again—and Dillee's cominent: "Would you ever have dreamed that a little water could have such a cocktail lift?" Back to the landing. a drink or two around; and as I was placing the cans handed me gingerly from a dry step 22 22

above I noticed a brilliant green object on the second terrace above the stream, some ten feet ahead of the canoe's nose. A huge bullfrog-one of that variety we later saw displayed in the St. Louis zoo and described as indigenous but rare and so wily that few persons ever get the chance to observe them in their wild habitats. Here was the first of these giants either Kelley or Dillee, native Missourians, had ever seen in the open and at such close range. "How about frog legs?" -soft words, spoken to indicate the find to other eyes. 

The sight brought to my mind many a Chicago feast on this connoiseur's tidbit. Excitedly I tossed a stick at the mottled green head. A miss. But the gentleman remained motionless and apparently unperturbed. Was he sick? Would those ham-like legs of his be fit to eat?

Dillee, his gun in the depths of a duffel-bag, crept with the finesse of an Osage hunter along the upper rim of the bank. Then an adept toss of a heavy stick caught our victim squarely in the head and rolled him, stunned, into the water. Mud or no mud I was on him instantly. 

He must have measured a good twelve inches from nose to toe, with two meaty legs. Since we were probably at least four hours' paddling from our bar, killing and butchering on the spot would not have given us the freshest of meat for supper; so we merely bound his hind feet securely and turned him loose in the bottom of the boat.

At work on the log (a pad on which I scratched the day's events)-the sun hitting us obliquely and thus far more mercifully-Kelley and Dillee at the paddles, stripped to their waists, and pausing now and then to vent a few words of defamation on a fly digging for blood in back or leg-a question from me: "Say partners, we haven't given last night's camp a name. How about it?" From Kelley: "There's only one possible name and that's Misery Point." A general nodding of heads, and thus it took its place in the pages of our private history. "Look over there!" This time it was Dillee's voice.

His raised paddle pointed to a mud-bank waterfall where in the shallow inlet stood, some four feet high, several bluish birds, skeletal, motionless, graceful. Already we had seen dozens of these, Great Blue Herons, 23

usually one at a time, feeding on inlets and bars, and in their cargo-plane flight; but, like the bridges thrown across the river at intervals of some fifteen to forty miles, they never ceased to excite our admiration and wonderment. At my side the bullfrog struggled and croaked with a note reminiscent of the basso-profundo of a Cossack chorus. Now we were skimming the jungle banks along the outer fringe of a bend. This time it was our turn to be seen. A squirrel romping along, now in a thicket, now on a shelf, now in the air over a gulch, ran after us for the pure joy of running a race. "Shoot him?" Answer, "Aw shucks, why spoil his fun. . . and our's too." 

Seven o'clock-longer shadows-cooler air-bird songs, including that of the quail, pouring from the woods. Time to be on the lookout for an island. Such was the average spacing of good bars that by beginning to look for a bar at about this hour we were reasonably sure of finding a fairly satisfactory landing field by nine at the latest. In that time we would have traveled at least ten, and perhaps thirteen or even fourteen miles. Eight o'clock, and Kelley indicated a distant bend and a long retard; behind it still another retard. . . a likely spot for a bar. A half mile, and we found ourselves at the hoped-for island; a heavenly spectacle when placed opposite the memory of Misery Point. But what was that beyond? Wasn't it another and better spot? Kelley and Dillee standing on the low shore and working their feet into the cool quicksand looked at the dark and low-lying outlines. They listened patiently, however. "Don't you think we ought to explore the sand beyond the second retard? It's farther into the river. Besides, it's more in line with the wind coming down the channel. Shouldn't be many skeeters on that breeze." Kelley: "Well, a guy's got a right to argue." "And paddle us back"-from Dillee-"if it's a lemon."

I did not have to paddle back. Here we found all (almost all) that had been argued for, and more-plenty of wood for boiling the potatoes for breakfast! Giving our island a quick going over Kelley announced: "How about calling this Pitcairn's Island?" It had for us on this day all the South Sea haven had for Bounty mutineers; so, into the log it went as Kelley suggested. 24

Into the log also went the rather remarkable fact that we had made seventy miles. We were nearly a hundred miles from home. I was pulling for longer intervals and more powerfully. No one was griping about anything as yet or what is worse giving any indication of having an inner struggle to hold back unpleasant feelings pressing for expression.

First among our objects of interest for supper was our frog. Who were to get the legs? Dillee opened the subject with this question: "What does it taste like?" 

I answered, "Just like the best southern fried spring chicken you've ever eaten." Kelley smiled. He knew from remarks Dillee had already dropped that our partner never did like fried chicken. I also knew this, and perhaps had it in mind when I made the answer. I might have suggested the likeness to fried rabbit, or possibly-just to get him to try it-to a pork chop. But 

I did want that leg, and so I told the unvarnished truth! 

Frog legs served up in the roadside restaurant, encased in crispy crust with a heap of French fries were somewhat more appetizing than the kicking sub-reptilian green monster I had ungraciously dangled before Dillee's eyes when I put the question as to the disposal of the legs. Was I still sure I wanted one? Since Kelley remained undaunted I proceeded with the butchering. 

One chop from our razor-sharp hatchet-guillotine and

Mr. Amphibian had atoned for all his depredations; another stroke and his fat torso dropped from the anticipated drumsticks. Then we found the explanation for his unaccountable torpor of the early afternoon. In his stomach were three freshly caught crayfish; each of them at least two inches long, armor,scissor claws and all. Thus the frog, protected by no chitinous interlinked armor plates, with fragile jaws and microscopic teeth (if any), was master of nature's whippet tanks by virtue of his superiority in chemical warfare-the power of his digestive acids! Gorged and possessed of a superabundance of the feeling of God's being in his heaven and all being well with the world the bullfrog undoubtedly lacked the emotional power to envisage anything but good intents and worshipful admiration in the special attention with which we showered him. 

And he was deserving of admiration. His legs, equal 25

in size to those of a small chicken, rolled in pancake flour and fried in butter left Cap'n protesting his acquisition of an ineradicable taste for this delicacy. This, despite Dillee's remark during the course of the frying, "Do those things stop jerking before they are ready to eat?"

An hour later we turned in exhausted, with inner feelings nicely externalized in the mahogany elegance of our water and forest-garnished island. Clouds there were, but in every direction they were beating a retreat. Nevertheless, everything-canoe turned over this time, and under it our luggage, nearby the pup tent and all straps and ropes-lay in readiness for the first drop. Upstream, the roaring of the frustrated river. . . from the sides distantly but penetrating, the metallic zooming of myriad cicadas. below, the flashing of the beacon. . . overhead, crystalline clear, the first object of man's wonderment and the last and ultimate puzzle of his philosophy-the stars. 

But were we to be free of mosquitoes?-another insoluble puzzle to philosophers. The answer was not long delayed. It came on the swift, cool breeze, hurrying down a half mile of water and sweeping our island longitudinally-thin, steel-guitar cries. Floating on the breeze, then suddenly catching the mammal aroma which filled them instantly with blood lust they seemed to scream as they struggled with the wind: "Here's one"-shriek, shriek. "Blood. Oh, wheeee"-shriek. 

Now dive-bombing at ears and nose. "Pints of blood!

III 

"Steamboat comin' roun' th' bend!" Monday morning and Dillee's shout from the prow reflected our spirits, gay in their upspring from sound sleep, coffee, fried potatoes and eggs, from waters polished to a lapidary's taste, crisp, cool air, and a path of gold ahead of us. We were now an hour on our way. A greyish mass, black stacks giving birth to smoke clouds equally black, larger by far than any of the several government tugs and ships whose wakes we had successfully ridden was it moving or was it a shore installation? 

The Idlewild, an excursion steamer specializing in moonlight cruises for crowds of beer and popcornhungry landlubbers, had recently run announcements in the Kansas City Star of a forthcoming visit. Gingerbread canopied upper deck, pilot house, and soon, in full view, all the time-mellowed lacework garishness of the days of Mark Twain and Showboat-here was a sentimental Victorian nightmare measuring at least a hundred and fifty feet with a good forty-foot beam. 

It was the Idlewild. Happily for us she hugged the channel to our right. Her revolving stern wheel created a veritable maelstrom of splashing and plunging. 

This commotion, adding multitudes of mean little chops to the ship's wake, gave Kelley's voice a not exactly reassuring tone when he quietly drawled, "Got the sponge handy, Rev?" Its use anticipated, it lay at my side, a red rubber mass the size of a half gallon syrup can. Ahead of us the successive streamers of the wake curled and fell over themselves. Their crests in any other body of water would have been called "whitecaps"; these, although aquatically the same, were browncaps. Opposite us the fantastic floating castle plowed ahead as completely oblivious of our craft as if we had been a drifting cottonwood. The throbbing or thudding of the engines rose distinctly above the rush and hiss of the riot of waters. But nowhere in evidence was anyone who might ease the ship's speed or drop a skiff in case anything might happen to us. 27

If there was anyone in the pilot house our eyes didn't tell us so. We saw no one strolling her three tiers of deck walks, or leaning against the railings. She moved ahead unconscious like the elephants that are supposed to sleep while they move in line through the jungles. 

After all, who would expect to meet anyone else in this unfrequented water-willow-cottonwood wilderness; at least in anything the size of a canoe? Lips tightened. 

Whorls made by paddle strokes gurgled more audibly than usual. Cap'n had us headed towards the left bank. 

Then the wake drove at us broadside, no more than two rods away. Caught in this position we would certainly have rolled over. But we had seen Kelley's paddle in operation, and, although we were not surprised, we were not a little relieved when our boat swung around and dug her keen half-moon nose straight into the side of the roller. Behind it, another and still others. Then followed the rebound from the opposite shore and the incalculable building up, although the ship was now a quarter of a mile behind us, of wave on wave rushing upon us at angles which made the pilot's twistings and turnings look like the after effects of a case of beer. Calmer water. On both sides the polished walnut of the gunwhales was wet but we had shipped not so much as a spoonful! "Hip, Hip. . . '

With the Idlewild become a mere plume of smoke over the trees, our immediate trouble was by no means at an end. It beat down from above, one of the summer's most telling heat waves. On water, burning comes not only from above, as has been said, but from below, where, acting by way of a mirror, the sun sears almost as effectively as when direct. So our heat wave, as we had anticipated, came not singly but doubly. At the prow paddle I dipped the crown of my hat regularly into the water. This, twenty-five years before, the farmhands had taught me to do in the Minnesota harvest fields. Also onto the areas of our shirts, not entirely soaked with perspiration, we tossed cool (hourly becoming warmer) river water. However, despite hats with brims pulled down and shirts with collars buttoned and pulled up, the burning process went on apace. Three noses steadily progressed from lobsterpink to lacquer red-also cheeks and chins. My hat had 28

the widest brim. Moreover, I had refused to let the hobo image I saw whenever I looked into the water make me shave, and incidentally shave off much of the skin surface. Consequently my facial burns were less severe than Dillee's, which were worse on nose and lips, and 

Kelley's whose lips suffered most. Burning first roasted the skin, which, although we plastered it with olive oil, became hard and then cracked. With the hardening came itching and the temptation to scratch and help along the inevitable peeling. This meant blistering. Our skins were now in the most painful, although not the most uncomfortable stage, the burning stage. My casualties were my wrists, the large patches of white skin exposed with each stroke of the paddle. There broiling proceeded without let or hindrance-aggravated by the sliding of the wet cuff over the now thoroughly tenderized epidermis. Nevertheless, my paddle stroke, despite muscles that felt at each sitting like the aftereffects of football scrimmage combined with track try-outs, was comfortably stronger. The faint burning at the side seemed to be lessening. 

"Well fellows" Kelley talking out of his large experience in canoeing on the river "this is about the time when the real trouble ought to begin, the kind people make for themselves. Anyone feel like griping? Anyone getting a dirty deal?" For hours it seemed, we had been going the rounds of poetry quoted by Dillee, jokes, stories. Some of the stories had been salty like the ocean water, some clean and refreshing as a mountain stream, and many riotous and of the tint of the water surrounding us. Consequently laughter, echoing from the trees along the shore, laughing back at us, as if we had with us a fourth party who was also enjoying the vaudeville yarns and gags, had been the order of the morning. Kelley's question came during a lull when minds, comparatively unacquainted with each other, were fruitlessly raking over the hilarities of lifetimes, and were settling instead, with increasing helplessness on sensations arising from abdominal regions. "Well, if you want a perfectly honest answer" - Dillee speaking "my belly's griping. My gastric morale's getting lower and lower." 

Stretched out Roman fashion, half an hour later, 29

before the poncho spread on the sand beneath a sparsely covered young cottonwood, and feasting on a can of beans, a can of sandwich spread, and slices of bread thickly spread with an ambrosial strawberry preserve from the Kelley cellar, we listened to Cap'n. Studying the Book of Distances, he announced: "I can't see any reason why we shouldn't make Glasgow this afternoon instead of tomorrow. How'd you fellows like a bucket of ice water, 'n' an ice cream sundae, 'n' an ice cream soda, 'n' some cookies, 'n' another bucket of ice water 'n' have it this afternoon instead of tomorrow morning, when it'll be too cool for us to make the best of it all?"

The heavenly prospect-Glasgow, a dim blue water tank, a spire or two and several housetops on a hill some eight or ten miles ahead, glimpsed after two or three hours of paddling-put new power into the rhythm of our strokes. Immediately in the foreground, however, seated on the poles of a long retard, a dozen or more large coal-black birds presently took our attention off both hell and heaven. Standing there, not making a sound, they couldn't be crows. Nearer, we noted their bright red heads. They were Turkey Vultures. But were they? Passing the retard, we in turn became the objects of interest. Flapping heavily, but not buzzard-like, they mounted into the air, spread their glider wings and soared by us and around in front. Now we saw their heads-not the revolting featherless head of the carrion eater, but hawk-like. Down farther another group soared whirlwind-like over some luckless object in a willow clump not far from the riverbank. Still farther along we again came across at least another twenty or more soaring and circling in a group. What kind of hawk, eagle, vulture this bird was neither of the native 

Missourians could answer. But thanks to their appearance and rarity-we saw no more during the weekthey precipitated enough ornithological speculation and argument to keep our minds at a safe distance from sweat and burns until we pulled up at the trim U. S. 

Engineers landing.

A large, freshly painted sign confronted us: "No 

Trespassing." "Heck, that's not for us. That kind of sign's always for the other fellow," announced Kelley 30

The clean-cut and mild-mannered superintendent glanced at our orange cockleshell and, with a look in his eyes that suggested amusement over our pathetic harmlessness, smiled to himself and consented to give our boat and cargo the protection of the sign. Tempting as was the faucet at the side of the Engineers office, we hurried up the hill to the stores, with but half an hour to go before their closing. Perched on a Main Street curb, Dillee mopped his forehead and, holding his watch before us, urged, "Cummon, we've got time for a sundae first. By putting the grocery-buying in the middle of our time we can get a soda later." Agreed. Plunging into a near-by drug store, and for the first time in hours into shade, each, before looking at the order card, emptied his waterglass in one gulp. A second round, a third. A nonplused clerk substituted larger glasses. Finally he brought a sweating pitcher and dropped it with an irritated thud in the center of our table. From then on we needed all the water we could get to flush down the vilest, puffiest frozen something-or-other covered with the corn-starchiest chocolate-brown sauce we all avowed we had ever sat down to. But it was cold and we had paid for it. And in the end, resisting the impulse to shoot up the place, we made for the grocery. More oranges, more bacon, bread, sweet rolls, cookies, and a fly swatter; all in all a puzzlingly civilized and daintified assortment for men circumnavigating civilization! 

Then a huddle-how about lemonade? . . . "Oughta go swell with ham 'n' eggs."

The result was my being assigned the job of getting the juice out of half a dozen rocky lemons which were to go into a pail already half full of ice and ice water. 

This operation took place on the bottom of the canoe an hour later. But how about a lemon squeezer? Squeezing out the juice by brute force, and with the canoe swinging and diving in response to Kelley's piloting against the wake of a huge U. S. The Nazis were driving into the Caucasus, we had read in the drug store. "Thunderstorms expected," we had also read and very specifically noted. All over the world people with roofs over their heads shouted or cursed or trembled over what the Germans were doing to far-away Russia. That news was most real to themas real (perhaps more real to many) as the heat and streams of sweat and the backs of dresses becoming wringing wet. It was real enough to be exciting, diverting to stab them into awareness, to make them feel as if they were still alive and not automatons in a machine-like social and economic environment. But for us what was happening in Russia had suddenly lost first place in poignancy and reality. What were the 

Nazis to us? An idea-terrifying in a sense, but nevertheless an idea, a mental construct of something none of us had ever seen on a street or in a concentration camp. And the Russians? And the war so far distantwhat was it? What was it but a glorified prizefight to the man at our side in the drug store turning from the sports page to the battles of the first? Thunderstorms. 

Clouds to the north of us, red and rampart-like again.

But what were clouds to the newspaper reader living in his second-hand world of sports columns? What could clouds be to readers with raftered roofs over their heads and insulated from the real by newspapers, account books, checking accounts, sales talks, pulp magazines, bridge, and automobiles sufficiently speedy to make a blur or at most a sort of newsreel spectacle of the best and worst of landscapes? And wind, wind tossing handfuls of sand in one's eyes, wind banging and cracking the canvas roof over one's head, likely any second to rip it to shreds or topple it over. Rain, rain that poured through the roof, that seeped through 32

to the naked skin and raised goose-pimples and made one shiver, and left one's shirt and trousers dishrags and the loaf of bread a mass of dough. All this reduced in everyday polite human intercourse to a casual and mechanical form of greeting, or excuse for opening a conversation: "Looks like rain, doesn't it?" "Nasty weather we're having”—and then directly into the gossip or possibly the parroted editorializings which make up the ersatz substance of ordinary living. 

Because the weather item suggesting rain and wind, a shaking roof and cold bodies and spoiled food was real to us we knew also that when we got back under solid asphalt roofs the bombings and sufferings of war would be more of a reality to us. . . not a supplement to the sports pages. Contact with the river was giving substance to life, putting it on its ancient foundations again. 

The sun was a drunken humpty-dumpty on the western horizon when what our log describes as "Echo Island" rose up before us beyond a long retard. It was at least an acre in extent, high, dry, and of mudless yellow sand. Here for the first time we had the dozen inconveniences of modesty thrust upon us by the presence of a farm house nestled in a neatly wooded valley alongside a hill whose front had been sliced off by the river and made into a cliff. This cliff we quickly discovered reflected, lagging one word behind, everything we shouted as one or another called exuberantly for more wood or for the whereabouts of the matches or pointed out some peculiar dip or depression with which the bars are so crazily gouged and pocked. Exhausted, faces shining with the oil with which we had plastered them, we sat on our couches and literally swilled lemonade with the satisfaction that goes with well-earned rewards. We had made 61 miles and we were now 154 miles below Kansas City and 224 from the Mississippi; we were almost a day ahead of schedule. Now we had earned the right to loaf a little. A little, for-Cap'n's experience-laden voice: "A stiff southeaster might easily put us a day behind."

An officious farm dog warned us repeatedly that our encampment was an illegal encroachment upon territory under his jurisdiction. With darkness the flames 33

of our cottonwood fire, whipped up occasionally by a gust from the south, brought forth further angry outbursts from the canine custodian. Undoubtedly he associated us fearfully with the ominous sky to the far north where ruby clouds had rolled themselves into a bank that was slowly turning black with the thickening of twilight. It faded into the night, but its position was made glaringly distinct by an almost continuous flashing of lightning. Under that bank persons were getting weather that would probably make something more on the morrow than mere salutations as mechanical as most of the daily routines through which the living consciousness dozes and sleeps much of the time. 

Throughout the night an occasional helpful mosquito awakened us to the progress of the storm. At 

7:00 a.m. it had become a vast arch over the northern horizon. The outermost and highest rim of this arch was nicely rounded and of a bluish grey; then other and successive rims to a continuous deep dark blue at the center, the doorway into the hall of the Valkyrie beyond. The south wind in blasts extinguished the gas burner each time Kelley had it lighted; and all his explosive cussings were without power of incantation. 

Cooking was out of the question. The arch, looming larger and nearer, left no apparent alternative to our letting it pass over us. Then a calm. Instead of proceeding to the stove and to breakfast, Kelley and I quickly raised the pup tent over the sleeping Dillee.

A blast from the north, a sand blast by the time it reached us, whisked the cover off the kettle loaded with the potatoes we had boiled over the driftwood fire. The cover captured and securely weighted down, then another blast. The, inverted canoe, resting three points down, on prow, stern and side, began to rockup on one side, down on the other-and finally over on its bottom with a thud that both Kelley and I, as we confessed later, felt in our own ribs. Up again and over. 

Thud, thud. I was under the tent behind the orange crate I had just pulled in. Kelley, about to crawl in, turned and sprinted after our footloose boat, now sidesomersaulting with the greatest of ease, caught it by the rope and nose and brought its prow into the wind. 

Now it was the sponge's turn to go to sea. Turned out 34

of the boat and rolling along, a brilliant red dot in a wild cloud of sand, it flew toward open water, a hundred feet down the bar. Through the tent opening as from a grandstand we of the crew watched and shouted at the contestants. Kelley won. The triumphant return of the winner, this time sprinting against a sheet of chilling rain, aroused another roaring "hip, hip, hooray." This storm proved to be second to the first only in duration. The blankets, however, piled high in the center and covered by the poncho, a tent within a tent, came through unscathed. If rain dripped down necks and doused trouser legs, that mattered but little this time; we would soon be on our way, paddling vigorously; and if the sun didn't come out, we did have other clothes at hand. Out of the crate we fished rolls, oranges, cookies-all thoroughly salted with sand. "Boy!"-from Dillee, audibly crunching and spitting by turns-"next time I read a dispatch from the African front I think I'll have a little glimmer of what I'm reading about."

Everything in the sky that might have hindered the sun in its determination to fry the earth disappeared with the storm. Thus, from our sliding into the water at nine o'clock to the sighting, about 1:30, of a cliff with a convenient rocky and shaded shelf for rest and lunch, we sat and sizzled. So intensely did the rays beat down that they burned through the back and shoulders of my white shirt. Before the week's end I had thus acquired a perfect tanning of back and shoulders and arms, and without the usual exaction of the acute discomfort and all-but-blistered skin of direct burning. 

My wrists, however, would soon be caking and hardening. Let the skin crack and peel, and then the next worst stage, blistering. This I managed to avoid by tying a handkerchief around each wrist, knots underneath and triangle corners stuffed up into the sleeves. 

Dillee, his lips verging on cracking, begged Kelley to hold off with his stories. "One more like that and I'll be licking a split lip." Both Kelley and Dillee were noticeably slowing down-much to my satisfaction. 

Another day or two and my increasing strength might be a match for theirs. Then I at least would cease to be a potential major irritation. 35

Thus far each had paddled at least four hours to my three. Such an arrangement may work for two or three days, but with burning and sweating and storms, a disproportion of this magnitude is certain to be disastrous to morale. Was this thought lurking in the back of Kelley's mind when he brought up the question of getting something off our chests? Low morale in the form of grudging politeness, half-cynical smiles to oneself, averted glances, eyes in which one sees optical cursing-this, the wet blanket and negative response, I had seen on other expeditions. Compared to it burns and cold and rain are jokes. But worse still is the feeling that one might be an irritant and the cause of a crack-up in morale. Truly as Kelley had said: "If you want to know just what stuff a man's made of, take him on a camping trip."

Lunch hour. Above us rose a high protruding forehead of limestone. Near at hand lay a huge fallen cottonwood windbreak for the stove, a splendid flat rock for kitchen table and others for stools. Here, after eating a meal comprised mainly of potato omelet, we turned our attention to the cool wonderland of the cliff's surface: blue-gray lichens and liverworts, unafraid of the most barren spots of the earth's surface, invading the cliff's face-shelves of refreshing green mosses and here and there in the moss the artistic sprinkling of graceful, reddish-brown spore pods-fern sprays; then the quartz concretions we dug out of eroded slits in the face of the cliff. Broken, their crosssections presented rainbowed agate configurations.

Innumerable fossil shells, weathered out and protruding from the rock surfaces, epitaphs of life in Paleozoic seas that once washed the entire midwest regions, mingled with their living descendants—a veritable swarm of little pink land snails. A most singular family history: three or four hundred millions of years, and the children looking almost identically like and acting almost exactly the same as their fossil hermaphrodite parents. 

Long ago the abundance of these snails caught the interest of the Osage natives, who, river people that they were, traced their family line to this most unprogressive stock. The first Osage, they told Meriwether 36

Lewis, was one of these snails who lived on the banks of the Osage tributary. The luckless snail, however, was caught in a flood and washed into the Missouri which tossed him on an exposed mud bank where he lay baking and dying in the sun. Shortly before the opening of death's door, however, the Great Spirit appeared to him, changed him into a man and instructed him in the use of the bow and arrow. The 

Osage Adam, presently becoming unbearably lonely, sought out a beaver and married her. From this union came the Osages!

Thanks to civilization we did not meet on the cliff any of the rattlesnakes which Lewis and Clark found in abundance in such places. But Kelley and Dillee, climbing a near-by steep ravine to the hill above, did find, on pawing over the ground here and there, not a few flint chippings which testified vividly to the existence of the Osages who figured so prominently in provisioning the 

Lewis and Clark expedition with game. Some two hours of rest and exploration at our majestic cliff-garden dining room were among the first fruits of our being so well ahead of schedule. Come what might, we would surely make St. Louis by Monday at the latest. But ahead of us lay another treat-a half-day's loitering in Jefferson City a real restaurant-cooked dinner, a look at the Benton murals, and more ice cream sodas. Thus enspirited by Cap'n and his vivid pictures of pleasures ahead, we gaily shoved off again into a realm which literally stood between a frying pan and a fire-between heat from above and heat reflected from below.

Despite the temperature, however, we carried the mood of the rocks far into the afternoon. The rocks and the stream of time and generations suggested by the fossils brought our own age into perspective. A halfhour's discussion settled the significance of the present 

World War. Presently the outcome of the New Deal became clear-the Jewish question quickly resolved itself and a glorous future opened up for Gentile-Jewish relationships. Germany we reorganized; Russia we showed how to win the war in 1942; and with British colonial policy thoroughly aired and reformed, we had the river of history well retarded here, riprapped there, 37

and the channel taking a navigable course satisfactory to all.

Some hours later when the heat of the day was at its post-meridian climax we nosed into the foot-deep mud of the banks below Boonville. A government, outboardpropelled scow, coming in from a day's hacking at willow thickets with machetes, had landed a minute before and thus provided us with a convenient landing spot. The engineer in charge, in keeping with the generosity and thoughtfulness of all the government engineers we met, directed one of his men to pour the icecold contents of his pressure fountain into our gallon containers. This, followed by the purchase at a near-by water-front-railroad-yard store of two rounds of soft drinks, a pint of ice cream each for Kelley and Dillee, a package of cookies (consumed on the spot), two Eskimo pies for me, fortified us for the day's final push which was to take us to within "dinner-distance" of Jefferson City.

Six miles below Boonville we ran into cliffs of such proportions that they dwarfed anything we had yet seen. From this point the scenery was to take a new turn as the river cut its way through a wide belt of low-lying lime and sandstone hills. These, rising a hundred to a hundred and fifty feet above the river, presented geological history to the passing eye in horizontal layers of sandstone, limestone and shale that represented tens to hundreds of thousands of years of paleozoic deposits. Now, however, these majestic fronts, standing face-on to the declining sun, were not so much history as apartment buildings. The apartments of countless thousands of white-breasted cliff swallows lay before us, tier upon tier, dug into soft yellowish sandstone layers which were interspersed, often at no more than a yard's distance, with harder layers. At an earsplitting shot from Dillee's revolver a veritable black cloud swept from the cliff and floated into the air overhead. Cliff beyond cliff stretched before us to the south; a bulwark against which the tough old Missouri, loaded to saturation with grinding grit, was making pitiably slow progress. Here, a cliff seemingly inhabited only by swarms of domestic pigeons which had reverted to a wild state. Now, an edifice we unhesitatingly called 38

"Hadrian's Tomb"-another even more astoundingly sculptured, a veritable cathedral front with flying buttresses, a rose window and an arched entrance. 

Over the rocky headlands, first in groups of ten and twenty and then often in groups of a hundred or more, flew wave upon wave of noisy crows. Their day's depredations concluded, they were evidently congregating somewhere to the south of us where we ourselves hoped to land before long. More crows poured over the hills, all aiming at an area below where the river glanced away from the headlands and struck off unimaginatively into the continuous forest of the alluvial plain. As we approached the roaring retard behind which we imagined we discerned our island for the night we saw the destination of the crows-a flood-swept willow grove paved with reddish-yellow sand. Their cawing, all the time we were setting up kitchen, dormitory and dining room, rose to such a crescendo that we literally had to shout to make ourselves understood. It was truly a 

Witches' Sabbath. Hundreds of black pranksters cawed at once; a competition, evidently, in which the loudest would win the privilege of holding forth. Their voices reminded me of an article about crows which appeared more than a decade ago in the Atlantic Monthly. Its writer, telling of his experiences with a crow he had caught as a fledgling and raised to maturity, insisted that the crow is totally devoid of sentimental feelingsalways ready to play a practical joke on the hand that feeds it, but never demonstrating, responding to, or seemingly needing the slightest tinge of affection. 

Finally one voice gained ascendency-the loudest and most raucous of them all. He cawed like an orator who had taken his lesson directly from a Miltonic Satan. "You devils!" he seemed impishly to be shouting. He harangued his hearers, who responded voluminously with occasional devils' amens, with so much relish that the pure joy of shouting curses all but nullified his deviltry at times. "What have you done to man today? 

Nothing, I dare say. Nothing! You yellow-livered black devils. Cummon, show your colors. Let's have a real day tomorrow-wake those gun-toters bleary-eyed before dawn, steal the corn they throw to their chickens, make their babies scream! Awake, arise, or be forever fallen." 39

The thin red rim of the rising sun had barely edged over distant hills when the air filled with a clamorous "Caw . . . caw . . . caw" from hundreds of birds putting into practice the accursed principles of the black mass of the night before. "Okay, okay"-Dillee, stretching his arm to get a look at his wrist watch, addressed the witches' chorus. "The sooner we're up, the sooner we're going; and goodbye to this bar and your cawing." 

The island went down in the log as "Devils' Haven." 

At 11:25 a.m. the round grey dome of the capitol appeared distinctly above the treetops of a distant bend. 

It took another two hours of paddling, however, to get us to the waterfront and tied up at the premises of a sand and gravel firm . . . 143 miles from the Mississippi. In the firm's office an obliging secretary volunteered to keep her eyes on our ship, nosed into the mud below.

That morning I was the only one who had held out against shaving. Thus with five days of beard and capped by my old farmer-straw, I kept several discreet paces behind the gentlemen of our party as we wound past more than one suspicious eye through the railroad yards and up the capitol hill and into a restaurant. Here, after regenerating ourselves with steak dinners and several rounds of colas, we made for the paintings that have done so much for the tourist business of Jefferson City. First, the paintings in the cavernous wing to the right of the sightseer as he enters the Missouri temple of government: static and sentimental picture-book illustrations of early dealings with the Indians, together with illustrations of other items of pioneer Missouri-all surprisingly dull and weak in contrast to the story-teller's stories of those days. Decorated domes, stained glass windows-pale symbols of agriculture, commerce, justice, Missouri Mother of the West, truth; then an immense (and costly, we were told) canvas by some Frenchman, his impression of Yankees abroad, supposedly symbolizing the World War in France. But perhaps the paintings were not to blame for our being unenthusiastic and bored. Perhaps our eyes were tired from the glare of the sun, our minds tired. 

But where were the Benton murals? A thinish, softspoken official in a uniform with the word "Lecturer" 40

on his cap wheeled about, and gaily dangling and jingling his keys as he swung along, ushered us into the lounge of the House of Representatives. However tired and dull of mind we may have been, we found that what now so vividly confronted us had the quality of stabbing us awake and making us suddenly forget the hundred symptoms of heat and fatigue. Morrison, the lecturer, was also awake-awake in the sense of being a person, a self, and not the phonograph one almost invariably finds in galleries and historic spots, impersonally droning off the long-since-recorded contents of his neural grooves. Describing the meaning of each item in the murals, from the early French trader plying the Osage with hard liquor, up and along the river-always the river in the background-to the porcine Pendergast at the conclusion of the Whoopee Era, he smiled and brought in seemingly spontaneous observations, as if this were the first time he had ever delivered his talk. 

Objections he seemed to be delighted to take up, and these he treated like questions: questions he used as an opportunity to take us farther into what he made one feel was the most thrilling history of any state in the Union. The question from the well-groomed St. Louis business man, one of a numerous party that had followed the three river men into the lobby, received no more courteous attention than the interrogations of the bum with the straw hat and red and stubbled face.

Later discussion revealed to us that he had been doing this several times a day for no less than five years! 

Here was an amazing as well as mystifying fact-to me, a public speaker. To what did Morrison owe his freshness and spontaneity? Was it the mural painting and its effect on him and on the audiences, putting life into them, awakening them, and the audiences in turn charging the lecturer; or was it just that the lecturer was remarkably alive?

Three minds, suddenly become art critics, and itching to get at the business of praising or burying these murals, provided the stimulation that hurried us to the post office, through three malted milks and some pieces of pie, through a drygoods store where I picked up a two-yard piece of mosquito netting, and back to the canoe. First, however, the space had to be used for a kitchen. Somewhere in the mixing process, either because of someone's failure to wipe the pan thoroughly with paper towel or because of the proximity of a sandy hand or foot, sand got into the batter. Sand was ordinarily in everything our hair, our coffee, our beds, our eyes, our trousers. But no one minded a little sand. The first fritters finally scraped out of the frying pan and into our pie tins, and the feast began. Dillee's first taste and, "Chester, that's simply wonderful . . ." Crunch, crunch, crunch. I figured could be propped up by one of the paddles at the head of my cot, and then the lower edges could be tucked in and under the head of my cot and draped down. I threw off the hot blanket and stepped gingerly from the pinhead mesa into the wet sand and in the direction of the pool into which Dillee had suddenly plunged, when bringing the canoe into port, from a level a little below his knees to a bottom that brought the water suddenly up to his neck. The bars on which we had camped were pocked with these indentationssome of them like this one, round as a plate and with sides falling almost vertically, sometimes as many as four or five feet. Boils, often fifteen to twenty feet across, had a habit of coming up suddenly alongside the boat with a noise as loud as the splash made by a leaping fish. After several hours of gliding through cool air and placid water-characteristic morning hours during which we passed a lone fisherman pulling and whipping at his long conical nets, and again, the sectional light keeper perched precariously on a piling, working on his beacon-we saw ahead tied up to a steep, muddy but well-shaded bank a sizeable houseboat. How about pulling up and having a talk with the "river rat?" What sort of person would choose this mode of livingalone, no house or village visible in any direction? 

Why should he choose this particular spot? Where had he come from? What did he eat? Where'd he get the money for fuel? Shoes? Would we be met with a growl and a gun?

The boat under the house, a barge, must have been twenty-five or thirty feet long, with a ten-foot beam, its deck rising about four feet above the water. In it, with a porch fore and aft and a fourteen-inch walk at either side, sat a neatly constructed house with a flat tarred roof and a tin smokestack. Swinging up from the rear, we moved alongside two skiffs. One of these was at least fourteen feet long and powered by a fourcylinder ex-Whippet engine, which for the moment was resting on bars across the gunwales and in the process of overhauling; the other and much lighter craft was merely for rowing purposes. A heavy gangplank led from the house upwards to a spot under large soft maples. There the occupant had a pile of at least a cord of sawed and neatly stacked stove wood and a large work table on which, glistening colorfully in some stray rays of the morning sun, stood, row on row, twoquart jars of what were evidently recently canned fruits. Other rows of two-quart jars stood on a table on the porch before us; and through a carefully screened door our eyes took in the kitchen with its oil stove, linoleum floor and shining kitchen cabinet. "Hi, boys!" This greeting from a Yankee-faced man in his middle sixties hurrying down the gangplank. His cordial tone of voice proved the open sesame to our landlubber curiosity. "Goin' down the river?... Heh?" 

He cupped his ear. "Hearin' ain't so good no more. 

Don't see many folk on the river. . . . Heh? Clear to 45

In answer to our questions he told us how he had been a sheet-metal worker most of his life in Kansas

I jes' figured I could live right cheap down here . . . and live decent too. If the river leaves our garden alone, why we, the missus 'n' me 'n' the kid, we can live swell on three dollars a week." He had been off and on the river for some twenty years. Now he was on for good. 

Bad ears and "eyes not so good no more-not good enough for gettin' hired but good enough for here." But there's no market now. 

Four cents a pound's givin' it away. We're going to have to move. But where, we can't decide. The missus likes the people yonder (pointing shoreward) and if we go anywhere she wants to go up. I'd like goin' down."

But how was he going to move his establishment, house and all? He smiled. "Why, that motor and boat can drag the whole shebang up the river any day at five miles an hour. On the river I'm a free man; I can put up anywhere."

So wet and flat was the beach and so precariously muddy, as was evidenced by the deep holes left by the feet of boatmen who had preceded us, that the retard offered the only clean approach. With our cans refilled at the stockyards (and community) pump, we paused for conference before a sign across the tracks, "Fish for Sale."

Looking into the fishy maw of a refrigerator fitted into the rear end of an old Ford, we listened to the much tattooed dealer elaborate on the superiorities of buffalo and drum. He also had been off and on the river over a period of many years. "She always welcomes a fella' back again when things don't go so well."

I encouraged him to press his sales talk because both Kelley and Dillee had approached the market with unconcealed reluctance. How much? Seventeen cents a pound. "How's the market?" His answer: "Why, I'd sell ten times what I do if I could only git the fish." 

Immediately our three voices burst forth with a plug for our houseboat friend, Redman. We left eventually, having been sold no less than three pounds, which we dropped into heavy brine in our camp bucket, and which I was now committed to cook by a special frying process guaranteed to make anyone eat fish, and like it!

I had better make the fish palatable. This imperative grew steadily into larger dimensions as the day progressed from the pre-heat to the broil and finally to the baking stage. So uncomfortable became the temperature that even the cliffs lost their power to entertain us. Geological time and all time perspective telescoped into a red-hot present from which it was impossible to extract the mind. Herons feeding along the inlets and bars, squirrels racing us along the banks, a frightened watersnake winding hurriedly away, a noticeable increase in the number of sycamores and soft maples, brought hardly a comment from anyone.

Even the government shipbuilding and repair community with its line-up of stately and colorful river steamers succeeded in doing but little more than pierce the hypnotic daze in which we floated, neither awake nor 47

asleep. Stories and jokes, now being dug out of early boyhood, came only infrequently. . . and the responses without the explosiveness and merriment of previous days. Our business had become exceedingly serious. 

Pleasure-even the pleasure of rising above discomforts and laughing at them-was nonexistent. Escape came only in the machine impersonality of the halfconscious rhythm of paddling. A glitter of the water in the corner of the eyes, a plunge of the entire body, arms firm and unbending to make the gravitational pull do the work, the soft gurgle of the miniature whirlpools following the blade, the upswing of the back, the raising of the paddle, the clean-cut sliding of the blade into a never-ending brownish liquid: all in all a graceful sight when seen from the shore and performed by two paddlers working in slow but perfect rhythm. 

Nothing in this called for much alertness, for thought; and thought, as usual taking the course of least resistence, became quiescent. Thus consciousness, self, all but disappeared for long intervals. A spoken word came unwelcomed, and it startled. One's answer was likely to be in the tone of "Aw, shucks, why wake me now? 

Let me have just another five minutes." Thus for an hour, silence each one in that silence immersed, submerged and floating along in a sluggish stream of consciousness that rose to self-consciousness only when the other paddler called, "Over!", or something in the river, a bit of drifting log resembling the corpse of man or hog, acted as a retard in the psychic stream. But when the serious point comes on a roughing trip, then, experience had taught me, "Watch your step." By now I was paddling as much as either of the others and as powerfully . . . or at least I thought I was. But for the moment the old water problem loomed large again. We had improvidently swigged our way deep into can number two before coming to the bar which we later named "The Isle of Golden Dreams." 

Boiling potatoes in river water had become by now an unquestioned practice. But how about coffee? "Well, this is what I'd suggest"-Kelley drawling slowly and meditatively: "If we'll all take a dip and get cool now we won't need much water before eating. 

Then, with an orange or two afterwards and another 48

dip, we'll probably get by on what we've got." Below us about two miles on a hill around a bend lay the handsome and sleepy old town of New Haven. We wouldn't have to wait long to tank up in the morning.

Our island was the largest, the highest and driest yet. 

All through the day we had come across bars which rated as six and seven o'clock islands. This one easily deserved a 5:30 rating. The frequency and quality of these bars followed undoubtedly the terrific severity of the recent flood in these parts. High waters had washed away most of the beacons in the last hundred miles, and those we had been following were merely makeshift crosses hung in trees or propped up on banks. 

Into the water we plunged immediately upon getting the canoe high enough to stick-in, stripped of every shred. Our beach proved to be wide and deeply laid with coarse sand. The water, rushing by, as it always does whenever there is sand, tore away at our footing. At each step the foot, or the hand trying to dig in, dug from eight to twelve inches into the sand, which gave way in peculiar vibratory jerks, before one could reach anything solid. Was this the dread quicksand? In spite of the fact that holding on called for effort, strenuous effort at times, we found the river sufficiently cool to take the edge off our perpetual thirst.

Out of the water, we proceeded automatically on the nudist colony principle-and how the soft breezes and the glow of the setting sun felt to the skin, skin craving for contact with something more than underclothes and sheets and harnesses, is beyond verbal description. 

Away and flying across the sands, plunging at times into the basins, slithering into mud bottoms, letting the soft cool stuff ooze between toes, then scampering across a yellow ridge, picking up a stick here, a tinderdry tree there-thus three fully mature men became little boys again and regained the lightheartedness so essential for the enjoyment of roughing it. 

The fish, rolled in pancake flour, fried in a quarter of a pound of butter, although not a little salty from their long brine bath, brought a smacking of lips and calls for seconds and thirds from every quarter. We did not get rid of all three pounds, however. Another long soaking in the swift stream, then welcome blankets. 49

New Haven, cozily snuggled among dignified old shade trees, had a semi-official greeter down on the stony waterfront with a warm welcome for us early 

Friday morning. "Hey, fellows! Goin' down the river? 

How far ya' been? I'll help you." Caught in an offshore eddy and uncertain as to where he could safely nose the canvas prow, Kelley held us poised precariously in a current that was determined either to dash us against the rocks or whirl us out and away. "Hold 'er there jes' a minit!" The welcomer, a substantially built Negro of about fifty-five years, had run out of a near-by shanty that bore midriffs the telltale high-water marks, and into his heavily constructed flatbottom. "I'll pull up here." He rowed mightily but like one to the manner born. "These rocks can't hurt this boat. You jes' pull up 'longside 'n' use mine for a dock." To this cordiality we responded with questions as to his own state of affairs. How about the flood? He laughed. That was a real joke. "Why, it left me with a present of two feet of the finest mud pie I ever did see." Well, what did he do; was he living there now? Hearty Negroid ha-haha's. "Didn't hurt me none. You see, I'm like a jack rabbit. I've got two or three homes. One's way up yonder on the hill, high 'n' dry. No, I know this river too well now, after 47 years of gettin' my livin' off'n it, to let her really hurt me. It's like a woman, you know.

She don't have to be so damn shinin' clean, and she don't have to be purrin' 'n' nice all the time, but if she looks arter yer kitchen 'n' don't keep yuh in a doghouse all th' time, 'n' yuh kinder like her too, why then a guy can put up with a lot, 'ticularly ef you've gen'rally gotta good idea when it's acomin'."

Dillee, at the stern, raising his paddle to point down the long roaring retard along which we were all but flying, called our attention to a long, curved tree lodged between poles and projecting at least ten feet above them into the air. A sculptured dinosaur, brontosaurus, could not have been more lifelike. Its neck stretched upward, its mouth open, it was the picture of a death agony. So fascinated were we with the reptilian spectacle rushing upon us that 50

we failed to take note how close to the pilings we were floating-eight feet, six feet on the lateral current. "HEY!" from Cap'n, just awakened to something lying ahead and making a disturbance in the water. "Off to the left, Ivan! More yet!" Before us lay a small cataract, made, quite evidently, by a huge tree whose base, lodged firmly between pilings, held it out at right angles against the millstream velocity of the current backing up the waters and creating a minor waterfall. 

"Pull, Rev, PULL. FOR GOD'S SAKES; BOTH OF 

YOU! PULL! PULL LIKE HELL!!!" Kelley was up on his knees directing the show whose comic conclusion was separated by a hair's breadth from the tragic, or pathetic.

Our plan for the day called for getting at least as far as historic St. Charles, the first permanent white settlement on the Missouri. We would have then but twenty-eight miles to the mouth. Thus Saturday's trip into St. Louis harbor would be merely a pleasure jaunt. 

The Missouri, however, seemed to become wider, the reaches became longer, and bends almost the exception. 

Thus for several miles at a time we found no especially swift currents on which we could glide with little effort; rather, the opposite-long open avenues which when swept by a firm but gentle breeze broke into waves sufficiently choppy to add considerably to the difficulty of paddling. In addition to this seeming impedence of our onward movement we also had again to face the sun, which soon rose to new heights of burning. 

Mid-afternoon, Dillee, sweating in the pilot's seat, sent forward the request that I, in the prow, try to change my stroke a little. I was pushing the canoe off course. "Try pulling in." Then in a few minutes: "No, that isn't it. Just hold your paddle this way and finish at this angle, and you won't push the boat over. I'm having to steer at nearly every stroke." And this instruction I seemed stupidly unable to carry through. 

Something was decidedly wrong about my paddling. 

Had I been causing them this trouble all week? Had they just been extra decent to me? I, physically the least conditioned, the newest to the river, had ordinarily sat in the prow and just paddled. Another sug- 51

gestion from Dillee: "How about shifting sides? My left never was any good." We shifted. Kelley, pouring oil on waters that were beginning to look squally, explained: "The front paddler should merely work at a pace he can keep up for some time and he should pull neither out nor in but simply straight back and parallel to the course. If each man paddles as he should, one on each side, then the thrusts toward opposite sides cancel each other and little or no steering is required and we follow a straight line." Nevertheless I was still pushing the boat off course-making it extremely difficult, it was evident, for the other man. Dillee, exhausted, and on the point of exasperation, acceded with little reluctance to Cap'n's timely discovery that his watch was ordering him to relieve the pilot. Still my paddling pushed the boat off course. Presently I found myself being instructed by two nettled steersmen. My inner response was not irritation or resentment but a deep chagrin that at so near the end of a splendid trip I should be the one to cause irritation and to tack on a bad conclusion. The dread breaking of morale was imminent. How about letting me try the rear position?

The proposal was accepted with an alacrity that suggested that it was probably long overdue.

Far ahead to the left rose the greyish outlines of a hill, St. Charles. If I could hold out in the stern for another hour, then flagging spirits could revive themselves on sodas and moppings with cold water. Then a refreshing night of sleep, and the crown of the week might be silver if not gold; certainly not thorns. Kelley in the prow pulled precisely, setting forth an example of what the well-trained and seasoned canoer ought to know. He thrust, and I thrust in unison. But what was this? At each thrust the canoe veered toward the side on which he paddled! To test my rising suspicions further I called "Over." The same veering against his stroke again! Were he and Dillee simply exhausted, and did the difficulty lie in the unconscious power of my stroke? Had I strengthened that much in five days?

The St. Charles riverbank was not of a nature to relieve the mood that had settled upon us. It was vile with hundreds of feet of putrefying garbage littering every inch of a twenty-foot incline and worked into 52

Broken bottles, shining as well as rusted and tattered tin cans, wire-and from a huge vent sickening fumes wafted along by the evening breezes and carried over the embankment and back into the town-a universal junk heap made of the river to which this town owed its beginnings and its rise. So littered with broken glass and jagged cans was the bank that we dared not pull our craft nearer than just close enough to permit a leap to shore. 

Anxious to do all in my power to help raise the spirits of my exhausted companions, I insisted on remaining with the canoe, which I pulled up into the midst of a thicket of leafless willow bush, where I held it for about an hour. As I had hoped, my mates returned from a drug store fountain with enlivened spirits which easily bore up under the test of getting into the canoe. 

A foot plastered with mire to far above the ankles and dangled over the canoe's gunwale brought a chorus of laughter. Now it was my time to sit in the bottom, to eat an Eskimo Pie, and then to prepare lemons for another drinking spree.

Kelley in the stern suggested to Dillee that he pull out a little. Dillee responded cheerfully and manfully. 

The canoe swerved off course. Kelley commented: "Deucid current we've got here." In a minute: "Over." 

Then presently: "Ivan, would you mind pulling out again? . . . Well, try pulling in." On went the suggestions together with analyses of difficulties-and all carried on with the most determined of cheer. They could not make the boat behave properly. I sat with head down over the lemons, striving painfully to steel against the wrinkling of insistent smiles. Hadn't it gone along steadily when I took over the stern? Later on and during the course of the evening each one commented privately to me: "Sure is surprising how quickly you toughened in."

Two miles below St. Charles we found a six o'clock bar, "Twenty-Five-Mile Island," with a shore swept by a current which came down to us, we felt sure, from the side of the river opposite the banks of the "vilest of towns." Nature, however, outdid herself to make up for the difficulties of the day. The sun beyond golden waters and silhouetting rows of majestic trees set in 53

plush splendor. Overhead, the crescent moon, thin and new, trailed the master of the skies by a scant two hours. On the heels of a disgraceful quantity of lemonade and a hearty supper came a long and lazy dip in the river. Then the jeweled and infinite canopy over our heads. Twenty miles to the south (forty-one by water), making its presence evident in a brilliant auroral glow, lay St. Louis.

Darkness. But in the skies loomed a spectacle so overawing in its blinking and luminescent immensity that closing the eyes would have been an act of profanity. A long period of silence, then out of the darkness a voice: "What a cozy little world this must have been for our forefathers who believed that the sun and the moon and the stars were merely hung up there to light the earth-all for man. Now it's all mystery and infinitude. Think back to the beginnings, a thousand years, a million years, a billion years, and where do you get? Think out into those spaces above us to the moon, to the sun, to the Milky Way, and then beyond this super-galaxy to other galaxies, and where do you get? Think into the future, swarms of suns burning, dying, flaring up again—on one little planet revolving about one of these suns, worms, fish, lizards, monkeys, man-everything changing, evolving, but into what? Cocky little men have lots of answers, but under the stars what squeaking mites they seem.

Frankly, I feel lost at times, literally lost . . . and panicky. I sometimes envy, really envy, those persons who can really believe the answers of the priest-what he says in answer to our questions why we are here, why the world, where we are going. All that people who accept his answers need to do is to put mystery and infinitude out of their minds, attend church or mass, confess or pray now and then; and that is the end of all their wanderings. It's all taken care of for them. 

They are at home in a small town; all else is provincial and outlandish." 

A voice coming from the direction of another cot: "To me blind belief isn't belief at all. Not in this day 54

and age. It's an act of disbelief, a fear reaction against inescapable mystery and infinitude to which they are desperately and fearfully trying to close their minds. 

Real belief isn't something you can get second-hand any more than you can get love or the feeling of parenthood second-hand. It's got to come out of mystery and doubt and trouble. It's got to come out of my own confusion and loneliness. When it comes this way it's real, real as thunderstorms and sand blasts have recently become real to us. Three men, conscious that in their discussion they had conducted themselves in a manner fitting and proper under the temple dome overhead, felt privileged finally to close their eyes and contentedly bring to an end another of a billion-billion yesterdays and tomorrows.

Saturday's sun set to work early to subject our morale to a final examination. By the time we had been on the river two hours the blue overhead became pinkish and fiery, flushed. The blazing ball heading toward the zenith no longer baked us: it seemed rather to sting any exposed patch of skin, however well tanned. 

Eyes fighting the intense glitter of the water became tight slits. Above, perched on imposing headlands, private country-club palaces, retreats of the St. Louis plutocracy, looked down on us. But none of the princely establishments aroused in any one of us the slightest nostalgia for millions-these possessions also sizzled. 

A mahogany-cabined speed boat, oblivious of our existence, and leaving a dangerously high wake, refused to 55

slow down (as is called for in the traffic law of the river) in respect to our flimsiness. The stiff and bored occupants had a roof over their heads and probably enjoyed a breeze at the rate they purred down the reach. But this again was nothing to us . . . nothing compared, in a few minutes, to the refuge we found along a cliff at the 9.3-mile point. It was a waterfall, dropping but a scant eight feet over a rock's edge into the river; but the water came out of a hole in the cliff, onto a shelf, and then over the crest for the final leap. 

Out of the mouth, as we skimmed past, determined to find a landing near by, poured a gust of air comparable to the inviting midsummer breath of a theater entrance. Pulled up to the rock shelf, three silent and sweating canoeists crawled upward through fern fronds and over moss carpets to the shaded shelf above with its botanical garden of foliage. Off with shoes and into the refrigerated stream flowing out of a greenskinned mouth extending back into a faintly roaring throat perhaps a yard in diameter-into the water as long as we could stand it, then out, and in again. 

Between bathings of wrists and necks in the crystal cascade we lunched on oranges and the remainders of the cooky box. Sitting in cool rapture, our imaginations traveled into history-thoughts of the soldiers tugging at the free end of the long tow rope (cordelle) attached to the mast of the heavy Lewis and Clark scow. Did they pause here to take deep drafts and to fill canteens? Then the Indians, back for hundreds, perhaps thousands, of years. . surely they planned their days to make this spot their stopover. "Now here" (emphasizing the here in contrast to certain man-made scenes), "here is something that's really beautiful," commented Kelley. "It's worth every burn and skeeter of the whole trip." Two listeners nodded enthusiastic affirmation.

"Here again is something that is really beautiful. A man sees it in the face of a woman and he goes crazy over her.
