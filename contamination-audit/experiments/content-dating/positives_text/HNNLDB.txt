FOREWORD 

Many of you who have been seeking enlightenment have attended innumerable lectures and have taken various kinds of educational courses. 

Several years ago I studied six weeks under six different psychologists who disagreed on fundamental principles. Finally one of the sextette confessed, "We have to make extreme statements in order to keep our names before the public." 

This remark set me to thinking. I made up my mind that I would do my own research work in order to find basic truths upon which to build a philosophy of life. I studied certain phases of philosophy, sociology, and religion, and compared the teachings of psychiatrists and psychologists. 99 My purpose in life is to do my part in rebuilding the American ideals that have been torn down by what Ernest Dimnet, the author of "The Art of Thinking,' calls "thought hindering insincerity." A large per cent. of society has become victims of poisonous formulas of Self-Realization that have been swallowed by hungry men and women who wanted to solve the riddle of life. The solution can not be found in books but in our hearts.

Our nation has need of great personalities, strong in purpose, and deep in feeling, who are not ruled by what "they say" or limited by arbitrary, man-made rules that are not based on principle. The salvation of our country depends on courageous leaders who are vii

Man can not be "conditioned" by environment, if he recognizes himself as a part of the all-pervading Wisdom and Power. In the process of evolution the supernatural of today becomes the natural of tomorrow. When our young people grasp the significance of their relation to the Infinite Mind, they will become a law unto themselves that can not be broken by individual selfishness, or political intrigue. Old conditions can be transformed by the renewing of our minds. "We see now events forced on which seem to retard or retrograde the civility of the ages. But the world spirit is a good spirit and storm and waves can not drown him." MONTAIGNE.

The spirit of the Declaration of Independence must be revived. Our country calls for recruits, not destroyers, but builders who will reconstruct the American ideals that have been torn down by dizzy men and women, who have lost all sense of proportion. Charles 

Milton Newcomb, psychologist and humorist, told the 

Executives' Club of Chicago that he heard of one man who was seeing things in such a large way that he went down the street and picked up what he thought was a dime, and it turned out to be a manhole cover. 

Our new contractors must be independent thinkers with courage, integrity of purpose, practical wisdom and unblurred vision. For the progress of civilization will be retarded until the debris that has been left by ambitious citizens, greedy for fame and wealth, has been removed.

Many aspiring writers have sought public favor by trying to destroy beautiful traditions. By knocking 

Sir Galahad off his pedestal one author showed his prowess. A college professor found satisfaction in 11

gathering data to prove that Pocahontas did not save the life of Captain John Smith. No doubt the intellectual gentleman was searching for truth, but he discovered nothing really basic that would be of value in teaching boys and girls how to solve the problems that are confronting a confused nation. After all it is unimportant whether or not the Indian maid actually participated in the dramatic incident portrayed by historians. 

One magazine writer, who sneered at Fred Stone's plays because they were so clean, finally stopped chewing his frayed and soiled philosophy that pictured life as a "dirty business from beginning to end." He frankly admitted that he was tired of the popular propaganda about realism and longed for some oldfashioned romanticism. This reformed skeptic should be commended, because he changed his viewpoint several years before commercial writers perceived the handwriting on the wall. Popular authors were still serving their squalid "pulp" to a public, already "sopped and drugged with sordidness."

Down through the ages man has been searching for reality. The World War, not only killed, maimed and shell-shocked our soldiers, but mutilated the moral designs of the universe. Our economic, political, and social life has been invaded by "minus" personalities, who have spent their time destroying patterns of wholesome living that conflicted with their distorted idea of individualism. The scoffers have a "distaste for excellence" that makes their cheap formulas appear to a disadvantage, so they built gaudy structures in which to play their jazzy tunes.

But moral sentiment survives all persecution even though some of the believers are burned at the stake by the cowardly un-

believers, who fear the power of leaders who have accepted the affirmations of their own souls. The walls built by the shallow worldlings are beginning to topple. Soon the valiant soldiers, who have been fighting for righteousness, will remove the tarnished rubbish and start building strong fortifications against crime and immorality that will give a feeling of security to honest citizens. 

Joseph Jastrow, the author of "Piloting Your Life," thinks that war is due to defects of national sanity and wisdom rather than to economic conflicts or ethnic interests. He says that a nation is subject to the same mental limitations as an individual, and that national hysteria, suspicion and fright have been brought on by morbid propaganda. I believe that the majority of clean-minded men and women will agree with most of the statements made by this practical, sincere and upto-date psychologist who has been studying the subject for over forty years. It is perfectly obvious to all students of human nature that lack of emotional stability has affected the whole world. 

Adolph Hitler is an interesting study in psychology. 

His various reactions show that he has lost all sense of proportion, a characteristic that is decidedly marked in people who are greedy for power. He is the type who refuses to accept the responsibility for his own actions. He, therefore, must find someone to bear the blame. The persecution of the Jewish population. furnished an outlet for his inner conflicts in which the lower side of his nature was victor. And yet deep down within, the better side is struggling to express.

He is tormented and turns to "Art" as a salve for his sick soul.

The chancellor, who describes himself as the guardian

and custodian of Art, is trying to dodge the blame for the destruction of civilized patterns of living by assailing Jewish intellectualism. He has now promised a "virtual millenium, but genius must be racially pure, imbued with Nazi ideals." Every one is cognizant of the fact that Germany has contributed to the field of science and music, but their philosophers have spread propaganda that has destroyed the moral stamina of thousands and thousands of people. 

If our neighbors across the water are going in for culture; and the "chief" wants to plant seeds that will flourish for a thousand years, he'd better start by pulling up the briers and thistles that have been planted by near-sighted scholars. His country is in a state of spiritual confusion which has been brought on by men who have sown seeds of disintegration that have sprung up in various soils in all parts of the world. 

The pessimistic philosophy of Arthur Schopenhauer, who published, in 1818, "The World As Will and Ideal," attracted popular attention. He was a decided advocate of analytical thinking which he carried to such extremes that he finally lost his mind. He and 

Descartes believed that the fundamental philosophical attitude was to receive nothing as true or beautiful but to consider everything as a problem. There is food for thought in this idea, but a more balanced and optimistic way of looking at the daily experiences of life, is not to doubt their beauty or reality, but to accept them as a challenge to our intelligence, an opportunity to choose the high or the low road. It is our decisions that show the quality of our character. 

Sigmund Freud, the originator of psychiatry, attributes most of the conflicts of life to sex. But Carl 

Jung, the Swiss scientist who first entered the medical

profession and then turned to psychoanalysis, attaches less importance to sex as a motivating factor. He believes that the battle for prestige is the dominating force that influences human relationships, and that the true object of the psychoanalyst is to overcome this evil by directing those who are suffering from inner feelings of inferiority to loftier ideals. 

The great feeling of inferiority in a child who never learns the joys of independence is expressed by a tendency to disturb a group of other children, to play the clown, or to be the bully. They desire favor and appreciation that should be won by courageously meeting failure and defeat, and by rising by their own efforts above the pitfalls of humanity. Children should be taught early in life that grandstanding and blustering give only temporary satisfaction. It is surprising how quickly a young child can distinguish between worthwhile self-expression and mere showing-off, if his thoughts are directed along this line. 

The propaganda about the danger of repression is one of the major causes of the moral let-down that has swept the world. Liberty which has been interpreted as license does not bring happiness. No one can have a real sense of freedom until he has a sense of assurance that his noble sentiments and moral ideas have ascendency over his animal tendencies. The sensualist, who gives free rein to his desires and tries to win the battle of prestige by sex appeal, limits his own possibilities because he is unaware of the heights to which man can climb when he has learned the lessons of self-mastery.

The struggle for happiness is a struggle for mental adjustment, an effort to regulate our desires so that they will harmonize with our powers of accomplish-

ment. We must overcome egotism, jealousy, sensuality, and laziness, a heritage that has been handed down by our savage, cave-dwelling ancestors. We need not expect the joy of a serene and uncontested victory.

The majority of people have vague ideas about selfdevelopment. Society is made up of a vast number of persons who think that the major purpose of life is to accumulate. But the materialists, who worship the golden calf, become victims of fear when their idol is destroyed. Great personalities have a broader perspective of life.

Julia Ward Howe, a devoted wife and mother, who was inspired to write "The Battle Hymn of the Republic," told the story of her abundant life in just eight words, when her daughter asked her what she considered an ideal aim of life. She paused a moment and then slowly replied, dwelling thoughtfully on each word; "To learn, to teach, to serve, to enjoy."

But in order to direct our activities wisely we must not be content with a mere accumulation of knowledge; we must test its quality We do not have to become a pedagogue to make our life complete, for all of us, consciously or unconsciously, teach others by our example. Every adult member of society plays some part in molding the lives of growing boys and girls who are susceptible to the influence of environment. A life of service need not be a life of sacrifice, but one of joy. 

When Julia Ward became the wife of Dr. Howe she found the adjustment to her new life demanded service and sacrifice, but she had no regrets. "I shipped as captain's mate for the voyage," she once laughingly remarked. I can not imagine a more practical interpretation of married life.

The joy of a happy marriage awakened in this noble

woman's soul a deep sense of appreciation for the man who inspired her. "But for the Chevalier," ('the noble rider' whom Miss Ward married), "I should have been merely a woman of the world and a literary dabbler." When she found herself, she was filled with a desire, "To give something that was vital-something that would fill a need and make a difference to people in the real world of action."

The person who is sure of his place in the great plan of life does not seek the praise of a fickle crowd, nor is he dismayed by the obstacles put in his path by men and women who ignore the rules of fair play; they try to win in the game of life by building handicaps that must be surmounted by honest sportsmen, but the final victory is always awarded to the runner who keeps his eyes on the goal of righteousness.

By righteousness I do not mean the negative goodness attitude that was adopted by the mystics who sought spirituality by drawing away from the world, and spending all of their time in meditation. There is work for all of us to do and we are not fulfilling the purpose of our lives by endeavoring to impress others with our importance, or by becoming a passive nonentity that dodges the daily experiences of life. When we reach a point that we see purpose in everything and begin to work in harmony with that purpose and not against it, we are beginning the fulfillment of our lives. 

It is through meditation and prayer that we receive the inspiration that awakens dormant potentialities that seek opportunities to express, for spiritual enlightenment should always be put to practical use by being applied to the everyday problems of life. "Hardship by hardship, task by task, conquest by conquest, we pull ourselves up a little higher on the scale." We must

digest every experience, if we want to gain sufficient spiritual strength to become masters of our destiny. 

Free self-expression means chaos, but the self-control and self-denial of the mystics is an ignorant substitute for self-reliance and critical self-direction. We progress by setting free the better forces of our nature and our purpose is not to worship God, but to express our divine potentialities. The emphasis should be placed on the affirmations of our finer qualities, rather than upon the repression of the ignoble. 

The subject of repression has been misinterpreted by pseudo-scientists who have overestimated the biological and mental attributes of man, and underestimated the spiritual; just as the mystics and certain religious cults of today overestimate the spiritual and mental and underestimate the importance of the physical side of human nature. It is when our minds, souls and bodies are unified in purpose that our lives become harmonized.

We can learn much from the synthetic thinker, Aristotle, who avoided extremes. He was simple in his ethics and did not preach about superhuman ideals but taught that the aim of life is not goodness for its own sake but for happiness. We choose honour, pleasure, and intellect because through them we gain happiness, or victory over our environment. The Greek philosopher declared that the peculiar excellence of a man is in his power of thought and that it is the development of this faculty that brings fulfillment. He did not believe that excellence is the possession of a simple man, but the achievement of experience, for it depends upon clear judgment, self-control, and symmetry of desire. The guide is the middle way. Between cowardice and rashness is

courage; between humility and pride is modesty; between "Hamlet's indecisiveness" and "Quixote's impulsiveness" is self-control. The golden mean is not an exact average between two means but is determined by "mature and flexible reason." Excellence is not an act, but a habit that is developed by training, that does not ignore the importance of thinking and overestimate the value of speed tests. 

James Truslow Adams, the historian, declares that the strain in America has become terrific, and that men with brains are all flying out of the country. This is a broad statement, but should be a warning to the socalled progressive educators who are trying to remedy the inadequacy of modern methods by adding more meal to already lumpy porridge Quantity is a poor substitute for quality. Too many interests overstimulate the nervous system and cause people to become high-strung and scatter-brained. The mental health of our nation can not be improved by sampling all of the complex and indigestible dishes advertised by educational faddists, but by a wholesome and well balanced diet.

If we are to cope with the realities of life more emphasis will have to be placed on training in habits of concentration and discrimination. Spending less time in grandstand activity that appeals to the shallowminded, and more time on reflective thinking about fundamental principles of living that can stand the test of any age will unify and strengthen our country. Our progress is retarded because so few people do their own thinking. It is so much simpler to accept the mandates of those who pose as authorities. But unfortunately men and women who can show superior records of scholarship are not always "plus" personal-

ities, who are strong enough to bring order out of chaos. 

People who depend on the ideas of others lack the magnetism which all original action exerts. Mere book learning is not a sign of superiority. Our social problems will not be solved by modern humanists, for as someone has said, "A highbrow is one whose learning has exceeded his intelligence." Ralph Waldo 

Emerson tells us in one of his essays that, "The relation of the soul to the divine spirit is so pure that it is profane to seek to interpose helps . . Whenever a mind is simple and receives divine wisdom, then old things pass away-means, teachers, texts, temples fall; it lives now, and absorbs past and future into the present hour."

The First Psychologist 

The modern trend has been toward materialism that has little time for thoughtful meditation that reveals the superficiality of our present standards of living. More simplicity and less stuffing is the tonic that this tired world needs to restore its mental equilibrium.

People, who have been led to think that the major purpose of life is to become a success in order to acquire its accompanying riches, are as badly maladjusted in times of financial depression, as the idealist was during the "decade of the dollar," when an idea was rated by its "cash value," and people asked themselves. "Will it get me anywhere?" "Self-culture implies and demands reflective thinking on duty, and on the motives to duty, on one's own nature, capacities and liabilities, and on those great themes of thought, which by their loftiness enlarge and exalt the minds that become familiar with them." 

The study of strong personalities makes us conscious of the fact that the motive that makes real greatness is a desire to help others. The power of the self-

centered individual is not permanent because as soon as his material gods are shattered he becomes a victim of fear and melancholy. It is much easier for a cultured person to make readjustments after a series of vicissitudes because his sense of enjoyment and appreciation is more developed. "He is at one with the past as he is with the present and future, because he finds a part of himself in the past that belongs to others." 

Buddha in his search for truth discovered an idea that is worth analyzing even though we accept only a part of it. Some orthodox Christians may resent turning to the life of a pagan for inspiration; certain critics do not even admit that he was a real historical character, but consider him a mere focus for popular legends. Nevertheless there is sufficient proof of his existence for the majority of historians.

Asoka, the emperor of India who ruled Northern Hindustan from 263-227 B.C., came himself and worshipped at the birthplace of Buddha and caused a stone pillar to be set up to show that the "Blessed One" was born here. Discoveries made in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries corroborate with the accounts of two Chinese pilgrims who visited these places many centuries ago. One disciple left an inscription thirty years after Buddha's death, a time too short in which to build a myth.

Any history of religion must be divided into two sections, pre-Christian and Christian. There was a time when religion and life were one, but when the elements of life are divorced from religion, and are given separate spheres of their own, society becomes confused and feels a vague sense of impotence, for religion is the power that unifies individuals and harmonizes the clashes of selfish men and women.

Often in times of great travail some soul, torn with conflicts, gives birth to some unifying concept of life, a new interpretation of all existence that satisfies individuals who are hungry for inspiraton. The shock which Buddha received when he was twenty-nine years of age changed his whole life.

The young prince of India was indulged and restricted by a doting, but irresponsible father who tried to create an artificial state of happiness by endeavoring to remove all sorrow from the environment of his son. When he went for a walk runners went ahead to clear from his path any unpleasant sight that might destroy his contentment. But destiny was stronger than his father's will, for, in spite of all of his absurd precautions, his son came in contact with misery, poverty and death. The prince was deeply moved by the sight of an old man, a very sick person, a corpse and a religious ascetic. Nothing his wife or father could do could make him forget that human suffering existed close to the walls of his three palaces, one for the cold season, one for the hot, and one for the season of rains, which had been built for him by his devoted father.

He was brought up in luxurious surroundings, yet he managed to develop remarkable strength. The fertile plains of India were rich, and the princes of the old Aryan stock lived a life much like that in King Arthur's time, many centuries later in Merrie England. 

They sang songs, made love, and played games and had tournaments. Prince Siddhartha (Buddha) set out to find the deeper meaning of life just as Galahad went forth to seek the Holy Grail.

While the four sights of human misery which he saw furnished the immediate cause of his renunciation

of a life that was saturated with ease and luxury, there were other motives that moved him to leave home the very week that his wife, after ten years of happy married life, presented him with a son. There is no doubt that the abnormal life of his childhood that eliminated all of the shadows of human tragedy resulted in a violent reaction that would be classified by most psychologists as decidedly abnormal. But social workers tell us that just before or just after a man's child is born there is a time when he is apt to desert his wife. 

Records of desertion show that this reaction is not only among the poor classes. Among some savage tribes it is the custom to keep a husband in bed during his wife's childbirth period. There are times when all young men have a desire to leave home; sometimes they do not feel this urge until they take on the responsibilities of marriage. But Buddha was still under his father's jurisdiction ten years after he married the princess Yasodhara. 

A study of the evolution of religion reveals the fact that many prophets and religious leaders at the age of thirty are motivated by a strong desire to save humanity. Zoroaster, the scientific mystic who tried to overcome the fear and superstition of the Persians, and the Christ, who taught the redemption of the soul by way of the cross, started out on their missions when they were thirty.

The Indian prince, after twenty-nine years of indulgence, silently left the palace. Historians tell us that his super-sensitive nature was even disgusted by the sleeping attitudes of the rows of female musicians who were made visible by the bright June moonlight. 

The young man traveled until dawn and then dismounted his horse, cut off his hair with his sword, re-

moved his royal garments and sent them and his horse back with his servant. He put on the yellow robe which later was a mark of Buddhism, and became a traveling monk with begging bowl, razor, needle, girdle and water strainer.

After traveling through three kingdoms he found two teachers, but they were of no assistance in his search for truth. Six years he fasted and inflicted selftorture in order to discover the secrets of life, but his painful ascetic strivings were not fruitful. He found out that severe mortification of the flesh was not rewarded by "noble knowledge or spiritual insight," so he decided that there was a better way of enlightenment. He concluded that he would be more apt to find truth if he kept a sound mind in a sound body, avoiding both sensual enjoyment and super-asceticism.

When he began to give his body proper nourishment, rice and sour milk, a diet acceptible at that time in the orient, he was deserted by his disciples who had more faith in austere sacrifice than in the Middle Way of temperance. Buddha afterwards referred to his self-torture as a "time spent in endeavoring to tie the air in knots." 

Fantastic stories are told about the Indian devil,

Mara, who tempted him, assisted by his armies and even his daughters. But finally this evil spirit departed saying, "For seven years I have followed the

Lord step by step. I can find no entrance to the All 

Enlightened, the watchful one. As a crow went after a stone that looked like a lump of fat, thinking 'surely here I shall find a tender morsel, here perchance is something sweet,' and finding no sweetness there, the crow departed thence; so like a crow attacking a rock, in disgust I leave Gautama."

After Buddha decided to live a more healthful life, he sat down under a Bo-tree (a species of a fig) and determined to sit there until he knew the truth. There is no doubt that his intense craving for enlightenment was the result of the way he had been lovingly deceived about the realities of life by a devoted but unwise parent.

The Indian mystic is supposed to have waited one month for the great revelation which was so exceedingly simple. The new thought, that all pain is caused by desire and therefore peace comes when one ceases to desire, struck him with such a blinding force that he was exalted for the rest of his life. He felt like an emancipated soul because he believed that ignorance had been dispelled. His soul was illumined as the dawn brightened, for his enlightenment came "in the last watch of the night.'

Two passing merchants became his first lay disciples for Buddha started out immediately to preach his newly-found truth. His first sermon which may have been a compilation of several sermons contains the central truths of Buddhism. He summed them up in Four 

Noble Truths :- 

1. All living is painful. 

2. Suffering is due to craving or desire. 

3. Release from suffering comes when desire ceases. 

4. The way to cessation of suffering is by the 

1. Right views 

2. Right intentions 

3. Right speech 

4. Right actions Eight-Fold-Path. 

5. Right livelihood 

6. Right effort 

7. Right mindfulness 

8. Right concentration

Buddha was the first practical psychologist, for he was the first man on this planet to assert that man's salvation and peace come, not from outside influences, but by the control of his own mind. He believed that if a man walks in the Eight-fold-path he will gradually improve until in some final life he will attain the extinction of all desire, which will be the blessed peace of Nirvana.

The new religion appealed to the people of the east whose worship had been a form of animism. Buddha strengthened the appeal by spiritualizing many current practices and giving a moral meaning to already accepted customs. He was a great teacher and an ef fective missionary, who, in spite of wealth and noble birth, adopted the life of the poor and denounced the rich who oppressed them. But he also rebuked the poor who were not living righteously, for he placed character above wealth, position or ritualistic religion. 

Christianity and Paganism

Both Buddhism and orthodox Christianity look upon the present world as an unsatisfactory place. The Reverend Samuel Shoemaker divides Christians into three groups. The Pre-crucifixion Christians are often interested in a moral ideal, and believe that the positive side of life must be emphasized. They do not pay much attention to sin and so do not like sermons which preach about it. They want to hear about the love of 

God, and try to dodge or evade all unpleasant realities. 

They are drugged with a philosophy that does not admit evil. This is an immature attitude that can not be accepted as sound by people who have passed through the heavier experiences of life, who know that

religious optimists leave out something without which 

Christianity is incomplete.

In spite of all of the statements made by followers of sugar-coated cults all life "moves into its crucifixion period." The Episcopal rector tells us in his book, "If I Be Lifted Up," "The unutterable tragedy of those who have keyed themselves up to a false optimism which does not work in the day of disaster, is one of the most dreadful fruits of this kind of belief which is running all through our world today, asking people to make believe the darker side of life is not there by not facing it. It is there, and it is coming to you someday and to me." Psychologists tell us that all growth comes through conflicts, therefore, the second period of Christianity is of vital importance for the development of faith and stability of character. 

The second type, the Crucifixion Christians, see life deep with sin and sorrow and gross injustices. Anyone with forty years of experience has in all probability been chilled by the shadow of the cross. Many 

Christians who have suffered deeply may remain outwardly calm and cheery but the wounds inflicted by their fellow beings heal very slowly. Some of the most enthusiastic optimists become the sourest of skeptics. during the crucifixion period of their lives. Many theological members of the church never get past this period. "Their weakness consists in thinking that

Christ did nothing else than die on the cross and that if we accept the philosophy of this event we are Christians."

But the world is blessed by the Post-crucifixion Christians, who know the beauty of ethical teaching and holy living. "They realize that the darker side of life needs interpreting. Any faith that is to take

a deep hold of our natures must accept this phase of life, because there can be no revelation except by way of the cross."

Modern humanists are trying to solve all social and economic problems by education, but the rector of Calvary Church of New York City, tells us that our deepest need is not for more instruction, but for more inspiration; not more knowledge about what to do, but liberated power to put into effect what we have already learned. He affirms that the power of the crucified Christ is greater than that of the living Christ who walked on earth.

The Post-crucifixion Christians know something of the joy of living that the pagans could not appreciate because, "It came into fullest flower in Christ." 

The modern idea of self-realization nurtured by the kings of the earth is only a half-truth; the ideal life is the kind that Jesus lived when he walked in Galilee and taught, healed and served. "The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the Lord, and against his annointed, saying, "Let us break their bands asunder, and cast away their cords from us." Psalms 2:2-3.

Samuel Shoemaker asserts that the most serious problem of today is pride. "Better drunkenness, and violence and crime, with honesty enough to call them by their names, than the state of mind which thanks God we are not as other people. We need desperately some pride-destroying element in our religion; something which will provoke pride to repent. Pride avoids common sin, and is complacent, or pride commits ordinary sin and does not call it sin." The old maxim,

"Pride goeth before a fall," should be a warning to autocracies that have not built their fortifications upon a sound basis. "For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good or whether it be evil." Ecclesiastes 12:14. 

Dr. Jastrow tells us in his book, "Keeping Mentally

Fit," that the most serious error of our generation is, "The over-emphasis of intelligence to the neglect of right emotions-putting head above heart." This "honest-to-goodness" Doctor of Philosophy believes that it is more important to have wholesome inclinations and sound morals than to be learned or intelligent, clever or wise. In the debate between virtue and wisdom he places virtue first. "Unless we can keep the moral standard as high as the intellectual standard-universities might become training schools for crooks instead of institutions for higher education-nobler mental fitness." 

Buddha told his disciples, "Go ye now out of compassion for the world and for the welfare of man. 

Preach the doctrine which is glorious. Proclaim a consummate, perfect and pure life of holiness." Some authorities say that Buddha did not believe in prayer, but he believed in meditation which is a form of prayer, for during the rainy season he, like Zoroaster, sought retreat during which time he wrote, meditated and planned his sermons for the summer preaching tours. 

There is no doubt that he was strengthened and inspired during these annual periods of solitude. 

Jesus taught that the individual life is full of meaning, while Buddha felt that each man should seek to lose his individuality. The oriental philosopher's faith

in natural and social laws is similar to the modern philosophy of the mechanists. The behaviorists, or the animal psychologists who study the emotions of fear, anger, and joy through the reflexes of animals, have discovered that for, "apprehension and ability to make deliberate rather than impulsive choice," the domestic dog and the elephant lead the list. 

So you see the sacred elephant of Siam that is worshipped by the believers in transmigration of souls is also appreciated by our animalistic scientists. 

Spiritual Laws 

The animistic theory of transmigration does not reach conclusions about the evolution of man by studying the reactions of animals, but the recurring cycles, or series of rebirths that were preached about by Buddha are distantly related to the doctrine of the Rosicrucians. The religious mathematicians map their lives in accordance with geometrical progression. 

The mystic seeks illumination by harmonizing with the divine mind through meditation, while the Rosicrucian becomes acquainted with his relationship to Cosmic cycles through the study of natural and spiritual laws. 

The Indian philosopher was a mechanistic mystic who had faith in the immortality of the soul that was denied by Santayana, the naturalistic philosopher, who declared that the soul is just a sensitive organism that grows each generaton out of a seed. 

Ralph Waldo Emerson had faith in mysticism that contradicts the lop-sided theories of the naturalists who have been riding the hobby horses of a noisy merry-go-round. The American philosopher asserts that "real action is in our silent moments." He be- 99

lieved that the intellectual life should be kept clean and healthful, not by speculations about the nature of man, nor about the theological problems that deal with the origin of sin or predestination, which he calls the "soul's mumps." He assures us that a simple mind will not be bothered by these enemies. “A few strong instincts and a few plain rules suffice us." 

But the modern skeptics who have tried to rob man of his divine potentialities scorn simplicity and are bored when virtue is commended. They do not agree with Plutarch who said, "When we see a soul whose acts are all regal, graceful and as pleasant as roses, we must thank God that such things can be and are and we must not turn sourly from the angel and say, 'Crump is a better man with his grunting resistance to all of his native devils.' 999 Emerson recognizes man as a medium for Divine expression. The great personalities of history were unobstructed channels through which God was able to work. We are wires made to carry the messages from the Cosmic, and not the creators of power. We are made "live wires" only by our contact with the dynamic energy of the universe. If all of us realized this fact our lives would be much simpler and happier. 

Emerson tells us that the wringing of hands and gnashing of teeth interferes with the optimism of nature. 

We refuse to realize that our lives are regulated by spiritual laws which execute themselves, for external nature teaches its lessons with calm superiority. We cause unnecessary friction by our everlasting fussing and fuming. "Nature does not like our learning much better than she likes our frauds and wars."

The radical advocates of Self-Realization, that promoted a selfish individualism that ignored the rights

of others and exalted their own, have little appreciation for the sacrifices or virtues of society. But selfishness defeats its own purpose, because there is a higher law than our will that regulates events. We may evade man-made legislation but spiritual laws are irrevocable. "None can wrong the universe. We prosper when we accept its advice and when we struggle to wound its creatures our hands are glued to our sides, or they beat our own breasts." The whole course of things is wisely ordered. We need only to obey. 

John Dewey's idea of abundant living that concentrates on industrial activity does not harmonize with Emerson's philosophy. He does not think it was necessary to put so much emphasis on the selection of occupation, for there is a "fit place and congenial duties for every one." But we can not find this place until we cease our petty squabbling for prestige and get still, so that the stream of divine power and wisdom can find a channel that is unobstructed by selfish desires. 

The experiences that we need for our development give us the opportunity of deciding the direction in which we are to travel. If our goal is wealth and power we will go in one direction; if we are seeking 

Cosmic perfection, we are going God's way and will be given something to do which could not be accomplished by anyone else, for everyone who has time to stop and listen will be called to do something for the Master. "If the labor is trivial, let him by his thinking and character make it liberal." The inglorious cat-gut can charm an audience when it is touched by the bow of an inspired musician.

Emerson did not believe that man's individuality is warped by external pressure unless he allows it to be. 

He tells us to accept our genius and say what we think,

because we are selecting principles that gather our like wherever we go. We take our own out of "multiplicity." We are moved to accept or discard according to our sense of values.

Dr. Paul A. Wolfe of the First Presbyterian Church of Evanston, Illinois, told a class of graduates of Park College that, "The real thinking of the world has been done by a few persons." He urged the young men and women to live on a plane above emotional confusion and economic disturbance, for revolt and socialism are brought on by a lack of discriminating judgment of restless men and women who have not learned the art of thinking. It is the unawakened human beings who are imprisoned by their inferior desires who are content to join in the lock-step procession with the other wooden soldiers who are not conscious of the power of creative energy that comes direct from the Infinite Mind.

We can never build a permanent happiness that will withstand the storms of life, unless we start with a desire for the peace that comes when we cease struggling to impress our fellow-men. "Be and not seem.

Let us acquiesce. Let us take our bloated nothingness out of the path of divine circuits. Let us unlearn the wisdom of the world. Let us lie low in the Lord's great power and learn that truth alone makes rich and great." For it is divine recognition that finds for us the place which we were made to fill.

III MAN VERSUS WOMAN 

"Deliver us from fear and favor, from mean hopes and cheap pleasure. Have mercy on each in his deficiency; let him not be cast down, support the stumbling on the way, and give at last, rest to the weary."

STEVENSON. 

Aristotle whose wisdom has been acknowledged for many centuries did not recognize woman's place in society. He asserted, "Woman is to man as the slave to the master, the manual to the mental worker, the barbarian to the Greek." He considered woman an unfinished man left standing on a lower step in the scale of development. The egotistical philosopher felt that the enslavement of woman was a rare accomplishment. He even belittled the courage of woman by stating that the courage of man is in commanding; that of woman in obeying. There is an element of truth in the last statement, for it does take courage to obey men, especially when they talk at random. 

Women have proven their prowess by countless victories but the battle still goes on. It has been said that man, was (or will be) the last thing civilized by women; for women are the agencies that establish a settled family and economic life. Modern women are now competing with men in almost every field of endeavor. There is no doubt about her efficiency, but there is a great deal of doubt about the final outcome. 

Are women solving social and economic problems or are they making them more complicated? Are the motives back of her revolt selfish, or unselfish? Is the

desire of women to assert their influence in all departments of life based on a sense of responsibility that is prompted by the mother instinct, or is it based on a sense of inferiority that seeks compensation by gaining mastery over masculine members of society? If a sense of inferiority is the major cause, the men have it in their power to remedy this mental affliction. In fact the men are greatly responsible for the vast number of discontented women. 

Members of the feminine sex may pretend to be so independent that they are indifferent to the opinions of men, but deep down within every woman's soul is a desire to be appreciated by the opposite sex, even though she has built a strong mechanism of defense to hide this perfectly human side of her nature. But in the last quarter of a century many women with superior intelligence have apparently preferred careers to homemaking. Why? The reason can be answered in one word, men. Now we'll ask another question. 

Why are modern men lacking in chivalry? That, too, can be answered in one word, women. 

Inez Haynes Irwing in her book, "Angels and 

Amazons," says that the woman of yesterday is no more. The inference is that now that woman is taking man's place in the economic world, she is losing the qualities of grace, charm, courtesy and refinement which were her attributes before she emerged into a new being. We must admit that wings would be a handicap in this busy world, for women have been forced to take on the masculine qualities of the sturdy oak, in order to survive. But is it necessary to discard all of the "angelic qualities" that once were so appealing? Real progression demands that we grow in grace as well as in wisdom. If we do, it may be pos-

sible that the men will honor us with the same respect and courtesy that they once gave to "the lass with the delicate air." Lady is not just a "pretty sounding word." It symbolizes good breeding, and surely that is not to be shelved in order to obtain legal and economic equality. The present position of women is not an ideal one, because they are beginning to carry more than their share of responsibility. 

Mr. Men, are you willing to admit that you are dodgers? Mr. Joseph Clark Baldwin in an article in the New York Times Magazine said you were. A democracy should be a government of the people, by the people, and for the people, but Mr. Baldwin does not think all classes are represented, because a large per cent. of the graduates of the high schools and colleges are out playing golf, while the real leaders, who never even saw the inside of a high school are herding the voters to the polls. It is unfortunate that the majority of educated citizens feel that they are too superior for small political jobs. The "better class" (mentally) have allowed those who are in the game for gain to take control; and, consequently, our political security has been destroyed by crooks and gangsters. 

Gentlemen, if you refuse to organize your power in order to save your country from the evil designs of organized crime and political dishonesty, and continue to spend your time improving your golf game, the "Amazons" may be forced to take over your civic responsibilities, and we will have to agree with the author of "Angels and Amazons" that some of the aristocracy has gone over the dam, or with Mr. Baldwin who politely insinuates that you don't give a D--n. 

If the men do not want a matriarchy they will not only have to take their political obligations more seri-

ously, but will have to make a greater effort to keep their wives contented and happy in their homes. Efficient wives often become discouraged or dissatisfied because they are just taken for granted by busy husbands and thoughtless children. The conscientious wife is frequently the neglected one, while the helpless clinging vine gets more than than her share of attention and consideration.

Restless married women who are seeking new experiences are weakening our economic structure by taking positions that should be given to men with families, or to the young graduates that are eagerly waiting for an opportunity that will make them independent of parental support. Women who have raised. their families and are blessed with a good home and an industrious husband can work for less than young people who must depend entirely upon their own resources. Therefore certain business firms eagerly take advantage of this state of affairs and favor the married woman who is satisfied with a mere pittance which she spends for extras.

The problem should not be solved by legislation, but by individual consciences, because a law forbidding married woman to hold any position would be an injustice to the noble and unselfish women who are working to educate their children, support their parents, or help a husband that has been handicapped by extenuating circumstance. If a married woman is carrying on some unique business that gives employment to others she may be an asset to her community. But the well-to-do business and professional women, who have husbands who can support them, who are riding around in expensive cars, living in air-conditioned homes or expensive apartments or hotels because they

are filling positions that should be filled by equally capable men and women who are handicapped by a lack of bare necessities, are "minus" personalities who will eventually be leveled by their own selfishness. 

The inequality of position in various homes is causing much unhappiness. An extremely efficient wife and mother is apt to become such a dominating factor that she makes a weakling of her husband and children.

The ideal home is one in which there is real mutuality, a harmonizing of desires and an equal sharing of joys and sorrows But the food, shelter and clothing should be furnished by the man of the family whenever it is possible, even though the family budget has to be cut in places. It is quite evident that many men do need a lift during strenuous times like these, but the morale of men is lowered when women take away from them the major responsibility of providing the needs of their families.

The wise and contented wife, who is in love with her husband and is, therefore, more interested in her home and family than in a mad whirl of social activities, quietly but firmly asserts her superiority by inspiring her husband and children, even though at times it is necessary for her to submerge her personality for the good of the family. The woman who is not playing to the grandstand is not necessarily playing the role of an inferior being. She often has far more influence in the great game of life than the busy club women who spend most of their time running around doing good with the emphasis on the "running around."

There seems to be a difference of opinion about the ideal wife. Mr. D. P. Wilson of the Los Angeles In-

stitute of Family Relations asserted that college women made the worst wives. But Joseph Sabath, Chicago's famous divorce judge says, "It's the beautiful but dumb girl, not the college graduate that makes the worst mate." The judge declares that "the divorces granted to educated women are infinitesimal because their education has given them a common intellectual basis for a happy marriage."

I do not think that the small number of divorces among educated couples is necessarily due to happy marital relations. I believe there is frequently less real mutuality among this class than in families where the wife and husband have had fewer advantages. 

College graduates are not as inclined to demand as much attention from their husbands as do the women who are "beautiful but dumb," for the educated women have more interests outside of their homes. They find an outlet for self-expression in their various clubs, and in turn they are perfectly willing to grant their husbands the same privileges. This attitude is not necessarily indicative of mutuality, or of unselfishness; in fact it is often a sign of indifference that becomes more pronounced as time goes on.

I believe that every woman is broadened by some interest outside her home. Women make better wives and mothers if they have some contact with a project that creates an interest in people who are not members of the immediate family. But too many interests. disintegrate the home life that is so essential in establishing universal happiness. The study of biography gives more inspiration for the time that is given to it than the average series of lecture courses. I have been making a study of club women and I have observed that there is a tendency among the highly

educated to swallow theories that are still too green to be properly digested.

It is my opinion that women who are educated in finishing schools often have more feminine charm and are less self-assertive than the "A.B.'s and M.A.'s" who assume a masculine air of importance. However, no general statement on this subject could possibly be accurate, because there are too many exceptions. This attitude of mannish independence that is adopted by so many women is, in many instances, a defense reaction to hide a hurt pride that has been wounded by a lack of masculine appreciation, or because they are not satisfied with the role of their sex. 

The cause of this attitude can be traced to either unpleasant childhood experiences, or to thoughtless husbands who take the loyal service and devotion of their wives just as a matter of course. This attitude on the part of men is one reason why women are gradually pushing them into the background.

There would be fewer conflicts between men and women, if they would realize that the masculine and feminine natures are complimentary. Men and women in the strictest sense of the word can never be selfsufficient, no matter how hard the feminists try to assert their independence. If both husbands and wives. would spend more time in acquiring a little broader knowledge of life and in trying to understand human nature in general, and their mate in particular, there would be fewer homes poisoned by decaying love, which leads to covert meanness, jealousy, and spite. 

In truly mated couples there is no fighting for supremacy because their personalities are so blended that the success of one is always the joy of the other. The cultural value of the relation of men and women is

determined by men's attitude toward women and this attitude is largely determined by the moral status of women.

Many intelligent and charming women who would make excellent wives and mothers prefer single blessedness to marrying because they have observed that the majority of eligible men are "leaners," who have been spoiled by women who ask nothing and give all. 

Many a man with the potentialities of a good husband and father has been ruined either by the possessive love of a doting mother, or by the attentions of silly creatures who are seeking transitory pleasure, or the more permanent prefix of Mrs. 

Men whose ego must be buoyed up by flattery and constant attention don't appeal to women who admire strength. But the man who is obviously trying to dominate is seldom a type of virile manhood, but one who is not sure of himself and tries to hide his deficiencies by self-assertiveness. It's always the superior man who attributes his success to his wife or mother. 

A weakling would never make such a statement. 

The saying that a woman can "make or break a man" is only partially true, for no woman can "make" a man. She may succeed in awakening him so that his dormant possibilities become alert and he is filled with new strength and determination to win in the face of all obstacles. A wife's faith in her husband may inspire him to reach great heights, but the change comes from within the man; and he can accomplish little or great according to his recognition of his inborn potentialities. But there is no doubt that a selfish or unscrupulous woman can stifle the better nature of a man by a possessive love that holds him enslaved. 

The woman who depends upon sex appeal to hold her

husband is frequently the victim of jealousy that makes her imagine that other women are trying to steal her husband's affections. This type of woman is to be pitied for there is no torment more deadly than jealousy, that is caused by a sense of inadequacy, which seeks compensation by placing the blame where it does not belong.

The lives of thousands of children are being wrecked by unhappy marriages which were made in haste and consequently were not based on the real congeniality that comes when the two partners have mutual interests. There is no finesse in most of the modern proposals. Courtship is almost obsolete. The realist thinks that the greatest compliment he can pay a girl is to tell her that just her presence thrills every part of his being. If she is not impressed by this approach, he tries to strengthen his point by asserting that any girl that has neither husband or lover will eventually get the "jitters," if she doesn't change her mode of living. This sort of reasoning is not logical because this malady is so prevalent among the women who have meekly said, "I do." 

No sensible girl wants to marry a "spoiled child," but the eligible bachelors and widowers have been so sought after that they are quite offended if their proposals are not immediately received with joyous affirmations. The average modern woman is given little time for reflection, for the impatient suitor is apt to prove his babyishness by quickly turning to someone who is more responsive to his manly charms, and often marries "the other girl" to punish his first choice for hurting his vanity. Any woman who is a logical thinker will be glad that she was not sufficiently susceptible to risk her future with a "now or never" neurotic.

The line of chatter used by the sex-sophisticates failed to impress a friend of mine who was counseled by an old fashioned father who told his daughter when she first began receiving attention from the opposite sex, "Remember almost any woman can arouse a man's emotions, but it takes a good woman to make him behave himself."

If more girls listened to this sort of advice their cultural influence would be greater. There are too many girls and women who are cheapening themselves, and womanhood in general, by resorting to the methods that were formerly used only by courtesans or by women of low breeding. Even if a wedding ring is obtained by this method, it is apt to tarnish much more quickly than one obtained by more legitimate means; for marriage that is based on respect, admiration and mutual desires will always outlive one that is the climax of uncontrolled passion.

I certainly would not advocate the extreme measure used by Hitler or Mussolini to push women back into the home, but there is a golden mean, and it's up to intelligent men and women to find it. Happiness and harmony are not gifts that are dropped from heaven, but rewards for an earnest effort to help others learn the joys of a more abundant life. Real self-realization must include more than concentrating on our own selfish interests. There are too many whining men and women who want the world with a fence around it, but are not willing to do their part to get out of the rut that has been made by thousands of thoughtless joy-riders seeking pleasure, rather than the permanent happiness that is found in a well-regulated home. 

I do not believe in the domination of the male, but I'm inclined to agree with Professor Paul Popenoe,

director of the Los Angeles Institution of Family Re lations, who told an audience in New York that homes ruled by husbands were more successful than homes ruled by wives. It is natural for women to have more respect for men who are capable of taking the biggest share of the responsibilities. A husband should stand between his family and the major conflicts of life but it is a wife's duty to try to make the home a haven of peace to which he can come after a hard day full of perplexing problems.

If a woman is jealous of her husband it is because she is not sure of her position. Inferiority complexes are the curse of mankind. This universal malady can never be completely cured; but it could be checked and harmony and happiness restored in millions of homes if husbands and wives would try to help each other develop latent potentialities that are struggling to express, instead of jealously trying to push their mates into the background. An attitude of understanding fellowship between men and women would cure many cases of loneliness and discontent.

IV  

"Be not uneasy, discouraged, or out of humour, because practice falls short of precept in some particulars. If you happen to be beaten return to the charge." MARCUS AURELIUS.

Foolish public speakers have done their best to disintegrate society by trying to establish artificial conflicts between youth and maturity. Walter Pitkin has shown his mental agility by jumping first on one side of the fence and then on the other. He apparently has no very definite convictions about anything but takes on the coloring of the data gathered by his pupils. Even though some of us have a feeling that the clever journalist is still "up in the air," his lively diction holds our interest. He certainly presents plenty of food for analysis; it's up to his readers to select the best and throw away the "padding." After finishing one of his books I thought of what Tolstoy said of one of his characters, "At bottom of his heart he really believed nothing, and found this attitude convenient and pleasant." In one of his optimistic moods 

Pitkin declared that "parents and children are not hostile clans but simply human beings under one roof." "But simply human beings under one roof" is descriptive of the homes in which mutuality of interests has been destroyed by the wrong conception of selfrealization. As Angelo Patri says, "Many are victims of false freedom." "I have a right to live my own life the way I please," is an anti-social statement that smacks of selfishness, intolerance, and poor judgment.

Such an attitude is bound to weaken any unit of society, for nobody can be free from obligations and responsibilities, for we are bound to other people by environment. It is true that unwise parental domination may cause frustrations that may seriously affect the child's emotional nature, but this danger can be avoided by guiding his desires so that he learns to find joy in being considerate of others. 

We often hear adults say, "You can't tell the young people of today anything. They think they know it all." There are two reasons for this attitude of selfsufficiency: one is the type of education that has been promoted by thoughtless leaders, and the other reason is the lack of confidence in the opinions of "mature" men and women who show their immaturity by their lack of mental stability. It is true that many of the younger sets are running wild, and are deaf to all counsel, but I have found from my own personal experience that young men and women frequently seek advice from an older person in whom they have confidence. Youth is naturally argumentative; some types are skeptical, but most of them are dissatisfied with social conditions, but are at a loss to know how to correct these conditions. 

I strongly disapprove of public speakers who try to gain the favor of boys and girls by telling them they know more than their elders This pernicious influence causes serious domestic conflicts that can accomplish nothing. We'll have to admit that children of today are in some ways more advanced than we were at their age. Not because the gods have given them any more intelligence, but because their mental growth has been stimulated by the many opportunities made possible by indulgent parents.

Every one pities a neglected child, but the overindulged "precious" should receive less censure and more sympathy, for sugar-coated concepts of life cause disillusionment and often maladjustment. The mother or father who plays the part of the selfsacrificing inferior who allows his offspring to take the center of the stage is giving him poor training for citizenship, for the child who is a dictator in his home is usually most intolerant of the rights of others and is, therefore, a liability rather than an asset to the community. Many conscientious parents have distorted ideas about repression and discipline. Intelligent discipline is not repression.

For several years I have been studying the problems of boys and girls in our high schools and colleges. 

When I was in Washington, I had the privilege of interviewing thoughtful parents and educators who recognize the weak links in our educational systems. 

Even the young people themselves realize that booklearning and superficial experimenting have taken the place of logical reasoning. A number of students have told me that they don't have time to thoroughly assimilate the lessons they enjoy most because they are kept so busy with new assignments. 

Visionary and impractical educators who are more interested in holding their positions than in the welfare of the children of our nation are gradually depleting the national treasury, to appear progressive and efficient.

Greedy school masters have tried to extract more money from the taxpayers by making elaborate speeches about our responsibility to the children of our nation, but, nevertheless, certain unwise stewards of public funds are willing to put a heavy tax burden on

the shoulders of the coming generation by taking all they can get from a government that is trying to buy popularity.

Giving generously to a city that is ruled by a political machine that has recently gained favor in Washington, has its advantages politically, but it is ethically unsound, for government money should not be accepted at this time of economic insecurity to promote programs of adult education, or extensive recreational activities, when statistics show that many rural schools, where the population is scarce and the votes of small importance, have been forced to close because of lack of funds.

The city that has succeeded in attracting attention by its newly-installed plan of adult education boasts of the success of its classes that enrolled 17,000 students; many of whom were Italians and colored people. We must do all we can in a cultural way for all races and all classes, but at the present time when incomes have dwindled it is unfair to increase the taxpayer's burden to strengthen the educational autocracy that will take all it can get and then cry loudly for more. 

Since the adult education program caught the public eye, this "progressive" city is now launching a recreational program that will employ a large number of recreational supervisors who will be paid out of funds donated by a generous government. 

I have no reason to have any grudge against supervisors of recreation; in fact I think their work is invaluable in slum districts or in crowded neighborhoods where there are no vacant lots and the school playground furnishes a safe place for children who would otherwise have to play in the streets. But I have no patience with the over-emphasis on supervised

play. Any normal, healthy child should have sufficient initiative to direct his own play. Even a good fight now and then develops a quality of sportsmanship that can't be taught by a series of rules laid down by grownups who, by their interference, weaken children's powers of self-direction. 

In the "progressive" city which has been dazzling the citizens with its new ideas the school board has appointed a play supervisor who will substitute play for the old-fashioned calisthenics or "setting up" exercises that, not only strengthened muscles, but developed a sense of alertness, rhythm, and precision that is needed to offset the "mushy" methods of supervised play.

Play is an essential element of every child's life, but it should be done outside of school hours. Elementary schools should be dismissed earlier in cities where the children are required to stay until four o'clock so that they will have more time for undirected play out in 

God's good sunshine.

If the educators would get away from the play idea and concentrate all of their efforts on teaching children how to find real joy in work and study we'd build a much higher type of citizenship. Schools should train boys and girls so that they will be capable of meeting the challenge of life. If we do not begin at an early age to teach them to find pleasure in the accomplishment of worthwhile tasks, when they reach years of maturity they will always be looking for something easy.

Society is punctuated by lackadaisical adults who can't stand alone. They are like dry sponges that do not become inflated until their thirst is satisfied by some associate. Too much supervision and over-organiza-

tion is fast developing mental cripples with undeveloped back-bones and over-developed wish-bones. 

One of the weak links of our national life is lack of self-reliance. There are thousands of men and women who feel handicapped by their lack of education who haven't sufficient power of self-direction to take advantage of the wonderful courses that are available in our well-equipped libraries. 

It has become a habit with so-called progressives to keep grasping for more advantages even though they haven't learned how to make good use of what they already have. Many adults act like spoiled children who are constantly asking for a new toy. There is bound to be a day of reckoning. Even though it seems a gross injustice it is quite obvious that the coming generation will have to pay a big price for the extravagant ambitions of their elders. 

Mothers who dodge their responsibilities and think that their personal problems should be solved by the community, stand out in vivid contrast to the mother of three children who took time to write an answer to an article written by a woman who was quite overwhelmed with the idea of having her son on her hands for the whole summer. She felt that some facilities should be provided to take care of his time. Some mothers are so presuming that they think their children should be taken care of during vacation by people who are paid out of public funds. But this communistic viewpoint is tabooed by real mothers who enjoy the companionship of their children. 

A mother of three has held her family together by teaching each member to do his part, for she believes that "Work makes all happy." Assigning children definite tasks and then seeing that they are satis-

factorily completed often takes more time and energy than it would take for a mother to do them herself but she is willing to make the effort because she knows that this is the only way to develop a sense of responsibility, a quality that is the basis of all success. 

It takes ingenuity on the part of the mothers to keep children occupied, especially in the city, but it can be done if the main object in life is to be an efficient wife and mother. Of course if there are too many outside interests the family is bound to be neglected. 

I shall quote a portion of the article written by the wise mother who has trained her boys so that they no longer "wail and grumble" when a task is "suggested" to them because they have found out that if they do their part promptly they have more time for their own hobbies. "I say suggested, for that is the modern way, you know; we must never do more than suggest; never enforce our dear ones into any unpleasant task-it may make an introvert or something out of him. We suggest to the spineless creature that the shrubs need trimming or the lawn needs a manicure and he raves until in desperation we hire 'one of these men who need the work so badly.' "Our suggestion that we offered with such inward trembling goes by unheeded. Then son eats a hearty lunch and wails again because there is nothing to do. "The only bright spot in the whole picture is that there are, here and there in this city, mothers and fathers with brains and will power, as well as the respect of their children, who dare to dream and use more old-fashioned methods in training their offspring for a useful and self-reliant future. They do not give

up and in desperation turn to military schools to make the younger generation walk the chalk line."

No amount of higher education can eradicate the damage done by parents and instructors who have been misled by impractical theorists. John Held, the humorist, tells us that boys don't go to college for an education but so they won't have to stammer when asked what college they went to. Dr. Clarence Little, a specialist in research and practical administration, says higher education imposes a responsibility for public leadership and further contribution to science. 

The progress in science is more than satisfactory, but are the colleges turning out young men and women who have well-developed qualities of leadership? "He who reads incessantly and to his reading brings not a spirit and judgment equal or superior, uncertain still remains, deep versed in books and shallow in himself."

The young people of today are better students of human nature than the flighty adults who have been hopping from one theory to another in order to appear progressive. The rate of real progress is determined by our attitudes toward the past, the present, and the future. If we study the old philosophies, we shall find that the truths they expounded are as true today as they were in the early centuries. Human vices and virtues are ever repeated down through the ages. 

There can be no leaps in development. Anything that is stable is developed by a slow progressive movement and not by "fits and starts." In the last quarter of a century we have been very active, but our activity has not deepened the channels of our lives. Much of our energy has been wasted because it has not been directed by leaders with sound judgment.

I have the greatest sympathy for the confused and discouraged mothers who lack confidence in their own good common sense and have substituted impractical methods that have developed in children a self-centered anti-social attitude that, in many instances, defies both parental and legal authority. If adults expect to hold the respect of alert-minded boys and girls they must prove that they have mature minds that do not absorb, like mere blotters, whatever idea comes their way without analyzing its merits and defects. Parents have allowed their children to become victims of experiments that have proven unsuccessful, because they haven't had sufficient courage to stand up against educational promoters who have posed as authorities. 

It is now time for all men and women to realize that their intelligence is a part of the universal mind and, therefore, can not be imprisoned, limited, or rated by educational autocrats. The mother of two daughters wrote to Anne McCall, who has charge of the department or human relations in the Women's Home Companion, I shall quote only a portion of her letter. "I graduated from high school but never went to college. I am often thrown with college women. and I feel so dreadfully inferior when I am with them. 

Also I dread the day when my two girls will go to college and I shall feel inferior to them."

Mrs. McCall's answer proves that she is an analyst and not just an educational faddist. I shall take the liberty of quoting two paragraphs that I think should be read by every man and woman who has been made to feel an inadequacy because of the lack of a higher education. "You suffer painfully from a sense of inferiority, but how well do you know or understand its cause?

How much do you really understand yourself and others? As much perhaps in proportion to what there is to know, as might be put on the point of a pin. 

Yet you want to talk of getting a college education as a kind of substitute for this other and essential knowledge. "But what will you or anyone do with a college education when you have it, unless it has given you a sound understanding and knowledge of human nature and its laws and a deep remedial understanding of our self? 

And the average college course unfortunately rarely does that. If you want really to arrive at a better security and a better and more worthwhile education, try putting the horse before the cart instead of the cart before the horse." 

If the mother who appealed to Mrs. McCall really wants a higher education she can acquire it for the libraries are full of opportunities for all who crave enlightenment. But if she is merely longing for the prestige that is bestowed upon those who have received the required number of college credits to put them into a class by themselves, she is just another gregarious individual who seeks security by following the trend of the times and enjoys basking in the reflected glory of celebrities.

Mrs. McCall suggested that the discouraged woman study psychology in some of its simplest forms. This is good advice but it is not quite complete, for the average person is not in a position to select sound psychology, and poor psychology is worse than none at all.

Therefore, great care should be used in guiding the beginning student lest he become hopelessly lost in a maze of high-sounding verbiage that makes "factitious mole holes into fictitious mountains." Many

people thirsting for understanding often injure their mental health by becoming too introspective or selfconscious. The study of biography should always accompany the study of psychology in order to create a scientific interest in human nature in general. We are often cured of a case of self-pity when we analyze the tremendous conflicts that test the endurance of great men and women. The study of human nature is the key that unlocks the door that separates us from the joy of loyal fellowship.

There are hundreds of efficient mothers who are suffering from an inferiority complex, so I shall suggest several books that are full of inspiration and practical common sense. "Keeping Mentally Fit," by Joseph

Jastrow is a book that is easy to read, but is full of enlightenment about the hopes, fears and possibilities of human nature. "Piloting Your Life," and "The

House That Freud Built," by the same author will fascinate the more advanced students. "Concentrated 

Common Sense" by Nellie Olmstead Lincoln, 2209 

Broderick Street, San Francisco, is an inexpensive book that is very valuable because it is "A Help to Happiness" book. "There is a spirit within me and the inspiration of the Almighty giveth me understanding, that I may have, Clearness of Vision-Steadfastness of Thought-and Trueness of Speech." "Remaking

Our Lives," by the Reverend John S. Bunting, rector of 

Ascension Episcopal Church, St. Louis, Missouri, and "The Secrets of a Quiet Mind?" Fleming and Revell

Pub., N. Y., by the same author, are full of illumination and practical advice that should be especially helpful to people who feel disorganized because of inner conflicts.

Parents have no reason to feel inferior to their

children who have had more advantages. The parents have proven their efficiency by their ability to give their sons and daughters advantages that were not given to them. It's not how much we know, but the use we make of our knowledge that rates our value to society. 

Highly educated people, who assume a superior air and look down upon their fellow beings, who have had to learn their lessons in the school of hard knocks, are often stagnant personalities. If our knowledge is not converted into service it is a worthless accumulation of fact and theory. 

It is fortunate that all mothers and fathers are not awed by their "bewildering offsprings." Sensible parents do not tolerate the rude self-assertive attitude that is adopted by children who have been raised by adults who followed John Dewey's advice and made the child the center of interest. Miss 

Emma Boyd was evidently much impressed by this theory because she has been quoted as saying, “The small child must not be restrained from giving definiteness to his movements amplified and reduced to completeness." In plain language this means that he should be allowed any action while the parent stands by cool and collected and sees his darling choke the cat, break up the china or whack the piano. I took a course in social education from a professor who at one time believed that a child should be allowed to unfold naturally without any restrictions. Corporal punishment was absolutely tabooed until his young son threw a plate at a guest. This drastic expression of uncontrolled passion changed the viewpoint of the lenient Ph.D. In his books that were written after the dramatic incident that humbled the pride of the college professor you will find that he leans toward the old

theory that there are times when it is unwise to "spare the rod and spoil the child."

Claire Williams in the Forum and Century tells us that far too many children grow up with self-interest paramount. She says that many young persons of good families grow up to the age of sixteen or seventeen "gloriously unaware that there is anyone else on earth who has any rights." This total disregard for the feelings of others may teach the grownup members of society, humility, patience and endurance but it is certainly not developing the quality of leadership that should be the result of "superior" educational advantages.

It is quite apparent that the "progressives" have neglected to teach growing boys and girls the elementary requirements of civilization. The most important lesson a child should learn is that the world is full of other people who are just as important as he is. He must be taught to respect their rights and privileges, and to be scrupulous in the fulfillment of his obligations. "Spoiled children" who have been raised by the "I

Want" method have not the stability of character, the moral stamina, or the integrity of purpose that are basic qualities of patriotic leaders who are not motivated by lust or greed, but by a love for humanity that is stronger than love of self.

Dr. James H. Wall, after studying one hundred male alcoholics at Bloomingdale Hospital, reports that twenty-seven of the men had alcoholic fathers, but thirty-seven had mothers who indulged and overprotected them in childhood. When these pampered boys became too old to be protected from the conflicts of life they took refuge in drink. Their resistance to

temptation was low because they had not been taught how to control their desires. The progress of the race is largely determined by our powers of sublimation, or our ability to direct urges into civilized patterns of conduct.

Sometimes if a woman's marriage has been a disappointment she seeks compensation from her child, whose infancy she prolongs by coddling and protecting him from all danger, thus denying him the experiences which are needed to build up his resistance. 

There are thousands of adults who have never been weaned. Many a boy has developed a mother complex that has wrecked his marital happiness. Doting mothers cause their sons and daughters to resort to infantile reactions after they reach years of maturity. 

Other parents set young people adrift before they are strong enough to stand alone. While domination incites rebellion, youth can not have its fling unregulated. 

The goal of education should be self-government, but the taking of the consequences method of training must have graded adaptation to each stage of the child's life. Sometimes undirected experiments lead to serious consequences.

A few days ago I received a letter from a friend who did not marry until she found a man with whom she was mentally congenial. I'm taking the privilege of quoting a few lines of her letter in hopes that they may stimulate some constructive thinking about ideal married relationships. "We have so much in common that we stay at home a lot because we do so enjoy our evenings together. I try to do everything that I can for his happiness and he does the same for me, so we get along fine. Thank goodness he has a mature mind and is above doing little petty things that aggra-

vate one. I hope you are not bored by my bragging about my husband, but he deserves all the praise I could give him."

This appreciative young lady is very fortunate to have found a man that evidently was not spoiled by doting parents. It is through sympathetic understanding of human nature in general that we become socialized. This understanding often culminates in a love that leads to sacrifice and altruism. "To enter into another's life is the core of emotional sensibility. To share another's happiness, to take pleasure in making others happy, this is the standard value of human equipment."

The unreasonable parent who bullies his children by demanding strict obedience to his whims, or stifles their initiative by sneers and sarcasm was a child who was either a victim of too much parental control or one that was allowed free rein. The negative type of individuals who enjoy a sort of triumph by saying "no," make poor parents. They are so contrary that they are seldom happy themselves and they do not enjoy making anyone else happy. They often entertain an exalted opinion of their importance and have seized upon the technique of "no" as yielding the greatest satisfaction.

The self-effacing, self-sacrificing parent who wants to give his children all of the advantages that he didn't have, is apt to put them at a disadvantage, because over-indulged boys and girls have a rude awakening when they leave the parental roof and are forced to meet the challenge of an unrelentless world that is not at all impressed by their sense of superiority which has been nurtured since babyhood by their proud parents.

The mother who smothers her children with her possessive love is frequently severely criticized by the father of the family. The critical parent may be jealous and sometimes with just cause, for some women in whom the mother instinct is over-developed neglect their husbands just as soon as a baby arrives upon the scene. But many women cling to their children because their love natures have been wounded or starved by men who have warped conceptions of the duties of a husband and father. So all of the blame can not be placed on the shoulders of the mother. 

I have observed that children raised under these conditions are inclined to be restless and discontented; a large per cent. of them lack initiative, because their powers of self-direction have been bound by protective love that carefully selected the experiences of life that appealed to the mother, who was afraid to let go of her darling lest he stumble and hurt himself. When young people are denied the opportunity of learning the lessons that are taught by the daily conflicts of life they are usually too weak to stand alone when they reach maturity.

If you want to determine the emotional age of an adult watch him when he is frustrated. The man or woman who pouts or uses unscrupulous means of getting even, is still just a spoiled child who hasn't sufficient courage to fight in the open. Parents who express their superiority by crushing the desires of their children are often responsible for nervous breakdowns that come about in middle life. But the repressed being is no more maladjusted than the individual without emotional control who gets what he wants by fair means or foul. The over-disciplined personality who has become a victim of frustration causes far less dam-

age in a community than the selfish individualist who cares not how much suffering he causes as long as he gets his own way.

The young people of today are not inately more wicked than the previous generations but it is perfectly obvious to any person that is not biased by his emotions that a vast number of the younger sets have no respect for parental or civic authority. Some of these young tyrants have been wielding their sceptors with a force that at times even shocks the adults who have led them on by flattery and indulgence. Faults have been ignored or excused, and cardinal virtues underestimated by the dizzy pilots who are responsible for a large number of ship-wrecked lives. 

The headlines of the newspapers are now awakening sleepy adults who have been drugged by poisonous propaganda about the danger of repression. The grown-ups who have been trying to develop leadership by planting the wrong kind of ideas in young minds are now seeing the results of teachings that failed to give the youth of America clean-cut concepts of right and wrong. 

Dr. Frank Crane in his book, "If I Were Twentyone," published by Willam Wise and Company, New York, says that he believes that we should make some "permanent, amicable arrangement with our consciences, because God, Duty, Death, and Moral Responsibility are huge facts which no life can escape.' 99 He believes that the "Great Common Divisor" of all faiths is, "to be honest, pure-minded, unselfish, reverent, brave and loyal." All good men belong to one religion because there is only one thing that really counts, "That one thing is to do right."

Dr. Crane's advice is so simple that it stands out in

vivid contrast to the elaborate theories of less sincere advisors. "So, let a man of twenty-one resolve, and keep his purpose, that, no matter what comes, no matter how mixed his theology may be, no matter what may be the rewards of wrong-doing, or the perils and losses of right-doing, he will do right; then if there is any moral law in the universe, man must sometime, somewhere, arrive at his inward triumph, his spiritual victory and peace."

"It matters not how strait the gate, How charged with punishment the scroll, I am the master of my fate, I am the captain of my soul."

WILLIAM ERNEST HENLEY. 

Society would be unified and strengthened if all ministers of the Gospel agreed with the beautiful philosophy of Dr. Crane who believes that if man makes "doing right" his anchor in times of storm, his polestar in nights of uncertainty, "he need not fear man nor woman, nor government, nor mischief-makers, nor the devil, nor God."

Human love that is not strengthened by discriminating judgment is too weak to reform the world, because people who live on certain planes of consciousness are antagonized by righteous living as portrayed by the life of Jesus. Even the pure love of Christ was not strong enough to overcome the hatred of the sly Herod and the hypocritical Pharisees. Sympathy and kindness can accomplish wonders when men and women are sufficiently developed to appreciate it, but there are undeveloped souls who refuse to turn away from wrong-doing until their mechanisms of defense have been destroyed by spiritual laws. 

In the olden days pastors tried to keep their congregations in the straight and narrow path by filling them with the fear of God. There is no reason why we should fear God because that name symbolizes Good.

But we should be afraid to do evil because it not only brings destruction upon us but upsets the divine plan of life, because error destroys itself. We can not evade this law. The elimination of all fear leads those who ignore the laws of God and man to carry out their selfish and unscrupulous plans with the daring of fanatics. Fear of wrong-doing should be established in the public conscience for men and women who boast of their tolerance are directly responsible for the blurred concepts of temperance.

The philosophic mystics sought temperance rather than renunciation; while the nature mystics worshipped 

God through nature instead of through the sacraments of the church. The Papacy sought to hold its power by teaching that the church was the ark of salvation, and that it behooved all men to get inside the ark and stay there if they would make sure of eternal life.

Francis of Assisi respected the dignitaries of the Church and believed them to be official possessors of supernatural power, but some of the sincere seekers of God were in revolt against a Church that had been corrupted by wealth. There was a desire for reform and struggles to return to the simplicity of the Gospels. 

Since an over-abundance of worldly goods had caused the decay of spirituality, it was natural that reformers turned to poverty as an expression of righteousness. 

The proud ecclesiasts considered this sheer madness in spite of what Jesus said to his disciples, "Freely you have received, freely give. Provide neither silver nor gold nor brass in your purses, neither scrip nor two coats, nor shoes nor staff, for a laborer is worthy of his food."

The battle for prestige between the church and the peaceable St. Francis, the nature mystic, who wedded

"Lady Poverty," is one of the most interesting episodes of history. For a time Francis gained power by agreeing with his adversary, but he died brokenhearted because his purposes had been thwarted by the shrewdness of Rome that averted all danger of schism and heresy by the publication of "The Brief," dated September 22, 1220, that imposed on St. Francis and his companions the ideals of the older monastic orders. The heroic period of his order was dominated by the church that was afraid of a man who was not a heretic in intent, but was difficult to control because it was evident that he would always take a line of his own which was in direct opposition to the Roman ideal that required abject obedience.

The papal authority at first did not withhold formal approval of his work when they saw it was succeeding beyond all expectations; but they realized that they must gain more definite control because this movement might become a serious danger to the church. As 

Henry Vedder says in his life of St. Francis, “An enthusiast is always a menace to a great organization; he is erratic, there is no telling what he may do next; but he is less dangerous inside the organization than outside." The Church won by making the Franciscans a part of the Roman organization; thus defeating the purpose of St. Francis who was anti-monastic. He was an apostle of liberty and love that expressed itself in poverty and service, but the Church forced him to bow to the dogmatists.

It was a tragedy to St. Francis to see his ideals perverted, and to see religion made a cloak to hide idleness and vice. The beggars, who in after years swarmed over Europe, wore the name and garb of St. 

Francis but they had none of his spirit, for his devo-

tion to "his dear Lady Poverty" did not mean begging or squalor; he saw no connection between filth and righteousness. His religion was not based on a desire to live off of the bounty of others; he simply wanted nothing beyond his daily needs. He became a successful wood carver and plied his trade without accepting any money. When a bishop remonstrated with him for living so frugally, he replied, "If we possessed property we should need to have arms for its defense, for it is the source of quarrels and lawsuits." He literally interpreted the teachings of Christ, but his simple way of living was in direct opposition to the churchmen who were jealous and looked with suspicion on any layman or groups of laymen who busied themselves with religion. Teaching religion, so thought the ecclesiasts, was the divine right of the Church, whose zeal for wealth and power was making it the "curse of the age." 

St. Francis hoped for a victory of love of man over love of things, for avarice was then the besetting sin of mankind just as it is today, but history has proven that human selfishness can not be destroyed by love. 

Since time began, the greedy people have always tried to take advantage of those who try to live by the Golden Rule.

St. Francis prevented a violent clash with the Church by combining "serpent shrewdness with his dove-like innocence." He made a pilgrimage to Rome and presented to the Pope a document called "The Rule" that has been lost; but it is obvious from accounts that have been given that the first Rule was made up of Biblical quotations from the nineteenth chapter of St. Matthew, the ninth chapter of St. Luke and St. Matthew 16:24-26.

Innocent III recognized spiritual values in others even though he himself was more intellectual than spiritual. This wise dignitary realized that it was impossible to return a flat negative since St. Francis was only asking that he and his companions be permitted to live according to the precepts of Jesus. But the penetrating Innocent did not give an immediate answer, but challenged Francis with questions, suggestions and criticisms, which did not swerve him from his purpose, for he was unmoved by flattery or intimidations. One of the Cardinals finally said, "If we hold that to observe gospel perfection and make a profession of it is an irrational and impossible innovation, are we not committed to blasphemy against Christ, the author of the Gospel?" The Pope still hesitated because there was a sentiment against founding new orders. The matter was terminated by a vision of Pope Innocent in which he saw a great cathedral tottering but held up by a single man clad in the same kind of robe as that worn by Francis and his companions. The Pope decided that this meant that this humble disciple was to be the mainstay of the church, so he showed his astuteness by requiring that Francis and his followers "Take the tonsure" thereby bringing them under the jurisdiction of the ecclesiasts. 

The group went away happy, thinking they had secured liberty, but in reality the Church had won the victory by changing the group of lay-preachers into a monastic order that must pledge allegiance to the power of Rome. But as yet Francis had no idea of the price he would have to pay for the Pope's concession.

The battle continued but the friar never thought of failure because he felt God had called him to this work.

His faith never wavered and in time great crowds became obsessed with the idea of poverty and sought admission to his order; but all the time the church was gradually perverting the ideals and aspirations of the loving saint who continued to minister to the sick and to the poor until his end visibly approached. 

His last months were spent in meditation and he is said to have received the Stigmata, or the marks of the nails and the spear that pierced the crucified Master. The testimony of some of his closest friends represent the marks on hands and feet, not as wounds but as darkly-calloused places; only in the side was there an actual wound, from which, sometimes, oozed drops of blood. The majority of Protestants are inclined to reject the whole story, as one of those miracles so enormously exaggerated in Catholic literature, but "the annals of medicine contain many cases, rarely happening, yet in the aggregate, quite numerous, of extraordinary effects produced on the bodies of persons of delicate, highly sensitive nervous organization by intense and prolonged mental states.' 99 This theory blends very harmoniusly with that of the psychiatrists who recognize that we are exposed to the influences of suggestion that may be either helpful or harmful; for there are many ways in which the delicate mechanisms of man respond. Pain comes by suggestion and goes by suggestion; we get mentally well by the same mechanism. So the Stigmata is no more wonderful than some of the cures of psychotherapy, which is the art of mental stimulation as well as suggestion. It restores the zest in living by giving graded doses of assurance and encouragement, agreeable occupations, and helpful companionship and wholesome diversion. It is one of the most humane

of arts because it requires, not only insight into the complexities of human nature but a sympathetic understanding that is opposed to the "shock method" advocated by the Freudians.

Even some of our modern ministers have allowed their religious views to become discolored by irreligious teachings that reverted to paganism that is now being spread all over the world by irrational leaders. 

Of course in our country we have no prominent men as radical as Dr. Alfred Rosenberg, the Baltic born leader who opposes Christianity because its doctrines advocate humility, non-resistance and basic pacifism. It would be hard to find a minister on this side of the 

Atlantic who admires the "noble savage" sneered at by Mussolini, or who approves of Nazi propaganda, but there are a number of religious leaders who have been influenced by the Viennese who founded psychiatry. "Freudianism may well be declared an outlaw, a pretentious usurper, so weak are its lines of defense" and yet it has played an important part in lowering morality standards.

Since the time of Adam and Eve the serpent has been in our midst, but we do not expect to find the viper in the churches. "For it had been better for them not to have known the way of righteousness than, after they have known it to turn from the holy commandment delivered unto them." II Peter 2:20.

The mental health of our nation has been undermined by the deception of leaders who lacked spirituality. But the sacred fires of religion have been revived and already the glittering dross that so fasci-

nated the irreligious is gradually being removed by sincere leaders who will guide wanderers back to paths that lead to the happiness that comes with righteous living. As Tolstoy affirmed, "They can do it because all the disadvantages and dangers which hinder a worldly man from opposing the existing order of society, not only do not impede a religious man, but rather increase his zeal in the struggle against falsehood." 

St. Francis was a fore-runner of the humanistic reaction because he cared nothing for theology or the rites of the church, but was passionately concerned with life and greatly interested in folks. But the agnostic in the only real humanist. While, like the scholars of the 

Renaissance he may not actually reject the belief in a divine Creator, he considers the kingdom of man of greater importance than the "Passing Kingdom of God."

Painters and sculptors grew tired of the conventional figures of saints and madonnas. Literary men wanted to write stories, songs and poems about life. 

Teachers realized that instruction should present the value of man and nature as well as of God. And philosophers did not want to limit their wisdom to divine revelation, for they longed to be free to speculate about man and the world. Man became the dominating factor, because the potentialities of human beings had been obscured by priests who wanted to express their own superiority. The haughty priests looked down on the "miserable creatures" who could not hope to see the beauties of heaven without the intercession of the consecrated hypocrites, who considered the layman a mere nonentity in the great scheme of life. It is no wonder that men rebelled for they felt that they had high powers that the clergy denied, because they

wanted to monopolize the arts and crafts of building, painting, carving, governing, thinking, and writing. 

The same sort of domination is going on today but not with the same intensity. 

Some ambitious Doctors of Divinity, anxious to be in tune with the times, have not been satisfied to concentrate their efforts on teaching religion, but have been vying with widows and unmarried women who have been trying to earn a living by giving book reviews. 

A good book review that is given by a clever analyst with a dynamic personality is refreshing. It is a blessing for tired mothers who do not get away from home very often because they are so busy taking care of their families, but the book review habit is making mental parasites of women who are too busy playing cards, gossiping, or fighting for women's rights to do their own reading.

Many of the modern ministers have attracted people to their churches by adopting a broad-minded cosmopolitan air that harmonizes with the loose morals that have been in vogue with parishioners who were looking for an easy road to heaven. There is no easy road to heaven because all mental and spiritual growth comes through conflicts with outer and inner forces. 

Of course the world is blessed with many superior clergymen who are faithfully performing the duties of their office. They have no feeling of insecurity because they are living according to Christian principles. 

They, therefore, feel no antagonism toward laymen who are trying to do their part in restoring the ideals that have been damaged by modern pagans. There is no reason why a minister should be jealous of any layman who is successful in helping others to establish a stable sense of ethical values that will stand the test

of any age. There should be no conflicts between sincere Christians, but I could give a number of instances where friction has been created by men and women who were leading people away from the misery that has been caused by the perverted ideas of popular speakers who have posed as wise counselors. 

One of the most striking instances of clerical jealousy resulted in the disbanding of a fine group of loyal young men and women who were under the leadership of a superior young matron who was generously devoting her time to a Sunday School class that was having its thirst for righteousness satisfied by the lofty ideals presented by this noble woman. These young citizens developed so rapidly under her leadership that they soon attracted the attention of the whole church. 

Even though only poor families were represented, the members of this class were able to solve certain financial problems that had been too difficult for the older members of the congregation. The pupils through their faithful and enthusiastic labors were able to carry far more than their share of the financial obligations of the church. Their outstanding success in overcoming obstacles caused a rift between this group and the rector who felt that his superiority was being overshadowed by the able leadership of one of his parishioners, who in her quiet and unassuming way was accomplishing what his blustering domination had failed to do. Young people, as a rule, do not readily bow to the mandates of dictators, but they were impressed by the spirituality that radiated from the soul of this woman, whose marriage had not blessed her with a child.

The success of this earnest and sincere Christian belies the theory that advocates only men teachers for

adolescent boys. There is no doubt that the influence of men is absolutely necessary for the best development during this susceptible period. But the idea that the influence of women teachers is actually detrimental is not accepted by all men. After many discussions at an international conference of physicians they decided that sex can best be taught by a woman. I can not accept this decision without reservations, but I do think that respect for womanhood, a phase of the sex subject that has been slighted in recent years, can be taught to a better advantage by a wholesome-minded woman past thirty than by "tough-minded" writers and teachers who have been influenced by radical sexsophisticates. I have observed that boys and girls are inclined to have a sort of reverence for an older woman who understands their problems, but Emerson spoke a universal truth when he said, "The purposes of noblest characters are sometimes too high for the appreciation of an ignorant populace. They are often misunderstood and subject to criticism.' 99 An unmarried woman may have a stronger instinct of motherhood than many of the mothers who can boast of families, for Dr. Knopf, who became a specialist in gynecology and psychiatry says that this instinct has nothing whatever to do with the physiology of motherhood, but is founded on a strong feeling of responsibility towards children, society, and the future of mankind, and a desire to contribute to the lives of others. But unfortunately the status of womanhood is lowered by shallow creatures who seek satisfaction by "vamping" young boys, but this type is never popular with the girls in the younger set who are quick to resent the presence of an unscrupulous rival. 

The motherly relationship between an unmarried

woman and an adolescent boy would be quickly challenged by the suspicious Freudians who favor communal relations. "Society must provide wisely for the satisfaction of the fundamental urges, otherwise there will be rebellion, unrest misery and a sense of frustration and injustice," for they believe that all human beings are animated and motivated by biological sexemancipation. There are but few religious attitudes and relations which psychoanalysis can explain. Therefore, it is not surprising that psychoanalytical rectors have become a bit confused.

"There are nettles everywhere,

The smooth green grasses are more common still; 

The blue of heaven is larger than the cloud."

ELIZABETH BARRET BROWNING. 

All of the blame for the mental confusion that has caused so much unhappiness should not be placed on the shoulders of men, for since time began it has been women who have established the moral standards of the race.

There is no doubt that the feminist movement that started in a fight for political freedom has been carried too far by women thirsting for power and experience. 

The serpent tempted Eve and Eve tempted Adam.

The downfall of a mighty civilization was brought about by the intrigues of the Roman Empresses when Rome had conquered the World. "Her government had reached a state of development as to serve as a model for every succeeding age;" and her architectural grandeur had become the marvel of the ancient world. The slogan "Votes for Woman" lead the suffragettes to victory, but the flapper reached her goal of satisfaction by using an entirely different technique. 

Every individual has to decide on what level of civilization he wants to live, but as Francis Bacon said, "We all belong to our age and kind, reflect and imitate, and absorb the view around us, and so repeat the errors and fall into the ways of the day." Many of our finest

specimens of manhood and womanhood have become maladjusted because they were unwilling to follow "the fashions of the tribe," that have been established by shallow members of society with inferior standards. 

The desire for prestige has lead to a cheap sort of grandstanding and insincerity that has been so apparent in cultural circles. Emerson was unquestionably correct when he declared, "In this country we have a superficial culture, and a profession of writing and expression. We parade our nobilities in poems and oration, instead of working them into happiness.' 

Everyone wants to be happy. It is a perfectly wholesome desire, but there can not be an abundant harvest of universal happiness until we pull up by the roots the various causes of unhappiness. We were put into this world for just one purpose to express God by releasing our divine potentialities. If the channel of expression is clogged by jealousy, hate or greed, the plan of life which is always working toward perfection becomes disfigured and the progress of civilization is retarded.

Life is a school in which we have many lessons to learn. We are blessed with a free will that gives us the privilege of molding our lives according to our own ideals. Only a weakling allows society to set the mold for his soul. It is through our reactions to environment that we establish the quality of our characters. Fate will present the filthy and the sordid, the clean and the beautiful, but it is only through the development of our discriminating judgment that we become wise enough to select the good out of every experience. Pleasure-loving boys and girls must be taught that real happiness is not won until we have gained a victory over destructive forces. We can not reach

the goal of fulfillment until our mettle has been tested in the furnace of experience.

Some people are severely criticizing the conduct of the younger generation, while others are defending them. The First Lady of the Land, who made a statement about the "appalling situations" existing in some of the younger sets of society, aroused a vigorous comeback from belligerent parents who, to express it in slang vernacular, "Just couldn't take it."

One irate mother accused Mrs. Roosevelt of discouraging millions of students struggling for an education by her "scathing criticism" of the younger generation. Because of her high position she was expected to hold up an ideal "to fire young imaginations." Devolution must precede evolution, so wrong ideas of conduct must be destroyed before we can build the right sort of concepts. "The finest kind of courage is to openly acknowledge our mistakes and then with grim determination, see that they are never repeated." 

There is no proud parent clever enough to build a mechanism of defense that can hide the evidences of moral let-down that have been sweeping the country. 

A quarter of a century ago a "nice girl" was protected by the chivalry of her men friends; for by far the larger majority of the men in polite society were gentlemen, and any man who dared to "proposition" a respectable girl would have been tabooed. Now-adays the pendulum has swung in the other direction and the men who have a high respect for womanhood are in the minority.

No generalization can ever be perfectly accurate. 

Of course all of the boys and girls are not on the road to perdition. But in spite of the fact that some optimists refuse to admit it, temptations have so multi-

plied in the last thirty years that the young people, who have sufficient moral stamina to keep their balance in times like these, are decidedly superior beings. 

Organized crime has been ruthlessly taking its toll while the sympathies of soft-hearted citizens have been encouraging criminals by the coddling of lawlessness. 

Lawyers who become rich by making it their business to defend criminals are little better than the men they defend. People who have reached years of maturity are not invulnerable to every temptation, but no normal individual should be excused for obtaining the objects of his desires through criminal devices. Coddling and catering to the feelings of self-centered egotists who have an exaggerated idea of their rights leads to crime and immorality.

One popular writer, with dramatic sensibilities, tolerantly stated, in one of her articles about the Lindbergh kidnapping trial, that if Bruno Hauptman had had a different training in childhood he might not have resorted to such extreme measures to obtain the comforts and luxuries of life. This viewpoint is worthy of consideration and should stimulate some constructive thinking among parents and teachers who are entrusted with the training of our boys and girls. But if it was written in order to create sympathy for a heartless criminal, it is a destructive force, for the codling of criminals is a national attitude that gives a yellow cast to American ideals. The insipid tolerance of men and women is one of the links in our civic life that is in need of repair; it is one of the reasons that justice has been imprisoned and we have become victims of crime. For there are types of personalities that are on such a low plane of development that they can not be controlled by love or kindness, but must be

controlled by fear of the law, for they have become spiritually stagnant because they have closed their ears to the voice of conscience and are, therefore, a public menace.

Donald R. Richberg, who was the Executive Director of the National Emergency Council, asserts that the modern practice of law demands mental ingenuity to help a client do anything that he wants to do. This dauntless gentleman goes so far as to say that he considers the practice of law "one of the most degrading occupations in the category of respectable employments." Of course this broad statement can not be unquestionably accepted as true, for all of us know sincere lawyers who are honestly and courageously fighting for justice instead of playing politics. But as

Marcus Aurelius told the Romans, "It is true that all people have some notion of what is meant by justice, and some notion about acting in conformity to this disposition; but experience shows that men's notions about justice are as confused as their actions are inconsistent with the true notion of justice." 

The gregarious type of individual who has no definite purpose in life, but merely follows the crowd in order to be called "a good sport" is accepted by society at large as a well-integrated personality. The chameleon type, who protects himself by taking on the coloring of his environment, will not be hurt by the persecution that is given to the man or woman who refuses to accept standards not his own, but stands firm in his own convictions of right and wrong. The negativegoodness, passive, self-centered individuals who do not bother about the affairs of the world unless they cause them personal inconvenience or discomfort, are of doubtful value to their communities. All progress is

made by liberating the stronger and better forces of our inherited natures and this freedom is the beginning of happiness. Dr. Howard Griggs tells us that "people should not be taught what to think, but how to think."

When the public mind becomes aroused to such an extent that it refuses to be led astray by ambitious men and women who pose as authorities on the vital issues of life, and starts doing its own investigating, our country will be saved from imposters. The intelligence of the average man and woman should no longer be over-shadowed by the educational autocracy that is so over-loaded with data that it can't see over it. The middle classes are the backbone of all nations. It is up to these classes to see that they are not squeezed to a pulp by the upper classes or the masses at the lowest round of the ladder.

When man becomes so inflated with his own importance that he vies with the Creator in dictatorship, he soon becomes the victim of his own conceit. The modern humanists who have been seeking self-realization by a one-sided interpretation of education have limited their possibilities. It is impossible to solve the complex problems of today, if we refuse to acknowledge the source of all wisdom, or the power that comes from the Cosmic Mind. God has always been the answer to the cry of human needs and whenever man, by his own volition, cuts himself off from the guiding power of the supernatural, he becomes lost in darkness. For as a Hebrew proverb says, "Man's soul is the lamp of God." When there is no inflow from the divine channel of inspiration, we become static personalities that are out of tune with the harmony of the spheres. 

Selfish, insincere and dishonest people show their

real colors when their positions are made insecure by the hand of censorship. The Pharisees and the Sadducees hated Christ because he dared to criticize them. 

The elaborate shams put up by the "formalists" were too fragile to hide the corruption of the haughty hypocrites. They despised the man that was not afraid because He knew that God was his strength and his power and that his way would be made perfect. A censor is never popular in the society of self-satisfied individuals.

A certain well-known New York lawyer shows his aversion for criticism by sneering at a group of "snoopers," who according to his version, made Boston ridiculous by threatening to ban from sale several books written by some well-known authors who had gained popularity by their radicalism. The Jack 

Horner attorney, who had gained prestige by his defense of writers who had been held in disfavor by wholesome-minded readers, feared that this wave of censorship would give our neighbors across the water the idea that our country had suddenly become childishly clean-minded. But I think the fact that we still have in our midst a goodly number of "supermen" who are proud of tolerating the intolerable, and boast about their vast amount of knowledge and experience, goes to prove that the much needed spiritual scrubbing brush has not been generally applied.

But the influence of our so-called broad-minded social lions, who have trapped the youth of America in a maze of conflicting theories, is on the wane. The versatile lawyer of Gotham, who has contempt for the pure-minded, has spent much time studying men and institutions. He declares, "One who has literary knowledge or experience in life is likely to pass a book

that shocks the ignorant. What price culture? He also thinks that the condemnatory reaction of a critical public has resulted in "films innocuous and sentimental, flapdoodle with neither thought, dialogue nor scene that could upset the sensibilities of political boards and censors.'

It is quite evident that in recent years the public conscience has become hypnotized by propagandists who have boasted about vice and sneered at virtue.

Many people with finer sensibilities have tried to dodge social and political responsibilities by ignoring existing conditions that are anything but a credit to our community life.

Sincere praise is a constructive force, but it can not create super-beings, nor can it cure diseased consciences. It is a tonic that must be used wisely, lest the recipient become intoxicated by its sweetness. Why laud young people before they have won their spurs in Life's tournament? What they need is not praise or censure, but a steadying hand of fellowship that reaches out to them when they meet problems that are too difficult for them to handle alone.

To study others, to know others, even in a moderate degree, helps us to get their viewpoint and the outcome of such knowledge must necessarily be at least some increase in patience, understanding and love, the attributes of real fellowship. Boys and girls need wise counselors, who neither over-estimate or under-estimate the discriminating judgment of adolescents. 

Many youth enthusiasts are expecting too much of the younger generation because it has been given so many advantages. Some of these advantages have proven to be disadvantages that have softened the moral backbones of the favored members of society

who have developed a pseudo sense of self-assurance that defies all authority. Self-assurance at times is a virtue, but when it scoffs at all rules and regulations it becomes a vice.

Sensitive parents, who take it as a personal insult to them and their children whenever the morality standards are questioned, must not judge general conditions by the environment in which their children are raised, for standards vary in different groups; but social adjustment in any group presents serious problems.

If everyone would try to get a broader vision of life, the human family would be much happier, because there would be more mutuality between youth and maturity. Inharmonious relationships can usually be traced to a lack of understanding of social values. 

Young people must be taught that free self-expression is a poor substitute for self-reliance which avoids wrong action by the process of thought. Our blurred visions will not be cleared until we can see beyond the shifting opinions of an uncertain crowd. 

In speaking of group customs Aristotle told the 

Greeks that they committed errors because they broke the rules of logic for they believed in "Not what could stand the test of proof, but what appealed to their likes and dislikes." When emotion runs high reason runs low and prejudice and suspicion run riot. Our nation should make a fight against enfeebling sentiment that is an indication of mental flabbiness. 

Strength of character is manifested by an ensemble of qualities that makes us masters of our instincts and passions. It is the emancipation of the mind from petty worries and jealousies that enslave feeble minds. 

It is through strength of character that we avoid the

error of obeying the promptings of the moment that may lead to future unhappiness. It is this quality that gives us the power to rid ourselves of the suggestions that are opposed to the resolutions we have made. 

I have a friend who has been going through a transition period that has presented complications of all types. Her endurance has been tested in all sorts of ways. She has been besieged with advice and criticism. 

If she had solved the problem of the moment by following the suggestions of various busy-bodies, she would never be able to carry out what she is destined to do. She has faced scorn and persecution because she has not allowed herself to be drawn away from her goal by suggestions that were incompatible with her ideas of a purposeful life. "All great inventions have made victims. All institutions have their martyrology; the social mechanism wills it so. There is not a single discovery whose mighty results have not been accomplished by individual misfortunes."

Persons of frail character are not masters of their acts, because they are ruled by their instincts and emotions. Yielding to their feelings becomes the habit of their souls. Ideas are not fertilized in the heat of enthusiasm, because it is impossible to prolong infatuation. That is the reason that so many modern marriages end upon the rocks. It takes strength to meet the domestic conflicts of life. Bravery is not measured so much by one act of courage, but by courageously performing each act of life. Little sacrifices often humiliating are greater than those done in moments of enthusiasm. When one has selected a goal he must not be lured into by-paths by merry vagabonds who live just for the moment.

There are many people who consider life just a sort of hodge-podge of the things they like and dislike. 

They are greedy for the pleasant experiences and try to dodge the unpleasant ones. The law of non-resistance is not the same as following the line of least resistance.

The mystics who accept the law of non-resistance welcome the experiences of life with a calm faith in the divine plan, that gives to each individual the experience that he needs to teach him the lesson that he must learn before he can reach a higher stage of development. Sometimes the same type of lesson must be given to us over and over again because we do not take time to find out just what God is trying to teach us. Often our Creator gives us tasks that are not to our liking, but if we follow his commands, he gives power and strength to carry out his plans. God's will is not always easy to follow because often we must be challenged by the censorship of man before we receive divine compensation. "And thou, son of man be not afraid of them, neither be afraid of their words, though briers and thorns be with thee, and thou dost dwell among scorpions: be not afraid of their words, nor be dismayed at their looks, though they be a rebellious house. "And thou shalt speak my words unto them whether they will hear or whether they forbear; for they are most rebellious."

Ezekiel 2:6-7. 

Real freedom is spiritual freedom that liberates the Christian from the fear of the rebellious pagans who defy the criticisms of man and the laws of God.

Clergymen are, according to intellectual standards, superior men, but their spiritual vision is sometimes dimmed by petty jealousies among themselves or against any unordained person who busies himself with religious interpretations that conflict with the viewpoint of the formalists. But in this day and age we can not be burned for heresy; in spite of all argument to the contrary, the doctrine of truth is not confined to colleges, universities, or theological schools. Truth is eternal and it can not be limited or imprisoned because it is universal.

In the dark ages the priests who posed as the keepers of the keys of heaven tried to make everyone feel that all salvation must come through the church and its sacraments. Arnold of Brescia, who appealed to the prudent ecclesiasts at the Third Laternian Council that was in session in 1179, was told to go home and obey his Archbishop. After he and his followers debated among themselves whether to obey God or the mandates of man, the Christian disciples decided to resume their work in spite of opposition. This so aroused the wrath of the church that "The Poor Men of Lyons" were excommunicated. 

The power of our country is weakened by the vast number of "men pleasers" who haven't the courage to stand up for their own conviction. They are afraid to disagree with organized vice, lest they become the victims of revenge.

A business man was visited by a representative of a dominant political organization that was noted for its election outrages and its protection of criminals. 

He was told that they did not ask anything from him or his associates except that they make no move that might annoy the political party in power. They prom-

ised in return that the business concern visited would be protected in anything it wanted to do, and furthermore if it got into a lawsuit, its interests would be looked after.

The business man was so disgusted with the whole political mess that he decided to devote all of his time to his business and left the problem of clean government to the loyal American citizens whose red blood has not been dried up by steam radiators. 

Many people gain temporary success by concentrating on how they can get as much out of the world as possible. Even poachers, who steal the ideas of others' for a while, reap benefits which they have not earned by hard work. But, "Fate appears as a vindicator, leveling the high, lifting the low, requiring justice in man, and always striking sooner or later when justice is not done. What is useful will last; what is hurtful will sink."

An orderly connected arrangement of thoughts and experiences tends to build a sincere and cooperative character, and only when a person dares to be himself, placing spiritual honesty above all else, does he become a self-directing creature. The world would be a better place in which to live if more men and women were willing to sacrifice popularity for principle. "The highest merit we ascribe to Moses, Plato, and Milton is that they set at naught books and traditions, and spoke not what men, but what they thought."

VII THE FIRST HUMANIST 

"If any man among you seem to be religious and bridleth not his tongue, but deceiveth his own heart, this man's religion is in vain." CONFUCIUS.

Confucius, the Chinese philosopher, Kung Fu-tsy (Kung the Master) was outstanding in his adherence to principle. He extolled the five virtues: kindliness, integrity, politeness, truthfulness, and sagacity. He attached great importance to obedience on the part of children to their parents. The ancestor worship that has so affected the progress of China, is a farfetched interpretation of the idea of veneration of family. The extension of his doctrine lead to regarding society in each kingdom as a great family. 

The progressive humanist, who had more faith in education than in the power of the supernatural, was a forerunner of the humanistic movement of the Renaissance, for Confucius told the Chinese to "respect the gods, but have as little as possible to do with them."

The Renaissance, the European Awakening, began long before the fifteenth century. Arnold of Brescia, four centuries before the time of Luther, induced the Romans to throw off the authority of the priests and established the first republic in the world since pagan days. He was hanged, but fifty years later the democrats were strong enough to make Pope Innocent III flee for his life. It was this aristocratic member of

the papacy who inaugurated the practice of murdering people who would not profess to believe what they did not believe.

Dante, the author of "The Divine Comedy," who was exiled when on a mission to Pope Boniface VIII, to check the excesses of Charles of Naples, belonged to a group of skeptical scholars in Florence, who owed their culture to the Mohammedans of southern Italy and Spain. The clash of the Crusaders with the Mohammedan world did not annihilate its influence, for the Moors and Jews penetrated Europe and vied with the influence of Greek and Roman literature. The invention of printing and paper also stimulated the desire to spread knowledge.

The burden of despotism was lifted from the minds of men and fostered the spirit of initiative and independence. But men were stirred more by the corruption of the church than by its tyranny. It fired the humanists like Petrarch and Erasmus, as well as Luther and Melanchthon. The Renaissance was not a democratic movement; it was aristocratic, scholarly and sensual; its standard was the pagan ideal. The reformation was ascetic, democratic and simple; it took man back to Christ and the Bible.

But the first humanist was born long before the French and English scholars received their humanistic stimulus from the Dutch scholar, Desiderius Erasmus, who taught that the clutch of the clergy upon Europe was preventing enlightenment, and stifling liberalism. 

He contributed to the Reformation, but disapproved of its fanaticism.

The ancient advocate of self-development through the medium of his traveling university which included in its curriculum music, poetry, history, literature,

civics, ethics and the little science that was known over five hundred years before Christ, did not encourage prayer or assemblies of worship. He ignored. certain religious practices, not because he was opposed to them, but because he did not consider them essential. 

Little is known about the boyhood of Confucius; but it is evident that he was eager for knowledge, for at fourteen he assisted in teaching the other boys in his class because he had learned all that his teacher could give. He was not a listless, pale, anaemic scholar, for he was an expert charioteer and an accomplished musician.

When the father of this versatile boy was seventy years of age, he had, as yet, not been blessed by a son to carry out the necessary religious rites and the later maintenance of family worship that are so sacred to the Chinese, so he decided to go to the House of Yen and propose to any one of the three daughters. 

The father called his three girls together and told them about the virtues and defects of the venerable suitor; he concluded his remarks by asking which one of his daughters would like to accept this offer of marriage. The two eldest girls showed their discretion by remaining silent, but the youngest, Ching-tsai, bowed deeply and showed her profound humility by meekly asking her most worthy parent a question, "Father, why do you ask us? That is for you to decide." The young woman who had so much confidence in her father's judgment was bluntly told that she would do. 

Within a year this dutiful daughter was rewarded, for what at first seemed a sacrifice of her youth, by becoming the mother of a son, who not only brought peace to the old soldier of the honorable House of

Kung, but wisdom to the uneducated people of China. 

It is said that the descendants of that son are now living in the very same province of Shantung and are still honored and greatly respected.

Some authorities say that the masculine parent died before his son was born and others say the child was three years old; but at any rate the boy was forced at an early age to earn a living for his widowed mother by hunting and fishing. There is no doubt that the necessity of taking on such heavy responsibilities, when just a mere child, played a big part in developing the leader whose influence is still potent in the land that has been so retarded by its individualistic form of government.

Certain schools of modern psychology that have been trying to train leaders by letting boys and girls follow their inclinations, have evidently been so busy evolving modern theories that they have not had time to study ancient facts. For if one takes the time to reason from effect back to cause, we will discover that the men of history who were slaves to their desires, almost without exception, defeated their own purposes, while those who were disciplined in childhood by numerous conflicts, developed a spiritual toughness that protected them in times when their endurance was tested.

At the age of seventeen, the precocious lad was given a government position that made him responsible for the storage of grain, and the supervision of a certain section of the public lands in the state of Lu, in which he lived. It was not a particularly important position, but his scrupulous devotion to duty soon won the respect and admiration of his community, for he showed more intelligence than ordinarily was found

in supervisors. He first showed his philosophy of life, when he settled the claims of rival herdsmen by lecturing upon the absurdity of quarreling. He particularly emphasized the bridling of one's tongue. 

The rough herdsmen could not understand the subtle philosophies and ethical nicities of the ancient classics of China, which our future sage had been studying, so it was necessary for him to make some simple rules of conduct; so he invented a sentence-statement rule of ethics for the uneducated shepherds. Instead of the ten commandments of

Moses he transposed the philosophy of the Golden 

Rule by saying, “Do not do to others what you would not want them to do to you." The negative form may not be good pedagogy, but this wise youth certainly went directly to the heart of proper social conduct. 

His well-developed sense of duty, not only won him a place of honor, but increased his income so that he was able to marry at the age of nineteen.

We know very little about his wife, but when a son was born he was honored by having the Duke of the province of Lu send his congratulations, which were accompanied by two sacred carp for the birth banquet. 

So the infant was named Li, which is the Chinese word for carp, in order to express the proper ceremonial courtesy to the feudal Lord.

In the sixth century before Christ when this young philosopher was settling local disputes, conditions all over China were chaotic. The great dynasty of Chou was degenerating and the nobles were the real rulers. 

The people were suffering from war and the extortions of tax collectors, who must collect large sums of money for the warring barons. An incident that happened after our oriental hero had given up his government

position to become a traveling teacher, dramatically pictures the suffering of the people who were held in submission by greedy rulers, who cared little about the cries of struggling humanity.

One day while walking along a country road near Mount Tai, the Chinese teacher saw a woman crying by the side of the road. When he asked her why she was lamenting in this desert place, she replied, "The father of my husband was slain here by a tiger, so was my husband and just now my son was killed in the same way.' When she was questioned about her reason for staying in such a terrible place, she answered, "because here there is not an oppressive ruler." This reply made a deep impression on the man who spent his life trying to raise political, social and educational standards.

This ancient sociologist, who was trying to establish harmony and righteousness among the people of his day, was unable to adjust himself to the responsibilities and limitations of a domestic life. One cause of the break in domestic felicity may have been due to his continued absence, after he abandoned his official position for the precarious profession of a traveling teacher. The new occupation of the Chinese sage may have given his wife a feeling of insecurity that destroyed her happiness, or on the other hand Confucius may have realized that his preaching mission was of such vital importance to his country that he would not allow his life's work to be handicapped by domestic responsibilities.

Some writers think that he acquired an "audience consciousness," when he was settling disputes of the herdsmen, and this developed into a desire to teach

the philosophic wisdom of the ancient by reducing it to simple proverbs and easily understood precepts.

Confucianism is a doctrine of living that has fulfilled the function which religion is ordinarily expected to exercise, whether it is called a philosophy, an ethical system or a religion. It may meet the requirements of the usual interpretation of religion, but it is not sufficiently inclusive, because it does not establish the vital connection between God and man. The Chinese people are unconscious of the power that comes from the divine when a contact is made by our conscious volition, because they have a vague idea of this relationship. 

While Confucius was not an advocate of all of the religious rites that had been in vogue, he was punctilious in observing the traditional ceremony of mourning, for he left active life and mourned for twentyseven months at the grave of his mother. It is quite evident that her death disturbed his whole inner life since she had become almost a part of himself because of her dependence upon him when he was only a boy. 

This long period of meditation may have been due to pricks of conscience, because he had neglected his mother after his marriage. She died when he was only twenty-four, and it was at this time that his estrangement with his wife occurred. No one knows the cause of his marital unhappiness but it seems logical to believe that over two years of sorrowing at the grave of a parent would tax even the patience of a Chinese wife. 

The death of his mother and the separation from his wife marked a great crisis in his life, which usually precedes the career of most of the outstanding religious leaders. In fact a long period of retirement is often followed by a decision to devote one's life to a public career. It is more than just probable that the

twenty-seven months of meditation at his mother's grave inspired him and enriched his teaching, for he became so popular that his classes grew to three thousand. His method of teaching, by walking from place to place accompanied by those who had absorbed his views of life, was the one that Aristotle used two centuries later in Greece.

The majority of modern pedagogues do not agree with the Greek philosopher that "the citizen should be molded to the form of government under which he lives, and that above all the growing citizen must be taught obedience to law, else a state is impossible." 

But, nevertheless, a blending of some of the best ideas of the ancient Greek and Chinese philosophers, if put into practice in our public school systems, would strengthen some of the weak places that are being brought to light by the disorganized lives of young men and women who have been spoiled by soft methods of education. 

As Aristotle says, "Man when perfected is the best of animals; but when isolated (by selfish individualism) he is the worst of all; for injustice is more dangerous when armed, and man is equipped at birth with the weapon of intelligence, and with the qualities of character which he may use for the vilest ends. Wherefore if he have not virtue he is the most unholy and savage of animals full of gluttony and lust." The wise 

Greek teacher realized that harmonious social adjustment, which is the beginning of universal happiness, is the outcome of righteous living, which is based upon intelligent control of our desires.

The subject that Confucius most enjoyed teaching was civics because his life concern was to improve standards of government. Therefore, he was quick to

recognize in his pupils any sign of oratorical or administrative ability. His curriculum particularly avoided feats of strength or the spectacular. He had no objections to religious ceremonies; in fact he was scrupulous in following some of the customs of the time, but he dodged discussions about the supernatural. 

The personal life of the Chinese teacher was an important factor in the education of his pupils, for he had a highly-developed sense of justice that colored all of his teachings. He never used a net to catch fish, or never discharged an arrow at a bird unless it was in flight, because he wanted them to have a fair chance to escape. He wore linen instead of silk because he had a dislike for killing silk worms.

Even though Confucius was a successful teacher, he was not convinced that it was his life work, because he was so intensely interested in the theory of government. He was constantly looking for a high government position, not because he wanted to obtain personal power or to accumulate wealth, but to try some of his ideas of establishing better living conditions for the average man and woman through proper administration of state affairs.

He made a trip to a neighboring state to visit Lao- Tsze, an old man who was noted for his wisdom. At the time of this visit Confucius was thirty-four and Lao- 

Tsze was eighty-four. While their philosophies harmonized in some respect, they were fundamentally opposed in others. The venerable sage wrote two books, "The Canon of Reason and Virtue" and "The Book of the Path of Virtue," that were purely ethical and contained no reference to superstition or the supernatural. Lao-Tsze stated in his writings; "To those who are good to me, I am good, and to those who are not

good to me I am also good. And thus all together come to good." But Confucius did not believe in recompensing injury with kindness. He believed in reciprocity and said, "Recompense injury with justice and recompense kindness with kindness." Moses also taught "the eye for an eye" dictum.

It is wrong for human beings to try to recompense injury with justice, when they have been wronged, for justice as interpreted by a person who has been wounded could not possibly be pure, for it is bound to be discolored by feelings of prejudice, revenge, or self-pity. When the feeling of resentment dies there is no desire to "get even." But I have learned from experience in dealing with certain types of individuals, that to try to recompense injury by expressions of kindness is an unsatisfactory way of bringing good out of evil, for friendly advances are often misinterpreted, and the evil doer resorts to even more violent means of asserting his superiority over one whom he considers an "easy mark" for his unscrupulous designs. 

The original philosophies of the oriental sages have been so grossly misinterpreted that some of them seem very inconsistent to modern minds. Buddha, Confucius, and Lao-Tsze have all been worshipped as gods even though they themselves were atheists. One of the fantastic stories that has been handed down is that Lao-Tsze reposed in his mother's womb for eightyone or eighty-two years and was born an old man with white hair, and was, therefore, called, "The venerable philosopher" or "the old boy." The latter title evidently does not present to the Chinese mind the same concept as it does to the mind of one familiar with American slang. This idealistic dreamer told Confucius that he must study to discover the central prin-

ciple of things, and that the only way to obtain it was "to give it room in one's heart."

Confucius continued to teach seventeen years after he received this advice and then the opportunity for which he had been longing came to him: he was asked to serve as a magistrate in his own state of Lu. He was so efficient that he was soon promoted to Minister of Justice. No longer was the main business of the government the collection of taxes. New ideas were fused into the ancient customs, for the new minister had a gift for establishing law and order. He taught each officer in the state, strict adherence to duty, but the reign of justice lasted only four years, because rulers grew envious of the progress in the state of Lu and planned to cause its downfall by causing a rift between the ruling duke and his Minister of Justice. 

Their wicked schemes were cleverly carried out. 

Eighty beautiful girls were selected by the crafty lords who knew the weaknesses of the duke. These dancers, who had been well trained in music and the other arts of their profession, were joyfully received by the ruler, who was getting tired of the severe regulations of his exacting official. The entertainment provided by the jealous enemies of the unsuspecting ruler proved successful, because the alluring girls played their parts so well that the disgusted Confucius was unable to secure an audience with the bewitched duke for three days; and the important ceremony of the 

Sacrifice to Heaven was hurriedly performed. The conscientious Minister felt that this negligence was inexcusable, so he resigned and wandered thirteen years looking for a "virtuous" man who would cooperate with his plans of efficient administration of state affairs. 

He went from town to town often facing dangers,

but always hoping. Finally he was called home, not to govern but to put his ideas into classical form, so the last period of his life was devoted to the completion of his writings, which he had started long before.

The Doctrine of Interest 

Of course we are happier, for the time being, if we are doing something in which we are interested. If we are masters of our own minds and are capable of keeping our thoughts in constructive channels our desires may lead to worthwhile accomplishments.

Therefore, the advocates of the "Project System" who allowed the flitting desires of children to

motivate the class room recitations were building character on a very fragile foundation. "The Doctrine of Interest," the basis of the Project System, is an over-worked theory that should not have been accepted without reservations. There are times when a child should be allowed to do what he wants to do, as long as his desires are not anti-social, for too much repression and domination by the grownup members of society does stifle the creative ability of alertminded children. But when the idea of freedom is carried to an extreme, it develops, not an original thinker, but an egotistic dictator who becomes, not a master of destiny, but a slave to desire. Any teacher who is trying to encourage originality by constantly asking a child what he wants to do is sowing seeds of disillusionment, for this sort of training is a poor preparation for life. No wise business executive starts the day by asking those under his supervision what they want to do, nor does he stifle their initiative by too much interference.

An efficient teacher does not waste time trying to adjust her program to the shallow desires of her pupils, but creates interest in the tasks that she knows will develop the potentialities of her class. The instructor who has developed a sense of discriminating judgment uses the popular "trial and error" method sparingly, because it over-estimates the value of action and under-estimates the importance of thinking. 

Allowing a kindergarten child to "work out his destiny" by building what he wants to build out of a pile of blocks is a pretty theory that will not stand an acid test. The “old-fogy" teacher who restricted the young citizens under her direction by not allowing them to touch their set of blocks until a signal was given,

and then set them a definite task to accomplish, was teaching respect for law and order and starting the training of the will, a side of education that has been sadly neglected in the last twenty years, but is nevertheless, the basis of all rational living. 

Naturalism 

If we turn the calendar back twenty-five years we can trace changing attitudes that have led to chaos. 

John Dewey was then teaching philosophy at Columbia 

University. He stood for a simple naturalism that emphasized the potency of experience. The value of culture was minimized in spite of the fact that Man's capacity for culture is what puts him above the animal plane.

Dewey followed Spencer's idea for more science and less literature; less book-learning and more actual practice. He had no regard for a liberal education for he did not think it fitted into a democratic industrial life, since "scholastic culture makes for snobbishness:" therefore, democratic fellowship in occupation was taught by turning school rooms into miniature workshops. His aim was to develop a better generation, not by book learning, but by actual practice. 

Eternal truths that have stood the test of time were ignored by the active "progressives" who worshipped the god of labor in the noisy halls of experience. Children and teachers had little time to think; they were too busy carrying out the expensive experiments that consumed much time and money, but failed to solve the problems of economic and social adjustment. 

The naturalists rejected metaphysics because they considered it a disguise for theology. Things were not

to be explained by supernatural causation, but by their place and function in environment. Dewey even criticized philosophy because its problems were confused with religion, whose purpose, according to Dewey's limited view-point, was to solve the perplexing problems of the after life and not the every-day problems of daily adjustment. He went so far as to say, "We must be true to the earth."

The kings of the earth concentrated on the illumination of human purposes and the coordination and the elevation of the human mind, but their concentration was not effective because of their narrow vision. Man learns the lessons of life through experience, books and the Cosmic mind which uses environment for a training school. Each experience is an opportunity for a victory over the meaner or weaker sides of our natures.

Pragmatism 

The contemporary of John Dewey, William James, who taught philosophy at Harvard University did not deny the power of the Cosmos, but considered it a sort of battleground of conflicting purposes. There are no conflicts in the Cosmic mind. The earth is the battle-

ground because our selfish desires do not harmonize with the divine design of our lives. There are very few Christians who earnestly and sincerely say, "Thy kingdom come; thy will be done." Mountains of ignorance would be removed and crime and immorality would rapidly decrease. But the pragmatist has little faith in this theory. 

The Harvard professor of practical philosophy had a particular aversion for the determinism idea of religion. It made him feel inferior because he argued that if God is omnipotent, we must be mere puppets moved by the divine will. God's will for us is health, happiness and harmony; and he has been patiently trying for countless centuries to teach us how to live abundantly, but we are too stubborn and too self-centered to listen. We have ears that hear not, and eyes that see not. The farther we get away from the core of wisdom the more hopelessly confused we become.

William James was earnest in his quest for truth. 

He had a passion for illuminating analysis, but his thinking was always toward things. He interpreted truth as the "cash value" of an idea. He was not in sympathy with abstract truths, or the assumptions of tradition. Even though he was the son of a Swedenborgian Mystic, progress to him meant movement and the acquisition of material blessings, rather than meditation and the illumination of spiritual values. 

In spite of all of his sincere efforts to understand the mysteries of life, he realized before he died that he had not succeeded in solving any vital problems.

The pragmatists, in their search for facts disclosed along the path of experience, became lost in the materialistic maze which they created. 

After Dr. James died, they found on his desk a piece of paper on which he had written his last and perhaps most characteristic sentences. "There is no conclusion. What has been concluded that we might conclude in regard to it? There are no fortunes to be told and there is no advice to be given. Farewell.”

There is something tragically pathetic about the farewell of this sincere student who became so lost in modernistic angles that he passed on into the next existence with a feeling of insecurity, because he felt that there is nothing permanent in the ever-changing civilization of restless human beings who are unconscious of the divine Pilot, who guides us whenever we call upon Him.

In fact John Watson, the first to use the term "behaviorism," studied man as a member of the animal kingdom and reached some of his conclusions after experimenting on rats. nored the higher and finer sensibilities of man, declared, "No foolishness, no bookishness, no consciousness, no imagination, no mind; there was only stimuli and response. 99 The behavioristic era was marked by a mania for standardized tests and measurements. Presuming supervisors tried to mold teachers and children into standardized puppets. Teachers were patted on the back if they gregariously followed the trumpeting of the dizzy leaders, and patiently burned the mid-night oil, grading hundreds of test papers that were used, so says a prominent Doctor of Philosophy, to give ambitious educators something to brag about. Many laborious hours were spent trying to get children up to the standard of addition that was set by the Courtis Tests in arithmetic; parents, teachers and children were worn ragged trying to train children to do what an adding machine can accomplish in a few seconds. 

Too much class-rating makes children self-conscious, and often gives them a distorted idea of their efficiency or inefficiency. The pupil who appears at an advantage in a speed test may rate much lower in character development than the slower type who becomes confused when hurried.

Time tests, if skillfully handled by a teacher who gradually raises class standards as her pupils increase in power, can be used effectively as a device to stimulate concentration. But great care must be used in order that nervous high-strung children, who do not work well under pressure, are not over-taxed, or that children who work slowly do not become discouraged. 

A "victory experience" now and then must offset a series of failures, if we protect boys and girls from inferiority complexes. "Comparisons are odious" is an

old maxim, but the mechanistic behaviorists are more interested in mechanistic efficiency than in professional courtesy.

The unrelentless slave-drivers falsified the whole scale of educational values by sacrificing accuracy for speed, culture for experience, and analytical thinking for exaggerated activity. It is true that environment plays an important role in our development. "Nature sets the conditions under which all of us must guide our crafts," but we are blessed with free wills which determine which route we are going to take. No one in the world is exactly like another, for the Creator fashioned all of us after different models. "The Mental Efficiency Manual," published in 1934 by Funk & Wagnalls Company warns us to avoid "chronic and contagious folly, fashion which changes our habits, our thoughts, our body and our life."

In recent years public opinion has frowned upon anyone who had sufficient courage to dare to disagree with the easily-shifted standards of the group; breaking away from unsatisfying social activities was considered by some psychologists as a sign of a disintegrated personality. But now authorities on social adjustment declare that we must not allow ourselves to be dragged into tastes which are not our own. We must learn to judge for ourselves and not be mere repeaters of the judgment of others. Personality is a noble quality, for to exist at all requires mighty determination. The freedom of our wills makes it possible to set our own standards of living, but "the indelible mark of nobility" will be placed only on those who are journeying toward the goal of righteousness, and can discern some beauty in every experience.

Agnosticism 

George Santayana, the Spanish-American philosopher and writer, who was a professor at Harvard University from 1889-1912, felt the call of beauty and the lure of truth. When teaching, he did not gain popularity by using the racy vernacular adopted by his contemporary, William James, but was respected and admired because of his aristocratic reserve. He was immune to the hustle and bustle of his age and viewed the petty systems promoted by immature intellectuals with a superior eye.

He preferred Plato and Aristotle to James, and accepted without criticism traditional assumptions that have been tested by many generations. He also accepted with unruffled sanity the blunt materialism that exalted the experience of the moment. He had faith in the intellect, but knew the "fallibility of science and the precariousness of reason." The poetic philosopher approved of the golden mean of Aristotle, who believed that "the peculiar excellence of man is in his power of thought; it is by this that he surpasses all other forms of life; and as the growth of this faculty has given him supremacy, so we may presume, its development will give him fulfillment and happiness.' 

Santayana told his pupils, "Everything ideal has a natural basis, and everything natural has an ideal basis," but he asserted that we add nothing to nature by calling it God. This idealistic skeptic did not believe in immortality. He went so far as to say, “Faith in the supernatural is a desperate wager made by man at the lowest ebb of his fortunes; for it is as far as possible from being the source of normal vitality, which, subsequently, if his fortune mends, he can grad-

ually recover." He, like Aristotle, did not believe in an external providence that designs earthly structures and events. He attributed divinity only to ourselves. 

He defines the soul as, "a fine quick organization within a natural body-growing each generation out of a seed." A wit once said that Santayana believed that there was no God and that Mary was his mother. He liked the beauty of catholicism more than the truth of any other faith.

The Spanish philosopher believed that Divine Providence is the operation of natural causes, and that universal mechanism prevails even in the remote recesses of the soul. He agreed with the behaviorists that life is mechanical and material, but he placed man on a much higher plane than that portrayed by Freud or Watson.

In spite of Santayana's skepticism he leaves us a unique and beautiful idea of immortality, for the loftyminded agnostic maintained that "he who lives in the ideal and leaves it expressed in society or in art enjoys a double immortality. The eternal has absorbed him while he lived and when he is dead his influence brings others to the same absorption.' 

Like Buddha, the first psychologist, Santayana did not recognize the power of the deity, but acknowledged the mechanical power of natural laws.

IX How GOOD IS HUMAN NATURE? 

"The wrong that needs resistance and the right that needs assistance are never aided by the coward or the deserter or the hopelessly unhappy. Happiness means strength." SANGSTER.

What this world needs to harmonize its conflicts is a dynamic expression of God. Everyone must work out his own individual philosophy. Some routes have been mapped out by great thinkers, but many of the good philosophies that have been worked by the race have been torn down, and doctrines substituted that were not based on the knowledge of past experiences. 

A wise pilot, before he charts his course, studies the laws of navigation. Only a fool-hardy seaman would throw away his compass before starting out on voyage. 

A man or woman who leaves religion out of his life is bound to be a drifter.

The introspection of the rationalists is a poor substitute for introversion, "looking within to have communion with God." The rule of the mystic is, "Be still and know." Studying philosophy and theology draw God nearer, but we must make our own philosophy elastic enough for new revelations; someone said that we must be willing to accept new truths. There are no new truths; for truth is eternal and is not affected by time or weather. We must all beware of closed minds, but in working out our formulas for living an abundant life we must be sure to recognize the gain that has been made in the past, and not

allow ourselves to be lead astray by some new theory that has not stood the test of time. Religion is the anchor that keeps us from drifting upon the shoals. 

Blessed is the Christian who knows how to find shelter in times of storm and stress.

The Love Mystics sought refuge in love, but when we make love of man the basis of religion we introduce grave perils. There is a tendency to over-estimate the power of human love which is so often adulterated by selfish desires. Sometimes it takes the form of hero worship that seeks reflected glory. To worship man is to worship an imperfect idol that may at anytime fall off of its pedestal. But, fortunately, the human soul is not destroyed by a fall, for often the lower it sinks the higher it can rise. Oscar Wilde's "De Profundis" portrays the beauty of a soul that was not destroyed by descending to the depths.

History proves that the destructive desires of men must be reformed into forces that will lift them above their petty clamouring for more power. We can not expect peace or happiness, until we broaden our perspective so that we can see beyond our own shadow. 

Seneca, the Roman philosopher, who because of his great wealth fell into disfavor with Nero, and was ordered to commit suicide, taught the creed, "No man can live happily who regards himself alone, who turns everything to his own advantage. Thou must live for another, if thou wishest to live for thyself."

Some optimistic schools of religion deny the existence of pernicious influences by refusing to recognize them, while certain cults go to the other extreme and cast a dark cloud of suspicion over the whole human race. The pessimist considers man just a vile creature who wears a mask of righteousness to hide his cor-

rupted nature and the optimists dwell on the thought that man is a perfect expression of God. 

Tolstoy believed that the cruel officials who had become invulnerable and impenetrable to feelings of compassion were by nature kind and gentle, but "magnified their office and its requirements to the disadvantage of their duties to mankind. If a man were to admit that there could be anything of more importance than the love one owes to his fellow-man he might commit any conceivable crime that a man could commit and yet call himself innocent." When men's finer sensibilities become paralyzed by their desire for power they defy the laws of God and accept laws that are not laws. 

Sometimes they grow to hate their co-workers who have respect for their own ideals and listen to the dictates of their own reason and obey, regardless of public opinion, which sways like a pendulum from one side to another. 

Courageous individuals who will not bow down to gods with feet of clay are often victimized by the "mood of some gendarme, public prosecutor, magistrate, governor, or minister. Some official is feeling restless or wishes to show his authority or zeal. The problem in Russia was to corrupt and debauch as many persons as possible in the shortest and safest way. The commonplace, everyday men of Russian peasant life abandoned the principles of Christian morality, and adopted the prison standards that justified every form of violence and outrage that seemed of advantage to themselves."

But Walter Pitkin tells us that we are free to erect on the old sites whatever we chose. He thinks that we are luckier than our descendants who will find the splendors in which they are born commonplace. But

we, "who have been choked by the stench of diseased millions will taste the full tingling bouquet of wine which is about to be tapped for the first time." 

We are to be delivered from incompetence by the awakening of our consciousness. This attitude would tend to prove that the clever journalist has faith in the innate goodness of man. But he is so mentally agile that he can quickly jump from optimism to pessimism.

He acknowledges, "Europeans shake their heads over our tragic, comic, pseudo-culture." People are so busy trying to make an impression on each other that they have not time for inner growth and "the turtle remains all shell." A large per cent. of our population is so intent on doing what the Joneses do that they obliterate every trace of their own personalities.

The versatile Pitkin, who has as many eyes as a spider, usually has some sort of remedy for the particular evil which he happens to be discussing. He tells us that in order to live abundantly after forty we must learn to "tackle fundamentals." We must learn to think, to read, to talk, to write and to observe, for "He who has subdued the fundamentals travels a smooth road." His solution for adjustment after forty is apt to leave his readers with a sense of incompleteness; for the average man and woman have a very vague idea about what constitutes the fundamentals of life. The person who is able to outline the basic principles of living to his own satisfaction has filled a large order.

But every modern traveler who is searching for reality is lighted on his way by the same rising sun of truth in which Zoroaster, the Persian, had such sublime faith. The firm conviction of the oriental mystic, that good always triumphs over evil, has woven itself

into the fabric of thought and inspires those who earnestly pray, “Thy kingdom come: thy will be done on earth as it is in heaven." 

The Honorable Ramsay McDonald assures us, "All happy results, national or international, issue from good sense." He feels that the mental ills of the nations could be healed by international understanding, for isolation in a political world is heavy with danger, since it breeds fear and suspicion because of a lack of mutuality. People who do not understand each other are influenced by rumors, "lies that float round the world to poison opinion." The former Prime 

Minister calls to all peace loving nations to do "justice by their people by seeing that they have a decent standard of living, decent homes, proper food for their bodies and proper food for their souls." For each of us must deserve to be trusted and in turn we must trust one another.

There are certain types of individuals who would scoff at the simple remedies suggested by England's counselor, for they have no faith in the candor and sincerity that appeals to a Scotch mind. Schopenhauer argued that "love is a deception practiced upon the individual by the race." But Pierre Laplace, the French astronomer and mathematician, is reported to have said on his deathbed that science was mere trifling and nothing was real but love.

The optimists over-estimate the goodness of human nature, and the pessimists see evil lurking in every corner. The average man or woman is neither a noble saint nor a wicked sinner, but the direction in which he is going is determined, to a large extent, by the strength of his desires, the quality of his knowledge, and the power of his will.

Marcus Aurelius told his contemporaries, "Thou canst pass thy life in an equable flow of happiness, if thou canst go by the right way. These two things are common both to the soul of God and to the soul of every rational being, not to be hindered by another; and to hold good to consist in the disposition of justice and the practice of it, and in this to let thy desire find its termination."

People who have not developed a sound sense of values are easily lured from the path of righteousness in their quest for things that often prove "rotten and trifling." Those fighting for prestige and wealth, so says Aurelius, are like "little dogs biting one another, or little children, quarreling, laughing or weeping who are unconscious of the power of fidelity, modesty, justice or truth."

The only way that society can be harmonized is for men to act "according to the nature of rational and social animals." What appears good to the selfish person is of little value when compared with what is really good, such as faith, integrity, and wisdom. If we are masters of our own souls, we are not discouraged by the ridicule of cynics.

The strong man stands "like the promontory against the waves that continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it," while the weak man becomes a slave to his inferior desire; and not until he is set free will he "be content to forego the luxuries of life and the conspicuous consumption of goods, and become a worthy Guardian of public welfare."

Plato was astounded at the folly of leaving the selection of public officials to "mob caprice, or to the wealth-loving strategists who pull the wires behind

1 the democratic stage." The famous Greek teacher bemoaned the fact that when we are ill we call a trained physician, only a specially trained person will serve our purpose, but we take it for granted that everyone who knows how to get votes knows how to administer city or state. Plato wanted to devise some method of barring incompetence and knavery from public office. He wanted to free society from the strategy of party and the spoils of office. He was an enemy to the evils of excess as expressed in the different forms of goverment, that perish because they carry to extreme their basic principles.

Aristocracy defeats its purpose by limiting its power to too narrow a circle. Oligarchy ruins itself by its scramble for wealth, while democracy in its fight for equal freedom and power overcomes its opponents by slaughter and banishment. Democracy has steadily grown in popularity; but it is not by any means an ideal form of government, because the people are not sufficiently well informed to select the best leaders or the wisest courses. The Greek sage had no confidence in the "unscrupulous flatterer" who calls himself the protector of the people and caters to mob rule, "for mob rule is a rough sea for the ship of state to ride; every wind of oratory stirs up the waters and deflects its course. Therefore, the problem that confronts us today is how can a higher type of leadership be developed? 99 

Social uplift is hampered because, as Herbert Spencer says, "We are harassed by a multitude of prejudices-personal, educational, theological, economic, political, national, religious and by the hasty omniscience of the uninformed." While Spencer is inclined to rush into generalizations, T. Swann Hardin, a sci-

entific skeptic, looks at the "riddle of knowledge" from a different angle. He declares that those "who work in material science are exceptionally predisposed to indifference towards phenomena which we are indoctrinated to think cannot exist. But suppose that instead of devoting ourselves to instruments of precise measurement and to experimental procedure, we had for generations specialized in silent meditation, breathing exercises and prolonged fasts, who knows what we might be now, just what powers we might have, what quality of perception we should have acquired, what stimuli we might sense which we now ignore? No one knows."

This reaction to stimuli is of vital importance to the development of the human race. The difference in the methods used by the various schools of mental healing is determined by the stimuli that they use to arouse the sensibilities of those who have been stunned by the blows of environment, or whose desires have been walled in by the doctrines of the realists. 

The spiritual development of a person can always be determined by what he enjoys. We can not accurately judge men and women by the books they read because the majority of people pretend to enjoy what is popular. But if we could actually determine the amount of real enjoyment received, then we would have an insight into the inner nature of the reader. 

There are certain minds that revel in mental companionship with that which is filthy or abnormal, and some go to the other extreme and live in an unreal imaginative world in which they try to eliminate from their lives all that is unpleasant. Either mental attitude is unwholesome and is to be avoided. Some people have the happy faculty of being able to study

life in all of its phases, but can filter the evil from their own lives, so that when you talk to them your ideas are not soiled or distorted by prejudice or perverted interpretations. But there are other types that eagerly absorb the sordid and become so permeated that they distort or discolor whatever comes within their reach. 

The attitude of men to the Deity largely determines their interpretation of the innate nature of man. Popular propagandists who try to restore health by "probing for suppressed shames" evidently haven't much faith in the inherent goodness of their fellowmen. 

Their complex methods stand out in decided contrast to the simple procedure of Christ, the greatest healer the world has ever known. He required no elaborate program of "free association," or of confession, but immediately forgave the mistakes of the past, and quietly, but firmly, counseled his followers to sin no more.

For many centuries men have been trying to decide whether or not we are free agents controlled by our own wills or creatures directed by external influences. 

The animal psychologists think that we are conditioned by environment; they are right to a certain extent, but they don't go far enough, because they have an incomplete interpretation of the law of cause and effect. They are as far off of the course as some theorists who have been teaching eager disciples that the fulfillment of our desires depends entirely upon our concentration upon our individual wants.

Charles Milton Newcomb, who was introduced by President Theibault of the Executives' Club in Chicago in 1921, as a psychologist who speaks in a language that you can understand, talked to these prac-

tical business men on a constructive plan of overcoming the effects of our economic collapse. 

This popular scientist and humorist, who was enthusiastically welcomed for the sixth time by these keenminded men seeking enlightenment, believes in evolution but uses a bit of satire when referring to the Darwinian theory. "Of course, as the scientists tell us, life is a matter of evolution. Our remote ancestors used to wear tails, then they invented the revolving door, and of course they did away with that custom." 

Mr. Newcomb attributes the cause of the depression to what the psychiatrists call "delusions of grandeur," or in simple diction, our economic structure toppled because men and women lost their sense of proportion. 

He amused the members of his audience by telling them that after the market nose-dived, he saw a sign on 

Sixth Avenue which read, "I. Rosenbloom, Suits

Pressed-Fifty Cents. Formerly with Hornblower and Weeks."

The humorous psychologist does not consider our present economic disturbance just another one of the ever-recurring cycles. He declares that we have not been challenged by another depression, but by a business revolution that was brought on by wrong thinking, but I believe his statement would be more accurate if he had said brought on by wrong desires. 

Thought, according to Mr. Newcomb, is the human instrument that carves our place in an ever-changing environment. But it is a tool that is avoided by the majority of men and women who want generous portions of life without any mental fatigue. "In order to avoid thinking as much as possible, we have standardized not only objects, but ideas. Not satisfied with standardizing tooth-paste and flivvers,

and underwear, which is a good thing to do, we have also begun to standardize ideas in newspapers, magazines, radio and motion pictures. 'Why,' says the man on the street, 'should I bother myself with thinking, when by walking in lock-step with the mob, wearing the crowd uniform and repeating the old catch-words and shibboleths, I can avoid the necessity of this tiresome exercise?' Not that this is anything new in the world's history. It is the old story of the one-talent man who wrapped his gift in a napkin instead of putting it to use, and thereby merited the 

Lord's condemnation."

Robert E. Lee is an example of a brave man who was a noble loser. Charles William Eliot is another type of an American gentleman. Dr. Eliot began his life of educational service when he was made President of Harvard at the age of thirty-five. While he encouraged enthusiasm for Harvard standards, he preserved the conservative principles of the quiet American gentleman, "quiet for the best of reasons - namely, that effectiveness requires steady, close attention and that attention implies stillness and a mind intent." He stood for practical purpose and cultural progress. 

While he piously believed that education is the means of self-advancement, he thought it wrong for high schools, supported by public funds, to devote the major part of their time in preparing their pupils for college, because such a large per cent. of students are not able to participate in the advantages of a higher education.
