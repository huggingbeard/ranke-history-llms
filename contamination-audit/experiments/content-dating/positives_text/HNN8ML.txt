APART from a dry crackle, as of kindlings in a fire, there was no other suggestion of warmth in the voice of Mr. Justice Lampeter. His summing up, which had endured over two hours, was a pattern of emotionless, legal dissection. A handful of sand might have been at stake rather than a human life. 

Following the sledge-hammer blows and passionate rhetoric of counsel for the prosecution and defence, the cold analysis of the Judge divested the case of drama, and presented it in the light of a dreary puzzle, the solution of which was outside his personal province. 'Members of the jury,' said he, 'I have only a few more words to add and then I am done. 

Neither you nor I have any responsibility for the consequences falling upon the prisoner at the bar of a crime of this nature. That exquisite responsibility is the State's and the State's alone. I will ask you now to retire and consider your verdict.'

The jury, which was absent for four hours, returned a verdict of murder.

The clerk of the court, naming the prisoner in full, asked: 'Have you anything to say why the court should not pronounce judgment according to the law?'

The prisoner had nothing to say and revealed the same stoic and emotionless front preserved throughout the trial. 

And then the usher: 'Oyez! Oyez! All persons are strictly charged to remain silent while sentence of death is passed upon the prisoner at the bar.'

The doors of the court were locked. The judge's clerk advanced and put the black cap on the judge's head.

The chaplain moved forward and stood beside him.

Sentence of death was pronounced. place of execution and hanged by the neck until you are dead. And the Lord have mercy on your soul.' 'Amen,' said the chaplain.

There is something about first novels which inspire a form of hysteria alike with public and publishers. 

Adrian Chiddiatt's first novel was neither better nor worse than many others written by precious youths. Setting out with the intention of being rebellious and revolutionary, it followed welltrodden if muddy paths through a morass of sex stuff and abuses of all healthy traditions. Its success was prodigious. Fifty thousand copies were sold in three weeks before the Home Office took

action and abolished it. After that it was reprinted in Paris and appeared between vellum covers at one hundred francs a copy. 

Adrian Chiddiatt rose to the rank of martyr in the opinion of a large number of muddy-minded moderns, and was regarded with much distaste by persons of healthier appetite. The headmaster of the public school to which he had belonged wrote a letter to The Times disowning him, and for a full season, in which he marched hatless about Bond Street and the West End in company with a huge Borzoi and a tasselled cane, he enjoyed an unenviable fame.

His passion for publicity and notoriety was unassuageable, and led him into follies. 

Not the least of these was a characteristic portrait in one of his later novels of no less a person than Lord Studholme.

Newspaper proprietors taken in the aggregate may be, and doubtless are, persons of high moral integrity and fairness of mind. This fact notwithstanding, it is clearly inadvisable for any young man practising the art of letters to direct against them, individually or collectively, that class of criticism or obloquy which undermines the lofty opinion in which they are held by countless subscribers to their publications. 

The effects of so doing are likely to be immediate, painful, and permanent. It is manifestly foolish

to open fire with a toy pistol against a brigade of artillery.

The Studholme Press, a by no means inconsiderable engine of destruction, cranked up and descended upon him as a juggernaut crushingly. 

Adrian Chiddiatt, skilled in using any fuel to generate limelight to shed upon himself, in public, declared these abuses hugely amusing, but found them expensive in private. 

His spirits may have risen, but his sales went down. Sometimes he laughed loud and long and said that fame could only flourish in face of opposition but at other times he wept openly being a temperamental youth in no way ashamed to display his emotions. 

His marriage to Anna was such a complete astonishment to his acquaintances that it revived quite a lot of interest in him. In that sense, if in no other, it must be regarded as a success. 

Actually he married Anna out of spite and because he wanted someone to curse and grumble at at home.

The gurgling laugh was the only answer Studholme could make, and Holland believed he was being laughed at. He didn't understand that a man was dying before his eyes. It was help that

The roar of the explosion was muffled by reason of the fact that the muzzle struck Studholme's temple as the charge detonated. The body went limp and collapsed into the chair. A hand struck the table-top with a thud. A blaze of light from the burning letters gleamed on a single strand of harp-string hair standing erect from the scalp. 

Holland saw it sag and fall limply into a black, spreading pool. The outstretched hand moved once and was still.

'Studholme,' Holland whispered. 'Studholme.' 

But he knew that there would be no answer.

All through his life Holland had been known as a man of quick decision and action. There was not a hope in a thousand that the truth would be believed, and in less than fifteen seconds his mind was clear as to what he meant to do. Recovering the pistol, from where it lay beside the chair, he wiped every part of it with a silk handkerchief and slipped it beneath the fingers of the outstretched hand.

Moving swiftly to the fire he covered the burnt ashes of the letters with coals, then returned to the table and stood for a moment surveying and analysing the picture before him. 'Best I can do,' he said, moved to the smaller door, unlatched it silently, and listened. From some distance away came the crack of Chiddiatt's pistol and a murmur of voices.

Holland slipped into the hall and closed the door. Somebody brushed past him in the dark.

The door of the drawing-room was open, and he felt his way across to the window. Everywhere people were shouting warnings and needs-everywhere save in the library, where Lord Studholme lay dead at his table among the dancing shadows. 

The gong crashed and the lights came on. There was a knock at the library door, but no one answered. Dawson entered sedately. The sight that

Chiddiatt, you couldn't deny that it was a bit of fun. Dawson covered his mouth discreetly to muffle a laugh and went out, closing the door.

In the hall he met one of the footmen. 'Good as a play, in there,' he said. 12 George V, by the grace of God, King of England and Defender of the Faith, was graciously pleased to grant to Colonel Sir John Holland a free pardon for the crime of which he had been convicted. 

It was the least, but also the most, that he could do, for the law of England is precise and has no machinery to quash a death sentence, but can only pardon the offender.

In consequence, although another man was detained at Broadmoor during the King's pleasure,

John Holland bore with him into freedom the responsibility for having shot Lord Studholme. But life was none the less agreeable on that account. 

His popularity did not diminish, and he was not required to resign from his clubs.

The newspapers were lyrical in his praises. They

did not publish the facts of the case because they never knew them, but some shrewd journalistic guesses were made of a kind calculated to inspire wild scenes of enthusiasm among the hero-worshipping classes. Also the Princess gave a dinnerparty in his honour and was scolded for it afterwards.

One of the most regrettable results of his release was the appearance at Scotland Yard of 

Chief Inspector Ramage, not only in drink, but in song. Even more regrettable was the action of his superiors, who should have taken severe disciplinary measures. Neglecting this obvious duty, they united their voices with the inspector's. A mighty chorus arose and filled the building, so that passers-by were drawn to believe that either its business methods had undergone a change or that the Royal College of Music had taken possession of the premises.

Holland's resignation from the office of Chief 

Commissioner came as a matter of course. He argued that it was impossible for him to serve an institution whose officers he had been guilty of wilfully deceiving. But he left behind many sorrowing hearts and carried away with him into private life a gold épergne, of the most embarrassing splendour, to which no less than four hundred members of the police force had subscribed.

The one virtue of misery is its impermanence.

Joy may endure, but the tenure of woe is short.
