HISTORY OF THE UNITED STATES COURT FOR THE EASTERN DISTRICT OF KENTUCKY THE 

HE first session of the First Congress of the United 

States, after the adoption of the Constitution, was held in the city of New York, beginning March 4, 1789, and continuing to September 29, 1789.

On the 24th of September, 1789, an act was passed, known as the first Judiciary Act, to establish the courts of the United States. In this act it was provided that there shall be a court, to be called the Virginia District, to consist of the State of Virginia, except that part thereof called Kentucky, and a court was established for the Kentucky portion of Virginia, to be called the Kentucky District. Thirteen districts in all were created, one of them being the Kentucky District. Kentucky, at that time, was not a Territory, but an integral part of Virginia. Nevertheless, at the time the new Federal government came into operation, in March, 1789, Kentucky was seeking a separation from Ilishment as one of the States of the ginia and estab- 

Union, although it did not become an independent State and a distinct member of the Union until June 1, 1792. In 1789, however, the population and general progress of Kentucky, together with its geographical position and area, were such that Congress deemed it proper to provide it with a separate United States Court, called the District Court of Kentucky, and this it did in spite of the fact that Kentucky was yet a part of Virginia. The court, so created, was to be held at Harrodsburg.

4 

Two days after the passage of the Judiciary Act, President George Washington, on September 26, 1789, appointed the Honorable Harry Innes, a Virginian then resident in Kentucky, Judge of the District Court of 

Kentucky. Judge Innes was born January 4, 1752, in 

Caroline County, Virginia. He was a son of Rev. Robert

Innes, a cultured clergyman of Scottish birth, and his wife, Catherine Richards Innes. After admission to the bar, Harry Innes moved to Bedford County, and, in 1776 and 1777 (probably in association with his fatherin-law, Colonel Callaway), he was employed by the Virginia Committee of Safety to superintend the working of the Chiswell lead mines on New River, in what was then Fincastle (now Wythe) County. By an act passed by the General Assembly of Virginia, at its May session, in the year 1782, a court of general jurisdiction, known as the Supreme Court for the District of Kentucky, consisting of one presiding judge and two assistant judges, was established. Harry Innes was chosen as one of the assistant judges, but, on September 9, 1782, he declined the appointment. Colonel John Todd, who had been named presiding judge of the court, having lost his life in the Battle of the Blue Licks, on August 19, 1782, Harry Innes was nominated to succeed him and, in October, 1782, this nomination was confirmed by election of the two houses of the Virginia Assembly. 

Shortly thereafter, Judge Innes came to Kentucky and, in conjunction with Samuel McDowell and Caleb Wallace, the two assistant judges, opened court at Crow's station, near Danville, on November 3, 1783, the first term of the Supreme Court having been previously held by John Floyd and Samuel McDowell at Harrodsburg, on March 3, 1783. Colonel Floyd having been killed by 

Indians about the middle of April, 1783, his place was taken by Caleb Wallace, who was elected to the office of assistant judge in June, 1783.

Walker Daniel, Esq., "late of Halifax County" and subsequently founder of the city of Danville, who had been chosen as Attorney for the Commonwealth in the 

Kentucky District in June or July, 1782, entered upon his duties promptly, but he, too, fell before savage foemen on July 12, 1784. Not having found the judicial office either sufficiently remunerative or to his liking, Harry Innes thereupon resigned as Chief Judge of the 

District Supreme Court and, on November 17, 1784, he was elected "Attorney General" for the Kentucky District in succession to the late Walker Daniel, and this office he continued to exercise until his appointment as the first Judge of the United States District Court of Kentucky, in September, 1789. His successor was Colonel George Nicholas, commissioned by Governor Beverley Randolph of Virginia, who qualified as Attorney 

General by taking the prescribed oaths before the Supreme Court for the Kentucky District on June 7, 1790.

James Innes, a brother of Harry Innes, had previously been appointed Attorney General of Virginia, succeeding Edmund Randolph in that office on November 23, 1786. Upon the erection of Kentucky into an independent state, in June, 1792, Harry Innes was offered, but declined, the office of Chief Justice of the Court of Appeals of Kentucky, which was tendered him by Governor Shelby.

The energy, ability, and fidelity which Judge Innes had displayed both as a lawyer and judge made it fitting that, at the early age of thirty-seven, he should have been selected by Washington for the office of United States District Judge. He held the position continuously for twenty-seven years, until his death on September 20, 1816. Judge Innes was twice married, his first wife being Elizabeth Callaway, a daughter of Colonel James Callaway, of Bedford County, Va. She died in 1791, leaving four daughters surviving her. He subsequently married Mrs. Ann Shields, whose only daugh-

ter, Maria Knox Innes, became the second wife of John

J. Crittenden. 

Judge Innes was succeeded as judge by the Honorable Robert Trimble, who was appointed by President 

Madison, January 31, 1817. Judge Trimble was born in 1777, in Berkeley County, Virginia (now West Virginia). In 1780, with his parents, he removed to Kentucky. He was largely self-educated, taught school for several years, studied law under George Nicholas and 

James Brown, and was licensed to practice in 1803.

Having previously settled in Paris, Kentucky, about 1800, he there carried on his professional work, and, in 1802, was elected a representative from Bourbon County to the State Legislature. He was married, on August 18, 1803, to Miss Nancy P. Timberlake, of Bourbon County.

In 1808 he was appointed a judge of the Kentucky Court of Appeals, and served in that office for five years. In 

1813 he was appointed United States District Attorney for Kentucky, and, in 1817, became judge of the district court, as above stated. On April 11, 1826, Judge Trimble was appointed to the bench of the Supreme Court of the 

United States by President John Quincy Adams. While holding that office, he died at his residence, near Paris, Kentucky, at the early age of fifty-two, on August 25, 1828. Trimble County was named in his honor. 

On October 20, 1826, the Honorable John Boyle was appointed by President Adams to succeed Judge Trimble as United States District Judge for Kentucky. He took office November 8, 1826, and was confirmed February 

12, 1827. Judge Boyle was born in Virginia, in what is now Russell County, October 28, 1774, and removed with his parents to Kentucky in 1779. The family presently settled in Garrard County. Judge Boyle's early education was limited but thorough, and, having studied law, he was admitted to the bar in 1797. In 1802, he was elected a Representative in Congress from Kentucky, and served three successive terms, declining re-election

in 1808. In 1809 he was appointed Governor of Illinois 

Territory by President Madison, but declined the appointment, and, on April 4, 1809, went on the bench of the Kentucky Court of Appeals, to the Chief Justiceship of which he was promoted on April 3, 1810. From the time of his appointment as District Judge, in 1826, he held the office until his death, on January 28, 1834.

Boyle County was named in his honor. Judge Boyle is justly esteemed to have been one of Kentucky's most eminent jurists. 

On the 8th of March, 1834, the Honorable Thomas

Bell Monroe, a near relative of President James Monroe, was appointed District Judge by President Andrew

Jackson. Judge Monroe, who was of Scotch and Scotch- 

Irish descent, was born in Albemarle County, Virginia, October 7, 1791, and came with his parents from Virginia to Scott County, Kentucky, in 1793. He had few educational opportunities but of such as were within his reach he took full advantage. He married a daughter of General John Adair, Governor of Kentucky from 

1820 to 1824, and, after marriage, he removed to Barren County and represented that county in the State Legislature in 1816. In 1819 he began the study of law and transferred his residence to Frankfort, attending the Law School of Transylvania University in 1821-'22 and graduating at the close of the term. He was Secretary of State, under Governor Adair, from September, 1823, to September, 1824. In 1825, he was appointed Reporter of the decisions of the Court of Appeals by Governor Desha, and reported through seven volumes, covering the years 1824 to 1830. In 1833-'34 he was District 

Attorney of the United States for Kentucky, and, in 

March, 1834, became the District Judge, as previously stated. During his incumbency of the bench he managed to devote much time to delivering law lectures to students in his own home, "Montrose," to classes in the 

University of Louisiana, at New Orleans, in the Tran-

sylvania Law School, at Lexington, and in the Western 

Military Institute near Frankfort. In September, 1861, upon learning that an order for his arrest had been issued by Federal military authorities upon the ground that he was a Southern sympathizer, he left his home, near Frankfort, and took refuge within the Confederate lines at Nashville, where, on October 6, 1861, at the age of seventy, he took a formal oath of allegiance to the government of the Confederate States. Several of his sons and several of his grandsons served as soldiers in the Confederate Army. He died at Pass Christian, Mississippi, December 24, 1865.

In his place, the Honorable Bland Ballard was appointed Judge of the United States District Court of 

Kentucky by President Lincoln, on October 20, 1861, and his nomination was confirmed January 22, 1862.

Judge Ballard, who came of a splendid pioneer ancestry, was born in Shelby County, Kentucky, on September 4, 1819. He was a lawyer of unusual ability and natural gifts, and became distinguished as Judge of the United States Court during the Civil War and through the years immediately succeeding, when a very great increase of business came to the court by reason of the questions growing out of the war, and especially the internal revenue law and the bankruptcy law. Judge

Ballard held the office of District Judge until his death, on July 29, 1879. Previous to his elevation to the bench,

Judge Ballard practiced law for a time in partnership with the Honorable Henry Pirtle, afterwards the distinguished Chancellor of the Louisville Chancery Court.

Judge Ballard was credited with a somewhat austere manner in the discharge of judicial duties; yet it has been said of him that he was "formidable to the older lawyers, but the idol of the younger ones." 

On September 6, 1879, the Honorable William Henry 

Hays was appointed Judge by President Hayes, and was confirmed December 10, 1879. Judge Hays held office

only a few months. He died March 7, 1880, in Louisville. Judge Hays was born August 26, 1820, in Washington County, Kentucky, and was a son of William 

Hays and his wife, Eleanor Birchim Hays. He was educated in the schools of Washington County and, in 1843, began the study of law at Elizabethtown with 

Honorable James W. Hays. Upon his admission to the bar, in 1845, he located in Springfield, and was highly esteemed as a lawyer and citizen. He was elected 

County Judge of Washington County in 1851, and reelected to the same office in 1854, serving until 1858. 

In 1861, he was elected a Representative from Washington County to the State Legislature, but resigned in December of that year. He served during the Civil 

War as a Colonel of the Tenth Kentucky Volunteer Infantry, U. S. A., which, as Lieutenant-Colonel, he joined in 1861 under Colonel John M. Harlan. He succeeded Colonel Harlan in command of the regiment, upon the resignation of the latter in February, 1863.

Harlan, who left the military service with the rank of Brigadier-General, later became an Associate Justice of the United States Supreme Court, to which he was appointed by President Hayes on November 29, 1877. In "The Union Cause in Kentucky," by Captain Thomas 

Speed, it is said (p. 311): "The 10th Infantry, having begun service in the fall of 1861, and fighting at Mill 

Springs and in all succeeding campaigns, was not mustered out finally until June, 1865." Colonel Hays, at the head of his regiment, is said to have led the final charge on Missionary Ridge (November 25, 1863) in the last of the series of battles which began on November 23, 1863. Judge Hays is buried in the Springfield Cemetery, and a portrait of him hangs in the court house at 

Springfield. He was never married.

By the Judiciary Act of 1789, Congress established in each district a Circuit Court, and, by that act, the District Court of Kentucky was given Circuit Court

jurisdiction. In the beginning, the Circuit Court was held by two Justices of the Supreme Court, aided by a district judge; but in the course of time the growth of business in the Supreme Court made it impracticable for the members of that tribunal to continue to sit in the Circuit Court. As a partial measure of relief, in 1807, the States were grouped into circuits, and a single Justice of the Supreme Court was assigned to each Circuit 

Court. On April 10, 1869, Congress enacted that for each circuit there should be appointed a Circuit Judge, with the same power and jurisdiction as the Justice of the Supreme Court allotted to the circuit. By the act of March 3, 1891, creating Circuit Courts of Appeals, an additional Circuit Judge was provided for each Circuit Court. The Justices assigned to the circuit including Kentucky have been as follows: Thomas Todd, 1808; 

Robert Trimble, 1826; John McLean, 1829; John Catron, 1837; Noah H. Swayne, 1862; Stanley Matthews, 1880; 

David J. Brewer, 1890; Henry B. Brown, 1893; Howell 

E. Jackson, 1894; and John M. Harlan, 1896.

The Circuit Courts were finally abolished by the present Judicial Code, enacted by Congress on March 3, 

1911, effective December 31, 1911, and their jurisdiction was transferred to and merged with that of the District Courts. The place of Circuit Courts in the Federal 

Judicial system was virtually taken by the United States Circuit Courts of Appeals, originally established in 1891, with Kentucky assigned to the Sixth Circuit. Within the jurisdiction of the Circuit Court of Appeals for the 

Sixth Circuit are comprised the States of Ohio, Michigan, Tennessee and Kentucky. It now consists of four judges.

Shortly after Judge Innes was appointed, in 1789, the original Federal Court for the entire State was organized and held at Harrodsburg, Kentucky, December 15, 1789. It continued there until September 16, 1794, when it was removed to Frankfort, by act of Congress of date, June 9, 1794 (1st Statutes at Large, page 397). 

On the 3d day of March, 1797, (1st Statutes at Large, page 518), Congress enacted that the court should be held on the second Monday in March, third Monday in 

June, and third Monday in November of each year.

September; and, also, at Paducah at such time as the 

District Judge should appoint. A clerk's office was to be kept at Covington and at Paducah.

To accommodate the practice in equity in district courts to the enlarged jurisdiction conferred upon them by the Judicial Code of 1911, Section 9 of that act provided: 

"Sec. 9. (U. S. C. Sec. 13.) The District Courts, as courts of admiralty and as courts of equity, shall be deemed always open for the purpose of filing any pleading, of issuing and returning mesne and final process, and of making and directing all interlocutory motions, orders, rules, and other proceedings preparatory to the hearing, upon their merits, of all causes pending therein. Any district judge may, upon reasonable notice to the parties, make, direct, and award, at chambers or in the clerk's office, and in vacation as well as in term, all such process, commissions, orders, rules, and other proceedings, whenever the same are not grantable of course, according to the rules and practice of the court.' 

With the omission only of the words, "as courts of admiralty", the foregoing section of the Judicial Code is reproduced as Rule 1 of the New Federal Equity Rules

of 1912. The old Equity Rule No. 1, which it supersedes, applied only to Circuit Courts, and was rendered ineffective by provisions in Sections 289 and 290 of the present Judicial Code. 

The liberality of the federal practice in district courts is further shown by New Equity Rule No. 2, which provides: "The clerk's office shall be open during business hours on all days, except Sundays and legal holidays, and the clerk shall be in attendance for the purpose of receiving and disposing of all motions, rules, orders and other proceedings which are grantable of course."

In actions at law, the court is not held rigidly to regular terms but, by Section 11, of the Judicial Code, it is broadly provided: 

"Sec. 11. (U. S. C. Sec. 15.) A special term of any District Court may be held at the same place where any regular term is held, or at such other place in the district as the nature of the business may require, and at such time and upon such notice as may be ordered by the district judge. Any business may be transacted at such special term which might be transacted at a regular term." 

And, in the same spirit of liberality, it is provided by Section 10 of the Judicial Code that: 

"Sec. 10. (U. S. C. Sec. 14.) District Courts shall hold monthly adjournments of their regular terms, for the trial of criminal causes, when their business requires it to be done, in order to prevent undue expenses and delays in such cases."

After pointing out that Section 9 of the Judicial Code relates to interlocutory proceedings, prior and preparatory to the hearing, the Supreme Court, in Abbott v. 

Brown, 241 U. S. 606, 611; s. c. 60 Law Ed. 1199, at p.

1202, in commenting upon Sections 9, 10 and 11, of the 

Judicial Code of 1911, used this language: "The statutory provisions referred to are designed to render the district courts readily accessible to applicants for justice in all branches of the jurisdiction; and while they require those courts to be always open only as courts of admiralty and as courts of equity, they permit 'special terms' to be held at any time for the transaction of any kind of business. 

"The celebrated remark of Lord Eldon: 'The court of chancery is always open' (Temple v. Bank of England, 6 Ves. Jr., 770, 771), evidenced the great adaptability of the practice of that court to the needs of litigants; and modern legislation has shown. a strong tendency to reform the practice of commonlaw courts by facilitating the transaction of their business in vacation. The sections we have quoted from the Judicial Code indicate a policy of avoiding the hardships consequent upon a closing of the court during vacations. The general rule in question was evidently designed to carry out this policy, and should receive a liberal interpretation consonant with its spirit: that is, as keeping the term alive, by adjournments from day to day, pending the temporary absence of the presiding judge, so that court might and should be actually opened upon such days as there was business of any character to be transacted."

The Honorable A. M. J. Cochran, of Maysville, was appointed by President McKinley Judge of the United

States Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky on 

April 24, 1901, and took the oath of office on the 1st day of July, 1901, at Covington, Kentucky. This was a recess appointment. The appointment was renewed by President Theodore Roosevelt on December 17, 1901, and same was promptly confirmed. Judge Cochran qualified un-

der this second appointment on January 13, 1902. From the date of his induction into office, on July 1, 1901, he served, first, as both District and Circuit Judge, until the close of the year 1911, and as District Judge continuously thereafter until his death on June 12, 1934. 

His period of service, covering almost exactly 33 years, was longer by several years than that of any other judge who has occupied the Federal bench in Kentucky. After 

Judge Cochran's death, the Honorable Charles I. Dawson, of the Western District, was named ad interim Judge of the Eastern District by designation, and, so far as the regular business of his own district would permit, acted as presiding judge of the Eastern District. Judge Robert 

R. Nevin, of Dayton, Ohio, one of the United States 

Judges for the Southern District of Ohio, also presided occasionally, for short periods, at Covington and Lexington. 

On March 19, 1935, the Honorable H. Church Ford, of Georgetown, was nominated by President Franklin D.

Roosevelt, as Judge of the United States Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky; the nomination was confirmed on March 27, 1935, a commission was issued to him on March 28, 1935, and he was inducted into office, at Lexington, on Saturday, March 30, 1935.

After the Marshal had formally opened court, Judge 

Moorman said: "The lamented death of the Honorable A. M. J. Cochran created a vacancy in the judgeship of this district, to which vacancy the President has appointed the Honorable H. Church Ford, and his official commission has been issued and received. The court has here met to signalize, by a brief ceremonial form, his act of qualifying for and entering his new judicial office. "The clerk will read the commission." 

S. W. Stacey, clerk, then read the commission and

Judge Moorman continued: 

"The act of qualifying is the taking of the judicial oath. Its simple and impressive words were prescribed by the first judiciary act, that of 1789; and for more than 140 years every justice of the supreme court, every circuit judge and every district judge, appointed to the federal judiciary, has, by these words, dedicated the remainder of his life to the public service. "For the substance of the oath we go back much further than 1789. The first judges known to our sacred literature were charged as to their duties by the first great law-giver. In the 16th and 17th verses of the first chapter of Deuteronomy, it is recorded that Moses said to the judges: "And I charged your judges at that time, saying, Hear the causes between your brethren, and judge righteously between every man and his brother, and the stranger that is with him. "Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great; ye shall not be afraid of the face of man.' "It has been customary in this court since an early

day for the judge to take the oath with his hand resting on these passages.

Standing in front of the bench, with his hand resting on the Bible, opened at the passage quoted by the presiding judge, Judge Ford then took the oath of office, which follows: 

"I, Hiram Church Ford, do solemnly swear that I will administer justice without respect to persons, and do equal right to the poor and to the rich, and that I will faithfully and impartially discharge and perform all the duties incumbent on me as United States District Judge for the Eastern District of Kentucky, according to the best of my abilities and understanding, agreeably to the constitution and laws of the United States; and that I will support and defend the constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic; that I will bear true faith and allegiance to the same; that I take this obligation freely, without any mental reservation or purpose of evasion; and that I will well and faithfully discharge the duties of the office on which 

I am about to enter." 

Judge Moorman then continued: "It is the custom of all federal appellate judges and most of the federal district court judges to wear a robe, the historic symbol of their judicial office, upon the bench. The appellate court of this circuit has asked 

Judge Ford to conform to this convention. The marshal will now put upon Judge Ford this symbol of his office, and will then bring him to the bench."

Mr. Hammons, the marshal, then assisted Judge Ford in putting on his robe and escorted him to the bench, where he took his seat beside Judge Moorman. Court was then adjourned and the ceremony was brought to a close.

At the conclusion of the ceremony, the new judge retired to his chambers, adjacent to the court-room, and

there received the congratulations of the large gathering of persons who had been present for the installation.

Andrew McConnell January Cochran was born in 

Maysville, Kentucky, February 4, 1854, and died, in the very same house in which he was born, on June 12, 1934. 

He was the eldest son of Robert Armstrong Cochran and Harriet Frances January, his wife. Andrew Mc- 

Connell, his great-great-grandfather, on the maternal side, was one of the pioneer heroes who lost his life in the Battle of the Blue Licks, on August 19, 1782.

After due preparation in the public and private schools of his native city, he entered Centre College, at Danville, from which he graduated with honors in 1873, with the A. B degree, and received from the same institution the degree of Master of Arts in 1878. He took his law course at Harvard, from the law school of which he graduated in 1877. In the same year, he was admitted to the bar and practiced law at Maysville for twenty-four years, from 1877 to 1901, being associated successively as a partner in the firms of Cochran & Barbour, Cochran & Son, and Wadsworth & Cochran.

On April 24, 1901, Judge Cochran was appointed (by 

President McKinley) Judge of the United States Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky, and qualified under this appointment on July 1, 1901. This, however, was a recess appointment, and, on December 17, 1901, the appointment was renewed by President Theodore 

Roosevelt. This second appointment was promptly confirmed by the Senate, and Judge Cochran took the oath of office thereunder on January 13, 1902. Judge Cochran's service on the Federal bench lasted almost exactly 

33 years. During that time he never postponed, delayed, or missed a term of his court, with the single exception of the June term of the District Court at Lexington, which was scheduled to begin on June 11, 1934, the day before his death. He laid down his duties only with his life.

Judge Cochran rose rapidly to prominence as Judge, and continuously maintained a commanding position as a jurist. It has been truly said of him that he "impressed the leading members of the profession in Kentucky and outside, where he had held court, as possessing all the desirable and none of the undesirable elements of a judge, and that, as a trial judge in important cases, he certainly never had a superior." He was an unwearying student, and kept pace with the progress of jurisprudence in the changing conditions of the times. Where a case involved points of general interest, his opinions were exhaustive and were repeatedly referred to by higher courts as containing everything material to the subject in hand. He had an almost incredible ardor for research and labor. Judge Cochran's moral qualities matched his mental qualities. He was absolutely unbiased, impartial, and just in every decision. He knew nothing but conscientious devotion to judicial duty. He had no patience with fraud or chicanery, but abhorred and rebuked them, wherever found. He was always unafraid, and his character was such that his sincerity was never doubted. 

In Judge Cochran's long career he decided many interesting and important cases, but any review of them here would be wholly impracticable. They constitute part of the permanent judicial literature of our country and have done no little to advance the course of Anglo- American jurisprudence. He was repeatedly called to sit in the Circuit Court of Appeals, and delivered that

Court's opinion in a number of interesting cases. 

On May 24, 1882, Judge Cochran was married to 

Miss Lucy McElroy, of Marion County, Kentucky, and she and their three children, John McElroy Cochran, Harriet Cochran Duke, and Robert Armstrong Cochran 

III, survive him. A son, A. M. J. Cochran, Jr., died in infancy many years ago.

It is difficult to do adequate justice to the noble life and fine public service of such a man as Judge Cochran. 

At the close of Memorial Resolutions prepared by Honorable Edmund F. BANQUET SPEECH BY JUDGE COCHRAN OCTOBER 26, 1901 

Mr. Toastmaster and Gentlemen:

At the outset of what I have to say I desire to return thanks. Those who have spoken here tonight have said many kind things about me. I want to thank them for this. I desire further to thank those to whom we are indebted for conceiving and providing this splendid entertainment and those, also, who have graced it with their presence and enlivened it with their brilliant remarks. But, above all, I must avail myself of this opportunity, the first I have had, of thanking in mass those who, by what they said and what they did in my behalf, were so instrumental in enabling me to secure the high position which I now hold. 

On the 29th of June last, for the first and only time in my life, I met President McKinley at the White House in Washington, where I had called to pay my respects and express my deep sense of obligation to him for his consideration. I hope I violate no confidence in quoting him as saying on that occasion that the political influences in Kentucky were largely against me, but I was fortunate in that I happened to have the President on my side. That attitude on his part towards me and my consequent success was due largely to the representations

which had been made to him concerning me by the members of the bar and bench in Kentucky, in this city, along the southern border of Ohio and elsewhere, and to the kind offices of a few personal friends who did have political influence. For all that was thus said and done, I now tender my sincerest thanks.

This is the agreeable side of those representations. 

They have another and quite distinct aspect-one that you may never have thought of, but one which has given me no slight concern. Indeed, it is a very serious view of them to me. They have set a pace with which I will be expected to keep up. They have erected a standard to which I must in some degree conform and by which I will be judged. Notwithstanding this, I have determined not to be disheartened but to view these representations as an incentive to the very highest endeavor, and I hope that out of a conscious weakness and inability to square performance with them will come strength to endure and power in some measure to meet expectations. 

In view of the very flattering character of what has been then and here said concerning me, and the high and noble sentiments that have been expressed in all the responses to the toasts proposed on this occasion, I feel that something equally elevated will be expected from me in what I may say in answer to your hearty call.

Pardon me, therefore, whilst I unbosom myself, as it were, to you and reveal my heart-searchings and aspirations in connection with the work upon which I have entered. 

You, no doubt, will consider them as quite exalted, and it may be that in presenting them to you the impression may be created that the purpose in so doing may be to increase your esteem of the speaker. If such a thought should creep into the minds of any of you, please look upon their intended relation to him as solely that of "self description and not self eulogy." 

And a word or two in justification of such a course.

HONORABLE H. CHURCH FORD

It certainly is not improper for one to put a high valuation upon those things within his breast of which he is really conscious and which are entitled to such valuation. Aristotle has characterized the man who is worthy and knows himself to be such as a magnanimous man. There is such a thing as magnanimity towards oneself. Paul cautioned every man among those to whom he addressed his letter to the Romans "not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think," which certainly implies that he should think of himself as highly as he ought. And Swinburne somewhere speaks of "an excellent arrogance." Besides, no one can know one's self as well as he himself does, and others will never judge him beyond his own measure. The spies, who brought the discouraging report from the Promised Land, had this to say concerning the giants whom they had seen there: "And we were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight. 99 The giants judged them as they judged themselves. 

And please bear in mind that what is said has relation solely to aspirations believed to be real after much heartsearching addressed to those whose kind words and deeds lead me to open my heart to them-and not to performance. 

In the first place, I would direct your attention to the view of the work upon which I have entered, which 

I would always have in mind.

The function of those moral heroes of the Hebrew race, the prophets, was twofold-to predict future events and to speak the truth as revealed to them by God. In modern times emphasis is put upon the latter function; in earlier days it was placed upon the former. This twofold function has been expressed in this way: "They were foretellers and forthtellers."

Now the work of a lawyer is largely that of a prophet in the sense of uninspired foretelling of future events. 

This has been forcibly brought out by Professor Holmes, now Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of Massachusetts, in an article on "The Path of The Law," in the Harvard Law Review. His thought and the phraseology in which he has expressed it is largely this: In societies like ours the command of the public force is intrusted to judges in certain cases, and the whole power of the state will be put forth, if necessary, to carry out their judgments and decrees. The law, as contained in the sibylline leaves of reports, text-books and statutes, is prediction-prediction of the incidence of the public force through the instrumentality of the courts. They are prophecies of the past of the cases in which the axe will fall. Indeed, the primary rights and duties with which jurisprudence busies itself are nothing but prophecies. A legal duty is nothing but a prediction that if a man does, or omits certain things, he will be made to suffer in this or that way by judgment of the court; and so of a legal right. Every new effort of legal thought is to make these prophecies more precise and to generalize them into a thoroughly connected system. To make them easier to be remembered and to be understood, the teachings of the decisions of the past are put into general propositions and gathered into text-books, or statutes are passed in a general form. People want to know under what circumstances and how far they run the risk of coming against what is so much stronger than themselves and hence it becomes a business to find out when this danger is to be feared. The work of a lawyer, therefore, in advising clients is that of interpreting these prophecies to them and with these oracles before him of prophesying what will be the action of the courts if the case in hand ever comes before them. As that action will depend upon the interpretations which they, and not he, puts upon those prophecies, there is a

considerable element of uncertainty in his prophesying.

For it will not do to forget that, practically considered, the law of a given case is no mystery, not what you or I may think it to be; what the judge or judges, who have the last word in regard to it, say, that it is. This is well brought out by an incident that is said to have happened in a Boston court. A lawyer was arguing a case before a judge on a certain occasion. In the course of his argument the lawyer stated a certain proposition to be the law. The judge interposed and said that it was not the law. The lawyer simply responded, "It was until your honor spoke."

Now, gentlemen, I am done prophesying in the sense of predicting future events-of foretelling to clients what the courts will decide.

The view, then, which I would have of the work before me on the bench is, that it is the work of a prophet in the sense of speaking the uninspired truth-forthtelling and not foretelling. It shall be my aim to cause it to be understood that my court exists for no other purpose than to search for the truth-to ascertain it and to declare it. I would have myself, the juries, every lawyer that appears before me, every witness, and every officer of the court, understand that that is the business in which it is engaged, and each has his own proper part to perform therein. I would have that high ideal pervade the very atmosphere so thoroughly as to banish from its sacred precincts everything that is base and false and mean. Success in causing this to be felt will not only add to its respect and standing, but will lend interest to its labors. No room will there be found for a discussion of the old question as to which is the more enjoyable, pursuit or possession. No occasion will exist for one's saying, as did a Frenchman once, "If I had truth in my hand like a bird, I would let it go that I may catch it again." There will never be a time that I will

not be both in possession of truth believed to be ascertained and in pursuit of that yet unascertained. 

Again, permit me to indicate the characteristics as a judge that I aspire to possess. They cannot be better or more forcibly expressed than in the words of Moses' charge to the judges of Israel, who were set apart to that work at Horeb, upon which, when I was sworn into office by Judge Lurton, I placed my hand at his request.

Open-mindedness, as inculcated in the words, "Hear the causes between your brethren"; righteousness, by which 

I mean a disposition to consider nothing but the very right of the matter in hand, as brought out in the words, "Judge righteously between every man and his brother, and the stranger that is with him"; absolute freedom from prejudice or partiality, which puts each party on equal terms, as contained in the words, "Ye shall not respect persons in judgment; but ye shall hear the small as well as the great"; and, lastly, fearless independence, as expressed in the words, "Ye shall not fear the face of man."

And, in conclusion, a word or two as to the spirit with which I feel that I have taken up the work. I cannot better point this out than by a reference. Professor 

Adam Sedgwick, a geologist of some fame, was appointed to the Woodwardian Professorship of Geology at Cambridge, when he knew nothing about the subject. His own explanation of it was this: "I had but one rival, Gorham of Queens, and he had not the slightest chance against me; for I knew absolutely nothing about geology, whereas, he knew a great deal; but it was all wrong." 

I will not confess either the ignorance of the one or the error of the other, with reference to the matters that will come before me, but I have persuaded myself to believe that I have entered upon the duties of my position in the spirit of Professor Sedgwick, as indicated by these words, which he is reported to have used:

"Hitherto, I have never turned a stone; henceforth, I will leave no stone unturned.” 

I hope you will pardon the extreme sentimentality of these remarks. It has been well said that one cannot lift himself by tugging at his waistband. If he is to rise at all, he must come under the influence of the drawing power of uplifted ideals.
