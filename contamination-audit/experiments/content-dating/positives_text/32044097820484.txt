EXECUTIVE COMMITTEE

Foreword

Every ten years since 1909 the citizens of the United States have held a White House Conference to study the needs of our children and youth. The sixth of these decennial conferences will take place in March, 1960, and will be known as the Golden Anniversary Conference on

Children and Youth. 

The call for the 1960 Conference was issued by President Eisenhower on May 27, 1958. The President named a National Committee of 92 citizens-known for outstanding work in fields which touch the lives of children-to guide the conference and asked the Governors of each of the states and territories to appoint committees to assist in preparing for the Conference. 

Governor Buford Ellington asked the Tennessee Commission on Youth Guidance to coordinate the planning for Tennessee's participation in the 1960 Conference. The 

Youth Guidance Commission, serving as the Executive 

Committee of the Governor's Planning Committee, began its work early in 1959. The Committee selected ten broad areas of study based upon the theme adopted by the National Committee, To promote opportunities for children and youth to realize their full potential for a creative life in freedom and dignity. These areas of concern covered community forces, programs, services, and facilities affecting children and youth.

Citizens throughout the State were encouraged to participate in the planning program. The county judge in each county was asked to serve as chairman, or honorary chairman, of the planning program and to create a committee to study and report on conditions in each of the study areas.

The Planning Committee thought that two purposes could be served by this "grass-roots" approach. First, the information gathered would reflect the citizens' own evaluation of their county's facilities and services for children and youth, and could be used to compile a state-wide report on conditions, services, and facilities. Such a report

could serve as a basic source of information for the dele gates of Tennessee to the White House Conference. Sec ond, it was hoped that citizens would be stimulated, as a result of the study, to take steps to overcome the deficiencies observed in their own communities. 

The widespread participation in this project is most gratifying. This report is based upon the written reports submitted by twenty-seven counties, the questionnaires submitted to high school students in four hundred high schools, the county inventory completed by seventy-six counties, activities indicated by fifty-seven state and national organizations, reports by several state agencies, departments, and private groups, and studies done on a state-wide basis by three state colleges and universities. In addition, many hundreds of dedicated citizens have contributed time, effort, and concern in the formation of this report. The final report was compiled by the Executive 

Secretary of the Tennessee Commission on Youth Guid ance. The Division of Surveys and Field Services of George 

Peabody College for Teachers edited and processed the final report for the Planning Committee. 

The Committee wishes to express sincere appreciation to all those who have participated in this study and have given so generously of their time and efforts. The results of these months of endeavor are much more than the compilation of a state report for the White House Conference. 

Many communities have already begun to act to bring about needed changes and improvements in their services to children and youth revealed by this study. New combinations of "community forces" have been established within counties, and these combinations predict new creative approaches to the problems of children and youth.

CHAPTER ONE THE SETTING 

To understand people one must study their environment-those conditions which determine their way of life -their values and ideals, the problems they face, and what conditions may be altered to improve their system. Within this framework members of the Tennessee White House Conference on Children and Youth-involving thousands of citizens from all walks of life-joined together to examine the forces in our State which seek To promote opportunities for children and youth to realize their full potential for a creative life in freedom and dignity. 

These efforts were centered on a study of: • The values and ideals of our society The effects of the rapid changes in this country and the world upon the development of children and youth

Personal and environmental influences on children and youth

Dedicated citizens and numerous state and local agencies have given serious attention to the problems of children and youth. They have studied patterns of belief, existing conditions, and forces for change, and their implications for the citizens-children, youth, and adults -in the various counties of Tennessee. 

Since societies are judged on the basis of what they believe and what they do, this report contains both the aspirations and the performances of Tennesseans regarding persistent problems of their children and youth. 

WHAT WE BELIEVE 

A philosophy is necessary to give direction to any action program designed to improve community life. Five important patterns of beliefs and values characteristic of 1

Tennesseans were identified in the recent study of public education in Tennessee. They are: • Recognizing individual worth 

Developing economic well-being • Improving physical and mental health • Cooperative action in the solution of problems • Fostering moral and spiritual guides for conduct¹ These five patterns were adopted and used by members of the Tennessee White House Conference on Children and Youth. They are broad enough to include most of the major values individually expressed by county committees and state and national organizations concerned with the problems of children and youth. 

WHAT WE DO 

The values and ideals of a society can be seen in its actions—its means of expressing what is cherished and dear. 

Selected positive expressions of each of the five values identified can be found in various county and organization reports.

INDIVIDUAL WORTH 

Americans long have recognized the inherent worth and dignity of the individual. This concept of individual integrity is expressed again and again by Tennesseans in their White 

House Conference reports.

From the mountain regions of East Tennessee, the Hawkins County report lists its concepts of the basic values and ideals as: 

Individual freedom of choice, speed and action as long as it does not infringe on the rights of others • Importance of the individual • Equal opportunity for developing a good life • Equality in the eyes of the law

The Knox County report emphasizes the need for “a rethinking of life's ultimate values in our churches, schools,

Survey Subcommittee of The Tennessee Legislative Council (Nashville: The

civic and social organizations, and the like, with emphasis on the worth of every life." The report also observes that much current religious education is merely a type of "religiosity" and, for practical purposes, insulates the pupil from the deeper values. 

In a discussion of their pattern of values, Moore Countians express concern for individual integrity through expanded recreational facilities, for care for children of working mothers, for increased guidance services through the schools, for the need for juvenile specialists and detention facilities for juvenile offenders, and for increased attention to community health problems and their solutions.

This same expression of concern for the individual is found in almost every report. Shelby County suggests that:

Each normal individual has the capacity to make a contribution to the common heritage. 

Opportunity should be provided for every child to develop his capacities in an atmosphere of freedom with the belief that the best life for each is the best life for all.

The report of the Boy Scouts of America in Tennessee points out that its program is based on the recognition of the individual's worth. Reference is made to the fact that:

While the boy population, ages 8 to 17 years, in Tennessee has increased 26 per cent during the past ten years, the membership in Boy Scouts in

Tennessee has increased 165.6 per cent during the same ten years.

The creation of the Tennessee Council for Handicapped Children in 1957 again indicates the citizens' undeniable concern for the individual worth of all children regardless of the condition of their physical bodies, their mental abilities or disabilities, or their social or anti-social behavior. It is also apparent that Tennesseans believe that 3

intelligent decisions for self-government and of everyday life are made only when all people are well-informed, selfdisciplined, and socially responsible. Governmental attention at local and state levels to the care of the mentally and physically handicapped, to the less fortunately endowed economically, and to the socially deprived child indicates concrete expression and application of the value of recognition of the worth of individuals. 

ECONOMIC WELL-BEING 

An idealistic approach to individual concern is fruitless unless at some stage these ideals are translated into specific practices which enhance the esteem and circumstances of the individual. Poverty by any other name would be just as degrading and frustrating. Mention is made in several of the reports of concern for the economic well-being of individuals. The report from Fayette County indicates this concern for economic advancement as being a basic value of Tennesseans:

The American way of life as established by our forefathers and as since practiced in the United States was based on freedom of speech and press, freedom of religion, the capitalistic system, and unlimited economic opportunity for all who have the ability and energy to raise themselves to a high standard of living. Men like Henry Ford, John D. Rockefeller, and Walter Chrysler are shining examples of how some have risen from poverty or modest means to become men of great wealth and great economic power. The fortunes earned by these people have been used not only to raise the living standards of millions of other people through mass production and in many other ways, but the charitable, religious, and other foundations set up by them during their lifetime and after their death have meant a permanent way of aiding in research, building libraries, securing college scholarships for needy and talented students, and many other useful things. 

Other county reports express concern for economic

well-being through their recognition of certain economic factors which affect their citizens. Statements such as the following are noted: • Out-migration is usually the result of a desire to realize better economic rewards.

As industry raises the standard of living of many, it brings a need for change in the curriculum of the schools. . People must be trained to take the jobs available to them in their respective areas. 

PHYSICAL AND MENTAL HEALTH 

Out of public concern for individuals has grown a vast number of services and facilities to care for the health of countless citizens. Such expressions of interest previously have been considered quite individual and private. 

The value which the various county committees and state and national organizations hold for adequate physical and mental health can be seen in this expression of concern for provision for increased health services. Important public services advocated are local health services, communicable disease control, clinical laboratory services, vital statistical services, indigent hospitalization provisions, crippled children's services, and sanitation facilities. 

Recognition is given in several of the reports to the problem of mental illness. Reference frequently is made to the need for expanded programs of research and training, education, psychological services, and treatment and rehabilitation facilities. 

The Montgomery County report states that "the values of health, physical and mental, are coming more into prominence." The Mental Health Center is a new venture in the Clarksville community, and in the words of the report, "was started primarily because of the needs of children."

Knox County reports on its successful development of a mental health center during the past few years, but adds this statement: 202 5

The need now is for specialized services for children such as a facility for the diagnosis and treatment of children with severe emotional problems. This total effort should be accompanied by a major increase in health education, which is clearly one of the major lacks in both the public and voluntary health agencies.

COOPERATIVE ACTION 

"No man is an island unto himself.”

While men may have lived in relative isolation in Tennessee in pioneer days, such an existence is no longer possible. The problems of twentieth-century life are too awesome and complex for individual action to be effective. Even in the earliest days of settlement, the people of Tennessee found cooperative action essential for survival and the realization of their deepest needs.

Much evidence can be seen in the county reports that the citizens of Tennessee are establishing the machinery for and realizing the value of cooperative action in working out problems and issues. Examples noted in the reports are the various community planning and improvement agencies, planned actions for industrial expansion, the attempts to coordinate the efforts of all agencies providing services for children and youth (even the establishment of agencies is a sign of cooperative action beyond individual action), and in the consolidation of one- and two-teacher schools into larger centers. 

The Madison County report indicates this very trend toward greater cooperative action for civic betterment. "The Committee felt that there was a definite need for coordination of services for children and youth, and that such a Committee should continue to function after the 

White House Conference. Cooperative action for planning and urban renewal projects suggests yet another sphere for development in Madison County: “There is no planning commission on a county-wide basis. However, since this Committee filed

reports, a planning commission for the city has been set up."

In a larger metropolitan county the need for cooperative action becomes even more imperative. The Knox 

County report contains the following recommendations for needed improvements in this area:

The civic and service clubs and organizations should act to fit their massive and amazing activities on behalf of children and youth into the most productive niches in our total framework. The 

Family-Children Division of the Council of Com munity Services should be strengthened and a county-wide review made of our child welfare services. Metropolitan Recreation planning, a

Health Council, a Hospital Council, a Citizen's Planning and Housing Council, have been mentioned. A Human Relations Council should be created with governmental support. A Council of Churches characterized by lay-ministerial partnership should be created on a county-wide basis. 

The Knox County Committee of the White 

House Conference should remain active for the next ten years to stimulate realization of this report's recommendations and of meeting the many needs of our children and youth as we see them. 

Another metropolitan county suggests an advanced stage of productive cooperative civic action through the establishment of a Council of Community Forces. 

This agency has a full-time executive with specified office and staff. Complete information regarding the children and youth in the county and their needs is compiled by the Council. . . . 

During the past ten years fifteen studies or reports concerning children and youth in Hamilton County have been made by the Council of Community Forces. . . .

Cooperative action to solve problems is exemplified best by the type of participation developed in preparation for the 1960 White House Conference on Children and Youth. The preparation has brought about close working

relationships and cooperative action among state and local governmental agencies and departments, private organizations, interested civic groups, and concerned citizens both of voting and nonvoting age. Thousands of persons have worked on this endeavor. Their willingness to work cooperatively, to join together in their mutual concern for children and youth, demonstrates again that no problem is too big if enough concerned citizens exert cooperative efforts toward its solution. 

MORAL AND SPIRITUAL GUIDES 

Americans have always given verbal recognition to the concept that this was a "nation conceived under God." In earlier days, almost every activity carried a moral sanction. Our early life carried a strong connotation of theocratic organization, and since that time a strong theme of spiritual purpose has existed. It does not seem amiss, therefore, for civic and governmental agencies to express concern for moral and spiritual values in our public life as citizens, and our private lives as children of God. 

Many of the county reports indicate a strong conviction regarding moral and spiritual values, and their place in Tennessee life. Frequent expressions are found indicating concern by groups for advancing the general spirituality of the citizens of Tennessee. Illustrative of this approach is the report submitted by the Dyer County White House Planning Committee. This report emphasizes that the most valuable of all values are the spiritual ones-especially those associated with love. Love, in the words of the committee, encompasses the following: (1) 

Love for country; (2) Love for self; (3) Love for home; and (4) Love for God.

Fostering moral and spiritual guides for conduct is best expressed in the deeply ingrained recognition of the family unit in Tennessee as the institution for the nurture and upbringing of the youth-as the shelter for their protection as they grow into adult life. Strengthening family

life is cited as a need in most of the county reports, and many cross-references indicate this concern. 

A spokesman writing for the Catholic school system in Tennessee states:

Since education (formal) consists essentially in preparing man for what he must be and for what he must do here below in order to attain the sublime end for which he was created, it is clear that there can be no true education which is not directed wholly to man's last end. From this fact flows the supreme importance of Christian education, not merely for each individual, but for families and for the whole of human society whose perfection comes from the perfection of its elements.

The role of the Church is touched on by every county report, and 57 of the 76 counties reporting estimate that over 50 per cent of their entire population holds church. membership. Most of these same counties report and note, however, that membership and active participation are not one and the same. Several reports challenge the churches in their community to modernize their services (without changing doctrine) in order to attract and hold the youth of the community.

Most of the county reports express certain faiths regarding the values and ideals of Tennesseans. At the same time, many county reports caution that all is not as well as might be wished; performance is less bright than our dreams and aspirations. Expression of belief is not enough --for democracy to truly work, belief must be translated into practice.

FORCES AFFECTING TENNESSEE'S DESTINY 

Tennessee has much in which to take pride. The 

State possesses a tremendous number of valuable assets. 

Even our most severe problems contain elements of challenge which may, in turn, generate new and valuable

courses of action which will benefit our families and our communities. 

The fabric of Tennessee society is richly textured. 

The State and its people are bountifully diverse. Any study of our resources would include the following ma jor elements: 

• Diversity of human resources 

Economic forces 

Natural resources • Changing forces Tennessee stretches the width of three states, reaching from the Mississippi River into the Great Smoky Mountains, spanning a sixth of the continent in longitude, and embracing the past and future in terms of sociology, economics, and general cultural achievement and heritage. 

The endowment of natural resources in Tennessee is bountiful and varied. Long an area rich in forests, rich agricultural land, and ample rainfall and water supplies, Tennessee also possesses many kinds of mineral deposits. 

The future holds much promise for additional exploitation of minerals as new deposits of rare metals are discovered.

The advent of TVA has marked the beginning of many new kinds of industrial and avocational pursuits. 

Hydroelectric power, an abundance of clear, pure water for industrial and recreational purposes, and plenty of economical land available for the location of new manufacturing plants has meant the expansion and establishment of many new industries. The terrain and temperate climate have made the area an exceptional one for the attraction of tourists and outdoor enthusiasts. 

DIVERSITY OF PEOPLES 

Tennesseans may be described in many ways. Within a radius of 25 miles in

East Tennessee some people are working at the spinning wheel and with horse-drawn plows, while others nearby are employed in the production of atomic 10

energy apparatus; in West Tennessee, cotton fields adjoin major manufacturing facilities; and Nashville, the Capital, has a half-dozen major colleges and universities, and more folk music publishing and recording companies than New 

York City.

From the mountain coves in the east to the river plantations in the west, one finds diversity, variety, and individuality—all existing companionably amidst traditional pride and respect for these differences. The children and youth of the State are enmeshed in the social fabric which reflects the blending of the past and the future, the shades of subculture, the sharply contrasting economics of the regions, and the changes brought about and being brought about by the growth and expansion of industries, businesses, services, professions, and radically new technologies. 

The finest land and mineral resources, the most modern plants and facilities, and the most beautiful lakes and hydroelectric power they represent, would be of no avail without people to service them. Tennesseans are proud of their folk, and of the willingness to work as indicated by their previous efforts. Industry finds that Tennesseans regard their neighbors and friends as the greatest resource of the State. Many corporations have found that sources of semiskilled labor are easily available, and that new skills can be learned quickly when the need arises. The human resources of Tennessee, while very real, are intangible and without adequate financial and quantitative measure. 

ECONOMIC Change of socio-economic patterns occurs 

FORCES 

in Tennessee at a constantly expanding and accelerating rate. Industries employing thousands of skilled technicians did not exist less than a quarter-century ago. Changes involve not only machines and techniques, but also men and their families and drastic alterations in a previous way of life. There has been a rapid gain in industrial activity as an important component 11

in the economic base of the State's economy, a sharp decline in agricultural pursuits and related service businesses as an important segment of the economy, and a corresponding mass migration of people from the farm and rural communities to urban centers. There appears to be no doubt that these basic socio-economic tendencies will continue for the foreseeable future. The old self-sufficient agricultural economy in Tennessee is failing to furnish a satisfactory income level, and in recent years there has been a heavy emphasis in public policy to attract and encourage industrial development. 

Economic expansion to some people may indicate large industrial plants, expanded markets, new subsidiaries for subcontracting, and expanded transportation services, but the real advantages are more personal. Industrial growth means new jobs, new opportunities, and a rising standard of living. Children and youth are also directly affected by these industrial changes. 

The Legislative Council's survey report shows that: 

The relative increase of personal income from manufacturing has not only outstripped agriculture, but income from this source has risen faster in the state than in the region and the nation. From 1929 to 1955 personal income from manufacturing in Tennessee increased by 679 per cent as compared with 609 per cent in the South and 458 per cent in the nation. By 1955, income from manufacturing was a more important source of civilian earning in Tennessee than in the region and almost equal to its relative importance in the country at large. The total dollar income from this source in the state had become so important that only two states in the South and fourteen in the rest of the country exceeded it in 1955.2 

Translated into personal terms, this industrial expansion has meant a higher standard of living for most of the "Ibid., p. 2. 12

population. In 1929 the per capita income of Tennesseans was $377, and by 1955 this amount had risen to $1,256. 

Although these gains have been considerable, the 1955 per capita figure for Tennesseans represents only 68 per cent of the national average. The average income of Tennesseans is still well below the average income per person throughout the United States.3

The Tennessee Industrial and Agricultural Develop ment Commission's report shows that 1,750 new industries were established in Tennessee from 1946 to 1957 along with 1,885 expansions of existing industries. The total investment in industrial growth for the ten-year period was approximately $1,134,000,000. However, the State has an overabundance of low-value-added, low-wage-paying industries, such as textile and apparel mills. High and medium value-added industries, such as chemicals, fabricated metals, machinery, paper, and rubber, are needed. 

Approximately 40 per cent of the State's labor force is employed by low-value-added industries as compared to 26 per cent of the nation's labor force so employed. The direct effect is that the average weekly salary of a Tennessee worker is only approximately 79 per cent of the national average. Chemical and primary workers' earnings are approximately 90 per cent of the national average. 

This suggests an area of profitable future development. 

Two-thirds of the State's total manufacturing output is concentrated in five metropolitan centers-Chattanooga, Knoxville, Memphis, Nashville, and the Tri-Cities-Bristol, Johnson City, and Kingsport. University of Tennessee studies suggest that industrial growth should be dispersed to furnish employment for youth throughout the State and to counteract the decline in population in rural counties. The University of Tennessee report states that agriculture produced approximately 23 per cent of the total income in 1929, but less than 7 per cent in 1958; and that from 1950 to 1957 the State had a net migration loss 

Ibid., p. 3. 13

of approximately 255,000 persons from a potential population increase of 406,000 persons due to natural growth. 

Of greater concern is the fact that the most significant portion of this migrant group was composed of youth reared and educated in Tennessee. Not only is there a population loss, but these youth represent a considerable capital investment by the citizens of Tennessee, and their productive efforts will not be expended in their native state or county. This report further indicates that the primary purpose of migration by these youth was to take advantage of greater employment opportunities outside of the state of Tennessee.

CHANGING FORCES 

In earlier sections of this chapter of the study, resources and forces affecting Tennesseans have been discussed. Such descriptions must, of course, be considered approximate, since all forces and conditions are in a state of change. 

Twentieth-century American life is mobile, almost nomadic. Tennessee is also caught in this transient life. 

During the past few years, more citizens have migrated from the State than have entered from other states. However, natural population gains from native births have increased the net population figures. Even so, fundamental changes are occurring which will have a vital effect upon the future life in Tennessee.

The family farm is rapidly disappearing as an essential economic unit. As larger farms develop-thoroughly mechanized and supported by large corporations-it becomes increasingly difficult for the small farm to continue to operate successfully. What is to become of members of these farm families? Statistical reports show us quite specifically. Large farm families are breaking up. The elder members-too old to learn new skills in industry, and reluctant to accept an alternate way of life-remain on the farms and in the small rural communities. 

Younger farm family members complete their school- 14

ing in the home community, work there for perhaps a limited time, and then migrate to the larger population centers to seek gainful employment. "Moving to town" is a very common family controversy down on the farm. And yet the hurt is deeper than family disagreement. What happens to the rural community which taxes itself to provide educational and other service facilities for its children and youth, and then loses them to other communities?

What happens to any community when its young people are gone? Is the quality of life fundamentally altered? 

What, too, is the condition in the larger communities?

Industry has been attracted to Tennessee, as earlier sections of this study have reported. Tremendous population expansion in the new community strains community utilities, taxes existing service agencies and facilities, creates conditions conducive to lawlessness, crime, and violence, and alters the nature of the town or city. These shifting forces create new jobs, remove the necessity of some old ones, and by their newness foster uncertainty and concern for all peoples. Changes in the new community, too, present family controversy. Children and youth are not under the easy supervision of parents and neighbors. New attitudes are formed which are foreign, perhaps alien, to those expressed in the community previously. "Newcomers," not used to the "community's way of doing things" alienate older citizens in the community by their ignorance of custom. Hostility and suspicion breed further disharmony, and cooperative community action becomes more difficult.

Citizens in the larger communities, which are in a state of flux, attempt to insulate themselves from the threats of these changing conditions. A mass exodus of prosperous families to the suburban ring of the city results. 

As better housing accommodations are developed, and as cleaner, more spacious neighborhoods are created, many businesses and community agencies, particularly churches, move out to the suburbs to serve the prosperous migrants. 15

Deteriorated neighborhoods and substandard housing remain to welcome the migrants from rural areas. Unused to "city ways" the late farm worker is at the mercy of those who seek to capitalize on uncertainty and naivete; he generally experiences some frustration, or complete failure, in securing immediate gainful employment; and, social agencies are soon swamped with requests for additional services. 

How are the problems of children and youth related to these general problems of social and economic maladjustment? Are not these concerns and conditions intensified for the youth? Problems of children and youth are the problems of all citizens in a democracy. The problems of adults also have a grave impact upon the lives of young citizens; problems of one age group are problems of all. 

Children and their difficulties, therefore, must be studied within the context of problems of metropolitan deterioration, urban migration and rural community life atrophy, suburban expansion, increasing school enrollments, expanded community service demands, altered employment opportunities, and other significant social and economic problems.

Problems of children and youth may be compounded when the older adult members of transplanted families attempt to follow family patterns of existence in the previous setting, which may be either inappropriate or in conflict with family patterns in the new home. All too often, these personal and family tragedies are increased since neither adults nor children and youth are aware of the true nature of the problems confronting them. 

Because of the transitory nature of the life in Tennessee, the nature and conditions of one part of the State, or even one county, are of concern to all parts. As citizens move about, they leave one set of problems but bring others with them. The economics and natural geography of all regions within the State are interrelated. The problems of one group of citizens are the problems of all. 16

CHAPTER TWO THE PROBLEMS WE FACE 

Describing where one is presents the possibility of some disagreement among serious citizens. Defining the problems to be faced is slightly more difficult, and determining a course of action to remedy these problems is the most difficult task of all. The hard-working White House 

Conference Committees attempted all three phases of problem solution as they studied the problems and needs of children and youth in Tennessee.

This chapter presents the results of deliberations of the committees as they assess the problems which arise from the conditions and resources described in Chapter 1. 

Each factor discussed there is now explored as to the significance of present and potential problems for children and youth in the State.

BREAKDOWN OF VALUES 

Certain important values and ideals have been considered as a vital part of the resources of Tennesseans. Of great concern, however, is the fact that many of these values and ideals, in practice, seem to be undergoing some erosion and change. Excerpts from various reports from the county committees lead one to believe that the dominant forces in some communities are restricting-rather than promoting the development of moral and spiritual, cultural, and economic values. For example, Knox County indicates that certain attitudes on the part of citizens are injurious to the development of their children, and list these attitudes as: • Emphasis on maintaining the "status quo"-too often many citizens are satisfied with life as it is and in keeping it unchanged.

Economic considerations-often the material things 17

of life are highly treasured and many local operations are gauged by the cost figures rather than by human need.

Desire for respectability-the norm of behavior is shifting from righteousness to respectability and people are inclined to act according to what is acceptable in a community rather than according to standards of right and wrong.

False attitude of independence-citizens do not feel free to intrust themselves to others in cooperative enterprises.

Little statistical information can be found to show that crime and juvenile delinquency rates, or illegitimacy, or other anti-social behavior rates are going down. Can a society long endure when its basic values are undermined? 

What is the role of citizens in protecting and reinforcing this vital core of social existence?

BREAKDOWN OF FAMILY 

Several county reports indicate concern for the stability of the family unit, and direct their attention toward those services in the community dedicated to preservation of the family and its influence on moral guidance of children and youth. The Shelby County report indicates that many services are provided to maintain the family unit, but that additional services and expansion of existing services are greatly needed to enhance family unity and keep children in their homes.

Many families can be kept together if counseling services are available to them. More of these services in the voluntarily and tax-supported agencies should be available. This community experienced an excellent example of the value of this service during the special project, developed by the Tennessee Department of Public Welfare, in which Aid to Dependent Children (ADC) families were given extensive help with their problems and as a result some of these families became self-sufficient and no longer required public aid. 

Other counties reporting on this problem are in basic 18

agreement with the statements quoted from the Shelby County report. There seems to be general agreement among both the lay citizens and the professionally trained "child expert" in Tennessee that the problems of child neglect, desertion, illegitimacy, and delinquency are closely related, and that the primary cause is found in "family breakdown." This "breakdown" is not confined to divorce, or infidelity, or to financial instability; it also includes the factors of limited or unstable values, lack of or conflicting authority and control expressed by the parents, and complete lack of harmony, "love and devotion." 

Family Service Association submitted several case records which illustrate the relationship between "family breakdown" and delinquency, neglect and desertion, and the need for services. One case cited told of a mother seeking help from a Family Service agency because of a serious problem within her family. "They placed her on the waiting list, and eleven months later service was given on a continuing basis." During the waiting period before service could be rendered the father moved from job to job. "His pattern of drinking had begun again, as well as his periods of attacking his stepdaughter." The mother had passed the point of wanting to cooperate with the agency's staff, and she and her daughter could only be helped by moving them out of the situation. "The training of personnel to work with families in both public and private agencies is perhaps the greatest need of all." For very often-as in the case reported-the program fails because qualified people are not available to provide service when needed.

The Big Brothers Association report is concerned with the problem of family breakdown and the association's unique program in which volunteers work with boys who "lack wholesome adult male companionship." Figures indicate more than 75 per cent of the approximately eight 19

million boys (ages 8-18) who present behavior problems in the United States come from homes without male influence. 

Married women with children are entering Tennessee's labor force in increasing numbers. The absence of the working mother from the home in some circumstances decreases the time spent together by family members. It is difficult to say just what this growing situation means, but it has been assumed that the security and acceptance necessary to the growth of children can come only from a family environment in which there is time for family activities and manifestations of warmth and affection.¹

PROBLEMS OF DELINQUENCY 

Criminal outrages perpetrated by children and youth are always disturbing to adult citizens. It is bad enough when an adult minority performs anti-social behavior, but when this crime is the result of actions of minors, the very foundations of the society are shaken. 

Seventy-six of the 95 counties in Tennessee completed the Tennessee Inventory as a part of their preparation for the White House Conference. County reports of the number of arrests of juveniles-youth 17 years of age and younger-during 1958 are sobering. One-half of the counties reporting indicate that the incidence of arrest was 2 or more per 1,000 of total county population. Definitions of criminal act, and measures of crime as compared with actual arrests, may be misleading. Even so, a serious social condition exists when arrests of juveniles alone surpass 21

per 1,000 of population. Further study of this problem is indicated.

The previously mentioned sections have documented some symptoms of value breakdown, and indications of serious social dislocations. These values are continually being eroded and questioned, if not violated directly. Can we maintain our way of life, and advance our basic ideals and principles when we constantly subject these values to scrutiny and doubt? Does an alteration in belief necessarily indicate an erosion in faith and practice? Do changing economic and social conditions demand change in our values? 

What values and ideals must we hold constant in our changing society and world?

PROBLEMS OF POPULATION MOVEMENT AND ECONOMIC CHANGE 

The problem of population mobility embraces two major trends affecting the children and youth of Tennessee-youth are leaving the State in search of employment; and children, youth, and their parents are moving within the State from rural to urban areas. The second phase of the problem is further complicated by the rapid movement of families from within the cities to the "fringe areas" surrounding the cities.

During the period of 1950-1955, nearly two-thirds-62-of the counties experienced heavy out-migration losses. As has been noted, the problem created by the rural-to-urban movement is further complicated by the rapid movement of families from within the cities to the "fringe" areas surrounding the cities.

An implied ideal of Tennesseans in the first chapter of this report suggests our concern for the individual integrity of all persons. EDUCATIONAL PROBLEMS 

In the face of the population trends already noted in this chapter, public education in Tennessee has a twofold obligation to children and youth. The schools have the obligation to function, wherever appropriate and possible, as an agency for encouraging and fostering further economic growth. Furthermore, the schools must be aware of the fact that Tennessee youth will continue to migrate until the economic picture changes and children must, therefore, be made capable of adjustment to geographical as well as occupational changes. 

University of Tennessee studies suggest that educational programs may need redesigning in order to prepare youth more adequately for the industrial expansion which is changing the Tennessee employment picture. Industrial and business education for a skilled labor force is essential to economic and industrial growth. School curricula should include, for example, mechanics, electricity, plastics, and other subjects fitted to local industry. 

The Tennessee Legislative Council recommended to the 1959 General Assembly the establishment of three regional state vocational schools and one industrial school for 29

training craftsmen and technicians. Steps are yet to be taken to implement these proposals.

The exodus of rural youth intensifies the problem of providing educational opportunities in the farm areas where the better trained and thus the more productive educational product is constantly being lost. Those remaining on the farm are the very young and the aged. As is pointed out in the recent study of public education in Tennessee,* this loss of productive manpower contributes to income inadequacy in rural areas and places a greater burden for educational support on fewer productive people. This exodus also forces into educational planning the problem of consolidation of the smaller school units to enable children remaining on the farm to be as well educated as those in the city.

The influx of population into metropolitan areas, especially in the suburbs or fringe areas, magnifies the problem of expanding the educational system and programs to meet the needs of a population that is constantly expanding. Knox, Hamilton, Davidson, and Shelby counties report a 25 per cent increase in their school attendance since 

1950. Nine other counties report increases of over 25 per cent, and 31 counties indicate increases greater than 10 per cent.

If population remained static, many counties in Tennessee would still face the need for considerable expansion or improvement of their school systems in order to meet minimum standards prescribed by the State Board of Education. Thirty-three counties report that some of their schools do not have adequate or well-equipped playgrounds and do not meet minimum standards for school sites. Three counties report that none of their schools meet minimum standards. Thirty-two counties indicate that less than 50 per cent of their school buildings and equipment are considered adequate by the superintendent.

The problem of school drop-out. It is estimated by the Tennessee Department of Employment Security that more than 30,000 boys and girls left high school in Tennessee in 1958 for employment. Of this number, 15,175, or about one-half, were drop-outs. A drop-out is usually defined as a student who leaves school without graduation as a result of academic, financial, emotional, physical, or family problems.

The girl drop-outs usually accept employment in occupations such as sales, or in service jobs such as waitresses or domestic help. The greater portion of the boy dropouts go into various unskilled jobs in manufacturing, take as common laborers, or become unskilled service attendants in service stations, and the like. jobs Both management and labor prefer to hold permanent jobs, with possibilities for advancement, for young men and women who have finished high school. In today's industrial world, there is no market for the permanent labor of adolescents. This means that boys and girls who quit school will be recurrently unemployed, as well as being deprived of constructive social relationships and leisuretime activities with their age peers.

All citizens in our difficult world need all the education possible, and every boy and girl deserves the opportunity to utilize a high school education to realize their greatest creative potential.

WELFARE SERVICES 

The primary and ever-present problem in child welfare is the need for additional, well-qualified social workers. The University of Tennessee report echoes several county, institutional, and state department reports when it says: 

In all areas of child welfare a demand exists for additional personnel, new and expanded programs, and more facilities. None of these needs can be met until private citizens and government officials realize the need and are willing to give financial support. These needs are especially heavy in urban areas. Rural counties must often carry out welfare programs with a county welfare director assisted by regional consultants. Welfare agencies can secure personnel with bachelor's degrees for beginning level positions, but the real need for persons with graduate training who can offer more skilled services in a variety of welfare fields remains unsatisfied.

Cost is a major factor in the expansion of professional staff for social work. The shortage of qualified welfare workers results from three factors:

Social work graduate programs require two years. 

An integral part of social work education is clinical training, but limited agency staff cannot give the amount of time necessary for adequate clinical training and supervision. • High training requirements and low salary return combine to defeat recruitment efforts for competent and dedicated workers.

Day-care centers represent another major problem area in welfare services. These facilities, established to provide shelter and service to the children of young working mothers, are in short supply. The University of Tennessee report contains several observations on this point: 36

Day-care centers for pre-school children of working mothers are not adequate in numbers and services. The only pre-school day-care centers are those privately owned and operated under a state licensing program, with the exception of limited facilities in a few city school systems and 

United Fund day-care centers. In 1959 the 182 commercial centers in the State were unable to meet the demand, as their waiting lists indicate. 

Commercial centers often offer care only from the hours of eight to twelve, and so fail to help parents who work all day. The hours of operation also rule out care for school children after school hours. Service charges of commercial centers prohibit their use by low-income families. The need is more acute among the Negro population than the white.

Within the experience of one metropolitan community, the Davidson County report further clarifies the need for day-care centers: 

Nashville ranked seventh among U. S. cities in the percentage of women in the labor force (37.4 per cent) in the 1950 census. None of the big, industrial, northern cities had a higher percentage. This affects children in many ways.

With salaries lower in the South and yet more women working, payment for adequate day-care services is difficult if a subsidized program does not exist in the community. The parent is either reduced to expecting substandard care for his child or to leaving the child without adequate supervision at home. Infant-under-two care is an even more urgent program.

Reference is also made in several reports to the fact that day-care centers for children can reduce the need for later corrective action. State and local governments can meet this need by subsidizing day-care centers, especially in low income residential areas; by encouraging more commercial centers; and by expanding pre-school centers in the public school systems. 37

Foster homes for children is a third major problem area in welfare services. Several statements in the various county committee reports indicate the need for placing in foster homes those children who have been separated from their parents and placed in institutions. In most cases, these children are the completely innocent bystanders to family dissolution and tragedy. Our traditional answer to this problem has been institutional, impersonal placement, and protective care. Too often in the past this care has been neither protective nor careful.

Children are entitled to a homelike environment, their own or a foster placement. To make this possible, an expansion of child guidance and family counseling services by public and private agencies is necessary. Also necessary, and not currently available, according to county reports, are welfare workers to examine and approve homes for foster placement.

PROTECTIVE SERVICES 

The care and protection of delinquent children is a function of state and local governments. Effective juvenile courts and correctional institutions are needed to realize this purpose. These courts and institutions need to be staffed with professionally trained personnel to rehabilitate children and to assist them in achieving a normal adjustment to society. Only the more highly urbanized areas in Tennessee possess these services and personnel. The shortage of probation workers, social work officials, policemen and juvenile law enforcement authorities, and adequate detention and correctional facilities hamper correctional and rehabilitative programs for youth. 

The report of the Tennessee Department of Correc tion indicates that "it was with this thought in mind, that the Division of Juvenile Probation was created by Act of Legislature in 1957." This Division serves the Juvenile 

Courts in Tennessee through pre-service investigations and after-release care and supervision. With this step, boys and 38

girls may be rehabilitated without committal to correctional institutions. This is a step forward in dealing with the juvenile delinquency problem.

Several reports from county committees have indicated the deficiencies in juvenile protective services which are recognized to exist in these counties. A tabulation from several counties' reports on the county inventory reveals the following salient factors regarding personnel: • Thirty counties report a single law enforcement officer for each one thousand persons. • Only seven counties in the State report that 90 per cent or more of their law officers have completed a training course. • Fifty counties report that less than 25 per cent of their law officers have completed a training course other than target practice or ordnance work. 

Facilities for juveniles are almost as deficient as the training and availability of enforcement officials. The county inventories, when tabulated, indicate that most of the counties with populations under 50,000 and a few with populations over 50,000 need to give attention to the juvenile detention facilities available in their county. Fortyfive counties report that children under 18 years of age are held in the county jail either in cells separate from adult offenders but where they can see, hear, or come in contact with the adults, or are in cells with the adult offenders. 

The latter is in direct violation of the laws of Tennessee; the former is in violation of the intent or spirit of the law. 

Juvenile delinquents are considered to be law violators, and when arrested by enforcement officers who have had no training or experience in juvenile protection, serious circumstances can result. Furthermore, most county facilities are not designed for the care of juveniles. A statement from the Loudon County report illustrates this problem: 39

It is difficult to obtain correct information on the number of arrests of juveniles or adults since there is no uniform reporting system in the county. The prevailing attitude toward juveniles is to get them out of jail within twenty-four hours as facilities for confining them are rather poor. 

Although there is a jail in Lenoir City, there are no facilities to provide meals for either juveniles or adults. No attempt is made for counseling juveniles who come to the attention of the court since no trained social workers are available. The only assistance the county judge has is from the Welfare Department, which has no trained case workers. The new State Juvenile Probation Serv ice will offer some supervision if a trained probation worker is obtained.

The League of Women Voters, because of its concern regarding the Juvenile Court System in Tennessee and the facilities and services to children in state-supported institutions, sought the opinions of the nine Juvenile Court Judges who collectively handle the majority of cases in Tennessee. Answers were solicited from the judges to the question, "What are the greatest needs today?" The nine judges responded: 

Greater interest on the part of citizens, civic organizations and agencies

More adequate detention facilities with trained counselors available to administer the rehabilitation program More definite and adequate vocational training in the State juvenile correctional schools 

Correctional schools, homes, or camps for the young less serious offenders

Planned studies to develop projects to prevent delinquency

More facilities for dependent and neglected children 40

More case work to keep children in their own homes More attention to the problems of the emotionally disturbed and mentally deficient children (IQ's 50-70) 

More trained probation officers

More juvenile courts separate and independent of the county judge's office

More flexibility in the juvenile court law 

More foster homes

Delinquency control in the spirit mentioned here is not punitive. Redemption and rehabilitation of a potentially useful citizen are the goals sought. The report from the Dyer County Committee expresses a philosophy regarding the problem of delinquency control which speaks for most county committees reporting on the subject: 

When malaria hit West Tennessee we did not condemn the mosquito. We cleaned up the breeding places. When we see a pile of leaves burning in the forest we do not condemn the cigarette thrower, we stamp out the blaze. Community attitudes toward juvenile delinquents must change. Condemning the child won't put out the fire of delinquency. Someday we shall look back and wonder how we ever could have sent children to a reform school which not only failed to reform but in many cases returned the child (to a community that did not want him) rebellious, anti-social and with a determination to "beat the rap." Poor foolish child. Poor foolish us.

The Davidson County Committee adds to this philosophy the recommendation that we (the community) find and provide wholesome recreation, provide and enforce legislation to protect youth against the sale of alcoholic beverages, involve youth in the selection and planning of their own activities, and give them a responsible role to play in all our community affairs. In their words, 41

"Why do we relegate them to a child's world, label them as juveniles, and prevent their normal growth and emergence into adulthood along with us?"

RECREATIONAL AND CULTURAL ACTIVITIES 

Increasing recognition is being given to the importance of adequate recreation and leisure-time activities in developing the personalities of youth. 

The mechanization of farming and the existence of an urbanized, industrial society have resulted in more leisure time for the Tennessee family and an expansion of recreational facilities. Both public and private agencies are providing recreational facilities and programs in Tennessee, but inadequacies exist. The degree of adequacy varies from city to city and county to county.

Madison County reports on the adequacy of recreational facilities and programs. This report states: 

Glaring weaknesses were brought to light in our study of recreation and parks. A diversified full-time, year-round recreation program is available to less than 50 per cent of the children in the community. In our study of special recreation facilities it was shown that there are no golf courses, swimming pools, skating rinks or bowling alleys for Negro residents. There is a correlation between this lack and juvenile delinquency. 

County recreation departments charged with the responsibility of planning and administering a recreation program for children and adults are, for the most part, nonexistent within the State. Only four counties indicate that they have a recreation department on a county-wide basis. Only 26 counties report that one or more municipalities within the county have a recreation department or agency.

Few counties provide adequate amounts in their annual appropriations for recreation and parks. Twenty-nine counties make no appropriation, and 32 counties appropriate less than $1.00 per capita. It would be unreasonable 42

to expect to find many year-round recreational programs available with such limited financial support. Twenty-five counties indicate that they have no organized program of recreation, and the programs in 16 other counties are available to less than 50 per cent of the children. Even "reasonable cost" commercial or public recreation is not available in many counties. Seventy-six counties reported:

No golf courses are available in 45 counties. • No tennis courts are available in 33 counties.

No swimming pools or beaches are available in 22 counties. 

No roller skating rinks are available in 28 counties. 

No bowling alleys are available in 55 counties. • No "teen-centers" are available in 45 counties.

No drive-in movies are available in 22 counties. 

Golf courses are available to Negroes in only 3 of the counties reporting; tennis courts in only 18 counties; roller skating rinks in 5 counties; and "teen centers" in 10 counties. None of the counties reporting has commercial bowling alleys open to Negroes.

The interests of youth are not confined to participation in and observation of sporting events. Inventories of students' interests in Tennessee high schools indicate a high degree of interest in cultural activities, such as art, literature, and music, for which there is little opportunity for development and expression. Seventy-five seniors from a consolidated high school in rural middle Tennessee indicate that they would enlist in Saturday, evening, or summer courses related to cultural appreciation if such were available.

The reading of good books provides both recreation and a means of cultural advancement. Sixty-two counties report that free public libraries are available to all citizens within the county. The other 14 counties reporting state 43

that library services are available to only a part of their citizens. It is encouraging to note that 31 counties have their library under the direction of a librarian with a de gree in Library Science. 

The State Regional Library Service through its book mobiles is filling a gap in the area of library services. The regional library program was created in 1939 and first received its support from the Tennessee Valley Authority. 

Although state funds have been available since 1943, additional funds are needed to employ the necessary trained library personnel to expand these services. 

IMPACT OF MASS MEDIA 

Mass media has become an accepted phrase in the American idiom. The term refers to those forms of communication which supply information and modify attitudes of the great mass of people in this country. The term often implies that the information is received whether the citizens want it or not. The newspapers, radio stations, television stations, and movie theatres are usually the first to be considered as distributors of information to the masses. 

More careful selections include: drive-in theatres, book and magazine publishers, billboard display and placard printers, song and music publishers, phonograph distributors, and the like. The list is apparently endless and the complexities of distributing those various products which communicate ideas, ideals, and purported values are such that it is almost impossible for any one individual or group to grasp.

Tennessee's first educational television station was established in Memphis in 1955 through the support of the 

Tennessee Department of Education. This fact probably makes the statement of the Shelby County Committee one of the most authoritative of those presented. The Committee has this to say:

Although significant mass media research related to the youth of Memphis and Shelby County 44

is not readily available, certain generalities and recommendations can be made concerning the effect of radio, television, newspapers, motion pictures and magazines on this age group. It has been found that most of the harmful effects first attributed to television have proven untrue. 

The expected harm to eyesight did not materialize, no measurable detrimental effect to school work has been discovered, and as a matter of fact, circulation of books has increased rather than declined since the advent of television. This is true of that hypothetical person, the average child, who now watches television in excess of twenty hours each week. This time he has taken from such activities as sleep, play, going to movies, and reading children's magazines. Whether or not this substitution of television per se for other activities is bad, we do not know, but in general, parents approve of television as a means of keeping children amused and occupied, of providing a family type activity, of keeping youngsters off the streets, and of generating more harmonious homes. Television should do more than this, but in the broad context of judging television as a medium, we must consider it effective, and if you will, good.

We should recognize that television has a tremendous impact on all age groups, that it will be a continuing important factor in the growth of our children, and that, as in the case of other media, our primary consideration is for the material, its quality and quantity, which is thereby disseminated.

A statement from the Hawkins County Committee emphasizes the need for adequate regulation of all mass media. This committee concludes that the impact of radio, television, movies, newspapers, and magazines on youth is tremendous for both good and evil. It is their belief also that the major portions of this impact is harmful because of emphasis on sensationalism, sex, brutality, crime, and low cultural standards, with too little stress on good programs, good movies, and good books. 45

HOW DO WE BUILD BASIC VALUE CONCEPTS? 

This report has documented expressions of idealism by Tennesseans, and various county and organization reports have been quoted to demonstrate that some of these values are being eroded, while others are in a transitional state. Every problem in human relations and social conduct has as its base some decisions which rest upon value judgment. Thus sound values and ideals are too important to be left to chance.

We must recognize and accept our responsibilities for teaching basic values and ideals to our children and youth. 

Many of the county organization reports indicate that most Tennesseans recognize and accept the responsibility of teaching the common values of society and moral norms to their children. This learning by the child cannot be by rote. These values and ideals must become a part of every child's conscience. That this condition is far from realization can be seen in the problems of child neglect, desertion, illegitimacy, and delinquency discussed earlier in this report. Individuals, youth organizations, adult civic groups, recreation agencies, and radio, television and 47

the press have a role to play in transmitting and teaching the basic values to the youth of the State. Examples of the work of certain organizations illustrate what can be done:

The Kentucky-Tennessee District Kiwanis Interna tional is undertaking a project which has the theme: "Teach by example.. Work with and for youth." The four main objectives of this project are: 

To help to create greater tolerance, understanding, and brotherly love among youth

To help youth to appreciate good government, emphasizing the dignity and importance of public office and public service

To help youth realize that safety is a way of life precious to every person

To help youth in developing human resources This organization reaches thousands of youth in the State and involves thousands of adults in a program to "help youth build values into their personalities."

The Tennessee Association for Children Under Six points out that their organization is built upon the philosophy expressed in Proverbs 22:6: "Train up a child in the way he should go." This organization concerns itself with the setting of standards for and stimulation of the development of kindergartens and day-care centers for preschool children. An objective of the organization is to help children "to learn to share, to lead and follow, to assert or accept as the group demands, and to get along with others."

The Middle Tennessee Radio and Television Council has special projects designed for television viewing to help prepare children for their first year in school, to encourage children to receive immunization against polio, and to afford the youth of the State an opportunity to share in panel discussions concerned with values of present-day society.

The county inventories indicate that many communi- 48

ties are making ineffective use of the available established youth organizations. Eleven counties indicate that 16 or more national or state-wide youth organizations are actively working with the youth of their communities. Forty-seven counties report, however, that less than 50 per cent of their youth population participates in these organizations. 

Our teachers must be more self-conscious of the value roles they play.

The research project conducted by the Tennessee Agricultural and Industrial State University for the Planning Committee focuses upon the "extent to which the 600 teachers surveyed give value concepts form and direction as displayed through their everyday behavior as they direct the learning of children in Tennessee." The Study Committee concerned itself with the five patterns previously listed in this report, and prepared a survey to determine: 

The extent to which teachers understand and agree with the expressed beliefs and values of Tennesseans The extent to which teachers are attempting to correlate more closely their educational practices with those values and beliefs Some of the findings bear directly on the responsibility of adults to communicate values to children and youth. The study suggests that "the outcomes of education in terms of modifying children's behavior is the result not merely of what is taught and of how it is taught, but of the atmosphere reflected in the classroom." It further concludes that, while teachers agree with the expressed beliefs and values of Tennesseans, there is a difference between their educational beliefs and their educational practices. 

The possible effects on students of the teacher's discrepancy between value concepts and practices is not known. The extent to which this variance influences the student's personal interest, his approach to human relationships, and even his basic direction in life can only be inferred. It is perhaps significant that the teacher usually 49

identified as having the power to cause students to reorient their lives and adopt new and generally more socially acceptable goals is one whose own value commitments are firm and openly expressed.

Implications of the role of the teacher in value guidance is further clarified in the following statement from the report of the Tennessee A and I study on value concepts:

Many teachers would probably concede that there are limits beyond which teaching cannot and should not go in the inculcation of values, and that students' values are influenced more by family, friends and the community in which they live than by the teachers with whom they come in contact. Yet, the teacher cannot evade the responsibility for the communication of values. It is, therefore, important that teachers become more self-conscious about the value roles which they play. It is necessary that they clarify their own goals, and evaluate realistically the outcomes of teaching in terms of changing beliefs and influencing behavior of their students. 

Educators must be ever aware that intellectual understanding is no confirmation of change in behavior. The implication of this study is that an educational program which will bring beliefs and practices into closer agreement must use all of the resources society can command, including educational, social, civic, and fraternal organizations.

We must set standards for our children by example. 

Inescapably the home and parents have a vital and continuing responsibility for the transfer and inculcation of values for their children and youth. Standards are established in the home and modified elsewhere. Seldom will a child be found with a higher moral standard than that expressed and lived by the parents in the home. The 

Shelby County report dwells upon this theme: 

The old philosophical adage: "If the old horse and the mare trot, you can't expect the colt 50

to pace," still goes.... The behavior patterns (of youth) are often mirrors of patterns set by the parents. The role of the parents, yesterday, today and tomorrow is one of God's richest blessings; and yet it is one of the most difficult. . . . 

Good citizens are those who early in life were taught the full meaning of responsibility and not to expect the comforts and luxuries to be handed them on a silver spoon.

Parents do have the responsibility for setting standards for their children, and actions and examples are far more effective than words. Various county reports indicate that part of the problem lies in the inability of the present society to establish clearly a "standard" behavior which is acceptable for persons of different ages and for persons with different positions of responsibility. Despite this difficulty, it is clear that the old admonition of "do as I say and not as I do" will never suffice in rearing children and youth. Instead, the Hawkins County report suggests several ways in which parental responsibilities for teaching basic values may be realized:

WHAT ACTIONS ARE DICTATED BY POPULATION TRENDS AND ECONOMIC CHANGES? 

Profound changes in the population of Tennessee have occurred. A decline in rural population, the rapid growth of metropolitan areas, shifting population from cities into the fringe areas, and continuation of higher birth rates are among the factors which account for these changes. Many drastic steps may need to be taken to either solve the problems created by these changes or, in some instances, to impede the changes themselves.

Our educational systems must further emphasize programs to prepare youth for industrial employment. 

The University of Tennessee studies show that Tennessee's future is dependent upon industrial development. 

Agricultural employment and income are declining. Revised curricula in our high schools, and additional vocational schools are needed to produce a skilled labor force and to enable rural youth to transfer more successfully from the chores of the farm to industrial employment. 

Several reports stress the need for centrally located vocational schools to prepare boys and girls to go directly into some trade or vocation. Expressed in the county reports is the belief that industry can be attracted to a county if a qualified and adequate labor force can be provided. 

The school system, if it is large enough, should provide such training. Several small systems might cooperatively establish and support such an undertaking, which is authorized under existing legislation. There is considerable national discussion, however, in both educational circles and in the field of industrial management, as to the type of training that should be offered to youth for employment opportunities in 1970 or 1980. Technological advances appear to be moving faster in industry than comparable advances in the related educational fields. 

Our educational programs must produce individuals who are able to adjust satisfactorily to new surroundings. 56

Many of our youth now migrating from rural to urban areas, both within the State and out of the State, find it difficult to adjust to an entirely new setting. People in the urban areas may appear to be different, and the personal touch which the youth experiences in the small community may be replaced by the impersonal contacts in urban existence. These feelings of "difference” may create the attitude that the individual is an “intruder," an “outsider," and unliked. Such feelings may result in anti-social behavior, physical hostilities, a lack of regard for or understanding of law enforcement, apathy toward essential public services, and difficulty in adjusting to occupational requirements and regulations. 

We must make concerted efforts at both the state and local levels to expand educational facilities and provide additional instructional staff to meet increasing school enrollments.

The increase in Tennessee's youth population, 0-18 years, has been considerable and the University of Tennessee's Bureau of Business Research estimates that by 

1964-65 the attendance in Grades 1-12 will increase by 60,000 pupils. By 1972-73, it is estimated that approximately 110,000 pupils will want to enroll in colleges in Tennessee-which doubles the present number of 55,000 college students.

The major portion of the increased college enrollment must be absorbed by public institutions, for indications are that private facilities will not expand at a rate sufficient to maintain the same percentage of total enrollment in 1973 as they now maintain. If additional staff and facilities are not provided, the youth of Tennessee may be deprived of the opportunity to realize their full potential for a creative life. 

Our cities and counties should create local industrial and agricultural commissions and should cooperate with private and civic organizations in the promotion of industrial development. 57

Economic developments-with emphasis upon the attraction of new manufacturing-is now a major state policy, and substantial results are helping to overcome the lag in industrial development. The Tennessee Industrial and 

Agricultural Development Commission publicizes the industrial advantages of the State. The Commission also conducts industrial surveys and organizes local industrial development activities. In 1957, local industrial development groups were operating in 153 towns and cities. All cities should make use of the Commission's assistance to create local development organizations and to cooperate with private and civic organizations in promoting industry. 

To attract industry, our local governments must provide the services which industry considers essential to its operations.

Before local governments can advertise their industrial advantages they must provide services which industry considers essential to its operations. Industry hesitates to locate in a community whose government has not furnished or is incapable of furnishing adequate public services. Industries desire and often demand recreational and cultural programs for their employees. The need for a school program which will supply an adequately skilled labor force has been discussed under an earlier topic. In addition to essential services, an equitable tax base must be established. Industry is cautious about moving into a community which has inequitable property assessments. 

We should see that all youth are made aware of the economic advantages of their own counties.

The McNairy County report laments the fact that, while its high schools are offering more courses in agriculture than ever before and its soil is much more productive than in the past, fewer young people enter agricultural occupations. To help remedy the situation, the report suggests that high school boys and girls should be taken on field trips to see some of the progressive farms 58

and farmers within the county. Suggested also is the promotion of a county fair each year to give young people an opportunity to observe the progress taking place on the farms.

Youth also need to be reminded that migration does not guarantee economic security. The Fayette County report points this out in the following way:

There has been a shifting of population, both white and Negro, from the farm to the city. In the case of the Negro, the migration has been largely from the farms of the South to the industrial centers of the North. Some of these migrants have secured economic betterment from the move. 

In other cases, however, the absence of steady work has put them in an environment which causes us to wonder if their lot has not suffered rather than having been improved. 

HOW CAN WE PROVIDE ADEQUATE SERVICES FOR CHILDREN AND YOUTH? 

The present services now provided children and youth and the improvements needed to make them adequate are discussed in an earlier section of this report. Essential steps toward adequacy include the provision of additional financial resources, the coordination of the efforts of the many agencies providing services, and more effective use of all voluntary organizations providing services for youth. 

We must find ways to raise additional revenue to provide more adequate programs and personnel in the fields of education, health, welfare, protective services, and recreation.

The financial structure and policies of local governments are directly related to the development of the community. Certain courses of action which may be taken by local governments to secure, directly and indirectly, additional revenue to meet the needs of children and youth are recommended.

Essential to the financial stability and well-being 59

of local government is the enforcement of tax collection laws and the use of effective collection procedures. 

Standards suggest that a 98 per cent collection rate is excellent. Only 9 of the 76 counties completing the County 

Inventory have reached this collection standard during the past five years. Seven of the counties report that during the past five years more than 10 per cent of their property taxes remained uncollected. Taxes are levied on all persons and tax collection officials should fulfill their legal responsibility to collect all taxes. Uncollected taxes not only deprive the community of desperately needed funds to which it is legally entitled but also weaken the confidence which citizens have in their local government. • City and county governments in Tennessee haveas a rule-inequitable assessments of property at a low percentage of its market value.

County assessments range from less than 10 per cent of market value to 80 per cent of value, even though the State law requires assessment at 100 per cent of market value.

In the 74 counties reporting, the average ratio of assessed valuation of real property to actual cash value is 

50 per cent or more in 2 counties, 331 to 50 per cent in 

4 counties, 20 to 33% per cent in 26 counties, 10 to 20 per cent in 36 counties, and less than 10 per cent in 6 

counties. The University of Tennessee report states: 

Revaluation and reassessment programs would result in the equalization of property valuations and provide an equitable basis upon which to make assessments. The revenue potential of local governments could be more fully exploited if assessments were more in harmony with legal requirements and market values. Following a reassessment program, tax assessors should revaluate property at regular intervals to place increments in property values on the tax rolls. 60

Local governments can also take steps to provide a more immediate and direct enticement for new industrial development. 

With the expectation of expanded community payrolls and a broader tax base from industrialization, tax write-offs may be offered to incoming industry, and bonds may be issued for the purchase of land and erection of buildings for industrial use. However, caution should be exercised because bond issues and tax write-offs can place a burden on local finances and industrialization may cost more than it is worth.

The State should continue to make financial grants to local governments to provide minimum level services, and to stimulate local agencies to exceed the minimum through grants-in-aid. 

An investigation and revision of legal restrictions on local financial flexibility and the promotion of industrial development by the State would assist local governmental finances. However, local governments should not be encouraged to shift responsibility for their children and youth to the State when they are making less than maximum effort locally to fulfill that responsibility. 

Federal aid is now given to state and local governments to provide certain health, welfare, and educational programs for youth. The role of the federal government is to extend its aid to guarantee minimum programs and equal opportunities for youth throughout the United States and to stimulate state and local governments to exceed the minimum programs. The federal government may also render assistance through its services-in-aid. Research, distribution of information, technical assistance, and training programs are services which can reduce the financial burden of state and local governments. 

The needs of children and youth cannot be properly served unless there is coordination of all community forces and agencies.

It would be difficult to list the many and various sug- 61

gestions to improve and unite community support of and action for children and youth. The important fact is that more than 100 state-wide and/or national organizations indicate their willingness and desire to join in united efforts on behalf of and with the youth of Tennessee. 

Each of the counties reporting lists coordination of services to children as one of the areas in which they want to improve. From the four major metropolitan counties to the small counties with fewer than 20,000 citizens are strong indications that solutions to these problems can be had when and if the various organizations, civic clubs, churches, and governmental agencies work collectively with one another.

Many counties outline the steps or action they have planned to initiate this program of unified action to solve major problems and to provide needed services. The state and local governments and voluntary organizations can make a significant contribution toward the solution of problems of children and youth by educating the citizenry regarding existing needs. An informed citizenry is more willing to contribute its time and financial resources to promote the individual fulfillment of youth. No expression of awareness and concern is more apparent than the effort put forth by the thousands of citizens who participated in the preparation for the White House Conference on Children and Youth. 

Our voluntary agencies have an important contribution to make in providing needed services for children and youth.

All reports agree that services for children and youth are the responsibility not only of public agencies but also of religious and civic organizations. Religious and civic organizations contribute much time and money to assist in providing programs and facilities for the care, treatment, protection, and development of youth. Voluntary organizations are sources of leadership, ideas, and money. These 62

organizations should realize and use their potential in conjunction and in cooperation with public agencies. In so doing, the financial burden of public agencies could be lightened. Care should be taken that public functions and obligations are not transferred to private or voluntary agencies.

The Memphis-Shelby County report reaches the following conclusion:

Service for children could be improved by the redefining of agencies' areas of responsibilities. Both tax-supported and voluntarily supported agencies need to take a fresh look at their programs to determine if they are effectively meeting needs, also, that the difference between their program and that of other agencies is clearly defined.

Our county governmental structure may need alteration to provide all needed public services. 

Several county and state reports suggest a study of governmental organization. These reports point out that, while county governments were originally designed to meet the needs of a rural society, urbanization has created a demand for public services which many county governments find difficult to provide because of their basic organizational structure. The county judge and the members of the Quarterly County Court need adequately organized assistance, preferably under strong executive leadership. As illustrated in the county surveys, conflicts of authority and responsibility develop between city, county, and special districts. One solution, suggested by the Knox County report, to the conflicts of authority and accompanying duplication of services is city-county consolidation. A single, well-organized unit of government in a community or political area can contribute much toward providing for the needs of youth. The University of Tennessee adds that the unit of government must be headed by a responsible administrator who is assisted by 63

a competent staff. If this ideal situation were achieved, the local government could more adequately devise and execute a comprehensive plan to reduce the unmet needs of children and youth.

In the event city-county consolidation is not feasible, or not desired, efforts should be made to consolidate similar functions performed by two or more public agencies operating within a county. The most progressive steps in this direction have been taken in the area of public health services. Three of Tennessee's four metropolitan counties operate under a single, county-wide health program. In addition, more than one-half of the counties in the State have cooperated with two, three, or four neighboring counties to form County District Health Departments. 

We must establish the machinery necessary to longrange planning for the welfare of all citizens. 

Several counties have discussed needs for long-range planning and programming for social services and facilities. 

The Hamilton County Committee, writing on planning and urban renewal, states: 

Community planning in its various forms affects children and youth in two ways. First, planning in the long run is concerned with making human communities suitable and fit places in which human beings can live. Secondly, a large part of any planning program is of necessity devoted to the provision of facilities especially for younger people-schools, parks, playgrounds, libraries and community centers. The effectiveness with which this program is carried out will have a great influence upon the health, education and character of our youth.

Davidson County is also concerned with the long-range aspects of community planning and action. The report of the County Committee contains this statement from the 

Director of the Conference on Metropolitan Area Problems: 64

One of the impediments to metropolitan government is the type of popular thinking that somehow this whole question of urban expansion can be handled on an ad hoc, short run, transitory gush-of-enthusiasm basis. There is nothing, which suggests that we will stem this tide of urban expansion, or . . . achieve a static balance between urban and agrarian life. . . . .. We are not going to make progress until it becomes a matter of public conviction that we are faced with a long time trend of urban expansion which simply will not respond to the short run attempts to cope with the problems incidental to the trends.

Every problem has many facets. Any serious planning encounters barriers and limitations to action. The Loudon County report indicates many county improvements which have been made, and others that could be made, but suggests that one of the county's chief limitations is the absence of a coordinating group. The report outlines the first function of such a group as follows:

Among the first endeavors of the coordinating group could be the creation of a planning commission which would formulate and enforce a county-wide building code, zoning restrictions, and regulations. Lack of such regulatory measures is seen in the shanties, junk yards, shabby buildings near and around the city limits of our town and communities. Such ugliness depresses the minds of our youth as well as grown ups, destroys surrounding property values and defaces the natural beauty of country scenery. 

Each county and city should have an active planning commission established and organized under Tennessee's planning legislation. Only 9 out of the 76 counties report that they have a planning commission with county-wide responsibilities. However, 35 counties note that one or more of the major cities in their county have planning commissions. Forty counties state that no community facility plans of a comprehensive nature have been developed 65

within the past few years. Subdivision regulations are applied on a county-wide basis in only five of the counties reporting.

A planning commission has many functions to perform and tasks to supervise. A competent and recent analysis of the county's economic base, together with a study of population and its prospective composition, should be available. There should be a community-wide landuse plan indicating uses for residence, commerce, and industry, with general location and extent of lands needed for public uses, such as civic centers, parks, playgrounds, and schools. Based on an analysis of present and future needs, there should be tangible plans and advance purchasing of sites for community facilities, such as county buildings and schools. The county should maintain priority lists of needed public works and schedule their accomplishment by means of a capital budget. Subdivision regulations should be in effect in the county and coordinated with those applicable in the municipalities within the county. 

THIS WE KNOW

Tennessee's resources, human, material, and aesthetic, are bountiful.

Never before have the needs of children and youth been so well known. Our children's bounty consists of vast numbers of interested citizens, alert with concern for the gaps in services revealed by this study, an immense endowment of cultural advantage and tradition, a rich heritage of moral and spiritual life and attitude, an undergirding sense of strong religious belief and practice, and an economic potential adequate to provide those things which we consider necessary.

The future of Tennessee is her children and youth. 

This future looks bright as a reflection of the efforts and concerns of dedicated, thoughtful citizens. No task is too great when Tennesseans work and plan together. 66
