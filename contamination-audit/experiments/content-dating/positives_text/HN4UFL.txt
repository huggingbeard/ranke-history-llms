CHAPTER I The Coming of the Pilgrims and the Puritans The Puritans occupy a place of their own in the history of our country. They were the first colonists who came to the New World with a determination to stay. It was their purpose to build a state that should endure for all time and serve as a light for the persecuted and discouraged people of all the world. The Puritans were of two classes: those who withdrew from the Church of England and set up their own churches, and those who wished to remain within the church and change it to suit their own views. Both disapproved of the forms of the English Church. 

They felt that so much ceremony stood between them and God; they wanted sermons based on 

Scripture, and prayers said from the heart, rather than set forms read from a book.

The Pilgrims: The first body of Puritans to come to America were the Pilgrims. In England they were called Separatists (sep'ărā-tists), because they had separated themselves from the English

Church. They considered themselves Pilgrims, because they had travelled from place to place seeking a country where they might worship as they pleased. They had been driven out of England because of their religious beliefs. 

In Holland they had found refuge, but they became dissatisfied for three reasons: first, they wanted to support themselves by ways less hard and unfamiliar than those they had found in Holland; second, they thought it would be easier to establish their religious ideas in a newer country; third, they wanted to remain Englishmen; they did not want their children to speak a foreign language and adopt foreign ways.

Two men were sent from Holland to England to get the King's permission to establish their own church in America, and to ask for a place in the Virginia Territory. 

The Pilgrims had to accept hard terms from the Virginia Company, in order to be sent to the New

World, for they could not bear the expense of the voyage and settlement. The youngest and strongest of the Pilgrims volunteered to make the trip and make ready for the older and weaker ones to come later. From Holland these volunteers went to England, whence they sailed on the Mayflower in the late summer of 1620.

In the meantime, the Pilgrims learned a valuable lesson. They learned that to own property together was a source of trouble and bickering. Always there were those who would not do their share of the work. The women were unhappy because they had to cook and wash for men other than their own husbands, brothers, fathers, and sons. Then the men worked hard for their own families, and the wives and children went into the fields to help their husbands and fathers. 

From small beginnings the Pilgrims accomplished great things. 

As Governor Bradford said, they were "as one small candle" that lighted "a thousand, a light that shone on many." In the time of their greatest distress, word came from England: "Let it not be grievous to you that you have been instruments to break the ice for others. The honor shall be yours to the world's end." This is the great contribution of the 

Pilgrims; they broke the ice for a larger colony that has left its impress upon our country to this day.

The Puritans: In England, where many of the Pilgrims had lived before they went to Holland, a group of Puritans began to talk of what the Pilgrims had accomplished in the New World, and of the possibility of a Puritan colony there. You must remember that these Puritans did not wish to separate from the Church of England, as the Pilgrims had done, but only wished to reform it. They dreamed of setting up in America, a church founded on the teachings of the Old Testament. 

In England these folk were suffering from unemployment as well as persecution for their religious beliefs. Times were so har that "many thousands . [had] soulde all they [had] even to theyr bedd straw, and [could] not get worke to earne any munny." Even "dogge's flesh" was "a dainty dish." It was in 1627 that the

Puritans began to write to other Puritans and to raise the question of going to America.

A few years earlier, a group of merchants called the "Dorchester 

Adventurers" had established a fishing station at Cape Ann, on the present site of Gloucester,

Massachusetts. It had proved unsuccessful and most of the party had returned to England; but 

Roger Conant and three of his companions chose to stay in New

England and hold Naumkeag (later called Salem) as a refuge for people who might be driven out of England on account of their religious beliefs; they saw in the country about Massachusetts Bay a future stronghold of Puritanism. As the matter continued to be discussed, a group of Puritan gentlemen of wealth came to a decision to “offer the help of their purses" to send a selected group of the best Puritans to New England to set up a colony. John Endicott was chosen as “a fit instrument to begin this wilderness work." Upon this stern but good-hearted Puritan rested the task of laying the foundation of a great settlement.

A grant was procured which

gave to John Endicott and others a strip of land from "sea to sea, extending from three miles north of the Merrimac River to three miles south of the Charles River. 

At that time, neither the King nor the colonists had the slightest idea of the great distance between the oceans. In the summer of 1628, Endicott with his family and a few others sailed for New England, arriving there in September to set up a government over a population of about sixty people. After Endicott had sailed, the King confirmed the land grant and agreed to let the Puritans set up their own church. The Dutch were already trading on the Connecticut River, and might colonize along the New England coast. The King saw the wisdom of planting English colonies in order to check the Dutch. Then, too, in allowing the Puritans to leave England, he was ridding himself of trouble-makers.

The journey of the Puritans was carefully thought out, even before 

Endicott left England. They had able, well-educated leaders of modest wealth. They were clever enough to get a charter that allowed them to set up a church and government to suit themselves, without interference from men in the mother country. They left 

England with plenty of cows, horses, and goats, and money enough to buy what they needed for their new homes. The Puritans had observed the mistakes of the

Pilgrims and had profited thereby. 

It was easy to find people who wanted to go to America. In

England there was great political unrest, severe unemployment, and intolerable religious persecution. 

Many of those who went along were not Puritans, but they were willing to submit to Puritan leadership in order to improve their fortunes. So many people left England in 1630 that it was known as the year of the "Great Migration" (mi-gra'shun). Eleven ships. left England in the spring, and by late summer a thousand people had joined Endicott and his handful of settlers. This was the largest group that had ever left England, and it was notable because of the great number of them who were men of wealth and influence. 1 The leader of this group was 

John Winthrop. Because of his religion, he had lost his position as court lawyer, and could see little hope of gaining an important place in the English government. 

While Winthrop had a large income, it was not enough to keep his family in the style that was expected of a man of his class in 

England. John Winthrop was a deeply religious man. It was his dearest hope to found a great Puritan state which would be a defense against the Roman Catholic religion that was being carried into

As shipload after shipload of people landed at the fever-stricken towns of Salem and Charleston, they were dismayed at the prospect before them. Ladies and gentlemen, still dressed in velvets and silks and laces, were faced with hunger and lack of shelter, as were the lowliest of the immigrants. So great was the expense facing the men of wealth, that they released their servants, bidding them find fertile soil and make their own homes. 

By some mistake, Winthrop had come to Salem with corn enough to last only two weeks. This situation he met promptly by sending one of the ships back to the nearest port for food, Even the leaders had to live on acorns, mussels, clams, or whatever they could find to sustain life. For lack of houses, many were forced to live through a severe winter in tents and other rude shelters. Naturally, many of the people died from lack of food and proper shelter.

In order to produce more food, the colonists began to leave Salem in search of richer soil. Within a year, eight new settlements had been founded, some of which were the beginnings of towns. After the first year, the colony began to prosper. In 1633, others crossed the ocean to join the Puritan colony, and by 1643 the Massachusetts Bay Colony had a population of fifteen thousand people; probably as many as were in all the other English colonies put together. Because of the bitter persecution in England, the Puritans had come over in great numbers, but when conditions became more favorable in the mother country, they stopped coming to this country.

After 1641, few immigrants came to the colony. Supplies now became scarce and prices were high. 

Many went back to England leaving their debts unpaid. As yet, the colony could not produce enough surplus products to exchange for needed goods from England. Some of the settlers became discouraged and went to the West Indies, some to Dutch Long Island, some to other English colonies. Among the true Puritans, however, few left 

Massachusetts, for a loyal Puritan was willing to undergo great hardship for the sake of independence in government and religion. He was not willing to live among those who did not share his religious faith or who denied the principles which he believed necessary for good government.

CHAPTER II 

The Puritan and His Government The Puritans' main purpose was to found a Bible commonwealth or state. They believed that they were God's chosen people who were to go out into the wilderness, like the children of Israel, and set up a church that would stand as a shining light for all the world to see. In order to keep their church powerful and strong, they must have a powerful and strong government to protect it. They adopted the form of government which the Pilgrims had established at Plymouth.

A congregation of a Puritan church was the unit of government. 

While the town was subject to a board that had power to rule the whole colony, it managed its own local affairs so well that it could very well have existed without a higher government. Each town had a justice of the peace from the governing board, but its inhabitants insisted upon managing their own affairs in the town meeting by the vote of all male inhabitants. 

Rarely was a woman allowed to vote on any issue. The men of the town elected their own officers, including a board of seven men selected from the most prominent families in the community. These

There had been nothing like it in England, and this was one of the greatest and most important gifts that the 

Puritans gave to America.

Now we shall see how this spirit of independence in the town led to a great struggle between classes of society. In a certain sense it foreshadowed the struggle between the colonies and the English king that brought about the American Revolution. This spirit of independence was not a gift peculiar to the Puritans alone, but they possessed it in such a marked degree that they played a very important part in the movement that made us an independent nation.

At first the colony was governed by about a dozen freemen, including the governor, the deputy governor, and eight assistants. These assistants were magistrates or justices of the peace; one for each town. The governing board had the power to "correct, govern, punish, pardon, and rule" all the people that lived within the territory of the Massachusetts Bay colony.

They were not impressed by the fact that these twelve men were members of the company that furnished the money to found the colony. The General Court held its first session in October, 1630, not long after the coming of the first sizable group of settlers with John Winthrop. The settlers presented to the court a petition to allow 118 of their number to become freemen with the right to vote.

Governor Winthrop and his associates wanted to keep the power to govern in the hands of a few of the most loyal Puritans.¹ The governor firmly believed in the theory that the many should be governed by the few, and by only the best of the few, at that. The 

Puritans had come to this new land to establish a Puritan state. 

If men of lukewarm faith were allowed to take part in the government, it would be hard to agree on policy, and there would be too much quarreling and bickering.

The governor was in a hard position. He did not want to give the vote to these 118 men, but, if he refused, they might leave the colony.

From time to time, as their number increased, freemen were added to the list, but still only a few belonging to the upper and middle classes could vote. In 1632 the first important protest against the governor's policy was made by the people of Watertown, led by their minister, George Phillips. The

Watertown minister was an outand-out Independent like the Pilgrims. He shocked the Massachusetts Bay Puritans by saying that even the Catholics had a right to their beliefs. This brave gentleman caused the members of his church to protest against the power of the magistrates to lay taxes; he said that those who pay the taxes should levy them. Governor Winthrop did not like this idea,

and refused the Watertown petition; but there was so much grumbling that he did grant the freemen the right to choose the governor and deputy-governor. However, he set the Watertown representatives right in such a stern manner that they were very glad to go home and let the matter rest for awhile. The governor was angry.

He thought that the power of the freemen must surely stop at this point-they must be allowed to do no more than choose the governing board. 

Governor Winthrop found life full of worry.

His people were always wanting something, buzzing around like angry hornets, stinging him with demands. It was not long before he had another grave situation to face. By 1634, those who opposed the governing board were so well organized that two representatives from each town went to Boston and asked to see the charter, which the governor had all this time kept locked up in a chest. Because he could give no reason why they should not see the charter, he was forced to lay it before them. With of triumph, the representatives showed the governor that the charter read that the General Court, not the governor and assistants, had the power to make the laws. To which the governor made answer that it was not practical for so many to have a part in lawmaking; they could never reach a sense an agreement. Anyway, the colony was not yet ready for such an arrangement; it was too young to have so much power among the people, the governor said. 

In spite of Winthrop's rather stern attitude, the trend toward popular control of the government remained strong. When the General Court met a few weeks later for the annual election of officers, it was ruled that hereafter each town could send two deputies or representatives to all meetings of the Court, except the one in which the officers were chosen. However, the matter was not yet settled. In 1636, the method of voting again came before the General Court, at which time it was decided that hereafter all acts of the General Court must be approved by a majority of the assistants as well as a majority of the deputies. This reminds us that our Congress today cannot pass a bill without the majority vote of both the upper and lower houses of Congress. 

Eight years later (1644) another important step was taken toward lessening the power of the upper class. The General Court was divided into two houses. The governor and the assistants constituted the upper house, like our Senate; the deputies made up the lower house, like our House of Representatives.

It is amusing to note that this important act came about over a very trifling incident. As Governor 

Winthrop said, "There fell out a great business upon a very small occasion." There had been much conflict between the poor and the rich on account of the stray animals of the poor getting into the gar-

dens and orchards of the rich. 

About two years before the General Court was divided, a poor woman had lost a sow. She sued a very wealthy, close-fisted shopkeeper of Boston, accusing him of stealing the animal; he probably kept the pig for damages done to his garden. Everybody liked the woman, and few liked the merchant. The sympathy of the people for the poor woman made the case clearly one of the humble class against the wealthy class. There was so much excitement about it that a meeting was called by the magistrates to consider the question, which was attended by nearly all the preachers and many of the deputies of the colony; however, after all the excitement the case was settled out of court. 

After so much talk and turmoil, the General Court ruled that hereafter (after 1636) the assistants and deputies should sit separately. 

Like our Congress today, either house might introduce a bill, but the bill could not pass without the assent of a majority of both houses. 

It had always been the custom for the governor to preside over the General Court. Now that the 

Court was divided into two houses, the governor presided over the upper house or board of assistants. 

The deputies elected for their presiding officer a Speaker. Our 

House of Representatives today elects a Speaker at their first meeting after the elections to preside over their meetings for two years.

Our upper house or Senate is presided over by the Vice-President. 

Once a year a Court of Elections was called to elect the governor, the deputy-governor, and the assistants. The voting was at first done by a show of hands, but it was not long before the votes were written on slips of paper, a method of secret voting which is called "voting by ballot." The first time this was ever used in this country was in 1630, when the Puritans selected a preacher and a teacher soon after their arrival at Salem. At the Court of Elections all freemen from all the towns were expected to attend in person. 

This, however, soon proved a hardship, for it was not only hard for men of the frontier towns to travel to Boston, but it left their families. unprotected from Indians while they were away. In 1636, the

Court voted that a man could cast his vote by proxy, that is, he could allow another man to cast his vote for him. A little later this right was extended to all towns, but this had its drawbacks; so the Court devised a way by which the freemen could vote on a list of candidates, which they had helped to select, in their own towns. After the votes were cast, the freemen sent their votes by their own deputies to Boston, where the votes were counted by the General Court.

This led to great confusion, for different justices made different rulings. Nobody really knew what was the law. A demand then arose for the laws to be written down, in order that the people should know what they should or should not do, and that the judges would be bound to make their decisions according to the written law. To Nathaniel Ward of Ipswich, Massachusetts, we owe the first code of laws in New England. 

It was called the Body of Liberties, which was a more attractive name than "Body of Laws," for instance. 

It is always so much more pleasant to be told about the things one may do, than the things one may not do. Gradually, this code was enlarged, and seven years later it was published under the name of The Law and Liberties of Massachusetts. These laws were based on 

English common law, but the ishment provided for wrong-doing showed the influence of the Old 

Testament law of Moses. pun- We have seen how a great change in government came about within a few short years. The small ruling class had not meant to oppress the lower classes; but they had tried to have everyone think the same way, in order to keep their church strong and united. 

They had come to America to get away from the tyranny of the

Church of England, but, curiously enough, after they got here, these same leaders became just as unreasonable as the men they had run away from in England. Many of the settlers were good Puritans, but they had wills of their own. 

When they had set up the colony, the land had been divided among the settlers according to their needs. They lived in small towns close together, and shared pastureland and woodland together. This gave the settlers a sense of equality, and a feeling that they had a stake in the government. Being home-owners made them want to help make the laws, especially the laws about taxation and the money to run the government. This insistence upon everybody believing the same thing, and the unwillingness of the ruling class to grant more power to the lower classes, had a bad effect upon the colony. 

Some of the settlers left Massachusetts to found new colonies; some became disgusted and went back to England; some good Puritans coming out from England would not settle in Massachusetts, because of the narrow views of the governing board. 

The church and the state were so closely tied together that a person who held a different religious belief could be accused of disloyalty to the state. It was because of a difference in religious views that Thomas Hooker founded

a settlement on the Connecticut River, beginning the state of Connecticut. Hooker favored a more liberal form of government and less strict rules for church membership. He thought that by keeping the rules for church membership so strict, too many good people were kept out of the church. 

Roger Williams:¹ As early as 

1631, Roger Williams, a scholarly and likable Puritan, joined Winthrop's colony and went to Salem to preach. Williams was a man whose ideas were too advanced for his time. He could never rest until he told everybody about the latest pain in his ever-aching conscience. He had a sweet and loving spirit, but his ideas shocked the leaders of the Puritan communities. Like the Pilgrims, he was an out-and-out Separatist.

He at once began to preach against Winthrop and the ministers because they did not separate themselves officially from the Church of England.

Another disquieting idea advanced by him was that the land did not belong to the Puritans. 

It really belonged to the Indians, he said, for the King had no power to grant the Indians' land to anybody. Now the Puritans had been very careful to pay any Indian claim against any land upon which

Williams said that the justices of the peace had no rightful power to punish lying, swearing, or Sabbath-breaking. That it was wrong for a church member to administer an oath to a person who did not belong to the church was another unpopular idea brought forward by Williams. The governor and most of the people were horrified. 

They liked Williams personally, but they were convinced that his ideas were dangerous to their government. 

He was tried for heresy and banished from the colony. Williams had a few followers who went with him to found a colony in Rhode Island.?

Baptists and Quakers: The 

Puritan state won a victory, but it was a costly one. Both church and government had suffered a grievous wound. Soon a new trouble broke out. The Baptists began to come to Massachusetts. They, too, were driven out by hard words and harder laws. Again all was peaceful until 1656, when Quakers¹ began to come to Massachusetts to convert people to their faith.

The United Colonies of New 

England:¹ In 1643, the colonies of Massachusetts, Plymouth, New

Haven, and Connecticut formed a league called the United Colonies of New England, which existed for about forty years. Maine was not invited to join, because the Church of England had been established there. This league was formed for mutual protection against the Indians, the French, and the Dutch; to exclude persons whose religious ideas were considered dangerous; to Christianize the Indians; to settle disputes between the colonies who were members of the league; and to promote the common welfare of its members.

Even under the rule of a royal governor, the Puritans continued to press for the increase of the power of the assembly and for the decrease of the power of the governor. For several years the governors tried to get the assembly to vote them a stated salary, but they never succeeded. The assembly went right on voting the governor a "present" at the end of the year, or perhaps twice a year. If the governor had been agreeable, they voted him a larger present than if he had opposed them a great deal.

Crisis after crisis arose over the attempted enforcement of laws imposed by the British Parliament.

The colonists resented these laws and refused to obey them. They resented the laws because they hurt business and because they had had no part in making them. 

They stood for the principle that only the colony's own assembly, elected by the people, had the power to make laws or levy taxes. 

In 1772 a new question arose. 

England, in order to enforce certain revenue laws, decided to pay the salaries of the Massachusetts judges. This created great resentment among the people. If the judges were paid by the Crown, they reasoned, the judges would render decisions favorable to England, and that would be the end of justice. To avoid trouble,

Governor Thomas Hutchinson re fused to call an extra session of the assembly to consider what should be done about the judges. 

Samuel Adams then thought of a scheme by which the towns of

Each town appointed a standing committee, and as their business. was conducted by writing letters, they were called the "Committees. of Correspondence." This was the most important step taken toward the organization of the American 

Revolution before the Declaration of Independence. The Puritan idea of self-government was about to burst into bloom. The little seedling of liberty planted by the

Both the Pilgrims and the Puritans contributed to the Furitan

When the Pilgrims arrived at Plymouth, they already had an organized church. They had set up an independent church in 

Scrooby, England, even before their flight to Holland. In Holland they had had a church under the leadership of their kindly, sweetspirited pastor, John Robinson. 

Before they landed at Plymouth, they gathered in the cabin of the 

Mayflower and drew up that famous agreement known as the 

Mayflower Compact. This agree ment became the model for all the 

Puritan churches that followed.

Until the Pilgrim colony united with the Massachusetts Bay colony in 1691, the Mayflower Compact was the foundation of all Pilgrim government.

The Puritans, on the other hand, came to America with no definite plan of church organization in mind because they considered themselves still members of the Church of 

England. They disapproved of the

Pilgrims, because the Pilgrims had broken away from the mother church; but by the time the Puritans had reached American shores, they had lost a great deal of their feeling against the Pilgrims and soon sought their advice about forming a church. The Pilgrims, in a spirit of brotherhood and good will, showed the Puritans how they had founded their church upon the teachings of the New 

Testament. For everything the

Pilgrims did, they sought authority from the Bible.

In the first winter, a Pilgrim doctor was a guest of Governor

Endicott while he took care of the Puritans who had been stricken by a plague that proved fatal to many. During this time, Governor 

Endicott and the doctor thoroughly discussed everything concerned with the Pilgrim church.

In the spring, those of Endicott's party that yet lived formed an agreement between themselves as members of a church.

For a time they thought most of the Puritan population of England might have to come to America to live, but the Puritans in America wanted no one to join them. Their idea of religious liberty was that anyone who differed from them could leave the colony at will. In fact, such persons were urged to leave. Naturally, from time to time, trouble arose through those who had ideas. of their own. Such persons were expelled from the colony. They, in turn, went out and started new Puritan colonies with more liberal forms of government. Even in its intolerance, the Massachusetts Bay colony contributed to the upbuilding of a great nation.

About the time of Hooker's settlement at Hartford, a new Puritan colony was founded at New

They set up an independent colony

By the middle of the century the colonies of Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay, Connecticut, and

New Haven were firmly rooted in New England. Thus we see the 

Puritan church, in the interest of religious and political liberty, moving farther and farther out into the wilderness, establishing churches and towns. In 1638-39 the three Connecticut towns of Hartford, Wethersfield, and Windsor combined as the Connecticut colony and set up their own government, throwing off all connection with Massachusetts. Thomas

Hooker laid down in a sermon the same principles of free government that underlie the Constitution of the United States, written one hundred and forty years later.

As more and more Congregational churches were planted in the New World, the Puritans in England began to question the grow- ´ing independence of the American churches. This questioning and its consequent investigation led to the writing of an important church document called the Cambridge Platform. 

The churches of Plymouth, Massachusetts Bay, New Haven, and 

Connecticut met in a convention at Cambridge (1646) "to cleare up," by the aid of the Bible, the questioning of the Puritans in England. They wrote a Congregational Church constitution which the four colonies signed and called the Cambridge Platform. While the churches continued to run their own affairs, there was a new fellowship between them. They began to work together for the common wel fare of all. The Cambridge Plat form was to religion what the New England Confederacy was to government.

At the head of the church was the minister, assisted by a teacher, who was really an assistant preacher. 

Elders and deacons made up the rest of the official board. By 1700, however, in many churches, the offices of teacher and elder disappeared, leaving only those of pastor and deacon. At first these officers were chosen by the men of the church, but later all the voters in the township helped to make the choice. All voters were taxed for the support of the church, whether they were church members or not. After the Cambridge Platform was adopted, ministers from neighboring Congregational churches were invited to assist when these officers were ordained. 

The Puritan Minister: Puri tan ministers were usually men of great learning and culture. Many

of them had carried off honors in the English universities and had served as clergymen in the fine churches of England. John Cotton and John Wilson, two of the first preachers in Boston, were both graduates of Cambridge University. John Cotton had been vicar of St. Botolph's Church, which was considered "perhaps the stateliest parish church in England.” 

All this Cotton left to preach the Gospel in Boston within the mud walls of a church in a new world. 

Cotton was said to be a walking library, so much did he read and study. Samuel Skelton and Francis 

Higginson, the first ministers at

Salem, were Cambridge graduates and English clergymen of note.

By 1640, there were eighty ministers in New England, one preacher to three hundred people.

Most of them were men of some property, of good family connections, and of vast learning for their times. They could read the Old 

Testament in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek. They were familiar with the best in church literature from ancient times to their own.

The standard of living for the 

Puritan minister was no better than that of his people. He lived in a plain log house, which matched his meeting-house. As he went about the township serving his congregation, he rode horseback wearing the plainest clothes and a great three-cornered hat. Sometimes his wife went along, seated behind him on a pillion. She, too, wore only the simplest garments. 

As the people prospered, both they and their minister lived more comfortably and wore better clothes. 

By 1642, a Puritan wrote that: "The Lord hath been pleased to turn all the wigwams, huts and hovels... into faire, well-built houses, well furnished, many of them, with orchards filled with goodly fruit trees, and gardens with variety of flowers." preacher wore a full-skirted coat with ample cuffs, and knee-breeches with silver buckles at his knees. 

Silk stockings, well-brushed shoes with silver buckles, and a powdered wig added dignity and a measure of splendor to his costume.

The Because he was usually the most learned man in the community, the minister occupied a position of importance. His church looked upon him as a leader, a representative of God. If he represented 

God, he reasoned, then he must bear himself accordingly. In upholding his dignity, he sometimes became haughty and proud. He had the air of looking down from a mountain-top, as he indulgently offered words of wisdom to his followers. He was ever ready to give advice, but not even the boldest of his people dared to offer the parson a suggestion. Wherever he went, he was accorded the honor due to his office. Whenever he entered a room, everybody rose

to do him honor. The men bowed and the women curtseyed. All remained standing until the minister was seated. When the minister came to call, all stood while he gravely inquired after the health and welfare of each. 

At the very first meeting of the 

General Court in Massachusetts, the governing board ordered that houses should be built "with convenient speed," and that each minister should be paid one hundred fifty pounds from the township treasury. For a while the ministers were paid by voluntary gifts, just as our preachers are paid today; but the pastors had a hard time under that system. 

However, the minister was SO important to the town, that after a few years most towns raised his salary by taxation.

In addition to the annual salary, there was often provided a money settlement, in order that the minister's family might be supported if he should die in the service of the colony. Some preachers received each week all the silver donated by strangers; this was used as a fund with which to buy books for their libraries. It is hardly to be expected that the libraries grew very rapidly from such contributions. Often the minister was given two of the best town lots and was provided with pasture for his animals and wood for his fires. In the case of the wood donation, each church member carried his share of the wood to the parson's door.

The Meeting House: The Pur itan's place of worship was a community center. It was used for the town business as well as for the Lord's business. They called it a meeting-house, for here they came together to meet the Lord, they said. Cotton Mather, a Puritan minister of renown, once said that he could find nothing in the Scriptures to indicate that a place of worship should be called a church. He went on to remark that every New England town could say that they had "a modest and a handsome house for the worship of God, not set off with gaudy, pompous theatrical fineries, but suited unto the simplicity of 

Christian worship.”

The first houses of worship were log houses with thatched roofs. 

A plain desk was provided for the preacher and rough benches of split logs were placed on either side of the center-aisle for the people. Women sat on one side and men on the other. It was a curious thing that men and women lived and worked together, but when they came to church they worshipped apart. In the earliest days, the meeting-house was often surrounded by a wall of closelyset upright logs driven into the ground. A sentinel stood at the gate to watch for Indians, and woe to the man who tried to slip out before the service was over!

Within the house itself, the people stored their powder and valuables. 

The men carried their guns with them to church. Sometimes there was a platform built on top of the house for the convenience of the man who called the people together for worship or town-meeting. This he did by walking up and down the platform vigorously beating a drum. In some places this officer blew upon a conch shell or upon a trumpet. For such services he was paid from the town treasury.

After a few years the Puritans began to enlarge and improve their meeting-houses. The log walls were covered with boards and the roofs were covered with crudely-fashioned shingles. If the people could afford it, they might have a bell. The bell was hung in a tree, if the meeting-house had not yet risen to the dignity of a steeple. If there was a steeple to house the bell, it was called a "bell-free" or belfry. The bellrope hung down in the center of the center-aisle; the sexton stood exactly halfway between the front door and the pulpit. Sometimes a church had two steeples, one for the bell and one for a watchtower. From the watchtower a

sentinel watched for the approach of enemy Indians who might attack the town while the people were at church.

On three sides of the house there was a door, and usually there were two or three rows of windows.

The main door was placed in the center of one of the longer walls, the side-doors in the ends of the building. From the front door the center-aisle ran straight to the pulpit, which was set up high-a symbol, perhaps, that God's word should be handed down from on high. On three sides of the house, galleries were built, supported by great rough-hewn trees from the forest.

Over the pulpit was suspended a funnel-shaped sounding-board which looked something like a great candle-snuffer. It hung from the bare rafters by a slender rod of iron. Some of the earliest churches had dirt floors, but these were never common. After a while, pews began to be built. A pew for the minister's family was built first, and that was right in front under the pulpit. Now and then, the town gave permission to individuals to build pews for their families. Squares of floor-space, about six by six feet, were sold to become the property of the family from generation to generation. At first, each person built his pew to suit himself; but before long, the pews were required to be built with "wainscot worke, and all of a kinde." Within these high-walled pews were narrow seats, which were fastened to the walls with hinges, allowing the seats to be pushed up while the worshippers stood to pray. When the congregation sat down to hear the Scripture-reading, there was so much noise and slamming of seats that rules were made requiring the seats to be let down quietly. Small boys, of course, found much fun in the squeak and clamor of rusty hinges and wooden seats. 

There is an old saying that a man's "standing" in the Puritan community could be told by his "sitting" in church. A committee arranged the seating of all those who attended the services. This was called "dignifying the seats," because the seats were set aside according to the dignity of those who occupied them. One dared not sit in any seat other than his own, for that would have been a grave offense. Small boys were considered so troublesome that they were sometimes herded into a pew by themselves. In some churches they were made to sit upon the pulpit stairs with a special deacon to keep an eye upon their movements.

It was usually the duty of the tithing-man to keep order, but that official could not suppress all the wriggling of the young Puritans. even The meeting-house was never lighted except by the sun, for night meetings were considered

improper. As for heat, it was unthought of; Puritans had always worshipped in unheated churches in England. The women took footstoves to church; these were little tin boxes filled with coals. 

Instead of using footstoves, some ladies thrust their feet into fur bags made of wolf-skins. Men scorned such womanish gadgets, and kept warm as best they could in their heavy boots and manycaped greatcoats. Babies only a few days old were brought into these cold houses for baptism; often the ice had to be broken in the baptismal bowl. In the winter, when the Lord's Supper was celebrated, the bread was sometimes frozen so hard that it rattled in the plates. In 1783, the first stove was put into the Old South Church at Boston. The The Puritan Sabbath: 

Sabbath began at sunset on Saturday. The next morning at nine o'clock the service began at the meeting-house. Promptly the preacher arrived with his family in stately procession, his wife on his right, perhaps a negro servant on his left, the children bringing up the rear. As he entered the building, everybody rose and stood until he took his place in the pulpit, the men bowing and the women curtseying. Facing the congregation, on a low platform, sat the deacons; on a platform a little higher sat the ruling elders. 

Churchmen were very jealous of their rank and expected recognition of that rank. This idea they had brought with them from the Old World. It took a long time for it to disappear. To some extent, we are yet bound by it. 

This is the way the service was conducted. First, there was the reading of any requests or notices from the congregation. Next, the preacher prayed very solemnly for fifteen minutes. Then the teacher read a chapter in the Bible and thoroughly explained it as he went along. Reading without explaining the Scripture was called "dumb reading," and was considered wrong because it smacked of the ritual of the Catholic Church. 

The Scripture-reading went on for some time, for it was considered an important part of the service. 

A psalm was then sung by the congregation. It was the right of one of the elders to choose the psalm and set the tune. Because there were few books, the elder "lined" the psalm. This he did by reading it line by line, the congregation singing each line after him. These early Puritans knew few tunes, and the elder who set the tune sometimes failed to hold them true to the chosen tune. 

One Sunday morning in the Old 

South Church at Boston, Judge 

Sewall set York tune, but the people in the gallery picked it up and carried it straight into the tune of St. David. At times there were almost as many tunes going on as there were people in the church; such a clamor did they make, each singing lustily his own tune. There was no organ and no written music. All tunes were learned by ear.¹

After the psalm came the pastor's sermon. This usually lasted an hour. An hour-glass stood on the pulpit, and when the last sands ran out, the sermon was supposed to come to an end. There was one old Puritan minister who turned the glass two or three times saying, "Let's have another glass." Long sermons made work for the tithingman, for he not only had to keep fidgety youngsters quiet, but he had to keep the older folk awake.

The sermons were very carefully prepared. In later times they were written out in the most correct English, and were often published. 

At the end of the sermon, the teacher made a shorter prayer, another psalm was sung, and everybody went out for lunch.

In summer those from a distance brought their lunches with them.

In a later time booths were set up near the church for the sale of food. There the housewives could gather to gossip and exchange quilt patterns, the men to talk of crops and everyday affairs. In winter they went home with friends in town for lunch, or went to the tavern where they could warm themselves by the blazing logs in the huge fireplace. Some churches built "Sabbath houses" near the meeting-house-one for men, one for the women-where roaring fires were built to thaw out the half-frozen worshippers. 

At two o'clock the afternoon service began. At this service the pastor read and "gave the sense' of the Scripture-lesson; the teacher preached the sermon. After the closing psalm the offering was taken. With whatever they chose to bring, the people, according to rank, solemnly marched from their seats to the deacon's table to deposit their gifts of money or goods, and as solemnly marched back to their seats. After the offering came the church business and reception of new members.

Then the singing of a psalm, followed by a prayer, and a benediction by the pastor, closed a day of worship.

In the middle of the week a lecture on the Bible was given. This was as popular as the movingpicture of today. Even the young folk took their notebooks to church. 

Questions and debate followed the lecture. So much excitement could be worked up over the lectures that work was neglected to attend lectures in neighboring communities. The Puritan discussed the fine points of the last lecture at home or abroad, just as people today discuss football and baseball scores.

The work of the preacher among his people consisted of visiting the sick, comforting the distressed, relieving the poor, admonishing the weak and sinful, and educating the young in the Catechism and the Bible. In the latter duty he was assisted somewhat by the tithingman, who had under his charge ten families. It was the duty of the tithing-man to see that all went to church and to make sure that the children knew their catechism. There were no Sunday 

Schools in those days. The regular teaching of religious truth to the young was no small part of the 

Puritan minister's work.

The greatest of all workers among the red men was John Eliot, the Apostle to the Indians. He came to Boston the next year after the coming of Governor Winthrop.

In fact, he came on the same ship that brought the wife and children of Governor Winthrop from Eng land. He studied the Indian language until he could say the Lord's

Prayer, the Ten Commandments, and some passages of Scripture in the Indian tongue. Then he began to preach to the savages. He taught them honesty, neatness, and how to work with their hands. 

He showed them how to raise food for themselves, how to market their surplus products; he taught the women to spin and make clothing for their families. He taught them how to read and how to conduct religious services. 

The Indians who became Christians were called "praying Indians."

A Brief Summary of the Contributions of the Puritan Church: The gifts of the Puritan 

Church to the nation may be sum marized as follows: (1) The Church influenced the form of civil government. (2) It perpetuated the congregational form of church government, which the Puritans took from the Pilgrims; it lives today in the Congregational churches of the nation. (3) The Puritan religion held up a high standard of conduct. (4) The serious study of the Bible gave to the people the ability to speak and write pure and effective English. The Bible was quoted in trade and business, in the home and in the school; but more important was the Puritan ideal of giving the Bible to rich and poor, high and low, regardless of social classes. CHAPTER IV 

Harvard College Established: 

When the Puritans came to the 

New World, they brought with them a goodly number of educated people. All the ministers were college-trained men, who used their own minds to form opinions about the Bible. Puritan life itself was an education, with its great attention to the sermon and the lecture, and its frequent religious arguments. As soon as the more pressing business of getting settled in a new country was attended to, the Puritans realized that they must be training young men to take the places of the ministers who were growing old. 

In 1663, the General Court of 

Massachusetts voted a sum of money to establish a college at 

Newtown. Because so many of the clergy were graduates of the university at Cambridge, England, the town's name was changed to

Cambridge. The college was named for John Harvard, a young minister at Charlestown, who at his death gave half his fortune and his entire library of some three hundred books to the school. It is thought that Harvard's gift was the real foundation of the college, for it is doubtful if the General Court's contribution was ever paid. Added to Harvard's gift were donations from rich and poor from all the 

Puritan colonies of New England.

At one time each family in each colony gave to the support of Harvard College at least one peck of corn or something equal in value. 

This new school was housed "in a fair and comely edifice, having in it a spacious hall and a large hall with some books in it." In those days, boys went to college at a youthful age- -some as young as eleven years. The standards for admission were very high. To enter Harvard, a boy must be able to read Latin and Greek, and to speak readily in Latin in both poetry and prose. They were given their seats and arranged in their classes according to social position, just as their parents were seated in church. In the next century a boy who was to become the second President of the United

States was ranked fourteenth in a class of twenty-four, simply because he was not a rich man's son. 

The Founding of Yale: Har vard was the only Puritan college until 1701 when Yale was founded in Connecticut. Yale was really started in 1700 when ten Puritan ministers met to talk about founding a school nearer home for higher learning. Then, too, Harvard was beginning to teach liberal ideas; at least so these ministers thought. (29)

They gave forty books from their own libraries to establish Yale College. The school opened with eight students, and for seventeen years had a restless career. There was so much jealousy between the towns that it was conducted first in one town and then another. 

New Haven was finally decided upon, and there it is today. The college was named for a wealthy New Englander, Elihu Yale, who gave a large sum of money toward its support. 

Why the Puritans Could

Contribute to Intellectual Life: 

There were several reasons why the Puritans were able to make a worth-while gift to the intellectual life of the New World. Because of their peculiar way of dividing land, they could easily appropriate it for schools. Because they always thought first of the good of the group, they were willing to contribute to the support of the schools. Because they thought that each man should read the Bible for himself, they believed in education for all. They believed that only highly-educated men were fit to be ministers. The Puritans came from good middle-class English stock, having good minds and the ambition to use them. Their type of government with the townmeeting as the unit called for men who could read the Scriptures and the law. Living in closely-settled communities made their minds more active; they met so frequently in town meeting and church meeting to reach decisions by argument and reasoning. Boston became a great center of learning because so many highly-trained clergymen lived there and in nearby towns. 

By 1647 there were 130 ministers in the Puritan settlements, one to each forty families.

The Public School: To the 

Puritans we owe our school-system whereby each child may go to school free of charge. The rich and the poor have the same opportunity to get an education in schools maintained by taxes paid by property-holders. Our colleges that owe their existence to grants of land by the government are simply an outgrowth of the Puritan idea that the community is responsible for the education of all the young.

Between 1630 and 1645 a number of Puritan towns encouraged the founding of schools. All parents were supposed to see that their children were taught to read and write. However, by 1642 some families were so careless about this that the General Court ordered that each town should be divided into sections, each section to be under the care of a selectman.

It was the duty of the selectman to see that each child could read, understand the principles of religion, and the laws of the colony, and that each child was

put to some kind of useful work. 

Five years later, a law which has been called "the mother of all our school laws" was passed. Because it was the "chiefe project" of that "old deluder," Satan, "to keepe men from the knowledge of ye Scriptures," that same old deluder had to be outwitted by making it possible for all to read the Bible. This law provided that: towns of fifty families should appoint a teacher to teach all who would go to him to read and write; towns of one hundred families should set up a grammar school with a master who could prepare boys for Harvard College. A grammar school was one in which both Latin and English taught. It rounded out the system of schools in Massachusetts, and the "bay horse" "set the pace" in education as in government. 

Any town neglecting to obey the new law was subject to a fine. 

The general effect of this ruling was to stimulate the opening of schools. There were a few towns, however, that disobeyed because it was cheaper to pay the fine than to maintain the school. were The salary of the early schoolmaster did not exceed sixty or seventy dollars a year. 

He was often paid in farm produce, such as corn, wheat, rye, barley or peas. Each town ran its school or schools to suit itself. The town officers appointed the teacher, decided on how much they would spend on the school, and how they would raise the money. The expenses were met partly by tuition and partly by taxation. All 

Indian children received free instruction. Parents who could afford it, were supposed to pay tuition, but those who could not pay pay were allowed to send their children without fees of any kind. 

This method led to a certain amount of trouble. It was not long before all "scholars" were allowed "to go free." 

The schoolhouse in Dedham, Massachusetts (1649) was typical of the New England common school. This was a log house eighteen feet long and fifteen feet wide, with a lean-to on the back for a watchhouse. A window in either side, and one in the loft or "chamber" provided light. The lean-to, six feet wide, was set back of the chimney, and projected two and a half feet beyond the corner of the house on either side. It, too, had a fireplace in it. Four open windows allowed the sentinel to see from four directions, as he watched during the night for any possible enemy. In extremely cold weather the master held school in his own house; in summer when it was extremely hot, he was allowed to conduct his school in the meeting-house, if he would keep it clean and mend all the windows that the boys broke. 

The schoolhouse usually stood near the meeting-house in the center

of the town. Very often it suffered from lack of repairs. A schoolmaster of 1681 described his schoolhouse as in a "confused and shattered and nastie" condition; "the glass broke, and thereupon very raw and cold; the floor very much broken and torn up to kindle fires, the hearth spoiled, the seats some burned and others out of kilter, that one had well-nigh as goods keep school in a hog stie as in it." 

To warm the schoolroom, the parents were expected to bring firewood to the school, according to the number of their children in attendance. Some parents neglected this important duty and were fined. The children of such parents were often made to sit in the coldest part of the room; sometimes they were turned out of school. The only means of heating was a great fireplace which used up wood at a very rapid rate.

The school year in many towns began in October and ended in 

April. In the warm months, school began at seven in the morning and ended at five in the afternoon. 

During the short days of winter, school began at eight and closed at four. There was a noontime intermission from eleven to one on every day except Monday. On that day, from twelve to one, the pupils were examined on what they had learned the previous week; any who had misbehaved on the preceding Sabbath were punished. Every day at two o'clock the children were drilled in the catechism of the church. Every morning and evening the teacher said a prayer to which all were supposed to listen with proper reverence.

Children were instructed in good literature, good manners, and how to behave dutifully toward their superiors. The teacher had full power to punish any scholar in any way that he saw fit, and no parent could forbid it. "The rod of correction" was "a rule of God sometimes necessary to be used upon children." However, if any relative of a child, who was punished, thought that the master had been too severe, that relative might tell the master so in a "friendly and loving way."

Great emphasis was laid upon religious instruction. The child lived constantly amid set religious forms and services; he spoke and heard a language filled with religious terms. Children of three and four years were expected to know the answers to such terrifying and somber questions as, "What must become of you if you are wicked?" 

Parrotlike he must answer, “If I am wicked, I shall be sent down to everlasting Fire in Hell among wicked and miserable creatures."

Children were brought up on a heavy mental diet. Boys entered 

Latin or grammar school at the age of seven or eight years, having learned to read Latin by that time.

Textbooks were few and hard to get. The average schoolboy had a Bible or Testament, a psalter, and a catechism, or primer. Latin scholars, of course, had their Latin books from England. The only 

American textbook used before the American Revolution was

Latin grammar written by Ezekiel Cheever who taught Latin for seventy years in the New England schools. The text-books were designed to be both entertaining and instructive, but succeeded in being only dull and dry-something like an encyclopedia, or an illustrated dictionary. 

In college the studies were those suited for the training of ministers. 

Not much attention was given to mathematics and science. Hebrew was considered more important than arithmetic. One old Puritan, however, said that all he wanted his boy to know was "the Bible and figgers." 

The earliest spelling book was

Coote's The English Schoolmaster, first published in 1596. This was very popular for a century. Besides spelling, it contained prayers, psalms, a short catechism, a smattering of arithmetic and history, and some writing lessons.

The minister had a great deal to do with the town schools. 

Since he was employed to instruct the children, as well as the grownups in religion, he made frequent visits to the school to examine the pupils in the Bible and the Catechism, or on the sermon of the preceding Sunday. Sometimes a country preacher acted as a teacher, boarding the students in his home. In towns that had no grammar school he taught Latin to those who desired it. 

The "dame school" was a school for both little boys and girls. 

There was always some widow or spinster in the town who for a small sum would teach the children the alphabet and a little reading and spelling. Its chief value seemed to be that it relieved the mothers of the care of their children for a certain number of hours each day. 

All the time the dame was hearing the lessons she was busily knitting, sewing, or spinning. She seems to have listened more than she taught.

Eighteenth Century Colonial 

Schools: When the savages had been subdued, the people began to live farther apart. Some pushed out into the wilderness and lived on widely-scattered farms. This made a new problem in schooling. 

To meet it, the "moving-school" was devised. The school was conducted in one neighborhood for one part of the year, and moved on to some other neighborhood for another part of the year. This idea gradually expanded into the district school. The districts began to build schoolhouses, and by 1750 the schools ceased to be town schools; they became rural schools operated by their own officers.

In these early schools there were no blackboards, no maps. A globe was rare. There were no slates and no lead pencils. Sometimes a piece of lead was fitted into a goosequill, but that made a poor pencil. 

Goose-quill pens and red or black home-made ink were used on rough dark paper.

Copy-books were lined by the students and the teacher set the copy. It took a great part of the teacher's time to make and repair the pens for the school. The arithmetic books, called "cyphering books," were made with pen and ink on folded foolscap paper by the pupils as the master gave out the problems. 

Most of the number work was done on birch bark, smooth shingles, or scraps of waste paper, because paper was very scarce.

The handwriting of these children was usually very good. Every school-teacher had to be a good penman and a good instructor in that art. The spelling of words was not considered so important as the shaping of the letters. 

Sometimes the teacher set a copy very poorly spelled. It would have been hard to conduct a spelling-bee in those days, for few people spelled alike. 

The schoolmaster of the better class wore a three-cornered hat over a gray wig that fell in curls over his shoulders. His long, fullskirted coat hung down to the calves of his legs which were encased in white silk stockings to meet his "smallclothes of nankeen." The sleeves and skirts of his coat had silver buttons on deep cuffs and wide pocket-flaps. He wore a silken waistcoat of some bright hue and ruffles at his hands. 

A snow-white linen stock lent dignity to his appearance. Silver buckles on his shoes and at his knees added elegance to his costume. 

A pair of tortoise-shell spectacles sat far down upon his nose, over which he peered at his young charges. When he went out into the street, he carried a gold-headed walking-stick. 

For the punishment of naughty children, the master used a ruler or a light rattan cane; sometimes a rod of birch. Sometimes, a cowhide or cat-o'-nine-tails was used.

Even young men in college were whipped before their fellows when they disobeyed the rules. Some teachers had ways that were less severe on the body but hard on the pupil's pride. One teacher had a way of splitting the end of a switch and fitting it to a boy's nose, forcing him to wear it until he decided to behave himself. 

Another teacher punished an unruly pupil by making him wear a stick stuck into the back part of his collar while standing before the school for all to see. dunce cap, great leather spectacles, and placards were muchused forms of punishment.

The boy was an idler, he was made to wear a label marked "Idle Boy;" if he lied, he had to wear a sign marked "Lying Ananias.”

In the Puritan schools the New 

England Primer was second only to the Bible as a reading book. a reading book.

It was first published about 1690. 

It was one of the most important books in early New England culture, because it was the only school book used for children for half a century. It was used until 1830 when more than five million copies had been sold. The covers of this old book were made of thin sheets of wood, over which was pasted a coarse blue paper. 

Before the Revolution, the first picture in the book was a crude one of the then King of England. 

Another illustration that was supposed to have a good influence over the children was that of John 

Rogers, an Englishman in Queen 

Mary's time, being burned at the stake while his wife and ten children stood nearby watching him in his torment. A most solemn poem accompanied the woodcut, setting forth instructions of the dying man to his wife and children.

Most children were more interested in counting the children to see if they were all there, than they were in what the poem had to say. The general style of reading throughout the book was about the same as this poem, but because there was nothing better, it was very much liked. Cotton Mather called it a "little water pot," from which mothers should "continually drop something of the catechism on their children, as Honey from the Rock." 

Though the primer changed somewhat from time to time, the religious nature of the book remained. Its first lines, "In Adam's

The catechisms were considered the most important part of the primer. On certain Sundays the children were questioned on these catechisms by the minister. The primer was the chief tool by which morals were put into the minds of the children. Thus the "young vipers" were trained to become sober Christian men and women.

School must have been a dreary place with little fun; but out of all the solemn verses, stories, and proverbs came a strict sense of duty and high ideals. This, too, was an important gift of the Puritans.

Education of Women: The 

Puritan fathers were so busy educating boys that little was done for the girls until late in the 

Eighteenth Century. What a girl would know, she must get from her father or brother. Not until after the Revolution were girls admitted to the grammar school. 

In Boston in 1789 the first "double-

headed" school was founded. The girls were allowed to come at different hours from those for the boys. In warm weather, the girls attended school while the boys were at work in the fields. Boys went to the grammar schools to win honors, but girls were sent to learn only what "it was thought they needed."

In spite of this, some Puritan women did write some of the literature of their time. It speaks well for the men of their families that they were allowed freely to express themselves, for usually a woman who liked to study was laughed at; she was thought queer or forward. 

Mrs. Jane Turell, who died in 

1735, had read all the English poetry and prose in her father's well-furnished library before she was eighteen years old. Besides this, she borrowed all the books she could from her friends. She spent whole nights reading because the days were all too short for such pleasure. By the time she was sixteen, she was writing acceptable poetry. 

Mrs. Mary White Rowlandson, wife of the Lancaster minister, was captured by the Indians. For vivid descriptive writing, her story of the horrors of Indian warfare cannot be surpassed. 

Mercy Otis Warren and Abigail

Adams were noted for their letterwriting. They had no school education, but they belonged to families headed by highly educated men, who shared their learning with their women. Mercy Warren came to be considered a literary genius because of her plays and historical poems. Abigail Adams, as the wife of John Adams, second 

President of the United States, wrote American history into her letters. 

Anne Bradstreet and her young husband came to America in 1630 with a great body of Puritans. 

Anne's father became the governor of Massachusetts. Later her husband became the governor of that state. Anne absorbed learning from her father and husband. She is chiefly known for her book called The Tenth Muse Lately 

Sprung Up in America (1650). 

Into it she crowded four hundred pages of verse. 

Another remarkable woman of the Seventeenth Century was Madam Sarah Knight. Her father was a wealthy merchant who was arrested by his fellow-Puritans because he kissed his wife on Sunday, after he had been gone from home for three years. 

For such "unseemly" behavior he was made to sit in the stocks for two hours and think over that grave sin against society! 

Sarah Knight kept a dame school which was attended by two boys who became famous men-Doctor 

Samuel Mather and Benjamin 

Franklin. Madam Knight could manage a farm, run a tavern, or

teach a school. She held a high position in society and could risk being laughed at for using her wits.

Her most famous literary work is a journal which she kept while on a "perilous journey" from Boston to New York in 1704. 

Upon her return from this journey, Sarah Knight scratched with her diamond ring this verse upon a windowpane in her house:

Through many toils and many frights 

I have returned poor Sarah Knights

Over great rocks and many stones God has preserv'd from fractur'd bones. 

She had a keen sense of humor and a great gift for description. 

She recorded in a most amusing way the manners of the people she met, the hardships of travel, the customs of the time. Her diary is priceless as a mirror of life in the beginning of the Eighteenth

Century. 

These women have left us important writings which faithfully record the happenings of their times. Because they belonged to prominent families, they were enabled to educate themselves and contribute to the literature of their day. Society in general considered it indelicate for a woman's name to appear in print. Only progressive, forward-looking men would have allowed the writings of their women to be published. 

Puritan Leaders and Their

Contribution to Literature: The 

Puritans left behind them little in the way of stories or fiction.

They were so interested in religious questions that most of their writings were devoted to such subjects. 

However, these works have a sound historical value. William 

Bradford's Of Plimoth Colony is outstanding as a history of the Pilgrims. John Winthrop, the founder of the Massachusetts Bay colony and long its governor, kept a journal from the time he left England to come to America until his death in 1649. It is highly valued because it tells about the problems the Puritans faced in a new country and how they solved 

The writings of Thomas 

Hooker set forth much more liberal ideas than those of Winthrop. 

Hooker believed that the common people should decide things for 

Winthrop believed them. themselves. that a select few-men of wealth and education-should govern the colony, that it was the people's part only to obey. Roger Williams left writings which show that he believed in the equality of all men, regardless of class, color, or creedthat all are equal in God's sight. 

He was a seeker for a new and better way of life for all. 

Naturally, so many educated ministers living close together would do a great deal of writing. In fact, they seemed to run a race with each other to see who could get the most into print. This was serving the Lord, they thought.

The first book to come from a Puri-

tan printing press was the Bay Psalm Book, if we except the printing of a pamphlet called The 

Freeman's Oath. Some Puritan ministers put the original Hebrew psalms into English verse and introduced them in the church service in 1640. Among these translators were Richard Mather of Dorchester, President Dunster of Harvard, Thomas Weld, and 

John Eliot. All of these men were writers of good prose and were familiar with good poetry; but they wrote the psalms in verses as rough as the naked granite of the New England hills. They excused the lack of smoothness by saying, "God's altar needs not our pollishings."

The grand old minister of Puritanism was John Cotton. He it was, more than anyone else, who influenced the making of the laws for both church and civil government. 

What he said in the pulpit about local affairs was accepted as authority and put into law. He was "soul-melting" preacher who could bend people to his will. His sermons and his book called The 

Keyes to the Kingdom of Heaven are among his most important works.

The Mathers held sway over the colonists for three generations. 

The first, Richard Mather, was prominent in church affairs. His son Increase took a leading part in the witchcraft trials in Massachusetts in the Seventeenth Century. The most important book that Increase Mather wrote was

Remarkable Providences. 

It was filled with curious beliefs. Some say that it helped to make people ready to believe in witchcraft. 

The persecution of witches in

America lasted for only a short time, compared with the length of time it continued in England. 

Cotton Mather's principal work was the history of the Puritan Church of New England. It is a very important book today to those who want to know about the 

Puritan Church. Second in importance was his diary, which is filled with the every-day happenings of life in the Seventeenth Century. Less well known is his Wonders of the Invisible World, in which he set forth his belief that the Devil actually attacks the person who wants to be good. He once lost some sermon-notes, and always believed that the Devil stole them.

In spite of these absurd beliefs, Cotton Mather was a very learned gentleman.

Among these writing preachers, there were differences of opinion. 

Some were "stand-patters," who believed in the narrow creed of the old Puritans. Some were "New-Dealers," who believed in a more liberal creed. Among the "stand-patters" was John Davenport, who supported Governor 

Winthrop in his idea that the people should be ruled by only a few of the upper class. Then there

was Thomas Shepard, who wrote many books on religion. He could make the fires of hell so real that few could hear him preach, or read his works, without being frightened because of their sins.

Among the "New-Dealers" was 

John Wise. He was the first New 

England minister to break with the old Puritan Church. He led straight back to the church of the Pilgrims by teaching that the simple ways of the Congregational 

Church were the "safer way home."

He thought it better to travel a broad path of democracy than the narrow path of the old Puritanism. 

He started a movement back to independent thinking that was a forerunner of the movement that brought about our struggle to break away from England and become an independent nation. His writings on freedom in the New England churches were accepted as law for a century after they were written.

The most scholarly Puritan minister of the next generation was 

Thomas Prince, a graduate of Harvard in 1707. For forty years he was the pastor of the Old South Church of Boston. He built up a large library and wrote the Chrono logical History of New England. 

Prince laid down rules for writing history that are followed by the men and women who write history today. He said, "I would not take the least iota upon trust, if possible," and "I cite my vouchers to every passage." Not one thing would he write into the History of New England, unless he had positive proof of its truth.

A Puritan who was both minister and poet was Michael Wigglesworth. He put into his verses all the sulphur and brimstone of the old Puritan faith. In 224 verses of bad poetry he wrote his master piece, The Day of Doom. At the great day of Judgment, according to Wigglesworth, innocent babies, who had died at birth, would be brought before the Judgment 

Throne to answer for the guilt of Adam, who ate of the forbidden apple in the Garden of Eden; but because these babes were young, they would be given "the easiest room in Hell." His Day of Doom was a "best seller." It was the supreme poem of New England for a hundred years. It was memorized by young and old, because it contained the most important truths concerning the human soul; at least, so the the Puritans thought. 

Children were required to memorize it along with the catechism. 

The Day of Doom might well have been a book-of-the-century. 

Edward Johnson was a wealthy member of Winthrop's Company who wrote about the intellectual life of the Puritans. Nathaniel 

Ward wrote a brilliant and witty essay on Puritan life which he called The Simple Cobbler of Aggawam in America. He said he was willing to take all the honest

stitches he could to mend his country, which was tattered in both "upper-Leather and sole." You will remember that Nathaniel Ward wrote the Body of Liberties, the first code of laws in the Massachusetts Bay colony. His Simple 

Cobbler is regarded as a bit of literature worthy to be compared with some of the best writing of 

English authors of that time.

Judge Samuel Sewall is note worthy because of the diary that he wrote. When you read that delightful work you can see how the English Puritan became an 

American Yankee. Sewall was the typical Puritan church member who always drove a good bargain and gained riches, though he never broke a church rule. He had a puritanical sense of justice for which we must admire him. It was Sewall who tried to get the Massachusetts government not to list Negroes and Indians in the same class as hogs and horses. He wrote a little book against slavery, The Selling of Joseph, which made him very unpopular with his neighbors who owned Indian or Negro slaves. He opposed the death penalty for those who made counterfeit money, saying that "there is no proportion between twenty pieces of silver and liberty." He was brave enough to write a confession of shame and sorrow for his part in the persecution of witches. He not only wrote the confession, but stood in church before the congregation while the minister read it. 

The Eighteenth Century produced the two greatest writers of the Puritan era. These two men were totally unlike in character, habits, and thought. Both may be classed among the most learned and brilliant men of all time. They were Jonathan Edwards, preacher and writer, and Benjamin Franklin, writer, scientist, inventor, and statesman.

Edwards wrote the masterpiece of New England Puritanism-On the Freedom of the Will. It was a very learned work that only a few people could understand, written in a time when interest in such subjects was dying out. Benjamin Franklin, among other things, wrote his Autobiography and Poor 

Richard's Almanac. Both of these books were widely read. The 

Almanac was in nearly every home, because it was written about simple homely things, in language that the common people could understand. 

Jonathan Edwards lived a morally perfect life based on strict religious creed. He thought so much about the next world that he did not consider this one of much importance. Benjamin Franklin lived a good life and a useful one; he had a creed, but not one that a Puritan could approve. He made his own rule of life, and for that reason has been called "the ungodly Puritan." He devoted himself to doing

good in this world, trying to make life a little richer and easier for all. It has been said that Franklin was more concerned that the cobblestones in Philadelphia should be properly laid, than that he should some day walk the "golden pavements of the City of God.” 

Edwards looked back to the glory of the past; Franklin looked forward to a more glorious future for mankind.

Summary: We have briefly considered the intellectual contribution of the Puritans to American life. That contribution can hardly be estimated because it is so great. In democratic forms of education they led the other groups who came to the New World. It has often been said of the Puritans that where they found the ground too stony to raise corn that they built school-houses to raise men. The printing - press flourished among the Puritans. 

Pamphlets and books fairly rolled from the press. The Puritans expressed their opinions and left behind them a body of literature that is of great historical value. Their writings do not, however, possess great literary value; they lack the charm of expression and originality of thought that belongs to great literature. The Puritans were lovers and collectors of books.

There were books in Boston before there were decent houses to live in. 

Libraries were cherished and passed on from generation to generation. 

The Puritan curiosity of mind and independence of thought had a tremendous influence upon American history.

CHAPTER V  

The Puritans were a resourceful and busy people. By hard work, thrift, and daring, they were able to build a sound economic system by which all classes of their society profited. Everyone worked-master and servant, mistress and maid.

There was little real poverty. All had shelter, food, and clothing; all had work to do. The lowest had a chance to rise in the world, if only he were willing to work and save; to make the most of the opportunities that lay about him. 

Class differences slowly faded as industries developed. 

A great deal has been said of Puritan religion, government, and love of learning. No less important is the Puritan genius for business. In his peculiar economic system, based on free white labor, the Puritan made a contribution of the greatest consequence in American life. The Puritans came to this country with the idea of making their homes here, to make something out of a new land.

They developed a system of manufacturing and industry that fitted a frontier community. That system passed westward with each wave of settlements until, with few important changes, it reached the Mississippi River.

In any colony there were two kinds of business or industrial production. One kind was household manufacturing, the making of things by members of the family for home use. The other kind was the selling of surplus products for a profit. 

The second kind of production made little headway in the Seventeenth Century, but developed in the most amazing fashion in the 

Eighteenth Century. During the

Seventeenth Century, the colonists were busy making things for their own use. They had not yet acquired enough capital to undertake great business enterprises. They were laying the foundation for the future.

In the winter time, when the water was too rough for fishing and the weather was too cold for farming, the Puritans built boats. 

Because they had little money they built their boats on the simplest lines. In 1713 a new type of boat was built at Gloucester, Massachusetts, by Captain Andrew

Robinson. When the vessel slid into the water, someone cried out, "See how she scoons!" The captain instantly replied, "A scooner let her be!" From that time on that type of vessel has been called a schooner. It was a fore-and-aft rigged vessel with two masts, which were later increased to three, four, and five. It could lie nearer the wind than the old square-rigged vessels; it was more easily handled because it was not topheavy as the English ships were; it required a much smaller crew, which made it cheaper to operate. The schooner made an excellent boat for coastwise trade, because it could skim or "scoon" so lightly and swiftly over the water.

There was one kind of trading in which the Puritan engaged that in the Twentieth Century would be considered very wicked. That was the trade in "black ivory," or Negroes. The Negroes were brought from the coast of Guinea to be sold into slavery, the Puritans thinking no more of a load of 

Negroes than a load of horses or cattle. Again, one must judge them by the age in which they lived. One old Puritan merchant was known to offer prayer when his ship came in loaded with

The reason the Puritans used few slaves was that the blacks were not suited to the kind of labor done in New England.

The Puritans never seemed to realize that slavery was wrong.

Only a few saw the inhumanity of the practice, and fewer still had the courage to raise their voices against it. This dark traffic was a fateful gift to the New World, for it rent the country in twain and helped to bring on the War between the States.

The slave-trade was a triangular business. The Puritans bought molasses in the West Indies; they distilled the molasses into rum at home; they shipped the rum to the coast of Guinea and exchanged it for Negroes. On the way home, most of the Negroes were sold in the West Indies or exchanged for molasses. Then molasses, rum, Negroes; molasses, rum, Negroes around the triangle. Some merchants have been known to instruct the shipmaster to "worter [water ] ye rum." The Puritan conscience was a peculiar thing. In this case, the Yankee-Puritan probably justified the watering of the rum by thinking that weak rum would do less harm than strong rum, anyway. A Puritan would not willfully break a law, but he was an expert in figuring out ways to get around a law that stood between him and his heart's desire.

Lumbering: 

Closely related to shipbuilding was lumbering. 

Pine, cedar, spruce, and oak were sent to England or used in America for masts, spars, yards, bowsprits, and planks. Red and white oak were used for barrels and casks for wine, meat, fish, flour, sugar, molasses, and rum. lasses, and rum. Shingles, houseframes, and clapboards of cedar were in great demand. Coopers were regularly taken out on trading voyages to make barrels and casks to hold the molasses and sugar from the West Indies or the wine from the "Wine Islands." In 

New Hampshire and Maine there were logging camps where woodsmen felled the trees and hauled them overland to a sawmill or a harbor. The tallest and straightest white pines were marked with a broad arrow to show that they were to be used only for the King's ships. It took forty oxen to drag the largest of these mast trees to the sea.

Connected with the lumbering were the sawmills located at the falls of the rivers near the sea. 

The rushing water turned the water-wheel which in turn set the saw in motion. The first sawmill in Maine was built in 1623; the first in New Hampshire in 1635.

The first mills had only one saw, but that one water-driven saw could do the work of twenty men. 

It could saw a thousand feet of lumber in a day, earning a profit of five or ten dollars. By 1700,

many of these mills had four or more saws, it being cheaper to add more saws than to build more mills. By 1720, the New England sawmills had sawed up all the trees in a strip six miles wide near the coast from Boston to the Kennebec River. 

The little sawmill camps were the forerunners of the huge lumbering camps of the Old Northwest and the South. Knowledge of woodcraft, wasteful methods, and camp life were handed on until they have reached the Pacific 

Northwest today. Meanwhile, new inventions made the process of manufacturing lumber ever faster and more efficient.

The same water-wheel that drove a saw could be detached and put to driving the millstones of a gristmill. However, there were many little gristmills that had no sawmill attached to them. The gristmills ground the wheat, corn, and rye of the Puritans for their bread. 

Most of the Seventeenth Century mills could grind not more than ten or twenty barrels of flour or meal in a week. In slack times the miller ground malt for beer, or rags for the manufacture of paper. 

The miller took a part of the grain for his pay; he then sold his share "to the best advantage."

From the white pine of the New England forests were made pitch, tar, rosin, and turpentine. These products were in great demand in England for the repair and building of ships.

A by-product of land-clearing was the manufacture of pot- and pearl-ashes. When sawmills were not convenient, something must be done with the wood on the land to be cleared for farming. The logs were piled in great heaps and burned to ashes. The ashes were then boiled in water and strained. the strained liquid then boiled again and allowed to crystallize. 

These crystals were the vegetable salts of ashes, and in some pioneer communities were called "black salts." As late as the Nineteenth

Century, a pioneer newspaper of the West advertised to buy clean wood ashes at eight cents a bushel or "black salts" at four dollars a ton. Either cash or manufactured goods would be paid for the same. The Puritans could produce twenty-four tons of potash, for which they might be paid from forty to sixty pounds in English currency or trade. The English woolen mills used great quantities of potash in bleaching and scouring woolens. Pearl-ash was simply refined potash. Both pearl-ash and potash were used in soapmaking, and at a later date both used extensively in glasswere making. 

Iron: In the swamps and ponds of the Puritan colonies there were large deposits of iron. There was an abundance of wood to smelt

the iron ore.

In the Seventeenth Century the Puritans worked the iron for their own needs; importing iron from England, however, for their best ships. They made cast-iron for pots, kettles, and pans, and wrought-iron for edged tools. John Winthrop, junior, was the leading promoter of ironworks in New England, but he had more trouble than profit. The "undertakers" of the business quarreled so much that they "hammered out more lawsuits" than they "drew bars of iron for the country's use." 

Farming: While there was some very fertile soil in New England, farming was not the chief occupation of her people. There was a great deal of poor land, and for that reason the Puritans were forced to think of other ways to create wealth. Along the coast the farmers worked their little patches of ground with a hoe and fertilized them with waste fish to produce their own food. A threeacre corn patch would produce enough corn to keep a family one year. Even the rich merchant had his own vegetable garden and orchard.

Root crops were not raised in New England at first. Potatoes were imported from the West Indies. The story is told that when the first potatoes were planted in New England that the settlers cooked the seedballs and thought that potatoes were a poor food. 

Not until the next spring plowing did they discover the real potatoes under the ground. Potatoes were not used a great deal until the Irish came to New England in the 

Eighteenth Century; but even the 

Irish had gotten their potatoes originally from the West Indies.

The Puritans knew little about building up the soil. Cattle-droppings were used in a very inefficient way, and such a thing as rotation of crops was unheard of. After two or three crops of Indian corn, the soil was loose enough to grow wheat and other English grains; but corn was the main cereal because it ripened quickly, being ready to harvest at the end of the short New England summer. Hemp was raised for the manufacture of sailcloth and ropes, and flax was grown for linen cloth.

In Maine, flax was grown with the corn and grass crops. No cotton was produced in New England, but it was imported from the West Indies and woven into cotton cloth with a flaxen warp.

By the middle of the Eighteenth Century the soil of New England was becoming exhausted by constant cropping without the systematic use of fertilizers. Jared 

Eliot, a grandson of John Eliot, saw that the farmers were making a mistake in clearing the land too closely and by putting nothing back into the soil. Eliot travelled in Europe, even as far away as

Russia, to bring home the best methods of farming. He devoted his Yankee intelligence to the discovery of the secrets of Nature. 

He learned to feed the starved soil by spreading the black mud of swamps and fens upon its surface. 

Perhaps it was from Jared Eliot that George Washington got the idea of fertilizing his fields at

Mount Vernon with mud from the 

Potomac River. Eliot's greatest contribution to farming was the introduction of clover-sowing. It made the ground fertile again and provided a feed crop for livestock. 

Eliot brought a "horse hoe" from

England, which he drew with a yoke of oxen, spreading their yoke so that they bestrode one row of corn. This "horse hoe" must have been the forerunner of the horsedrawn plow.

The Puritans found it very hard to raise cattle when they first came to this country. They had no shelter for their animals and no fences. It was all they could do to build houses for themselves and provide for their own most pressing needs. As life settled down, the raising of livestock increased. There was plenty of hay in the salt marshes and good pasturage in the meadows. Within a few years, cattle-breeding became a highly profitable business. New settlers coming to the Puritan colonies preferred to buy their young cattle here; the risks of the sea voyage were too great. Then, too, a brisk trade was soon built up with the Southern colonies and the West Indies. At first, cattle were used for work animals, the milk being used only for home consumption. A little later, however, some shrewd Puritan had the idea of making butter and cheese for export. Beef tallow was used for candle-making; the hides were used for shoes and wearing apparel; the meat was used for food. 

Goats were more common than sheep, for they were hardier and gave milk, besides. The underbrush spoiled the sheep fleeces, making sheep-raising less profitable than goat-raising. No settler was considered a substantial citizen if he did not have a flock of goats.

The breeding of fine horses for export, especially saddle horses, became an important form of trade. 

Hogs were bred for meat and for cooking-fat; the surplus pork was cured and sent away to foreign markets.

Summary: We have seen how the Puritan turned even his disadvantages into profit. His selfrespect and sense of duty enabled him to work for the good of the group at the same time he was working for himself. He was intelligent enough to see that the prosperity of the individual contributed to the prosperity of the He showed his practical group. good sense by striving to give

each man a chance to earn his own living. It was good business to encourage the lowest servant to become independent and to aspire to a higher station in life. By this means more wealth was created and a better society was developed. The Puritan was practical and thrifty, fearless and adventurous. He fathered a great race of business men who furnished the capital for westward expansion and public improvements in the Nineteenth Century. The descendants of these men have settled in every part of the United States. They have played a great part in the development of this country from the Atlantic to the Pacific, from 

Canada to Mexico.

CHAPTER VI 

Puritan Society

The character of a nation depends upon its people. Each individual, each group, contributes something toward national honor or dishonor. The Puritans, like any other social group, had some fine traits and some mean ones. 

However, they had certain moral values that were a contribution of the first rank to American society. They were not the only people who possessed these values, of course, but they did possess them in an unusual degree. 

The Puritan was a great builder of character. He set up certain high standards of conduct and endeavored to live up to them. He formed in his children the habits of self-denial and self-discipline.

He had a habit of checking up on his neighbor's morals that was irritating to the neighbor, but highly gratifying to himself. He had a stern sense of duty and justice that made him zealous in the punishment of wrong-doing. He endured great hardship and suffering without whining. With an unconquerable spirit he refused to be overcome by unfavorable conditions for making a living. By sheer determination and faith he wrung comfort and plenty from a forbidding wilderness. He believed that he was one of God's chosen; that belief was a shield and buckler to him. In that shining armor he could not be defeated. He gave to us what we know as American pluck and originality. He worked hard himself and would allow none to be idle. That feeling that idleness is an evil and a disgrace created American "push."

The Puritan was America's "hustler." He put zest into his work and took pride in doing it well. 

The Puritan recognized his responsibility to his group. He must care for the poor and the unfortunate; he must cooperate with his neighbor. His ideas of right living, of justice, liberty, and honor characterize our most truly 

American institutions today.

Standards of Living: Trace the improvement of the Puritan's dwelling-place and you will see the unfolding of ambition and the reward of industry. The earliest 

Puritan homes were miserable, indeed. Many of the early Puritans dug into a hillside for their first shelter, "casting the Earth aloft upon timber." They made "smoaky fire against the earth at the highest side, and thus these poor servants of Christ provide[d] shelter for themselves, their Wives and little ones." Some dug cellars, which they spanned with spars and covered with turf, dwelling "in these (57)

holes" as "contented as moles." 

Some of these "holes" for "moles" had plank floors. The earthen walls were cased all round with wood which was then covered with bark "or something else to keep the earth from caving in." Partitions were put into these cellars to form rooms, according to the size of the family. Some of the wealthiest and most prominent men of New England once lived in such habitations for two or three years before they could have anything better. Some, who could have had better homes, would not, because they did not want to discourage the poorer members of the colony. The log house replaced the dug-out; the frame cottage replaced the log house. As the family grew and prospered, rooms were added. 

Some of the first houses had thatched roofs and clay-lined chimneys. Both of these features were fire hazards, and the time came when neither were allowed to be built. Every householder had his own ladder and fire-buckets. Whenever a fire broke out, all seized their buckets and formed double lanes from the well or river to the burning house. It is probable that 

Boston in 1650 had the first fire engine. It was made by Joseph 

Jenks of Lynn, Massachusetts.

By 1670, two-story houses were being built. Some of these houses had the second story jutting out a foot or two beyond the first.

Some say this was to afford better defense against Indians, but it is more likely that the builder was simply copying his old house in England. Houses were built that way in England, because the streets were narrow. By projecting the second story beyond the first, there was more room in the street to walk between the houses. Shingles sliced from blocks of cedar covered the house, and the walls were plastered with clay mixed with chopped straw and spread over laths made of split hickory saplings. Gradually brick houses came to be builtsome with bricks imported from

England, some with home-burned bricks. The earlier wooden houses were unpainted, and some had oiled paper in the windows. However, some of the very first Puritans brought window-glass with them from England. As wealth increased, finer houses were built and finer furnishings were bought. 

The wealthy gentleman of the Eighteenth Century forsook his town house for a country estate planned after the manors of England. He rode to town in his own carriage, drawn by beautiful horses in handsome harness.

The serving of meals was very simple. Cooking on an open fire must have been a complicated business but setting the table was easy. Sometimes a board was put across a pair of wooden horses, and there was a table! If it had depressions hollowed out along its sides, there were the dishes! The food was eaten from the hollows, which were then scraped and the board set away. The dishes were done just that easily. Even the governor's family ate out of wooden trenchers; two people sharing one trencher. In the poorest families everyone ate from the same trencher. Wooden or horn spoons were more in use than silver, though a few families had brought with them a few silver drinking cups or dishes; a silver spoon was unusual. No one ever thought of such a thing as an individual drinking-cup. Children stood at their meals, unless they were especially invited to sit down. They were expected to eat in silence and to leave the table when "moderately satisfied." This must have been a 

Puritan way of training the child in temperance and self-restraint.

Fashions: When the Puritans first came to the New World they dressed plainly. Plain dress, plain speech, and plain living were thought proper for God-fearing people. That there were among them those who loved color and rich fabrics is evident from certain laws passed by the General Court of Massachusetts as early as 1634. 

Lace, gold, and silver thread, slashed sleeves and embroideries were forbidden; but so great was the pressure for such things that the Court in 1636 softened the decree by allowing one slash in the sleeve and one slash in the back.

These slashes were cut into the garment to show a bright-colored under-garment. Those who brought fine clothes from England were allowed to wear them, for clothing must not be wasted. It was considered wrong to make new clothes of fine materials, unless one could well afford it. The Puritans frowned upon living beyond one's income. A narrow binding of braid was permitted on linen garments, but gold and silver girdles, hat bands, ruffs, and beaver hats were forbidden. Women's sleeves were required not to be more than an ell (45 inches) wide, and no "short sleeves whereby the nakedness of the arms" might "be discovered" were permitted.

In 1639, the men came in for their share of reproof. The Court decided that men should not wear "immoderate greate breeches," or double ruffs around their necks.

No capes or broad bands about their shoulders, no silk roses on their shoes, no broad cuffs were allowed. In Salem a man was brought before the Assembly in 

1652 for "excess in bootes" and for wearing too much ribbon and gold and silver lacings on his clothes.

The wearing of long hair by men was ruled against. It was the opinion of the Court that long hair was too much like ruffians,

Indians, and women. The fashion must have persisted, however, for in 1672 some of the old-fashioned Puritans presented a petition to the General Court, complaining of the Harvard students setting the mode of wearing long hair. Wigs were in great disrepute among conservative Puritans. Cotton Mather spent years denouncing wigs. He spoke approvingly of any man. who wore "his own hair." 

Women were reproved for the "wearing of borders of hair and their cutting, curling, and immodest laying out of their hair." It is known that women went to professional barbers to have their hair cut, presumably for these "borders of hair." The men were anything but gentle in their criticisms of the latest "hair-do," or fashion in dress for the ladies. 

Nathaniel Ward in 1645 complained of the "foole-fangles" worn by women. He scorned the "nugiperous Gentledame" who inquired

He considered such a woman as "the very gizzard of a trifle, the product of the quarter of a cypher," fitter to be kicked than honored. It was no wonder, he said, that they wore "drailes" on the backs of their heads, for they had nothing in the "fore-part, but a few Squirrills brains to help them frisk from one ill-favour'd fashion to another;" but even blistering criticism and rulings of the Court could not stop the well-to-do from aping the fashions of the British Court. In the Eighteenth Century both men and women wore their finest clothes to church to show them off. It must be remembered that they had no theatre or opera to attend. 

The middle-class ladies liked to dress in fashionable clothes, too.

But they were soon rebuked if they wore finer clothes than their station in life warranted. None must dress beyond their means. 

Two ladies of Newbury, Massachusetts, in 1653, were charged with extravagance in dress, because they wore hoods and scarves of silk. 

Upon proof that their husbands were each worth about two hundred pounds sterling, the ladies were allowed to wear their silken finery.

In small villages both men and women continued to dress plainly and to keep the older ways of plain living. They went to church to hear the sermon, and they liked it full of terror and brimstone. They did not want a soft and easy religion-they wanted it hard and plain like their garments. A certain minister was complained of because he preached a religion too comfortable-he set forth "too many dainties" for the old-fashioned folk. 

The Problem of Strong Drink: The tavern was the Puri tan's saloon. It was common to drink alcoholic beverages, but every one was supposed to be temperate. Laws were passed to protect the drinker from being cheated; he was entitled to get his money's worth. The tavernkeeper should sell his beer of good strength and at a reasonable price.

On the other hand, he must not sell "strong waters," or allow any customers to drink more than half a pint of wine at one time. Neither must he allow a customer to waste more than a half-hour in consuming that half-pint, or allow the customer to drink after nine o'clock at night. Decency must be observed at all times. Smoking was allowed in the tavern only in a private room.

By 1664, a new generation with less strict ideas had grown up. 

Then, as in every age, the old folk thought that the young folk were going swiftly down the broad path to destruction. Under pain of losing his license, the innkeeper was forbidden to allow swearing,

loud singing and noise-making, and "uncivil carriage" among the young people who met there for a little fun. 

These rules were not always enforced, and a feeling rose among the more sober element that taverns should be for the entertainment of travellers only, not a meeting-place for "Town drunkerds." It was thought that no strong drink should be sold at the tavern, because the sin of drunkenness was becoming too widespread. 

Joseph Noyes of Sudbury lamented that: "Our fathers came into the wilderness to enjoy the Gospel" and to convert the heathen; but instead of converting the Indian, they had taught him to get drunk.

Drinking became so common that tubs of strong drink were provided by churches when a minister was ordained. When the neighbors met to raise the frame of a church or house, the workers were refreshed by an abundance of rum and hard cider. At funerals, even the funerals of preachers, gallons of wine and cider were consumed. The records show that in the late Seventeenth Century as much as fifty gallons of Madeira wine at a cost of fifty dollars were drunk at one minister's funeral.

This custom finally became such a scandal that it was stopped by order of the Court. 

Law-Breaking and its Pun ishment: Before the Body of 

Liberties was written, punishment for wrong-doing was largely determined by the ministers. This was because there were few trained lawyers among the colonists and because the minister was familiar with the law of Moses. There were ten crimes punishable by death; among them were murder (with or without intent to kill), stealing, perjury, treason, witchcraft, taking God's name in vain, and two sex crimes. Later, highway robbers and burglars were put to death only after the third offense. If a son resisted his father, when the father attempted to punish him, he was liable to the death penalty; also, if he cursed or struck his father or mother.

Setting fire to a building, or denying the Word of God twice, merited death. Banished Quakers, who returned to the Puritan settlements, and those who practiced witchcraft suffered the same severe punishment.

For a lesser offense, the punishment might be very cruel. Cutting off the ears, putting in the stocks, the horsewhip "well laid on the bare back," and branding a letter on the flesh with a hot iron were common punishments. For some offenses, the penalty was the loss of the right to vote, a fine, or banishment from the colony. If a man said mean things about the minister or the magistrate, or failed to go to church, he lost his right to vote. A woman was sometimes put in the stocks or gagged

Puritan Society 63 for striking her husband or calling him a bad name. A case has been recorded of a woman being punished for calling her husband “an old Devil."

Public officials and men of wealth and prominence were subject to punishment just the same as the common citizen. Governor Endicott was fined forty shillings time after time for fighting, and Sir Richard Saltonstall had to pay five pounds for whipping two persons when no other officials were present. 

The Puritans constantly strove to reform their people and sincerely tried to live according to the Old 

Testament. That they often failed is no reflection upon their ideals. College students punished by being whipped in public, with prayer both before and after the ordeal. Courtship and Marriage: 

Courtship among the Puritans was less a matter of romance than a transaction of business. 

It was customary for a girl's nearest male relative to be consulted before he "bespoke" the maid. The parents of both young people got together and made certain financial agreements before the couple became engaged. 

There were ways, of course, to get around parental decisions; young people have ever had their own methods in love. 

If marriage was intended, such intention must be read in church three times. This was called "publishing the banns." 

This was a serious business, as one young man found out. For trifling with marriage banns, he was made to stand at the door of a public building with a paper in his hat, bearing capital letters which spelled out the words: "For setting up a faulse purpose of marriage att Topsfield.”

Elopements were not tolerated in a Puritan community. The wedding portion which the bride brought to her husband was of prime importance. John Hull, the Massachusetts mint-master, gave as his daughter's dowry her weight in pine-tree shillings.

For a long time the marriage ceremony was performed by the magistrate, but in 1682 it was made lawful for ministers to perform the ceremony. On the first 

Sunday after the wedding, the bride and groom walked to church (weather permitting), and stood before the congregation in order that everyone might see their wedding clothes.

Few men and women remained

unmarried. The state of society was such that it was hard for either a man or woman to earn a living alone. Girls were married even in their early 'teens. A woman of twenty-five was considered "an ancient maid" or an "antique virgin." This idea continued into the Twentieth Century. Widows and widowers quickly remarried, because they needed help in maintaining a home. A few marriages on record show that they took place within a week after the death of the former mate of the bride or bridegroom.

Marriages between Indians and whites were allowed until 1787, when a law was passed against it. 

Marriage of whites and Negroes was forbidden by law in 1705.

There was a great deal of family affection between the Puritans, though they made no public display of it. That Puritan reserve was handed down from generation to generation and still survives in some families of Puritan descent. 

Children never saw their parents kiss each other. Children were never fondled enough to become "spoiled." John Adams, who adored his wife, always addressed her in a letter as "Dearest Friend."

Children, in letters to their father, addressed him as "Honored Sir" or "Esteemed Parent." Yet there was deep and sincere family devotion. Large families were the rule, ten or twelve children being a common number. In the Eighteenth Century one old Puritan died, leaving behind him 157 living descendants-children, grand-children, and great-grandchildren.

There were few divorces. Unhappy couples were allowed to live apart, but the courtsseldom granted a divorce. Marriage was considered a life contract by the Puritans. 

Laws Regarding Women: Any 

Puritan man who established a home expected to take care of the unmarried women of his own or his wife's family. The maiden aunt, the widowed sister or mother, the poor cousin, found refuge in his home; but the toil of these dependents in the household was seldom given its just recognition.

For food and shelter and plain raiment, they gave in return long days of hard labor and true devotion to the welfare of the family. 

The Puritan father ruled his family as a patriarch of one of the tribes of Israel; the home was run to please the man of the house.

In return for woman's service, she was given some protection by law. Every married woman was free "from bodily correction or stripes by her husband," unless he was defending himself against her blows. If a woman had to be punished, the Court would assume that duty; her husband did not have that privilege. 

The men looked after the financial interests of their women. If a man died leaving debts, his creditors could

Eleven years later, a law was passed in Massachusetts to allow the widow one-third of her husband's property. In 1664 the Court ruled that houses and lands could not be sold without the wife's consent.

Puritan Children: The life of a Puritan child was not an easy one. He was born into a large family and each child was expected to do his share of the work of the household. He was set to carding wool or some other light occupation when he was scarcely out of his babyhood. He had little time to play, for idleness was sin. Children were to be seen, not heard. They were severely disciplined, and the strictest obedience to parents was exacted. If a child had a mind of his own, his spirit was broken by harsh punishment, even though the parent suffered while giving it. 

The Puritan parent felt that obedience was the first law of characterbuilding. Obedience was the first law of God, so the parents thought, and by that law only could the immortal soul of the child be saved.

Before the law, all children were equal, except the eldest son. was entitled to a double portion of his parent's estate. He

Children were taught certain accepted forms of polite behavior and were expected to exercise them. From a child's book we find that a child should not sit down at the table until he was asked to do so; never was he allowed to sit before the blessing. He must not ask for anything at the table, but "tarry till it be offered" him.

He should not speak at the table. 

He should break his bread but never bite it. He must take his salt only with a clean knife, and he must not dip his meat into the salt. He must hold his knife in a sloping position, never in an upright position; he must lay it down at the right hand of the plate with the blade on the plate. He must not "look earnestly at any other that is eating." Singing, humming, or wriggling at the table was forbidden. "Spit nowhere in the room but in the corner" was the polite way of performing an act that sometimes became necessary. 

When the Puritan child was spoken to, he was expected to stand up and reply most respectfully. an old story were repeated, the polite child must not say, "I have heard it before." If the storyteller told the story wrong, the child must not try to set him right If just let it go for the sake of good manners. The truth of what the older person had to say should never be questioned. Neither should the child be ungracious enough to "snigger" at the mistakes of his elders.

Children of the poor were placed by the court in families to work

for their keep. Girls of six were expected to spin flax, comb wool, or help with the household work. 

Boys worked outdoors with the men, and helped with the making of shoes or brooms, or other articles of home manufacture. In 1682, Boston built a workhouse in which poor children could do some kind of profitable labor. The Court advanced the opinion that these children had been shamefully wasting their time upon the streets.

Care of the Poor: The Puri tans brought to the New World the English idea that the community is responsible for its poor. 

Among the early Puritans there was little poverty of a lasting nature, for each family was given a chance to provide for itself. Old folk, widows and orphans, the unfortunate, sometimes became dependent upon the community. All 

Masters were responsible for their servants. The Court appointed a sponsor for each child who had no living parents. single persons within the town were put to some kind of work and told where to live. If a father failed to provide for his family, he and his children might be put to some kind of work by the Court. 

No stranger was allowed to stay in town without the consent of the town authorities. If he were likely to become dependent upon the community, he was warned to leave the town. If a lone woman came to town, she might stay if she would work for the town, going from one family to another to give her services. Sometimes she was set to spinning flax or wool for the town. A single man might stay in town, if he would live wherever the selectman placed him, and if he did the work they gave him to do. He must earn his living or leave town. A man with a family might remain and be given a plot of land, if he seemed willing and able to support himself and his family. By such methods the volume of poor relief was kept very low. 

There were few poor-houses among the Puritans during the 

Seventeenth Century. Boston, however, had one in 1660; in 1740 it had one hundred inmates. The selectmen looked after all dependent persons until 1691, when the first Board of Overseers for the poor was established in Boston. 

As the different cases were discussed in town meeting, it seemed that the meeting was more anxious to get the relief cases out of town than they were to help them. The 

Puritans were a tight-fisted lot. 

Many of the relief cases were simply passed on from town to town. 

Poor families who could barely exist exist were given certain rights. 

They could cut wood in certain sections of the town. They were given certain plots of ground for planting. Sometimes a poor man

Puritan Society 67 was allowed to build a house at public expense, the town taking a mortgage upon the property. If he failed to pay for his house, he was set out, of course, the town taking his property. However, the Puritans rarely invested real money in poor relief. Sometimes cows were owned by the town, and these cows were assigned to certain needy families. A cruel custom of auctioning off the poor at the village tavern after a town meeting was fairly common in the 

Eighteenth Century. The lowest bidder got the services of the poor individual or family.

State aid, which we hear so much about today, is not a new thing. After the Indian war, known as King Philip's War, a special act was passed by which the Provincial treasury financed relief for persons who had lost their homes in the war. This was supposed to be an emergency measure, but it set an example that has never been discarded.

In the case of children without homes, or children with improper homes, the town looked after them by placing them with some "honest godly family." This established a principle for which the state of Massachusetts is noted today-a principle that is followed in many different states. Instead of placing the children in a public institution, they were put in charge of selected families who were responsible to the selectmen for the care, education, and good treatment of the children.

There was a gentler side to Puritan charity. In 1720, Boston finished a hospital for the care of the sick. The town treasury was to pay for the care of all those who could not afford to pay. When any epidemic or disaster overtook any part of New England, the Puritans organized relief for the sufferers. 

Private charitable organizations gave out pensions to the needy, and looked after immigrants from Scotland and Ireland. Well-to-do women made regular rounds, taking baskets of provisions to the poor. The authorities respected the pride of those who had once been in good circumstances, who had been prominent in the town, or had had a good education. 

Help was given to such persons very privately, and few neighbors knew anything about it. 

Amusements: The Puritan was a serious, solemn individual, yet he had his own ways of amusing himself. One prominent leader in Puritan life recorded in his diary that he had amused himself by rearranging the coffins in the family vault "an awful yet pleasing 

Treat." The Puritan had a sneaking feeling that if he had a little fun, he must surely be guilty of sin. He counted it virtue to deny himself worldly pleasure, and dreamed of his reward in the next

world. He got a somber pleasure out of the constant examination of his own soul, and in watching for the shortcomings of his neighbors. 

Shuffleboard and bowling he enjoyed, but he seldom indulged himself, for that would be a waste of precious time. A certain minister was brought to trial for playing "shovel board," but he contended that he had committed no sin because he had not played in a tavern. There was at that time a law against playing shuffleboard in a tavern.

The Puritan amused himself by going to church and to the Thursday morning lecture. There he saw and conversed with friends, picked up the news of the community, and argued about fine points of religion.

Sometimes at the lecture or at church the Puritan heard a sermon preached to a criminal about to die. 

Not one terrifying detail about the tortures that awaited the criminal after death was omitted, as the doomed man sat in the front seat for all to see. The horror and vividness of the sermon were as thrilling to all, except the criminal, as the wildest Wild West movie of modern times. The doomed man offered even more entertainment on the day of his execution. He was taken through the streets of the town in a cart, while great crowds followed him to the gallows. Once when seven pirates were executed at the same time, people came from great distances from every direction. More than a hundred boats on the river were filled with spectators. Women went in great numbers to watch the criminals meet their death, but at the critical moment it was too much for their nerves. They "screeched so loud" that they were heard a mile away.

Funerals were conducted with great pomp and ceremony. The most important men were chosen to escort the corpse to the grave. 

Presents of gloves, rings, and scarves were given to the mourning friends. One thrifty Puritan sold a sold a thirty years' collection of funeral gloves for a tidy sum. 

Another boasted of a flagon full of rings "made" at funerals. The

Puritan was a true collector. "Stage-plays and Mixed Dancing" were frowned upon; but by the Eighteenth Century, both were becoming popular forms of amusement. Plays were given under the name of "moral lectures," in order that the Puritan might attend without injury to his tender conscience. Dancing schools were set up under various soothing names. One was advertised in 1735 as "a school for reading, writing, cyphering, dancing, and the use of the needle." Young people liked to go on picnics, boating parties, and barbecues "where a whole Hog was roasted."

Aside from Thanksgiving, the “training day” was a favorite holiday of the Puritans. The "training day" was a day devoted more to visiting and feasting than to military training. The Puritans set aside certain days for training, in order to keep fit for fighting as occasion demanded it. In Governor 

Winthrop's Diary there is a record of a thousand men taking part in training day at Boston in 1639.

The Governor added that the men had arms of many shapes and sizes. Sometimes a sham battle was fought with not much zeal on either side. Again, target practice with guns and cannon might be enjoyed by the men and small boys. The men shot at a stuffed dummy of human form. For the best marksmanship there was a prize of a silver cup, a pair of silver shoe-buckles, or a silver-headed pike. Prayer was offered and psalms were sung both before and after the exercises. The high point of the day was a big dinner prepared and served on the Boston Common.

After the diplomas were presented to the graduates, there was a great feast and a commencement cake to be enjoyed. To the dismay and sorrow of the college authorities, the visitors took over the commencement time and made it a week of revelry. They had come to have a good time and they had it. The college officials changed the Commencement day from Wednesday to Friday and held the exercises more privately; but there was so much dissatisfaction among the people, that the old way had to be restored.

Summary: The Puritan did not spend all his time praying and singing psalms. He had a very human side. He loved his family, his games, his music, his holidays.

He loved a joke, but his jokes were a little cruel. His wit was sharp and stinging. Whatever was done in the way of amusement, the Puritan wished it done with decency and propriety. He disapproved of all unseemly conduct.

Afterword When the Puritans came to the New World, they were the most accomplished and the sturdiest group that had yet come to found a colony. They came to create a new thing; a new kind of commonwealth. They were accustomed to comfort and some degree of wealth in the Old World. Their Their leaders were men of education; men who had held responsible positions in England. They came with the faith that they could not fail, because God had chosen them for a holy task. They were convinced that they, and they alone, possessed the Truth; it was their sacred duty to impart that truth to others.

The 

Only their best efforts, they thought, would be acceptable to God. Upon this idea they builded 

Puritan character. They were brave, for who could defeat God's chosen ones? They did their duty for duty's sake; each man accepted responsibility for himself.

Puritans were independent, selfrespecting, and proud. Not even to the King of Kings would they kneel or bow. To keep themselves pure and fit for a noble undertaking, they practised self-denial and self-restraint. They valued justice and equal opportunity for all. They believed that the greatness of a nation must depend upon education and work for all. By industry, thrift, education, and moral character they purposed to build a model nation. Such was the stuff of which Puritan dreams were made.

At the end of two centuries the Puritans were the ancestors of onethird of the white population of the United States. About four million people had by this time sprung from the four thousand families that had come from England. So many of these descendants had gone to New York and

Ohio that they made up half the population of those States. With them they carried the free school system that has spread all over our country.

The descendants of the Puritans made New England the intellectual center of the United States. 

They created literature that could take its place by the side of any other national literature in quality, power, variety, and originality of thought. In the period which 

Van Wyck Brooks calls "the flowering of New England," the Puritan mind was set free. No longer was it devoted only to religious themes; it went out into the fields of history, romance, poetry, philosophy, and oratory.
