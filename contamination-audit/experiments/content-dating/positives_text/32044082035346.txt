Economic Liberty Under the 

Sway of of the the Profit Motive 

All men are born unequal.

Some are born with ten talents, some with five, some with one, some with none at all, and others with a hundred; and all talents are different, and there are even different degrees of nonentity and infinity of talent.

Some men are born with no talents at all, and these we roughly classify as idiots, imbeciles and morons according to their deficiency; for even defectives are of different degrees of deficiency and nothingness. On the other hand, some men are born with an infinity of talent, and these we call geniuses; and here again there is inequality, for no infinity is like any other infinity. 

Even education does not make men equal; on the contrary, at the end of the educational process the differences are greater, for ability means educability. 

Inequality is stamped upon the very constitution of the universe. 

No two blades of grass, no two neurons, probably no two atoms or electrons are exactly alike.

All men are born unequal by a fiat of nature without; but all men aspire to equality by a divine afflatus within. 

This is by nature an absolutely inequitable world; but by spirit it is possible to correct the injustice by pooling and sharing our talents and our inequalities.

If we do not pool our talents, some are born to live in ease and luxury and some are born to live and die in indigence and want. 

That is what is happening today under our system of economic liberty, which entails a perpetuation and enhancement of the inequality, unrest, abandon, anarchy, chaos.

Our American liberty, our American Constitution and our world system of economics and politics, which we have inherited from a primitive past, are based on a lie and on the law of the jungle. All men are not born equal but unequal. Therefore individual liberty means for some liberty to live, for others liberty to 3

die; for some liberty to play, for others liberty to toil; for some liberty to pile up a superfluity of wealth illimitable, for others liberty to starve miserably in body and soul. It means $500 an hour for the banker who is lucky enough to be "on the inside", $500 a day for the psychiatrist who is lucky enough to have a rich client, and $500 a year for the hedger and ditcher who is lucky enough to have a job. It means sumptuous homes and clubs and churches for the few, and slums, settlements and jails for the many. And it means at last just what we are beginning to have today: paralysis, demoralization, decay.

During a depression, which such liberty makes inevitable with increasing severity, even those who have many talents often starve with the rest, because they have not shrewdly turned those talents to the accumulation of wealth, or because what property they may have accumulated in spite of the ordinary uncertainties of business investment, has finally disappeared in the general slump. 

Human life is a great root growing onward and constantly sending up new shoots, freaks, talents, geniuses, reversions, things new and old. We each of us get what this great human racial root, this storehouse of inexhaustible energies, gives us, by what principle nobody knows. Darwin professed to know something about heredity and environment; he confessed absolute ignorance of the cause of variation, and we have since got no nearer to a solution of that enigma. Deficiencies, therefore, are a racial product whose cause is unknown. The race, being thus responsible for the deficient, for the defective, is in duty bound to take care of them, and, as far as possible, to supply their deficiencies, while in many cases preventing their reproduction. That is but common justice. This, then, is the scientific basis of altruism, so far as it concerns the humane care of the defective.

On the other hand, if idiocy is a racial product, talent and genius of every kind are no less so. No man is responsible for, no man is the cause of, his own talent or genius. This too is a free gift from the race, a racial product. Did Shakespeare's, did Beethoven's parents will their genius or have anything to do with it?—much less those great men themselves! Was there even another Shakespeare or Beethoven, whose genius they inherited? And, if there had been, that fact would not make the genius theirs. They would again have derived it freely from others. The same is true of Carnegie and 

Hill and the rest of our financial and industrial leaders, who often

presumptuously style themselves "self-made men." For the same reason, then, that the race, the society, has a duty to fulfil towards the defectives it produces, so the talented men and geniuses of all kinds and degrees have a duty to perform towards the human race and the human society that produced them and fostered and enriched their growth. To that society and to the great human stock from which they sprang and which includes us all they owe their talents and their genius. Their talents are not an individual but a humanracial and a social product and possession. This is the scientific basis of the true Common Wealth; and, if we regard these talents as the free gift of God, then it is also the religious basis of the service of man and the kingdom of God on earth.

It is stamped upon the very constitution of the universe that we should, that we must, share our talents; because talents are a social product and therefore justly a common, a universal possession, and not an individual, a tribal or a national possession. No individual, no race, no nation, is complete in itself. We need to unite all our incompletenesses to make completeness, all our imperfections to make perfection. The only unit is mankind. We all need one another to make that perfect society, that perfect world, that Kingdom of God and Commonwealth of Man that has for ages been the aspiration and the longing of the world. 

Industry is stalled.

And yet there is an infinity of work that needs to be done, a multitude of idle workers eager to do it, a plethora of money in the banks to pay them and of food and clothes in the markets to feed and clothe them! What is at the bottom of this stupendous worldshaking absurdity?

An immense opportunity! But our society, as at present constituted, has no plan for all these idle men and women but to leave them in idleness or give them odd jobs and feed them like pigs out of a trough. To qualify for that trough the one requisite is to be entirely down and out; and in our planless, hit and miss kind of economy we are all headed for it. 

It needs no technocrat and no plagiaristocrat to tell us, in awesomely cryptic lingo, that the machine is capable of turning out, is indeed now turning out, an abundance of food and clothes for all. 

That is only too evident to everyone. Nevertheless the hungry are not fed nor the naked clothed. No machine can explain that nor help that. What is the explanation?

It is the fierce determination of every American, in common with most of the rest of the world, but more fiercely, to cling to the chance to profiteer and make a fortune, however unsuccessful he may personally have been at the game, and however much his fellows suffer from such selfish action if successful. This it is that, as inevitably and relentlessly as a law of physical nature, drains off the bulk of the common wealth into the coffers of the comparatively few most unscrupulous or most successful hoarders, while impoverishing the rest of "the Commonwealth" and "the Commonwealth of Nations." We cannot even make reciprocally advantageous commercial arrangements with our neighbors, so powerful and insidious are the propaganda and lobbying of our organized profiteers. And, not content with reducing their fellow-citizens to starvation, our profiteers, in order to make huge profits out of munitions, create wars and lead them to the shambles. And, as the publication of books, magazines, newspapers and pamphlets is in their hands, and even education to a considerable extent, there is a mass production of standardized and single-track minds, and the truth has little chance of being either heard or heeded. 

It is in fact the profit motive in industry that explains the present hopeless condition of the world's trade. That is the "economic law" and the "force of nature" that we are asked to respect as more sacrosanct than all the injunctions of religion and morality. 

The profit motive is a force of nature only in the sense that the desire to steal is a force of nature. To call them "natural" and "human" does not take them out of the category of propensities that need to be checked or eradicated altogether and a social motive put in their place.

These millions for whom year after year their country has no steady work, and these other millions for whom year after year their country has only a bare animal subsistence wage, might well exclaim: 'Is it possible that my country has no plan for me? What is a nation for, if not to plan for the individuals who compose it? 

Do we not unite for our mutual benefit? If not, why should we unite? If we must look after ourselves, every man for himself, while the devil takes so many hindmost, we could do it better as small autonomous communities than as a big, unwieldy, slow-moving, hard-headed and hard-hearted leviathan, hampered in its lucider moments by an antiquated Constitution that jealously protects an immoral economic system.' A society in which legal right is moral wrong is in a precarious condition.

Our economy is pre-eminently and basically a profit-economy. 

What do we mean by Profit? And what do we mean by a Profiteer?

Profit is essentially money that represents no work and no livelihood, and yet is convertible into wealth; it is surplus above the cost of production and service, when we reckon in the cost, as we evidently should in a just economy, a decent living in a democratic community for all concerned in the production and distribution, including the men who furnish the leadership and the ownership of the capital or stock. Profit is therefore essentially "water." When the plant is paid for and the materials, and when labor and investors are paid a just price, meaning by just a price that affords a sure and decent living to both worker and investor, and no morethat is to say, at the least a decent minimum wage and at the most a maximum income within the means of the economy to afford while doing justice to all and especially while paying the minimum wagethen whatever is exacted beyond these necessary expenses, which make up the cost, is Profit, and the one who exacts this profit and uses it or any part of it as private wealth, is a Profiteer. 

If we do not establish a maximum income, where would the money come from to pay the minimum wage to those not now receiving it, and to increase other inadequate wages and salaries? The employer would simply add the increase in the minimum wages to the cost of goods and services, or he would lower other wages and salaries that were somewhat higher to protect his profits, and we should not be any better off than before! Both of these things are being done before our eyes under the National Recovery Act. The profiteer can be trusted always to keep one jump ahead of the game, as long as business is allowed to remain a game and a gamble with profits and a fortune for stakes.

High bracket taxation as a means to redistribute income has countless objections. It leaves the vicious profit motive in full activity while making no real redistribution of the national income. It tends to cripple or hamstring what we dare not reform. It leaves inordinate power in the hands of the few who pay an inordinate share of the taxes. It makes the government a particeps criminis, a sharer of the swag, in this supreme and stupendous interlocking racket of rackets.

Some of the profit at present exacted by industry and possibly all of it in our sort of sweatshop economy, actually represents labor

unpaid for, as it cannot be maintained that labor has ever been paid with any thought of providing a decent living for all. 

Moreover, it is well known that the thirst for profits leads to all sorts of practices, legal and illegal, that make for inordinate profits and high living for the profiteers, and insufficient income and malnutrition, physical and spiritual, for the many. In spite of "blue sky laws" the people lose nearly two billion dollars annually in worthless stocks alone, and it is certain that no Securities Acts will ever make the people secure against the shrewd devices of business actuated by the immoral profit motive. The temptation to use foul methods is terrific in a system such as ours, in which selfinterest is paramount and profiteering the ordinary rule and habit of the game. In our present unjust economy, corporations, for example, call "profit" whatever is left over after the owners have voted themselves out of the receipts fat salaries higher than any reasonable maximum, i.e., higher than any maximum possible in a just economy, and therefore containing an element of profit, without regard to the effect of their action on the community at large, without regard, indeed, to the fact that such selfish unsocial "rugged individualism' rides rough-shod over the rights of others to a living, involving low wages for the many or no wages at all. And out of the profit that is left they will often pay to themselves and others huge "bonuses" and dividends (dividends, moreover, upon stock that is often “water”, representing no real value), while paying most of their employees as low wages and salaries as possible or keeping high the price of their goods or services, destroying goods or denying service rather than lower their price.

In small businesses whatever a man takes in, over and above what he has to pay out in wages, rent and cost of materials, he calls "profit" and he pockets it, again without asking himself whether the amount is reasonable and his action social, or unreasonable and his action unsocial in its effects. In the latter case it is profit; in the former case it is not necessarily profit at all: it may be just livelihood, which ought to be included in the cost, if other things (wages, prices paid and received, &c.) are equitable. For the economic problem is primarily a moral problem, and cannot be solved on any other basis.

The devastating effect of the profit motive in business could not be better demonstrated than it has been in the unsuccessful experiment made by the owners of the Columbia Conserve Company

in workers' management and ownership. In deprecating the adverse verdict of the Committee which investigated the conflict between the owners and some of their workmen, Norman Hapgood, brother of the president of the Company, makes the following illuminating statement: "I have never claimed an ethical purpose for this experiment. I am not interested in ethical criticisms of what we have been attempting to do."

Worst of all is the effect upon politics and politicians of this non-moral business motive. Political job-trafficking and graft are a direct off-shoot of profiteering business. Men accustomed to acting under the spur of an unethical motive in their "private" business find it difficult to change their whole psychology and become actuated by an ethical motive in "public" business. 

As Business has been unable to save itself from its own devotion to mere legality as opposed to morality, and as the spontaneous organization of its employees in unions to enforce a juster treatment by collective bargaining has proved so unsatisfactory and disappointing as a remedy, the people through their governments have been obliged to enforce upon business a more humane conduct by definite legal enactments. To curb the encroachments and injustices of private business under the spur of the profit motive, the State has been continually "interfering" and insisting on ever-new regulations regarding State inspection; accidents; hygienic and decent moral conditions in factories and mines; hours of labor; minimum wages; the labor of callously exploited women and children; the right to form unions and bargain collectively; the exaction of "yellow dog contracts" and "company unions," by which the defenceless workman is induced to contract away or abandon his rights; the abuse of injunctions and combines; unemployment, old age and accident insurance; state and federal employment bureaus; technical education; lower freight rates, rents and other rates; laws regarding interest; taxes on inordinate wealth and inheritance; the 

Securities Act and other "blue sky laws" directed against Wall

Street; federal control or supervision of certain interstate businesses that the State does not own but that threaten to own the State; and finally the NRA "codes of fair competition"

Such measures of Social Reform are, for the most part, just and right; but they have been powerless to mend the evils of our economic system; for they have dealt only with symptoms and have left uncurbed the root of the evil, which is the Profit Motive and the hoarding to which it inevitably leads, which stimulates a hectic

intermittent production, while at the same time paralyzing consumption, and which steels the heart against the promptings of religion and humanity.

Under an enlightened economic system the individual would have steady work and property, sufficient property-roughly commensurate with his service, to be sure, but in all cases sufficient for the ordinary necessities of a good life; but he would not have and would not need that wealth that arises from profits; for the more costly needs and services, such as education, cultural and vocational, justice, hospital attendance, scientific investigation, and adequate insurance of old age and disability, would be met by the Commonwealth from equitable taxes on all incomes. 66 Profit has no place in a just and sane economy. When out of the proceeds of the business the cost of the plant is provided for in amortization or rent; and interest, including a fixed reasonable dividend on money invested or stock (but not on "water" or fictitious value); and reserves reasonably sufficient to guarantee this interest and to meet future losses, but no 'common stock" on which interest or dividends rise and fall; and the cost of the raw materials; and the wages and salaries-none less than the legal minimum and none more than the legal maximum-for all, including the owner himself, if not already receiving the maximum income from other sources; and the provision of a reasonable amount for expansion; then any surplus, or profit, since it stands for nothing, would be made over to the government for social uses; and the concern or individual would endeavor largely to eliminate it by increasing wages and salaries and by lowering the price of their commodities or services, of whatever nature, real estate, industry, trade, agriculture, professional services, etc.

Under this plan of a minimum wage and a maximum income, therefore, there would be no profits; that is to say, there would be no surplus exacted by industry from the community above the requirements of a decent livelihood for all and the adequate endowment of industry itself.

The plan would require that everyone, and not merely, as at present, a comparatively few recipients of income, make a report of his income. The budgets not merely of banks but of all business would be open to State inspection, simply to see that the law of maximum income and minimum wage was being observed. The great majority of the people would make honest reports, so that the need of inspection would not be so great as might be thought 10

by people living, as we do now, under a vicious system; and it would become less and less with the growing loyalty and public spirit that so fair and benevolent a plan would encourage. There would be no need of "codes" or regimentation, for all business would become informed with a dignified professional spirit, and be viewed primarily as a social service, and even bankers would have little need of supervision.

Business assets would in fact have become-what they essentially are a semi-public property, and there would be no close corporations making partial and misleading statements to the public. 

There would be no more subway corporations, for example, plastering the windows of their cars with deceptive statements of their affairs designed to show the need of an increase in the five-cent fare, while behind the scenes shamelessly paying themselves fabulous salaries and declaring fantastic dividends-all to come out of those hard-earned nickels pouring in a steady stream into their eager coffers.

There would now, indeed, be three kinds of property; public, business and private. Private property would be what an individual might personally spend for personal account, i.e., at the most, the legal maximum income; business property would consist of capital for the purposes of the business; public property would arise partly from the profits of private business as explained (i.e., surplus not required for the conduct and warranted expansion of business and the provision of adequate amortization and reserves) and partly from the tariff and from a tax on all incomes of a certain fraction of the income, the same for all except the unmarried; and finally from receipts from the public exploitation of certain natural resources and public utilities, especially electric power, coal, oil, gas, the forests, telephone and telegraph. 

Moneys once invested in business could not be withdrawn for private purposes, except to supplement a deficient income. All business funds would in fact be in the nature of a trust. 

Under this system rents, interest and prices would no longer tend to be boosted to rates that only profiteers could pay; for, there being no profiteers any more, these rates would no longer be based on a false inflated valuation arising from hoards of wealth in private hands in the shape of profits having no real value. And of course dividends would not need to be so large. The profits, in fact, would gradually disappear in the increase of most wages and 11

salaries, the decrease of prices of services and commodities and the lowering of rent and interest.

Thus all the resources open to business at present would be open to it still; but profits and hoarding by the individual for private use would be illegal, and business capital woud be usable solely for business purposes. Thus all would be able to live decently and pursue the true ends of life with a new freedom and dignity, and the State would no longer exist economically for the sake of a comparatively few hoarders and profiteers. 

There is no necessary connection between profit and initiative. 

Work is a normal human activity natural to every well-born man, and is pursued primarily for its own sake with but a secondary regard even to livelihood, and, in the well-bred man, with very little, if any, regard to profit as here defined, namely such a quest of gain and power as necessarily excludes great numbers of one's own countrymen from the possession of any power, condemning many of them even to inactivity and dependence upon charity, public or private, in whole or in part. Indeed, much of the most socially valuable work is done without any remuneration whatever or with but a pittance. The importance of profit as a motive to industry and enterprise has been greatly exaggerated. It is in fact a selfish and unsocial motive leading straight to economic chaos and war in the long run, and to the condition in which we find ourselves today an industry sick unto death with the disease of profiteering, into whose moribund body the government is pumping successive shots of public money to stimulate it into a hectic and temporary semblance of life.

The natural love of work, the instinct of enterprise and initiative, the exhilaration of work well-done, the natural human desire to serve the community and to ensure productive labor to others, and the instinct to build a home, are motives much more important and fundamental to human nature than the selfish quest of profits and money-power that many business men and some economists still place so much emphasis upon. The workers are not a class but the great majority of the people of all classes and conditions and capacities; for work, enterprise, initiative, are the normal activity of muscle and nerve made for action and meaningless without it, just as play is the normal instinctive activity of childhood. 

How different the history of our civilization during the past century might have been, if from the first, in applying to industry 12

the new and mighty forces of nature, themselves so generous, we had recognized as normal that which is normal, namely the love of work inherent in every free and contented worker, and that natural feeling of brotherhood that the common work would then have inspired among all classes of workers, manual and intellectual, mercantile, professional, artistic. The co-operation of all workers on terms of justice and humanity, so essential to the prosperity of the whole Commonwealth, is a thing so natural that we wonder what force has been strong enough to defeat nature and give so malign a trend to human action.

Is not the force that has given so sinister a direction to human effort the fierce urge of the spirit of liberty, suddenly set free from its age-long imprisonment, and worshipped as a fetish to the disregard of every other human instinct and feeling? It is in the name of Liberty that Adam Smith himself, living in an age of foolish, outworn and unsocial trade-restrictions, declared for that unrestricted freedom to produce and to trade, to buy and to sell raw materials, commodities and the labor of men, which has sold great masses of our populations into a new slavery and brought about the shameful inequality in the distribution of the products of their industry that we witness today. men.

It is in the name of individual liberty that we have enslaved Let us now, therefore, invert the process and free men in the name of social justice and solidarity, voluntarily limiting our individual liberty to exploit one another in favor of the State-guaranteed independent status of all workers in a democracy. For democracy is not a form of liberty, it is a form of government, in which men voluntarily give up their individual liberty of action, deliberately limiting it in a thousand ways for the sake of the community as a whole, and of themselves not primarily but in the last analysis. 

Where such limitation is weakly or inequitably enforced, democracy is functioning badly. Eternal vigilance is the price of democracy!

Instead of making business an end, therefore, as hitherto, let us use it as a means for the common weal. Only by a wise socialeconomic policy strictly enforced by the State and loyally adhered to by the people, only by constructive co-operative social-economic planning for community, State and world, can we arrest the progressive enslavement of man in which we are all being caught, and dissolve the congested civic areas offspring of hoarders - which are 13

piling up problems of housing and transportation, of physical and moral hygiene, of vice, crime and political corruption, that are becoming ever more difficult of solution. Unbridled self-interest spells at last universal slavery.

A Chinese sage, 2200 years ago, speaking for all time, uttered these memorable words: "It is only enlightened men who are able to maintain a fixed heart independent of livelihood. As for the people, if they have not a certain livelihood, they will not have a fixed heart. And if they have not a fixed heart, there is nothing that they will not do in the way of abandon, of moral deflection and of wild license." 

Every oligarchy has been built upon the backs of an enslaved mass. Our present plutocracy is no exception; it has created a new serfdom, which can be ended peacefully only by the establishment of a universally beneficent Just Economy. A deep-seated moral disease cannot be cured by economic plasters and palliatives, however much the public may be stampeded by war-methods into its support. It can only be cured by a plan that eradicates the root of the disease, which is Profits. The adoption of such a plan would complete and perfect our democracy by making it an economic as well as a political democracy. 

It was in the eighteenth century of our Christian era that the 

Peasant Poet sang: 

It's comin' yet for a' that— 

When man to man the warld o'er 

Shall brothers be for a' that! and our own colored brother sings with naive but unanswerable logic: 

Didn't my Lord deliber Daniel? 

Why not, indeed? 

And why not every man? 

In "the year of our Lord" 1934 economic democracy is long overdue. 14

Economic Democracy Under A Just Economy 

The outcry being made in the press today by bankers, steel magnates, trust companies and the like over the very mild threat against their profits that the present administration is venturing shows that business, heedless of warning, is still wedded to the profit motive, still persists in regarding it, with Adam Smith, as the essential motive power to industry, whose prime object and criterion is "to make a maximum of profit on a minimum of capital." 

Yet it can be shown that the profit motive is not only unnecessary but positively harmful and even ruinous to business in the long run, individually and nationally. For proof we have only to look around us. For it is the profit motive that is the root of our trouble, the prime cause of our depression and of the perennial cycle of inflation and depression that has whirled its vicious round every decade for a century past: 1825, 1836-7, 1847, 1857, 1866, 1873, 1882, 1890, 1907, 1913-14, 1920, 1929-?*

Truly the Earth is the lords' and the fullness thereof!

The second table demonstrates that, by leaving in the pockets of that wealthy 1% of the 48,180,000 recipients of income, or 361,300 persons, an income of $12,000, which is still more than any of the other 99% of the recipients of income receive, it would have been possible for industry to pay a minimum wage of $1200 to all the recipients of income who did not receive that much, and the same amount even to the 2,500,000 unemployed employables in 1929. The other income groups would also have profited considerably by the diversion and conversion of these profits of the few into better wages and salaries for the many. It is therefore evident that $12,000 would have been a safe and fair maximum income in 1929, that is to say, all that our economy could afford in that year, if justice was to be done to all. Whatever, therefore, was exacted beyond that sum for private expenditure was profit, and he who exacted it a profiteer.

Moreover, business itself would have been greatly stimulated and strengthened—agriculture, trade and industry, the professions --by this benevolent stream of consumer money flowing to the many small consumers upon whose competence business prosperity is based, instead of being poured into the bottomless wells of a few profiteers and hoarders. Let anyone who objects to such a limitation upon his chances of making a fortune, tell us how otherwise— 16

short of the State or class ownership of all industry-a just distribution of the products of industry can be achieved. 

Thus distributed, this total income of 892 billion would be constantly turned over, passed from consumer to producer, and from producer to consumer, and from producer to producer, in currency and checks over and over, much of it being used as capital over and over again as well as for wages and salaries and other forms of income. Whereas, when distributed in the present inequitable way, there is a much less favorable condition for its constant use and flux as capital, and hence much capital in currency needs to be borrowed by industry from a profiteering and hoarding class to produce what there is no one with the wherewithal to buy and consume at the prices demanded to ensure the profits. Into the capacious maw of this profiteering and hoarding class all liquid capital tends to become sucked at last-including the moneys poured out by a desperate government there to become frozen. 

How much longer shall we stand staring vacantly at an inequitable and injurious distribution of economic power and exclaim with eyes raised to heaven: It's too bad, it's really too bad; but the only alternative is Communism, so it can't be helped! Would it not be more sensible and more statesmanlike-not to say more Christian-while recognizing the evils of violence and dictatorship that exist in present-day Communism, as the only response ignorant or impatient men know how to make to so cruel a system, at the same time to acknowledge the good that is in Communism, which gives it what vitality it has, namely the instinctive demand for a juster distribution of income, and incorporate that in our own system?

Called to a heroic effort of justice, shall we be content with charity and doles of money and jobs? Shall we be content with a new deal at an old game with profits and a fortune as stakes and the lifeblood of men? Or shall we end the profiteering gamble forever by putting our economy on the just basis of a decent minimum wage and a reasonable maximum income, thus answering the challenge of Soviet Russia?

The maximum income would everywhere bear a fixed proportion to the minimum, say the proportion of ten to one, which the statistics of income for 1929 would seem to justify. This ratio would obtain throughout the nation. That is to say, in whatever locality the minimum wage was fixed at $500 per annum, granting that such a small minimum could anywhere obtain, the maximum 17

income would on this ratio be $5000 per annum. Where the minimum was fixed at $1000, the maximum would be $10,000; when $1500, then $15,000, and so on. Wherever the minimum was changed, whether upward or downward, the maximum would be changed accordingly. And when it was thought economically possible to raise the maximum, the minimum would be raised proportionately. 

The size of incomes large and small would thus be determined economically. It would be a question of economic possibility to be decided periodically, not as now by the arbitrary action of the individual imposing a private tax upon the community for his own special benefit, but by the action of the democratically organized community through their elected representatives in Congress on the basis of investigation and discussion. Then we should have at last not only political democracy but economic democracy. 

There is a very different kind of equalization taking place in America today,-a levelling down towards servitude both economical and intellectual; for, when the great mass of the industrial and agricultural workers are receiving low incomes or none at all, the rest of the people, who serve them and must get their living from them the doctors, editors, lawyers, teachers, clergy, scientists, artists, writers, translators, civil servants, social workers, etc.—cannot be decently remunerated. 

The rise and fall in the cost of living would of course be an important factor in the periodic adjustment of the legal minimum and maximum incomes; and the incomes between would tend to vary in accordance with such adjustment. 

No industry deserves to exist that is unable to pay the minimum wage for the locality. In a just economy there would be no reason under the sun why the minimum should vary for different industries. In an unjust economy such inequalities are, of course, in order. It is an unjust and inequitable proceeding to force one "code" of wages and in general of hours upon one business and another "code" upon another. The business that claims to be unable to afford decent living conditions to its employees and demands special treatment on that account, does not deserve to have employees. Why should the State and the Law protect any man who thus exploits his fellows? Is it in the interest of the State that any of its citizens should be forced to a bare animal subsistence? 

How long will the American democracy be able to survive half slave and half free? 18

The minimum wage of $1200 has been arrived at by a study of the investigations that have been made since the beginning of the century into the question of standards and planes of living. It would have been a just minimum in 1929. While subject to variation, as we have already said, for time and locality, as well as from considerations of economic possibility, it would never be allowed to fall below the decent minimum requirement for health and growth, physical and spiritual. It would of course not be variable for sex, color or industry, for such things have nothing to do with the question of a decent minimum wage in a democracy. The figure we have finally fixed upon after much study, into the details of which there is not space to enter here, is roughly what was necessary in 1929 for the support of a married pair, including $200 or one-sixth, which would be paid to the government as a tax-premium, that is to say, partly as a tax and partly as a premium to ensure the same income to the married pair when the breadwinner or breadwinners have reached the age of sixty-five. 

For all recipients of income would pay one-sixth to the government for these purposes, and in return, at the age of 65, receive from the government the average income upon which he or she or both had paid the tax-premium. We have not set out to guess what it would be "nice" to have as a maximum income! We have set out to ascertain what it would have been economically possible to fix as a maximum income in 1929, if we had had a just economy. 

This is $12,000 for the combined income of a family, including husband, wife and children under sixteen years of age. This is therefore also the maximum annuity. And the annuitant, whether married or single, would continue to pay the tax-premium (and, if single, the extra tax), as they could well afford to do. 

Disability, temporary or permanent, through disease or accident, also orphanage, would be taken care of equitably. 

Such a plan would remove fear and provide means for cultural growth throughout life. It would revolutionize our economy, changing it from a thrift or production or business and aristocratic economy, with exclusive prizes as its goal, to a true consumption or cultural and democratic economy with the common weal as its goal-from the obsession of money-making to the true task of society, the making of men. This aim would also be supported by the provision regarding hours of labor and vacations. There would be as many hours for leisure as for work, namely six, for each of 19

the six week-days; which would leave half of the twenty-four hours for meals and sleep and the activities surrounding them. Means would be taken to ensure to everyone a month's vacation with no consequent loss of income, a provision which, besides making for health, longevity and efficiency, would result in an immense impetus to many important industries. 

These tax-premiums-one-sixth of the income of the nationwould amount to about 162 billion dollars, if we add the extra tax on the unmarried of 2% on incomes of less than $2000 (after deducting the tax-premium), 4% on incomes of $2000 and under $3000, 6% on incomes of $3000 and under $4000, and so on. It is certainly not good public policy to encourage and reward the sidestepping of a function that lies at the very base of the State. Even the single man or woman on the minimum wage would be well able to pay this additional tax, since that wage is based upon the requirements of the married man.

To establish two minima, one for married and one (lower) for single, would handicap the married man or woman seeking work. 

The same would be true of any differential for sex or color. 

The problem of taxation would thus be immensely simplified. 

As there would no longer be any rich to "soak" in this economic democracy, nor any bond-holding class of rentiers to borrow from, it would be the most natural thing in the world to pay as you go and apportion the whole burden of the budget among all the people according to income, the most equable form of taxation. This would deepen the general interest in a wise and economical administration, while at the same time making for self-respect and a sense of responsibility. At present the existence of the bulk of the wealth in the hands of the few makes for irresponsible extravagance and recklessness of public expenditure with a constant eye to this resource and continual resentful wrangling over the incidence of taxation.

It should be added that we were able to give the minimum wage to all who were not getting it, including the unemployed, without touching the profits of corporations. These amounted in 

1929 to $11,653,886,002. If we deduct the amounts allocated to dividends ($8,355,000,000 cash dividends and $1,288,000,000 stock dividends), there is still left a surplus of two billions, which it would be fairer and wiser to apply to the salaries and wages of those at present receiving the minimum but not the maximum than to add it to capital, as is usually done. Consequently we have 20

done this before estimating the taxes collectible. Instead of being so applied, however, this form of profits might in practice disappear in the lowering of prices. The effect would be much the same; instead of receiving more, all would pay out less. 

If anyone remarked that a lot of money was being taken in taxes from the people, the reply is first that the objector had better first ascertain what proportion of the national income is being taken today in one form of taxation or another. In England incomes of £1000 are taxed £153, which is between a sixth and a seventh and this tax is not at the same time an insurance premium. In the second place the reply is obvious that the tax-premium is very largely an insurance premium rendering other insurance and savings unnecessary, at least for the purposes of safety, the salient reason for saving. The reply also is that the bulk of the money is at once turned back into circulation by being paid to many consumers in ways we are about to describe-paid, moreover, to consumers who will consume and not hoard. 

How, then, are these taxes expended?

In bonuses of $300 per child for as many as three children under 16 years of age in families not receiving sufficient income from industry to meet such expenditure. These bonuses we estimate on the basis of the last census would amount in the aggregate to $6,341,583,904.

In old-age annuities, which we estimate would amount in the aggregate to $7,285,000,000. The total of bonuses and annuities is $13,626,593,904. Our taxes will take care of that sum and leave the substantial balance to the good of $2,808,321,543. To this should be added the tax-premium on more than a billion of income from bonds that are at present―for no reason under the sun —tax-exempt, and on the income of other tax-dodgers. This would swell our balance to between three and four billion dollars. This is a substantial surplus almost equal to the whole federal budget in 1929. A plan with so solid a financial basis cannot be scouted offhand as impractical.

With this substantial surplus there would be plenty of money to pay disability insurance and to finance the important enterprises that remain to be outlined. These are two, or rather one with two aspects: the work-army and the cultural and industrial centers whose creation would be the chief function of the work-army. 21

The next provision of our plan, then, is the universal conscription of youth of sixteen and over, or, if college-trained, upon graduation (not otherwise being vocationally trained), for a certain length of service as a work-army, or rather a work-school-army, where they would be trained vocationally according to their talents for the advancement and enhancement of life instead of its destruction,- -a peace-army and vocational school, to which also every man and woman out of work would automatically return for comprehensive examination as to their physique, mentality, character, ability, environment and family history, to discover why they were unemployed and how they might best be employed or trained for employment, and then for further training, if necessary, and for useful public service at the minimum wage until other work was found for them by the federated labor bureaus of the widest scope (taking in all occupations) founded for that purpose. 

Thus everyone would be assured steady employment at a decent wage (never more in the army, however, than the established minimum wage for the locality except for the managers and instructors), and the most prolific source of fear and worry, of poverty and disease, of vice and crime and general demoralization would be removed. The living expenses of the youth under training would be met, and all who were capable of doing and were required to do a full day's work would be given the minimum wage, or its equivalent (in part) in board and lodging. 

This plan of universal and compulsory vocational training is but the logical corollary and sequence of our system of public cultural education, which latter would be rigidly enforced everywhere up to the age of sixteen. Moreover, the statistics of unemployment both during depressions and even during times of "prosperity" sufficiently demonstrate the fact that men cannot safely and fairly be left without training and without the expert and informed guidance and assistance of organized society either to choose what occupation they shall prepare for or to seek work in any line. 

As a logical, sensible and perfectly democratic conclusion from such a system of free and compulsory cultural and vocational education, together with the provision of work for all willing to work, there would be an educational qualification for citizenship as well as a competence qualification: no one not receiving at least the minimum wage would be eligible to vote, nor would anyone who had not graduated successfully from the eighth grade or its equivalent. 22

To guarantee wages by a system of State Unemployment Insurance without at the same time guaranteeing work (the dole system) would be a suicidal policy leading to the progressive pauperization of the nation; and the more adequate the dole was, the more demoralizing it would be; whereas an inadequate dole is a mockery. 

No such lopsided policy should ever be adopted in America. 

Whether adequate or inadequate, such a dole would be open to the gravest objection as giving something for nothing and being a direct and constant encouragement to idleness and shiftlessness. With so much work needing to be done to provide a healthier and safer and worthier environment for man, it is uneconomic to dole out either private or public charity without setting the recipients of it to work that is part of a thorough-going, comprehensive plan for the creation of a more healthful and beautiful environment for human life.

This work-army, then, would be used to abolish the slums and gradually decentralize population by creating throughout the country numerous nuclei of model communities with central electric power, heating and water plants, telephones, telegraph and post office with savings bank having checking as well as interest accounts, -all publicly owned and operated by a corps of trained specialists in a well-guarded civil service with the aid of the work-army; a cremation plant; hygienic regulation of food and housing; a central publicly-owned market to which the farmer and gardener could bring their produce freely, and containing a booth for the sale of good literature; and hospitals managed by groups of physicians and nurses and other personnel organized around the hospitals as community medical centers in accordance with the recommenda tions of the Committee on the Costs of Medical Care. Liquors would be sold under some such plan as that being followed successfully in Sweden (the Bratt system), but with no profit for anybody. It is not in the public interest that government any more than the individual should profit by the sale of either alcoholic liquors or munitions and armaments. All connected with these businesses would be on salary from the government. 

There would be established in every cultural center banks licensed by the government and associated with the Reserve Banks and subject to strict government auditing and inspection by the government as well as by the Association of combined banks, with joint insurance of deposits by the totality of the banks in the country and by the government that licenses them; also parks and play- 23

grounds as well as schools and colleges, cultural and vocational (with specialization in some one or more vocational directions in every cultural center); library, lecture and music halls, theaters, museums, radio broadcasting stations, etc., all publicly owned, to bring culture to every community and give all a chance to hear and see the best things at moderate prices. Thus in time industries and people would be wooed away from the congested areas and the market brought to the farmer. Good roads and improved means of communication, public and private, would bring every farm within reach of some cultural and industrial center, and on the other hand bring the residents of the cultural and industrial center within reach of the farms, gardens and orchards; for it would be the policy to encourage a unity of agricultural and industrial life in an environment favorable to the highest culture. It would be a happy condition, indeed, if every man, whatever his trade or profession, could, at the same time, be in a small way a farmer; and the increased leisure contemplated by our plan would make this quite possible at least in the conditions created by the cultural centers.

In these cultural and industrial centers there would of course be no extra taxes beyond the regular tax-premium already mentioned; but each center would pay its way with receipts from such of its public utilities and cultural enterprises as were municipally owned.

The number and location of these centers and their relation to the present towns, townships, counties and states are matters of detail that would be worked out with an eye to unification, simplification and economy. Housing would be viewed as a State problem to the extent that it might be found necessary for the State to assist in providing housing at the price contemplated in the fixing of the minimum wage, namely eight dollars per room per month. 

Where a cultural center was being built de novo, the Post Office, Town Hall, Court House, Market, work-shops, factories, stores, banks and utilities would be grouped in the spot most favorable for business. Another group would be formed by the hospitals, educational institutions, libraries and other cultural enterprises. Around these two foci would radiate the homes of the people, each lot being an acre of ground. The English way of pulling down slums and building up near-slums on the same ground, retaining the herding feature, is not the method for this broad land. It is foolish in 24

England. With the marvelous improvement in transportation there is no longer any excuse for such herding. It belongs, with the slum and the plague, with war and violence, to stage-coach days like that fatal year 1666, and to stage-coach lands like Japan with all her camouflage imitation-progress. 

As the inception of these cultural and industrial centers would require much thought and care in their building and in the selection of people and industries to start them, the government would need to appoint able and versatile men to form a Cultural Center Planning Board. This General Planning Board would everywhere seek the aid of local planning boards composed, not of politicians and job-seekers, but of old and tried citizens of the neighborhood who had helped to build up their community and give it light and leading.

This, then, would be the other function of the work-army,to serve not only as a vocational training institution of the widest scope, but also as a sure economical resource for the building of the cultural centers and other public works, such as roads, bridges, parks, the elimination of grade crossings, power-plants, the ownership and exploitation by the government of the forests and of coal, oil and gas (all of which have been so wastefully exploited by private industry), irrigation works, reforestation, the reclaiming of waste or exhausted land, draining, and the co-ordination of the elimination of the slum with the establishment of the cultural and industrial centers. Such work, growing out of a comprehensive national plan, would furnish the practical experience and training for the young recruit at the same time that it would take up any slack in industry, and be a resource always open to men and women out of employment for technological or other reasons, or, indeed, desirous of training for an employment that accorded more with their tastes and abilities. 

All feeble-minded persons would be segregated in farmcolonies to prevent their reproducing their kind. Non-productive idleness and vagabondage in every form, including prostitution and racketeering, would be a crime punishable by imprisonment. All prisoners not feeble-minded would be occupied for the full legal labor period at the regular minimum wage, their wages going to support their families and to form a resource when they were released. Their keep would of course be deducted from their wages, and it would be the aim to maintain the prisons entirely by their 25

work. Thus there would be no reason to fear the competition of prison labor, and the best means of combatting crime, namely the education of the prisoners in spiritually uplifting surroundings and the inculcation of the habit of industry, would be provided. 

This plan would assuredly benefit the farmer in many indirect ways and particularly in giving him a better market and better conditions for co-operative marketing and processing. But, as he could only be assured a proper minimum income by fixing a minimum price for his products, a Commission for fixing the minimum prices to be paid the farmer and the foreign exporter for farm products would be appointed by the government to work under the general supervision of the Agricultural Department. In the case of each product the price paid to the foreign farmer would be not less than to the native farmer. Thus the foreign farmer would have no advantage over the native in securing a market for his products, except occasionally and exceptionally through greater nearness to our markets. This plan is designed to give everybody -native and foreigner—fair treatment. We have not set out to plan an economy unjust to our neighbor the foreigner. 

In case any farmer complains that he is still unable to earn the equivalent of the minimum wage, his case will receive sympathetic and intelligent attention, and, if necessary, financial aid given him at a low rate of interest by the government, say three per cent, seeing that the government pays but two per cent in the Post 

Office Savings Banks and but 24% on its bonds. If it be found that certain farmers are not cut out for farming, they would have the option of joining the work-army to be trained for their proper work. At the same time they might earn part of their living by farming, as by our plan everyone would in time be within the radius of some cultural center. 

It will be the policy of the government to conclude treaties of unrestricted reciprocity with other countries, but only with such as have adopted the same policy of a minimum wage and maximum hours of labor the equivalent of our own, and minimum prices to their farmers for farm products not less than those paid to our This will prevent dumping. For the rest, the tariff would be no longer designed to protect profits and create millionaires, but only to protect labor by ensuring that the competition of the foreigner was fair and wholesome. Countries should get together for the purpose of aiding and supplementing one another, instead own. 26

of remaining in combative aloofness. America is the prize proposer of pious peace pacts. Should we not cut a better figure if we would enter the League of Nations and the World Court and work for peace, striving to bring about the establishment of an international economic planning board under the League (the logical conclusion of national, regional and local planning boards), and an international police force, with a perpetual moratorium on national armaments other than necessary police? 

Under our plan of the extension of democracy to the economic field, each economic group would be given much more power over its destinies. Judges, for example, would be appointed by the political executives from nominees of the bar, head-teachers from nominees of the teachers, hospital heads from nominees of the medical profession; and all workers in every trade and profession would be given much more power over the conditions under which they work. But leadership, initiative and mastery would never be scorned or hampered. Such changes, also, like the other changes that our plan contemplates, would be brought about democratically, and not by the dictatorial fiat of any impatient group or party, blue-print in hand, usurping the functions, whether permanently or "temporarily," of the political government duly elected by all the people.

In an economy thus democratically organized with a view to everyone's happiness, moral considerations will be primary; and many things done quite in the way of business today by our leading business men, with a host of small imitators, will be looked back upon with much the same curious interest with which we read today about the barbarities of the old time pirate and slaver. Under such a social system, also, the country would be spared the spectacle of veterans cashing in on their patriotism. 

We know how necessary are the experience and the reasoned judgment of all thoughtful citizens for the devising of a just economy, and we would welcome above all things their constructive criticism. As for the New Deal, it seems to have been conceived with the mentality of the Old Dealers, not only in the destruction of goods but in the policy of wages and prices. In 1932 

Coolidge and Willard exclaimed: "All we've got to do is to start buying!" And Ford joined in: ""All we've got to do is to start selling!" And so the new dealer says: In prosperity (the "prosperity" of 1929 for example!) we had high wages: therefore let us raise wages and break the buyers' strike! In prosperity we had 27

high prices: therefore let us raise prices and break the producers' strike! So shall we recover our glorious prosperity! There is little thought of social reconstruction here. It is almost "Back to normalcy!"

The hopeful thing about our President is his openness to suggestion and his willingness to experiment: 

If I don't see where I'm going, Tell me! I'd like to know! 

But th' important thing in going 

Is to go.

Let us not crowd behind the President like a great flock of sheep. Let us give him the kind of candid and thoughtful inspiration that he has a right to look for in a democracy. With so much courage and good-will, Roosevelt II must not become but another edition of the disappointing Roosevelt I. 

Here is a humble contribution. If anyone dislikes this plan in its entirety, let him suggest a better. I can conceive of no other plan short of Socialism, Fascism or Communism, that is to say, short of the ownership of industry by the democratic State (Socialism) or by a class-dictatorship (Fascism and Communism), that will effectually curb the profit motive, which is the root of all our trouble. 

Do we want to pattern after the Communists, for example? (and what better plan have the Socialists?):-commandeer all the farms, introducing large-scale, mass farming, and distribute the product with food-tickets? And to democratic Americans no Hitlerish agitation will ever make any white-shirted dictatorship look any better than the red-shirted variety. Yet that is what we are coming to if we do not work out a better plan. That is what profiteering capitalism has already brought us to in part industrially with its Standard Oil, General Electric, chain store, holding company, interlocking directorate octopuses, squeezing initiative out everywhere or shrewdly confiscating it for the sake of profits, making a few lords and many dependents, and making the most of us mere recipients of jobs and tips from the rich. Whom shall we blame for this?-these few rich? Would it not be more just and sane to blame ourselves in a democracy, and set to work to change so disastrous a trend by a plan that removes its cause and restores industry to the people? 28

Everywhere young people, for whom our profiteering civilization has no worthy task, individually or collectively, are meeting in groups and talking in terms of revolution, bloodshed, cataclysm, and magazines are talking about "The Next War." But what is intelligence for if not to devise a plan that would make us forego and forget those stupid methods of a discredited past? And I would say to these young men: After you have had your bloody revolution, you will still have to have a plan. Don't you think it would be better to have the plan first! And put it through by the modern, democratic, constructive method of agitation and a bill in Congress? And not by a civil war that would destroy more values than it created?

Is any helpful word likely to be spoken by the wealthy beneficiaries of injustice? Will they understand or pay any heed to anyone who appeals to them to "put humanity before profits”?

Judging by their public utterances, our profiteering economic system is perfectly satisfactory to Frank A. Vanderlip, Eugene G. 

Grace, Owen D. Young, Gerard Swope, Alfred P. Sloan, Ex-Secre tary Mills, John W. Davis and all the other bankers, industrialists and corporation lawyers whom it makes wealthy. Their scorn for all plans to mend it or end it is easily understood: but they have no solution for its evils.

The love of justice is too deep-seated in the human heart to be forever hypnotized and paralyzed by the flashy allurements of the business game that gambles with the very lives of the people. Let the wealthy beneficiaries of industry look around and see what kind of citizens with the sovereign right of the franchise this profitmaking game is giving us, and think what kind of country it is building and what kind of environment for their children. If business men would only take time to think and listen to the divine voice deep down in their hearts-deeper than their capitalism, deeper even than humanity that trusts itself-with that self-surrender to God that becomes a man, then there is no question but they would be led aright. Then this plan for a just economy would have a chance to win their thoughtful and impartial attention; and, if it appealed to them as a practical plan which would benefit the whole country and forever prevent a war of classes from arising in America, they would be prepared to make the necessary sacrifice that the plan demands of them for its success. For there are not any capitalists-i.e., investors of money and employers of laborwho would consciously sell their God and their fellowmen for thirty 29

pieces of silver or for thirty million. "Better is a little with righteousness than great revenues with injustice." 

On the other hand, the workingman, whether Communist or Socialist or Trade Unionist or entirely unorganized or even unemployed, has the same divine voice in his heart; and, if he would only listen to it, he would give up whatever desire he may be cherishing to dominate the country eventually by some kind of force or “direct action" in behalf of his peculiar aims, and would loyally accept a plan that commended itself to him as one that seeks to do justice not only to him but to the whole community, including the capitalist.

A good plan cannot be carried out unless honest men get behind it. But good men can do nothing with a bad plan. Heredity and environment, life and structure, spirit and form, are mutually operative. It is not a matter of indifference into what form of civilization (or barbarism) our children are born. It is not possible for good men to work effectively in a selfish profit-seeking economy that bedevils all merely symptomatic prescriptions of social reform. 

We are at the end of the Business Era. We are at the beginning of a Cultural Era. We are at the end of an era devoted to money and the things that money can buy. We are at the beginning of an era of devotion to mankind and the things that money cannot buy and that have been so conspicuously and disastrously lacking in the kind of world we have built for profits,-justice, honesty, purity, brotherhood, peace. The new age demands a new leadership of bolder imagination and saner enterprise.

If we have the will, surely we have the intelligence, to devise and live under a democratic plan by which we could get the world's work done without the bribing of the few and the enslavement of the many.

In his address to the NRA code authorities reported in the newspapers the other day, the President says: "In a word, we cannot tolerate abuses of economic powerabuses against labor, abuses against employers or abuses against the consuming public, whether they persist with the aid of codes or despite their prohibition." and yet this New Deal makes no attempt to eradicate Profits, but leaves in full play the mother of all these abuses, the Profit Motive. 30

Our President is engaged in an impossible task. Seeking to make America safe for Capitalism with its immoral basis, Profits, uneradicated, he is virtually engaged in the task of making America safe for immorality, a thing that no president, however determined, however popular and however "supported" by desperate bankers and industrialists with their backs to the wall,-will ever succeed in doing. 

The President issues this sportsmanlike and welcome challenge: "On the other hand, the critic is patriotic, whether he be a business man, a worker, a farmer or a politician, if he says, I don't like the methods you are using to solve the problem; I believe it would be far better if we were to use the following alternate method, -and thereupon outlines for the benefit of his neighbor and his government a helpful proposal."

Here it is. Here is a plan that at one stroke eradicates profits while retaining business capital in private hands and consequently private initiative, and renders unnecessary all the complicated NRA code machinery, bureaucratic, expensive, vexatious, unworkable, which must end at last in the fixing of all prices and wages, and whose futility is already abundantly demonstrated. Capitalism, under the slavish sway of the profit motive, while masquerading as economic liberty, might with more justice be called economic anarchy. It can therefore not be reformed and changed into economic democracy but by a major operation. We do not cut down a tree that is still capable of bearing fruit: we cut off the mouldering bough that threatens its life and its growth. We cannot have an economy that does justice to all and at the same time afford the luxury of profiteers.

The plan here outlined is clear in its aim and clear in its details. Its aim is not primarily prosperity but social justice and cultural progress; but precisely for that reason it provides a solid basis for true prosperity that neglects no "forgotten man." Its details are based upon solid statistical ground and are calculated to attain the aim in view. Its provisions are a logical conclusion from our democratic principle, completing cultural with vocational education, and with adult education, cultural and vocational, and completing political with economic democracy. It involves no arbitrary change, for its adoption and enforcement are not contemplated in any dictatorial way, whether fascist or communist, but 31

only in the American way of the people's will expressed in Congress.

It is up to the people. 

It is a shrewd man who distrusts his own shrewdness. Our 

President is a shining example. He is the beau ideal of a democratic Manager. If instead of depending upon a brain-trust of jangling elements, and instead of confidingly looking to the business world to reform itself, a business world, shot through with the profit motive, that has never in all its history shown any disposition to reform itself, and is in fact at this present moment shrewdly resolved to outwit him-if only the president had a plan of the wisdom of which he could be wholly convinced, a plan that makes a bold and open attack, direct and effective, upon the root of the evil, namely profits and the profit motive, then he would be the one man in the country today who could put it through and administer it, with the people intelligently as well as devotedly at his back; and business itself would bow to a popular will thus intelligently effective in a definitive plan, and adjust itself to the new order of business as a trust instead of a game, to a new plan that views business as a social and religious duty to the community instead of a predatory quest of power. 

It is up to the President.
