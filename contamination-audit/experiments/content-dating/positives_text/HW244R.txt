How This History of Wilkinsburg Came To Be Written 

HE Wilkinsburg Bank is 50 years old this year of 1946.

It was organized on January 23, 1896, received its charter on April 23 and opened for business on June 1. A 50th birthday seems definitely to call for celebration.

Men and women, as individuals, seldom look with happy satisfaction at the prospect of being fifty years old. It doesn't arouse much enthusiasm to realize that so much of your life is over, to find that the future is no longer quite so exciting as it once was.

Small wonder that many times the mind turns to the adventures, the pleasures, the accomplishments of the past—the kindnesses and friends, the funny incidents, the hard days, the successful undertakings, the griefs and the gaiety that made up half a century of lifetime.

A bank, being after all a group of men and women, feels pleasantly reminiscent too when it approaches fifty years of age. 

It enjoys thinking about the years it has spent as part of a fine community, like Wilkinsburg, for instance. Not in pride so much as in satisfaction, it likes to remember that it has kept well and safely the hard-earned savings entrusted to it over many years by its friends and neighbors; and how it has been able to help them to own their own homes and to finance their businesses, to assist them in meeting emergencies and getting things they wanted or needed. 

Brought face to face with our 50th birthday on January 23rd, we here at the bank also turned naturally enough to thinking about the home town in which many interesting and important events have happened since merging Indian trails were first tramped into great roads by American pioneers thronging west- 1

ward and Rippey opened his tavern here to provide them food and shelter. That was more than 160 years ago. It occurred to us that you might like to live through those historic and exciting years again with us.

It's a story of people, rather than business and streets and railroads and buildings, that we have been publishing in the 

Wilkinsburg Gazette over the past few months. People dream of houses and better ways to make a living, of a better city to live in and finer opportunities for their children—and then they struggle to make the dreams come true, against every sort of difficulty. And slowly but surely they win out—and their city, our city of Wilkinsburg, becomes a community to command our pride and affection.

Now we've made that story into this little book, thinking you'd like to have a copy in more permanent form. We hope you enjoy having it handy to refer to. We've tried our best to make it accurate and complete, even though it is brief. 

But while the bank turns to the past at its 50th birthday as people do, it takes even livelier interest in the future for 50 years is only a good start for a bank. We look forward to serving our neighbors in and near Wilkinsburg for another 50 years . . . or more. 2

A Brief History of Wilkinsburg 

1-Before Wilkinsburg Had A Name 

If you've ever thrilled to a James Fenimore Cooper yarn, you'll have no trouble in projecting yourself back into the days he wrote about, when Wilkinsburg was a forest wilderness through which moccasined Indian feet made trails between the Allegheny and Monongahela Rivers flowing serenely to their junction. 

Imagine yourself, in the year 1749, standing behind a great oak tree near the Allegheny, within a mile or two of present day Wilkinsburg, watching a captain of the French Army and his company of soldiers work their way through the undergrowth. The captain is Celeron de Bienville, and he has been sent by Louis XV of France to claim the land west of the Appa lachian Mountains.

Captain de Bienville knew it, so he was in somewhat of a hurry to reach the Indian chieftains in the river valleys, to convince them that the French would be better allies than the English. 

He carried nice friendly gifts of guns, gunpowder and firewater from Louis to prove his Majesty's goodwill. These beneficent presents the redskins accepted gratefully and five years later proved their friendship by doing battle for the French against the English.

But in the spring of 1754 the English came tramping in along the Indian trail that was to become the Great Road, unimpressed by French claims. Imagine yourself, this time, openly cheering them as they filed past you in the dense woods of a still unimagined corner of Penn and Ross Avenues-or feeling sorry for them as their remnants straggled back the seven miles from the triangle of the rivers a few weeks later, their partly built log fort surrendered to the vigilant French and Indians, who completed it and named it Fort Duquesne. 

Next year the English came back. This territory was the strategic gateway to the west and too important to leave in enemy control. But General Braddock, stubbornly holding to the open battlefield tradition of Europe and spurning the advice of frontier scouts, marched his brilliantly red-clad troops in bold battle formation down the Turtle Creek Valley to the Monongahela River, where death, crackling from behind every tree, mowed them down in the bloody battle of Braddock's Field, in which the general himself fell and died. At the juncture of

today's William Penn and Lincoln Highways, you could almost have heard the historic gunfire of "Braddock's Rout," only four miles away.

It took the British three years to get around to sending another expedition to the Allegheny and Monongahela Valleys, this time under a smarter leader, General Forbes, who widened the trail from the east into the Great Road to bring in his military vehicles. So ill that he directed the battle preparations from a litter borne by two horses, he cannily divided his forces some miles to the east, sending young Colonel George Washington and his troops over the Great Road and Colonel Henry Bouquet and his men on a swing down the Turtle Creek Valley and back up to meet Washington on the outskirts of present day Wilkinsburg, at a spot named then the Bullock Pens because of that early army's cattle corral, now called Beulah Church. 

But De Ligneries, French commander of Fort Duquesne, was no fool. After his Indian scouts had raced breathlessly into the fort to announce that the English, camped only seven miles away, were as numerous as the trees in the forest, he ordered the fort destroyed and escaped northward along the Ohio River. 

The English moved in next day, rebuilt the shattered bulwarks and named it Fort Pitt after William Pitt, then Prime Minister of England. 

Peace for five short years, while pioneer settlers poured in over the Great Road. Then Pontiac laid siege to Fort Pitt in 

1763. For a whole year stubborn Indians made life hazardous for the early settlers until Colonel Henry Bouquet broke the blockade by defeating the wily Cornplanter at the battle of Bushy Run, chasing him down the Ohio to the forks of the Muskingum where a treaty of peace was signed by the chiefs of the Senecas, Delawares and Shawnees.

It seems certain that there were then settlers in Wilkinsburg but no record of them remains. In 1770 they must have been busy felling trees to build their crude log cabins, hunting in the forest, fishing for their food and going to open-air religious services at Bullock Pens. The forest grows thinner, civilization has won a foothold in the patch of wilderness that was to become Wilkinsburg.

2-The Settlement Gets A Name 

The British Parliament made 1765 memorable by passing the Stamp Act, and four years later came the Boston Massacre. That same year the Land Company of the Colony of Pennsylvania opened territory for sale in its western counties. Among the early rush of buyers was Andrew 

Levi Levy, Sr., who made application No. 3122 for a tract of 266 acres which included present Wilkinsburg. More important was the Boston 

Tea Party in 1773, the first Continental Congress the following year and then Paul Revere was riding, and Concord echoed with the shot beard 'round the world.

The American Revolution retarded but did not stop the movement of settlers to the west and after peace with England in 1782 the Monongahela and Allegheny River Valleys felt the tread of many eager new feet. By 1784 the settlers around the junction of the Great Road and the present Lincoln Highway decided that they needed a place for worship, built a Presbyterian log church at Bullock Pens and called it simply Bullock Pens Church. 

Colonel Dunning McNair, founder of Wilkinsburg, and his wife, the former Anne Stewart, came west in November of 1786.

They liked the small settlement where the roads met, built a log cabin above the Great Road between what is now Mill and Coal Streets and named it the "Crow's Nest." Here, in January of 1787, their first child, David, was born and died shortly afterward.

Andrew Levy, Sr., had bought his Land Grant for speculation, but the years of confusion during the American Revolution gave him no market, so he conveyed the patent to General William Thompson of Westmoreland County in 1788 for 45 pounds and 5 shillings. General Thompson died within a year and his heirs sold the title to Dunning McNair for 322 pounds and 10 shillings, a matter of $763.18. It was that same year, 1789, that travelers brought the news that George Washington, the young colonel who had helped rout the French and Indians from the Pittsburgh area, had been inaugurated president of the new country on 

April 30.

The next year, Colonel McNair laid out a plan of lots and gave the village the name of McNairstown. He made the

One east and west street he named Ross, in honor of James Ross, his lawyer friend, and another Wallace, in honor of William Wallace, whose name appears on many early deeds as "Wallace, who claimed land." Each village lot had a frontage of 66 feet and a depth of 264, running clear through from its front street to the one behind it. Lot and house numbers began with 1 on the south side of Main Street at Nine-Mile Run, ran west the length of the town on that side and back on the north side of the street.

Chief excitement in the year 1790 was when the residents were aroused by the cry of "Fire" and ran to help Colonel 

McNair salvage what he could of the "Crow's Nest"-but it burned to the ground. The Colonel's new home, which he built immediately at what is now Hay and Kelly Streets, was the most pretentious in the community. It was faced in cobblestones set in mortar, which a facetious guest said looked like apple dumplings and "Dumpling Hall" it was called from then on.

In Dumpling Hall the McNairs dispensed a generous hospitality that made their home the most popular place in the countryside. But the Colonel was also a farsighted leader in his community. He started the first school by hiring Adam 

Turner as a tutor for his children and inviting other boys and girls of McNairstown to attend classes in his home. While

serving in the Pennsylvania state legislature he proposed that schools be provided for the youth of the state. He led the movement for a much needed division of Allegheny County into a number of smaller counties, each with its own county seat; and it was he who first proposed that slavery be abolished in Pennsylvania.

Along about this time, another famous influence, the Wilkins family, settled in McNairstown, and two brothers, John and William, promptly took an active interest in community affairs. 

In 1794, James Kelly, also to become important to the town, was born in Sandy Creek during the hubbub over the Whiskey Insurrection. Wilkinsburg's early great men were arriving. 

3-McNairstown Becomes "Wilkinsburgh"

Shut out of eastern markets for their grain by the high cost of shipping overland, early farmers of Western Pennsylvania had found it profitable to condense their bulky crops into whiskey which brought them 25 cents a gallon. It was more easily transported as well as sold locally. But when the government levied an excise tax of 7 cents a gallon on it, in 1794, 8000 of them hurried from all over four counties to Braddock's Field in open rebellion, planning an armed assault upon Pittsburgh. 

Word that President George Washington and 13,000 soldiers were on the way changed their minds and the angry group disbanded.

McNairstown was only nine years old in 1799 when the news came that George Washington had died, and old-time settlers gathered in sorrowful little groups on Main Street to talk about how he had helped rout the Indians in 1755, freeing the land for them.

As the new century started, so began an era of expansion and improvement for McNairstown and the nation. Thomas

Jefferson, the country's third president, had been inaugurated in 1801 and a year later the United States Military Academy at West Point was established. In 1804 McNairstown residents watched a strange procession make its way along the Great Road, a group of fearless men headed by Rogers and Clark, off to their discovery of the Northwest Passage and Oregon. That same year the name of the little church that had been built at 

Bullock Pens was changed to Beulah and James Graham installed as pastor. Alexander Hamilton fell, mortally wounded by a bullet fired by Aaron Burr, and three years later Burr was being acquitted of a charge of treason. 

In 1812, a deed recording the sale of two acres of land to 

Patrick Green by Dunning McNair, first mentions the name of 

"Wilkinsburgh" in connection with the settlement. A controversy still exists as to whether the town was named for John or William Wilkins. John was a large property holder, a prominent citizen, but held no public office and was said to be of a retiring disposition. William, however, was one of the best known citizens of the community, a founder and the first president of the Bank of Pittsburgh, and a county judge when the name of the town was changed. In the same year Samuel A. 

Rippey, keeper of the famous Rippey's Tavern, died, leaving $264.55 worth of property, including "four beds and bedding and five bedsteads," to his wife, Sutiah, who bravely carried on

with the business so successfully that McNairstown was often referred to as Rippeyville by travelers.

In 1816, when neighboring Pittsburgh became a city, Wilkinsburg was still a village of one main street. Wood Street was called simply "The Lane"; Center Street was "Center Lane"; 

Wallace Street was "Horner's Lane"; Hay Street was "Kelly's Lane."

About this time James Kelly came to Wilkinsburg to live. 

A well-built man of medium height, young Kelly was smoothshaven but wore his hair in a queue as a result of having lost an election bet, the penalty being never to cut his hair again. Wilkinsburg's first schoolhouse was built in 1840 at Center and 

Wallace Streets. "Old Jimmy" 

Kelly, whose policies helped mold Wilkinsburg into the borough it is today, donated the ground and the material for its construction.

And he never did, even through all the years he was Wilkinsburg's foremost citizen.

In 1820 local residents went to the polls and helped elect Judge William Wilkins to the State Senate-a step forward in the long and brilliant political career he was to have. Through him and McNair, Wilkinsburg's political influence was early felt far beyond its own village boundaries. 

4-Wilkinsburg Begins To Build Schools And Churches 

As early Wilkinsburg prospered, the man who had contributed most to its growth came upon unfortunate days. Deeply in debt, Dunning McNair had to sell his large holdings to 10

satisfy his creditors. He did not long survive the shock of his losses, dying in 1825 in his beloved Dumpling Hall, all that remained of his once great estate. 

The unofficial responsibility for the well-being of the village was undertaken by James Kelly, who succeeded to ownership of most of the McNair property. He moved into Dumpling 

Hall and carried on in the McNair tradition, even to maintaining the private school in the hall with Napoleon Bonaparte Hatch as tutor.

The big event of 1825 occurred late in May when Wilkinsburg people gathered in great excitement along Penn Avenue to watch the passage of a stagecoach and to cheer a famous French gentleman with white powdered hair who smiled and waved at them. He was the Marquis de Lafayette on his way back east after visiting the frontiers of the America he had fought to make independent.

The State of Pennsylvania had passed the first school law in 1824 but gave each community the choice of adopting it by popular vote. Sad to relate, for several years a wordy battle raged over it in Wilkinsburg. "Old Jimmy" Kelly led the fight for public schools, but it wasn't until 1834, three years after William Wilkins had been elected to the United States Senate, that the Pennsylvania Free Common School Act was adopted by the town. nues.

James Kelly, backing up his principles, donated the materials and the money to build the first public schoolhouse in Wilkinsburg in 1840, a one-room building at Center and Wallace Ave- The first teacher was Napoleon Bonaparte Hatch. The same year saw the establishment of a post office in Wilkinsburg with Abram Stoner as postmaster. The village was growing fast-you could count thirty-eight houses along Penn Avenue and its branching lanes.

The problem of fire was beginning to worry the townspeople that year and there was general satisfaction when an unofficial volunteer fire-fighting organization was formed. All males of the town over 12 years of age agreed to turn out at the sound of the school bell with any piece of fire fighting equipment they could lay their hands on. 11

In 1842, Williams Wilkins went to Russia on a mission for 

President Tyler, the pride of his fellow citizens in little Wilkinsburg. A year later the townspeople were watching carpenters building the Methodist Episcopal Church on the north side of Wallace Avenue-the first church to be erected in the village proper. Then in 1844 William Wilkins took over the highest government post in which he was to serve, Secretary of War in President Tyler's cabinet. Next year another church was being built in Wilkinsburg—the Reformed Presbyterian at Center and 

South Avenues.

The village could now boast a post office, a school and three churches. Craftsmen were beginning to appear. If an early resident wanted a chair made, he went to Sam Meyler. 

If he was affluent enough to have a carpet in his home, he called on William Minter, the carpet-weaver. David Lytle was doing carpentry jobs around town, Smith Leacock was the first blacksmith and George Johnston the first wagon-maker. There were four merchants-Abram Stoner (who ran the post office in his general store), Samuel McCrea, Edward Thompson and Charles Carothers.

If anyone in the village was ill enough to require the services of a doctor he sent for Dr. James Carothers. Dr. Carothers had a large practice for early records indicate that he was sometimes assisted by Dr. John Kuhn of neighboring Penn Township. A 

brother of Squire James Horner, the Justice of the Peace, Dr.

John Horner, lived in the old Horner farm on the old Frankstown road and what is now Oakwood Street and practiced in that section. 

In 1844, Alexander Morse startled the world with the invention of the telegraph and the message "What hath God wrought?" Two years later the Pennsylvania Railroad was incorporated and in 1848 Howe invented the sewing machine and the magic cry of "Gold" was heard from the far west, resulting in the famous gold rush to California in 1849. 

Wilkinsburg touched immortal greatness around 1850 when 

Stephen Collins Foster, composer of songs that are still among 

America's favorites, was a frequent guest in the Kelly, Wilkins, Turner, Duff and Morrow homes.

Streets. The first schoolhouse, erected only ten years before, was torn down and a new one-a two-room brick building, erected on the same spot. The village was steadily growingbut nothing gave more promise of future growth than the rumor that sped through the town that year that the newly incorporated 

Pennsylvania Railroad would run its track through Wilkinsburg! 

5-The First Railroad Train Arrives 

By 1851 Wilkinsburg was large enough to be listed in the Traveler's Guide as a stagecoach stop between Pittsburgh and Adamsburg. There were four taverns in the village to accommodate hungry (and thirsty) travelers and the favorite was the

Seven Mile House at Penn and Hay Avenues. 

The only school building in Wilkinsburg was the one at Center and Wallace Streets until 1852 when James Huston opened a private school which he called the Wilkinsburg Academy. 

It was co-educational and attracted students from miles around. 

It was the only institution higher than the common school in the section and prepared many local boys for eastern colleges. 

The first train into Wilkinsburg on December 10, 1852, was pulled by the puffy, selfimportant, woodburning engine, the "F. K. Heisley." A dinky string of redpainted passenger cars behind the engine were lighted with oil lamps.

All that year the talk of the town was the coming of the new railroad. The crews of men grading the roadbed and laying the track whetted the interest to a sharp climax on the day in 

December of 1852 when the first passenger train blew its whistle for the stop at the village station. Almost the entire population 13

came running to see the strange spectacle-strange because probably only a few of them had ever seen a train before. They stared in admiration at the puffy self-important "F. K. Heisley, a wood-burning, funnel-stack locomotive, with its colorful string of dinky passenger cars, painted red and equipped with oil lamps, strung out behind it. 

But much of the talk among the men was about the new opportunities the railroad would bring them. Wilkinsburg was now linked to its larger neighbor, Pittsburgh, where there was work to be found. There were few chances for trades or professions in Wilkinsburg and a daily drive to and from Pittsburgh was out of the question. The new trains untied the men from the land, took them to the city to make their livings, a development significant in Wilkinsburg's history and in its character today.

In 1855 the world took another great step forward with the completion of a trans-Atlantic cable between Cape Breton, Nova Scotia, and Newfoundland. The Crimean War, with England and France allied against 

Russia, ended with the seizure of Sevastopol on September 8th. 

James Huston, owner of the Wilkinsburg Academy, had been in ill health for some time, and by 1856 his physical condition made it impossible for him to keep the Academy going. 

A group of Wilkinsburg residents, realizing the value of the school, formed a stock company, reorganized the institution and housed it in its own building on Wallace Avenue at the northeast corner of Center Street.

Local people went into Pittsburgh in 1859 to see another innovation in community transportation when, with a clanging of trace chains and the creaking of new leather, the first horse drawn streetcar made its initial trip. The streets were crowded with watchers as the car bobbed and swayed its way out Penn Avenue, through old Bayardstown, to the forks of the Road at 

Thirty-Fourth Street. What will mechanical genius do next, they wondered.

The railroad opened up the coal age in Wilkinsburg history. 

Although the first locomotive to run through Wilkinsburg was a wood-burner, it was followed a few years later by others fueled with coal. Coal mines had been operating in the Pittsburgh area for many years but no record of a coal mine in the borough proper can be found before 1860. That was when

James Kelly opened one on his own property, north of Penn Avenue on Weinman Street, with his son, James, as operator. 

Others in the vicinity were the Duquesne Mine, also opened the same year, one up near Crab Hollow (so called by village youngsters who caught crawfish there-later Crab Hollow Road, now Swissvale Avenue), one at the head of Coal Street, the 

Hampton Mine, and one other nearby. Most of them were opened long after 1860, but all of them were called Dog Mines, so named because dogs were used to draw out the coal cars loaded by the miners. Mining tools were very primitive; one of them, called a sconce—an iron pick, one point of which was driven into the mine wall with a candle spiked to the other-was found years later in the Turtle Creek Valley. 

In 1860 the "Wilkinsburg Academy, Male and Female, English and Classical High School" was again forced to close. News from the 

South grew alarming in 1861. Jefferson Davis was elected President of the Confederacy. Fort Sumter was fired upon April 12 and by May 13 the first land battle of the Civil War was raging at Bull Run. 

6-Wilkinsburg's Civil War Years 

Wilkinsburg had a population of 100 in 1861, the first year of the Civil War, and eighteen of its young men went marching off to war. 

The bloody struggle surged through Virginia in 1861, with fierce fighting in Fredericksburg near the end of the year. In 1863 Abraham Lincoln 15

freed the slaves under the Emancipation Proclamation. Lee and the flood tide of rebellion were turned back at Gettysburg in July. Later that month Wilkinsburg was talking about the draft riots in New York City that killed over 1000 persons, caused over $2,000,000 in property damage. In November, the tall homely figure of an immortal president rose at Gettysburg to make a brief somber speech, destined to live in the literary annals of his country. Ulysses S. Grant became 

Commander-in-Chief of the Union Army in 1864 and the war moved to its finish. Sherman was marching across Georgia in May, by Christmas had captured Savannah. In April of 1865 the last shot was fired.

Wilkinsburg's young men began to straggle home. All came back save one, James McWilliams, who was killed in battle.

But even while the community was rejoicing over the return of its loved ones, it grieved over the news from the east. Abraham 

Lincoln had been treacherously shot by John Wilkes Booth in a Washington theater. Clinging to life for 24 hours, he died on 

April 15. His slayer was caught 11 days later near Port Royal, Virginia, and shot to death.

Wilkinsburg felt the shock of another loss in 1865; William 

Wilkins, its leading citizen, passed away. But even as the funeral cortege rolled through the grounds of the spacious mansion built in 1836, construction was going ahead on another dwelling that was to surpass the Wilkins home in magnificence. 

John F. Singer, an iron manufacturer, who purchased the land from James Kelly, was building his famous home on Wood Street, between John and Nimick Streets. 

In 1867 Alaska was purchased by the United States from Russia for $7,000,000. Two years later a gold spike was driven into the railroad track near Ogden, Utah, joining the Central and Union Pacific, creating the first transcontinental railroad in the United States. 

That same year of 1869 Wilkinsburg began to stir out of the post-war doldrums and to make beginnings in organizing industry and welfare and social groups. George Westinghouse was founding the Westinghouse Air Brake Company. A group of generous townspeople gathered to discuss the problem of the community's aged, and from that meeting was born the Home for Aged Protestant Women Association. A group of men founded the W. H. DeVore Lodge of the Independent Order of 

Odd Fellows on June 22. 16

James Kelly in 1870 gave the land on which "The Sheltering Arms," a home for aged Protestant men and couples, was built. 

This was a very active year for Mr. Kelly; among other things he took the lead in a movement to make Wilkinsburg "dry." 

His chief opposition, of course, came from the four tavernkeepers, whose revenue was largely from the sale of whiskey. 

It was an exciting controversy, with the Kelly supporters arguing for suppression of whiskey and the tavern-keepers and their friends shouting loudly for its continuance. Petitions were circulated, oratory was rampant, everybody voted-and the "Drys" won by a large margin. Wilkinsburg has remained "dry" ever since, except for the brief period after repeal of the National 

Prohibition Act in 1933. 

The year of the great Chicago fire, 1871, Wilkinsburg people felt themselves tricked when a group of smart politicians quietly annexed the town to Pittsburgh, making it the extreme eastern ward of that city. Again James Kelly led the local war for freedom, carrying the case to the Supreme Court at his own expense, proving that many of the names signed to the petition for annexation were those of people living outside the village. 

The Supreme Court ruled in "Old Jimmy's" favor and Wilkinsburg won its independence again. That same year Mr. Kelly gave another tract of land for the construction of a Home for 

Aged Protestant Women. 

In the 1870's Wilkinsburg's industries were a grist-mill, a saw-mill, a stone quarry and a few coal mines. There were no sidewalks, except occasionally one of board and cinders; no sewers; no lights except for an occasional oil lamp. The town spent its nights, for the most part, in darkness. It was part of 

Wilkins township, the government of which seemed to interest itself only in roads and schools.

Even then the public minded citizens of Wilkinsburg had a plan in mind to take over the government of the community themselves, to correct the wrongs of the half-hearted administration they lived under. They wanted to make the town a borough, but the time was not yet ripe for action. 17

WILKINSSUNG Wilkinsburg's first railroad station came to an unfortunate end in 1873 when fire burned it to the ground. Poorly equipped but willing fire-fighters had little chance to save the furiously blazing wooden structure. 

7-Wilkinsburg Loses James Kelly

Financial panic gripped the nation after the close of the Civil War, and with the rest of the country Wilkinsburg felt the pinch of the post-war years. Probably no one local individual was so disastrously affected as James Kelly. His entire fortune was in real estate, his equities spread rather thin through the mortgaging of one piece of land to help buy another. He had been a generous lender, too, and not always a wise one. 

In order to meet his obligations now be became a heavy borrower at the Pittsburgh banks.

The third public school to be built in the village was erected in 1872 on Wallace Avenue near the Wilkinsburg Academy. A squat, square building, the pupils nicknamed it the "Sheep Pen." 

That same year Wilkinsburg Lodge No. 384 of the Knights of

Pythias was organized, with club rooms in a log cabin at the corner of Penn and Wood Streets. The Pennsylvania Railroad 

Station burned to the ground in 1873. Next year, and the fourth year after the town had been voted "dry," the Women's 

Christian Temperance Union was organized in Wilkinsburg.

That was the year Wilkinsburg tongues buzzed with the news of the sensational kidnapping of Charley Ross from his home in Germantown, Pennsylvania, and the conviction of notorious "Boss" Tweed, political czar of New York City, on charges of fraud, sending him off to prison for 12 years. Those were the days of the Molly Maguires, an 

Irish-American secret society that had been terrorizing Pennsylvania miners throughout the 1870's; but they were less of a terror after 1877 when 11 of them were hanged for murder. 18

In came the final crash of James Kelly's fortune. The borrowings in Pittsburgh had only postponed disaster. His notes past due and unable to pay them, the unfortunate old man had no choice but to submit to foreclosure. His holdings were sold at a public sale, said to have been the largest sheriff's sale of individual tracts and lots in the history of Allegheny County. Most of the large parcels of land were immediately laid out in smaller lots that sold quickly. Many were resold several times, doubling and redoubling in price. It turned out to be a real estate boom, the town jumped in population and buildings, ironically too late to save the man whose misfortune had started it. 

Wilkinsburg got its first newspaper in 1880, "The Call" published by A. H. Silvey. In 1881 T. D. Turner was opening 

Wilkinsburg's first funeral home at 720 Penn Avenue. Undoubtedly one of the reasons for Wilkinsburg's growth during the early eighties was the fact that Pittsburgh was expanding out of itself; and many of its residents, attracted by the idea of more country-like suburban houses, saw in the public sale of James Kelly's holdings the opportunity to buy desirable land in Wilkinsburg for their homes. They could commute to and from the city on the Pennsylvania's famous accommodation trains. 

Mr. Kelly did not long survive the loss of his possessions. 

His friends arranged for him to continue to live in Dumpling Hall until his death in 1882. He left to the town in which he had spent the greater part of his life a great legacy of integrity, high purpose and vision, in addition to the more tangible gifts of land for schools, churches and benevolent institutions of every type.

That year local residents watched the completion of a new fifteen-room school building and the Home for Aged Protestants adjoining the Home for Aged Protestant Women. They couldn't help wishing that Mr. Kelly could have lived to see the fruits of his gifts.

The big new schoolhouse didn't last long, however, for in 1883 it burned to the ground. The determined volunteer firemen fought the blaze for hours without adequate equipment but could not save the building. One year later the Western Penn- 19

sylvania School for the Deaf was erected on land James Kelly had given for the purpose.

Wilkinsburg had a population of 800 in 1880-by 1885 it was a thriving community of 2500. The acute need of gas, water and electricity became an active topic of conversation; some way must be found to get them and to exercise control over them. A progressive group, led by such citizens as A. W. 

Duff, James Horner, James Wilson, James Ralph, R. A. Ralph,

Rev. W. C. Smith and others, began agitation for a borough government. 

8-Wilkinsburg Becomes A Borough 

In 1885 the foresighted People's Natural Gas Company, hearing rumors of a move for a borough government, began laying lines into Wilkinsburg. But that municipal development wasn't to come without a struggle—just about half the residents of the town were against it. In 1886 a petition for the new political set-up was circulated and signed by 213 persons, which was a majority of the resident freeholders in the territory proposed for incorporation. The opponents filed a counter-petition, claiming the signers of the first petition were for the most part persons who had only recently come out of the city and built homes in Wilkinsburg. The Grand Jury was convinced by this interesting logic and turned down the petition. The little group of progressives prepared to fight.

In the fall of that year a group of citizens proposed a tax on the merchants, to raise money for lighting the streets-but the plan fell through. On October 28, France's gift to America, the Statue of Liberty, was unveiled on Bedloe's Island before 1,000,000 people. A year later, another pro-borough petition was circulated. Again the opposition was vociferous but again the petition was signed by a majority and again submitted to the Grand Jury. In spite of the strenuous objections by its opponents, the Grand Jury this time granted the petition and on October 5, 1887, Wilkinsburg became a borough. Even then the objectors carried the matter to the Supreme Court, which closed the argument by denying their petition. The third

9—Wilkinsburg's Era of Civic Improvement

10-The Panic of 1893 

Although the population of Wilkinsburg had soared to 3000 in 1892, nothing had been done about the installation of a sewage system. This condition of danger finally spurred the Council to action and in 1893 it adopted a resolution to confer with similar committees representing the boroughs of Edgewood and 

Brushton, to consider a joint system for the three towns.

There was the usual delay, during which Wilkinsburg hired engineers to study the situation in other cities. Finally, after a public meeting, Council adopted a plan for a sewage system estimated to cost $160,000. A bond issue was put up for vote, there was opposition, as usual, but the people approved it and construction started. It is amazing to present day readers to learn that forty-five miles of pipe were installed at a cost of 24

$148,209, nearly $12,000 under the estimate and the money available.

The year 1893 started out busily. Joseph Daibler and E. M.

But toward the end of the year business collapsed and the 

Great Panic of '93 was on. Thousands of men were idle in 

Western Pennsylvania. Blast furnaces, rolling mills and glass factories closed down; coal works and coke ovens were bleak and dead, while throngs of men congregated in the towns and cities.

Wilkinsburg, though a factoryless residential community, suffered acutely through the next year when the panic was at its worst. As always, the spirit of charity was active in the borough in both public and private help to those in distress. 

Local people shook their heads at news of violence in Chicago, where both Federal troops and state militia were called out to subdue rioting workers striking by order of Eugene V. Debs, perennial Socialist candidate for president.

Coxey's Army, starting from Massillon, Ohio, marched through Wilkinsburg on its way to Washington, D. C., to demand work and adjustment of grievances growing out of the panic. In spite of the fact that Wilkinsburg's unemployed cheered the "Army" as it marched past, Coxey himself was arrested and thrown in jail. But the weary marchers continued on their way and Coxey was soon released to rejoin them. 

Not much relief in the nation-wide panic was noticed until 1895. Wilkinsburg residents first felt the pressure begin to ease when the Westinghouse Plant was built in Turtle Creek Valley that year.

Most of the youngsters were injured but luckily none was killed. The son of Judge Duff, borough solicitor, was first believed to be lost, but he was found under the wreckage. 

Though badly burned, prompt treatment revived him and he recovered.

16-Wilkinsburg's Golden Anniversary 

Wilkinsburg was hit hard by the strikes and unemployment, the financial troubles and business depression of the year of 1930. 

Relief organizations in the community were kept busy caring for the needy. For greater strength in the face of unprecedented conditions that tested even the most powerful industrial institutions, the First National Bank and the Central National 

Bank merged. 

The Wilkinsburg Bank sadly faced the year 1931 without the guidance of Robert L. Finley-one of the bank's first officers and president when he died in February. J. M. Ralston succeeded him and E. D. Rudolph was promoted to cashier. The 

Colored Community Club of Wilkinsburg was organized that year. Wilkinsburg read with interest the news of the completion of New York City's Empire State Building, world's tallest structure.

Teachers Association were organized. The Board of Trade changed its name to the Wilkinsburg Chamber of Commerce on

17-World War II Years 

In 1938 Hitler's Nazis were marching into little Austria and the flames of war were set burning again. John McDowell, editor and publisher of the Wilkinsburg Gazette, was elected to 41

Wilkinsburg's new municipal building, or city ball, was opened and dedicated on

New Year's Day, 1940. Housing all the legislative and judicial agencies of the borough, the new building also bolds the Wil kinsburg Library. 

Congress, and James A. Wilson, one of Wilkinsburg's first councilmen, died. Charles Marvel, president of the Wilkinsburg Bank, died and William G. Marshall succeeded him. Ground was broken for the new Municipal Building at Ross and Hay Streets. 

Construction started on the Pennsylvania Turnpike in 1939. Wilkinsburg and the world held its breath while an aging man with an umbrella tried to stave off war in Europe and worried when his efforts failed and France and England declared war on Germany. Thanksgiving Day was moved forward one week that year by proclamation of the President. One of Wilkinsburg's oldest landmarks, the Brace

Brothers Laundry building, created in 1880, was torn down. Parking meters appeared on the borough's streets.

The first day of the new year of 1940 the new Municipal Building was dedicated. The Standard Savings and Loan Association moved to its present location at 774 Penn Avenue. 

Wilkinsburg people were reading with great enjoyment the "Annals of Old Wilkinsburg and Vicinity," first fruits of the work of the Group for Historical Research on Wilkinsburg and Vicinity, founded six years before. The Selective Service Draft began in the fall and the nation's National Guard organizations were mobilized to train the draftees. A precedent-shattering election made Franklin D. Roosevelt the first third-term president of the United States. Mussolini cast his lot with Hitler, and 

Italy entered the war on the side of Germany. 

In 1941 the Red Cross Volunteer Nurses Aide Corps organized for work in Columbia Hospital. Dasch's Men's Store moved to 42

Wood Street. The United States had already inaugurated its Lend-Lease policy; the electrifying Jap attack on Pearl Harbor on December 7 ended all argument about isolationism and neutrality. Wilkinsburg folk gathered around their radios on the grim Monday of December 8 to hear the President declare the nation at war.

The Westinghouse Club became the Westinghouse Educational Center in 1942. Local conversation centered on the war-the fall of the Philippines, the arrival of U. S. troops in North

Ireland. By the end of the year Allied forces had begun to hit at the Axis at Guadalcanal and in North Africa. 

Next year Hanlon and Wilson, manufacturers, were developing an airplane heater for use in the war and the Methods Engineering Company was busy establishing a branch office in Kansas City. The Hamilton Venetian Shade Company moved in 1944 

to 205 Penn Avenue. The Lion's Club organized the Youth 

Center, furnishing wholesome recreation for the young people of Wilkinsburg. The Methods Engineering Company moved into Pittsburgh but by the next year it was back again, on the top floor of the Shields Building. Franklin D. Roosevelt was elected to his fourth term as president.

In April of 1945 Wilkinsburg and the nation were shocked by the sudden death of President Roosevelt. Vice-President 

Harry S. Truman was sworn in as Chief Executive and less than a month later the war in Europe was over when Germany surrendered to the Allies who had occupied most of the country. 

Japan gave up in August after suffering the effects of two atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Wilkinsburg remembered the experiments Westinghouse had conducted eight years before and felt local pride for the part Westinghouse engineers had had in development of the bomb. On Armistice Day, E. R. Kregar, a director of the Wilkinsburg Bank for 12 years, died. And Wilkinsburg was further saddened the following month by the death of Elizabeth Davison, co-author of "Annals of Old Wilkinsburg and Vicinity."

Wilkinsburg today is a city of 35,000, the largest borough in the state. Continuing in the way mapped by her early planners, she is a city of homes, churches, schools, hospitals and benevo- 43

lent institutions, organizations for betterment and progress; a city with a great tradition, a forward looking city. A city of people rather than one of industry and commerce, Wilkinsburg is a good place to live.
