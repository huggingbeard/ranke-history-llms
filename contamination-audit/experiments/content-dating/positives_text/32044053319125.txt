I will start by addressing Korea, move through the countries southward to Australia, then north and discuss the situation in Southeast Asia ending up with a report on Vietnam and the current situation in Indochina in general.

The North Koreans are unpredictable, but they are implacably hostile to the South Korean Government and determined to unify the country under Com munist rule. They pose a very real threat, with a large and well-equipped army, an air force with modern jet aircraft, and a growing patrol boat force. North 

Korea is dependent upon either Soviet or Red Chinese backing for any all-out assault upon the south, but has pursued a policy of harassment of South Korea and the United States in recent years.

North Korean incidents along the demilitarized zone and deep in South Korea reached a disturbing peak at the end of 1968, remained at a low level during 1969, and then took an upward turn near the end of last year. 

I suspect that Kim Ill Song has allowed these incidents to fall off because he would like for the United States, in particular, to believe that things are going to be very quiet which may further expedite the withdrawal of our own forces from South Korea. The North Korean conventional and unconventional threat to South Korea remains undiminished since the end of the Korean War. The North Korean Army is a well-trained, well-balanced force. It numbers at least 350,000 men, organized into the equivalent of 20 small, but powerful divisions. The army is equipped with tactical missiles, artillery and mortar pieces above 82mm, rockets, and tanks. The AK-47 assault gun provides the individual soldier with considerably more fire power than his South Korean counterpart.

Although numerically larger, the South Korean Army suffers a modernization problem. Much of the equipment is of Korean War vintage and some equipment still in use is of World War II vintage. Combat infantry divisions have less than the total number of required wheeled vehicles and engineer equipment is both in short supply and old.

The North Korean Navy has primarily a defensive mission. Major combatants include a small fleet of submarines and guided missile patrol boats. 

The South Korean Navy is small, but well trained and well disciplined. Its effectiveness is impaired by the limited speed and obsolescence of existing ships. 

There is a serious need to supplement this force with some destroyers and smaller anti-infiltration boats. Virtually all South Korean ships are World War II vintage.

The North Korean Air Force presents the major threat to the security of South Korea, primarily because of its capability to launch a surprise low-level attack. Included in the inventory are over 450 jet fighters and bombers.

Mr. Chairman and Members of the Committee, I am here today to testify in support of the proposed Foreign Military Sales and Military Assistance Program for fiscal year 1972 in the Middle East, Southern Asia and Africa South of the (491) ADD

Sahara, an area referred to as MEAFSA. As this committee is well aware, the 

Secretary of State, under the direction of the President is responsible for determining whether there shall be a military assistance or sales program and the value of the program for any country. The Secretary of Defense is responsible for managing, executing and supervising the programs. As the United States Commander-in-Chief of MEAFSA, I am responsible for recommending to the Secre tary of Defense what defense articles and military training should be included in the military assistance and sales programs for the countries in my area of responsibility. I am also responsible for the management, execution and super vision of the MEAFSA programs. A sketch map of MEAFSA and a description of my command have been provided for your use.

Before I discuss the proposed fiscal year 1972 military assistance and sales programs for MEAFSA, I would like to give you my views on the politico-military situation in the area.

From a security standpoint the most critical region in the MEAFSA area remains the Middle East. The prolongation of the Arab-Israeli dispute keeps alive the ultimate danger of a big power confrontation-particularly since Moscow has intensified its involvement by sending Soviet advisors, Soviet manned air defense units, and other highly sophisticated military equipment to the United Arab 

Republic. 

The reliance upon Soviet aid by the radical Arab states in the Middle East, and in the Horn of Africa as well, shows no sign of decreasing. The leverage Moscow possesses in the Arab world continues to grow. 

On the bright side in the Middle East is the increased stability of the Kingdom of Jordan, With the strong and successful actions taken by the Government of Jordan in the last ten months, the Fedayeen capability to apply pressure against Jordan and other Arab governments has distinctly decreased. However, there remains in the Arab world a radical nationalism which could jeopardize stability in the area even should a settlement be reached between Israel and the Arabs. 

With the British military withdrawal from the Persian Gulf scheduled to take place soon, the problem could become especially acute in that oil rich area. The 

Soviet naval presence in the Indian Ocean is available to further Soviet aims there.

Turning to South Asia, the hostility between the two major countries-India and Pakistan-has been aggravated by the events in East Pakistan. However, no escalation to open warfare seems likely. 

The internal problems of the subcontinent, however, are explosive, as shown by the problem in Pakistan. I see the situation in East Pakistan as something of a stalemate. The poorly organized and equipped Bengalis are clearly no match for the Pakistani Regular Armed Forces. However, the army faces the difficult tasks of controlling the countryside and borders. Even should they somehow manage this, the Central Government faces the more difficult task of administering a population of seventy million embittered Bengalis. In any event, it seems highly unlikely that Pakistan will be able to return to the conditions which prevailed prior to the civil war. In India, the overwhelming victory Mrs. Gandhi enjoyed in the March election will contribute to a stable government capable of ruling the world's second most populous country. Compounding the unrest in the subcontinent is the recent uprising in Ceylon. 

In Africa, South of the Sahara, Ethiopian military forces are currently engaged in operations against a radical Arab supported insurgency. At this time, the dissident element is not considered a serious threat to the present Ethiopian Government. However, the USSR continues its military assistance to Somalia and Sudan, Ethiopia's historically hostile Moslem neighbors.

(The statement follows:) ORDER OF PRIORITIES IN DEVELOPING COUNTRIES 

The policy of the United States, as clearly stated on numerous occasions, is to assist developing nations in their efforts to build the economic, political and social institutions which will meet their aspirations for a better life, with freedom, and in peace. The United States recognizes that all nations have a requirement and an obligation to provide for their self defense. It is our aim to achieve international peace and security in a way so that armed force shall not be used except for individual or collective self defense. 

Clearly then the economic, political and social development of countries are very high on the order of priorities. Yet we cannot overlook the necessity for a defensive shield behind which this development can proceed. Military development of each country is weighed against the threat to the country, ability of the country to finance the military expenditures, and the technological ability to maintain and operate military equipment and systems. The constraints built into the military assistance and foreign military sales systems and the evaluations made by responsible personnel at each echelon in the systems insure that only those defense articles and services required by each developing country are provided.

EUROPEAN COMMAND, JUNE 2, 1971 

Mr. Chairman and members of the the committee, it is a pleasure for me to appear again before this distinguished Committee in support of the United States Military Assistance Program-a program essential to the maintenance of con tinuing security in the European Command area of responsibility. 

(515)

You are of course well aware that this program had its genesis through military necessity and that it has been continuously tailored to the changing requirements of the world situation and our foreign policy. The program, suitably adjusted, has been repeatedly renewed because it produced important mutual benefits to the United States and to our friends and allies around the world through the years. On the premise that self-help is the best help in the long run, the programs have been continuously revised in order to shift as much as possible of the responsibility for, and the costs associated with, collective defense efforts to our allies' national budgets. In USEUCOM countries which have sufficiently increased their economic capabilities-through U.S. economic and military assistance, international trade, and their own considerable "bootstrap" efforts to be able to assume larger shares of common defense burdens, we have been able to reduce U.S. grant-military-aid programs. 

Before addressing the threat in Europe and the assistance programs we propose, I think it would be well to review what is meant by "Forward Defense" countries. These are countries directly confronted with a formidable threat and for which U.S. Security Assistance is primarily intended and designed-in accordance with the Nixon Doctrine to bolster their defensive capabilities by capitalizing on available indigenous manpower and, as a consequence, to reduce potential requirements for U.S. Forces to fulfill our collective security obligations. 

Within the European Command area, only Greece and Turkey are considered Forward Defense Countries. And I want to point out in this connection that both of those countries are making significant military contributions toward meeting the threat to NATO's southeastern flank. 

The programs planned for FY 1972 would provide grant aid to Greece, Turkey, Portugal, Spain, Tunisia, and Morocco and funds for credit and credit guarantees for military sales to Greece and Morocco. All these programs support the President's recently formulated International Security Assistance objectives, which place renewed emphasis upon strengthening the armed forces of friendly countries to enable them to assume larger shares of their own security burdens and to better serve interests that we and they share. 

In NATO Europe, the military threat posed by the Soviet Union and other nations of the Warsaw Pact fundamentally influences the form and extent of U.S. and Allied military efforts. It is important here to keep in mind that the Warsaw 

Pact military establishment remains under the firm control of the Soviet Government. In this most important respect the situation in the Warsaw Pact differs radically from that prevailing in NATO. 

During the past year, Warsaw Pact forces have substantially improved their strategic offensive and defensive capabilities. They have also made comparable increases in the tactical capabilities of their air, ground, and naval forces to conduct conventional warfare; and they keep their units in the forward areas ready for combat, with reinforcing divisions in the western USSR available for commitment within a few days.

Warsaw Pact ground forces are prepared for conventional warfare, as well as for a rapid and massive exploitation of nuclear strikes. Their inventory of major weapons and combat vehicles includes impressive numbers of tanks, armored personnel carriers, launchers for tactical missiles and rockets with nuclear and conventional warheads, and artillery weapons. 

MOver the past three years, the Soviet submarine-launched strategic missile program has advanced dramatically. The most serious missile threat to NATO 

Europe is posed, however, by medium- and intermediate-range and variable-range intercontinental ballistic missiles. 

Warsaw Pact air operations against Allied Command Europe could be carried out by large numbers of fighters, light bombers, tactical reconnaissance aircraft, and medium jet bombers based in the Pact Forward Area and in Western USSR. 

The Soviets continue to modernize their air forces with new-generation fighters, bombers, and transports.

Warsaw Pact electronic countermeasures (ECM) capabilities would contribute substantially to any military effort to penetrate NATO defenses, while their extensive and constantly improving electronic counter-countermeasures (ECCM) could provide significant protection for their air warning, missile guidance, and fire control radars against our electronic jamming efforts. 

The Soviets continue to make major improvements in their strategic defenses by expanding surveillance and tracking systems, increasing the density of de-

ployed air defense weapons, and by multiplying passive defense measures for vital installations such as airfields and air defense system components. Complementary deployments of modern automatic anti-aircraft guns also continue, and steady and impressive progress has been made in such passive defense measures as dispersal of revetted parking areas and the construction of bomb resistant shelters, called hangarettes, for single fighter aircraft. 

The naval threat to Allied Command Europe is posed by the Soviet Black Sea, Baltic Sea, Northern, and Mediterranean Fleets the last being made up of units drawn from the other fleets. Two guided missile helicopter ships have been built, and both have conducted deployments in the Mediterranean in an antisubmarine warfare role. General aerial surveillance and attack capabilities are provided to 

Soviet naval forces by light and medium bombers, reconnaissance planes, and antisubmarine warfare aircraft.

Major Soviet naval units have operated far from their home waters with increasing frequency during the last three years, and the most menacing of such deployments is still the Soviet fleet in the Mediterranean. The Russians first deployed there primarily to show their flag, but their fleet now constitutes a naval and naval air threat of growing significance. 

To facilitate operations in the Mediterranean, the Soviets have established deep-water anchorages throughout the area which are well-spaced along shipping lanes, but which afford little protection from the sea and weather. The 

Soviet Navy's ability to refuel, resupply, and repair at sea provides the general capability of sustaining the deployment of considerable naval forces independent of land bases. Aerial reconnaissance for the Soviet Mediterranean Fleet is provided by medium jet bombers and turboprop antisubmarine warfare aircraft based in Egypt.

Since the Arab-Israeli War of June 1967, the Soviets have also maintained a small amphibious force in the Mediterranean. This force, which has been quite active in amphibious training exercises, gives the Soviets the ability to quickly establish a presence in friendly Arab countries. 

Since the mid-1950's the Soviets have backed the Arab cause with economic and military assistance which has included large numbers of technical and military advisers at most levels of the Egyptian armed forces, and they now actively participate in the UAR's air defense system.

In summary, there are clear indications of a long-range Soviet objective to achieve a strategic, as well as a tactical, power advantage in the European area. 

Under the umbrella of their competitive strategic nuclear capability, the Soviets may choose to exploit the capabilities of their conventional forces in order to achieve their military and political objectives.

In discussing the specific proposals for military assistance for FY 72, I will begin with the plans for grant aid to Greece, Turkey, and Portugal. Next I will discuss the grant-aid portion of our plan for maintaining United States basing and operating rights in Spain and will describe our small assistance programs for Morocco and Tunisia. I will close with a few comments about military training assistance, the general role of U.S. military export sales in Europe, and the status of our Military Assistance Advisory Groups. 

The primary purpose of our military assistance programs in Greece and Turkey is to provide an essential part of the equipment and training needed to deter Communist aggression and-if an attack should come to successfully defend against it. The importance of these two programs is directly proportional to the magnitude of the threat I have just described, which increasingly menaces 

NATO's southeastern flank.

Despite very impressive defense efforts, neither Greece nor Turkey has been or is capable unaided of mustering resources to match defense requirements. 

Consequently, U.S. Military Assistance has played an essential role in filling a critical resource gap. I am convinced that our substantial aid to these allies over the past two decades has greatly helped to discourage aggression against either country.

In past appearances before this Committee, General Lemnitzer and I have testified concerning major materiel deficiencies in the Greek and Turkish defense establishments and the need to modernize their forces. And I am happy to report to you that we have made notable progress toward that goal. In recent years our emphasis has properly been upon development and modernization of the armies of these two countries, and the time has now come for them and us to commit more resources toward improvement of their air and naval forces.

Jet aircraft and air defense missile systems have been gradually introduced into the air force inventories of both these allies, but some of their squadrons remain equipped with aircraft either outdated or unsuited to their missions. The navies of both Greece and Turkey are small, and a number of their units require replacement or modernization, if their operational effectiveness is to be improved. 

For FY 72, the preponderance of all funds proposed for grant military assistance for countries in the European Command is earmarked for Greece and Turkey. These two programs, of course, differ considerably in size, for two main reasons: First, Turkey, because of her greater land area and exposed location, requires much larger military forces than Greece; second, Turkey is still less capable than Greece of assuming the operating and maintenance costs for her military establishment.

Both Greece and Turkey must depend upon allied help to enable them to finance even their minimum defense requirements; and without that assistance, their forces would deteriorate. The NATO community has been asked to help, and some assistance has been provided. However, Greece and Turkey remain primarily dependent upon the United States for what military assistance they require. 

The proposed grant aid program for Turkey is designed to support force upkeep and training and to provide some force-modernization. Major items include aircraft, armored vehicles, communications equipment, and submarines becoming available from the U.S. Navy.

The grant aid program for Greece will provide for priority force modernization and some essential training, as well as operating and maintenance support. 

Modernization items include helicopters, armored vehicles, ammunition, engineer and communications equipment, and modifications for MAP-provided aircraft. We believe that destroyer modernization can be accomplished by the application of retiring U.S. Navy ships and can be financed within the Greek defense budget.

To make it possible for the Greek Government to purchase other essential defense articles and services that will not otherwise be available within Greek resources even if augmented by grant military assistance from the United States and other nations-we have forecast a modest amount of credit for Foreign Military Sales in FY 72. A request for authorization of those funds is included in the legislation before the Committee. 

The Greeks are making substantial-and successful-efforts to help themselves, but they cannot yet stand alone. In my opinion, Greece's firm commitment to 

NATO, plus the miltary facilities the Greek Government makes available to U.S. forces, fully justifies continuance of our aid in the modest amounts proposed. 

Though we foresee requirements beyond this fiscal year to insure the continuing effectiveness of their forces, both of these important FY 72 programs will substantially improve the capabilities of the armed forces of Greece and Turkey to perform their assigned NATO missions and will help to develop their national capacities for self-sustainment. 

U.S. military assistance to Portugal has the objective of supporting a strategically located NATO ally by helping her make modest improvements in her NATOcommitted forces. The program proposed for FY 1972 is designed to support the training of key Portuguese military personnel and to provide a small quantity of spare parts and follow-on maintenance, primarily for the air force and navy. 

The grant aid program for Spain partially fulfills the military portion of the 

Agreement of Friendship and Cooperation signed last August. The military assist ance we are providing there is also in harmony with essential Spanish defense requirements. The grant aid proposed for Spain for FY 72 is intended to provide training and materiel items specifically requested by Spain. These include armored vehicles, artillery, communications equipment, and some ammunition and spare parts. The army and air force will receive the bulk of the funds earmarked to support training. 

I will turn now to the strategic North African littoral, where the U.S. is interested in assisting friendly and moderate regimes to develop reasonable defensive capabilities in order to improve stability in that potentially volatile area. 

As Soviet naval strength in the Mediterranean increases, Soviet political influence grows-mostly, in that largely unstable area, at the expense of that of the United States and the West. Algerian acceptance of substantial Soviet military assistance means that Algeria will be dependent upon Moscow for arms, training, and technical assistance for years to come. To that adverse influence has been added the radical and unpredictable character of the regime in Libya. 29010/0895

Morocco and Tunisia, the only two countries in North Africa now supported by U.S. military grant aid or credit sales, both want to strengthen their defense capa- 

Ebilities-objectives which, in the light of the situation I have just described, are considered to deserve our support, and which entail comparatively small costs. 

The potential dividends, on the other hand, are substantial: a continued prowestern orientation of these "moderate" Arab regimes may impede further expansion of Soviet influence in North Africa and the Western Mediterranean. 

For Morocco, the small MAP training program proposed for FY 1972 primarily provides the Moroccan Air Force with undergraduate pilot and technical training in the United States to enable it to use the aircraft we provided through Foreign Military Sales. These funds will also finance prerequisite English language training for student pilots and candidates. For materiel assistance in FY 72, we recommend a small amount of credit for Foreign Military Sales.

The recommended grant aid program for Tunisia is designed to assist in developing armed forces capable of a reasonable self-defense and, in conjunction with other indigenous security forces, maintaining the internal security necessary for continued social and economic development. The proposed program will support essential training and provide a few mortars, armored vehicles, artillery, other vehicles and trailers, some small arms and communications equipment, repair parts of several kinds, and a small stockpile of ammunition items. 

In summary, we propose to continue training for Morocco and to provide a modest amount of material and training assistance to Tunisia-primarily to help those countries develop modest defensive capabilities. 

I have mentioned training in connection with each of the country programs discussed, and I think that this part of our military assistance programs deserves additional specific comment. Aircraft, ships, and tanks are fundamental to an adequate military force; but without trained men, they are useless. There is reason to believe that one of the most enduring values of our MAP dollar is derived from training allied personnel in the United States, in the host countries themselves, and elsewhere within the European Command. 

In addition to the obvious contributions which MAP training makes to allied operational capabilities, there are important intangible common defense benefits. 

Training courses in, and orientation visits to, United States training establishments contribute to a positive attitude on the part of allied military personnel toward American ideals and principles. Allied officers selected by host countries to attend our senior service colleges generally are those destined to fill positions of great responsibility, trust, and influence in their respective forces and nations. 

Consequently, the friendships developed between foreign students and United States students and instructors yield valuable long-term dividends in understanding and rapport.

And there are other indirect benefits to both the United States and our allies. 

MAP students at our service colleges obtain a working knowledge of U.S. military doctrine and tactics; and this, together with common equipment and uniform procedures, simplifies logistic support and facilitates allied cooperation. 

Though I have mentioned the need for Foreign Military Sales credit funds to assist Greece and Morocco, I have dealt in my statement principally with our grant aid programs. But long-standing basic policy governing the administration of U.S. Military Assistance and Sales has been to shift as rapidly as feasible from grant aid to sales, wherever possible. The past, present, and proposed programs for countries within the European Command clearly reflect the fulfillment of that policy: for cost of the countries of Western Europe, the transition from grant aid to sales is complete. None of these countries is expected to require Foreign Military Sales credit, although some of them occasionally request credits from private banks or firms in the United States or from the Export-Import Bank. Aggregate U.S. military export sales-including all methods of financing-within the European Command area of responsibility continue to account for about half the worldwide total. 

That concludes my comments on the substance of our proposed programs. I would like now to say a brief word about the essential functions that our Military Assistance Advisory Groups, or MAAGs, continue to perform. 

Our MAAG people work closely with their host country military counterparts in planning and implementing the programs you approve. At the same time, they facilitate the attainment of United States objectives by maintainingthrough their daily person-to-person contacts-a mutual understanding and a channel of communication that is of substantial value. 60-973-71-pt. 3- -6

The Military Assistance Advisory Groups were of course originally established to administer U.S. grant aid programs in recipient nations. And in most of the countries I have discussed, this remains their purpose. However, as the Euro pean nations recovered from the effects of World War II and achieved greater levels of economic capacity, U.S. grant aid was phased out, and the MAAGS at reduced strengths continued to perform residual military assistance functions and assumed others connected with cooperative research and development programs, multilateral production programs, cooperative logistics arrangements, and foreign military sales. Each of these activities requires action by the MAAGs. 

A Resources Management Program-designed to achieve the most efficient allocation and use of MAAG resources has been actively pursued within the United States European Command. Under this program the functions and responsibili ties of our MAAGS are continually assessed to insure that they operate with minimum required staffs. It should also be noted that a good proportion of the administrative costs of supporting our MAAGS is borne by the host countries themselves: more than 25 percent in grant aid countries, and 85 percent in others.

In concluding, Mr. Chairman, I would say that in my view the United States 

Military Assistance Program and Foreign Military Sales continue to serve the security of our country, to support the foreign policy and general welfare of the United States, and to carry out the intent of the Congress to promote the peace. 

The stability of the European area well exemplifies the effectiveness of that program over the years in attaining the objectives of collective security among Free World nations. The new programs which I am recommending should continue to improve the military capabilities of our allies and consequently promote mutual security. They provide a sound, tested way to further the development and maintenance of the collective strength necessary to deter aggression in the United States European Command area. 

Accordingly, I recommend your support of these programs. 

Mr. Chairman, this completes my prepared statement.

General GOODPASTER. First, the direct military effects.

I think that is enough to outline the principal consequences.
