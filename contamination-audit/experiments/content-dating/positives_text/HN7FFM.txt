PREFACE 

Holy Trinity Parish, on Shawmut Avenue, in Boston, will celebrate its one-hundredth anniversary in June, 1944. The history of the parish possesses an unique interest. Naturally, its first and foremost achievement as a Catholic parish will always remain hidden within the sphere of personal religious life, but its external activities exhibit features which will engage the attention not only of its own parishioners but of all 

Catholics. 

For the devoted members of the parish the story is an inspiration to continue in the ardent Catholic faith of their forefathers and to emulate their loyalty to the church which they built with great and loving sacrifices. For all other Catholics it is a sturdy example of blessed and successful parish life rich in a tradition of real Catholic Action schools, charitable institutions and well organized societies.

The vigorous spirit of Holy Trinity Parish has spread its beneficent influence also into the civic and cultural life of New England with many effects, little known but deeply interesting. ما 5

Church and Parish EARLY BEGINNINGS 

Holy Trinity was founded in 1844. Its beginnings, however, go back to the year 1829 when the first group of German Catholic immigrants organized a congregation of their own. 

Three brothers, Melchior, Sebastian and Mathias Kramer, are commonly considered the first German Catholics of Boston. They were clockmakers from Baden, had lived in Philadelphia a few years, and in 1827 settled in Boston. Melchior established a business in Boston and being very successful became a well known dealer of imported goods for all New England. 

The Kramer brothers were soon joined by other arrivals from Germany. Descendants of these early families are still among the parishioners of Holy Trinity. Under 

Melchior Kramer's leadership the immigrants united into a congregation to safeguard their spiritual life, religious instruction and Catholic customs. 

Bishop Fenwick of Boston welcomed them to his Cathedral on Franklin Street, assigned them special hours for their services, frequently said Mass for them and, as he knew a little German himself, gave them short instructions in their native language.

PIONEER PRIESTS 

As the newcomers were but poorly acquainted with the English language, it was natural that they should wish to have a priest who could preach and hear confessions in their mother tongue. Through the kind efforts of 

Bishop Fenwick five secular priests successively arrived from Germany between 1836 and 1848, to labor in Boston. 

However, none of them stayed long, for the spirit of priestly zeal urged them to go West where they found wider field for their activity among the numerous German immigrants farming in Ohio, Wisconsin and Minnesota.

The names of these priests are: Francis S. Hoffman, Joseph E. Freygang, Alexander Martini, Francis Rolof,

Gerhard Plathe. These men were among the first of a long and famous line of pioneer priests and missionaries who came from various countries of Europe, to assist in building up the Catholic Church in the United States. 

FOUNDING OF THE CHURCH 

From 1838 to 1842, the Rev. John Raffeiner took care of the congregation, coming a few times a year from New York to administer the sacraments, give instructions and visit the sick. He soon became convinced that it was advisable for the German Catholics in Boston to have a church and parish of their own. After great efforts and endeavors his persistence was finally crowned with success, earning him and his faithful helper, Melchior Kramer, the honor of founders of the parish. In 1842 the cornerstone of the new parish church was laid on Suffolk Street (later Shawmut Avenue) in the presence of Bishop Fenwick. Fr. Raffeiner selected as the title of the new church the name of the Holy Trinity. 10

In June, 1844, the first Mass was said in the new church by the Pastor, Rev. Francis Rolof. Holy Trinity was at length a fully established parish. Built of Roxbury puddingstone with a massive and sturdy tower, the church stood until 1872, a well known landmark of old Boston. 

JESUITS TAKE OVER 

Because of the difficulties encountered in obtaining new priests for Holy Trinity after the early pastors had left for the western states, Bishop Fitzpatrick of Boston arranged in 1848 with the Society of Jesus to take over the care of Holy Trinity Parish. Since then all pastors and curates of Holy Trinity have been Jesuit priests. Some of the Fathers in those early years came directly from Europe, others from the Buffalo Mission of the Society of Jesus. For the last twenty years the priests of Holy 

Trinity Parish have been members of the New England 

Province of the Jesuit Order. 

The first Jesuit Pastor of Holy Trinity was Rev.

Gustave Eck, S. J. (1848-1854). 

FIRST SODALITY IN NEW ENGLAND 

For the young people of Holy Trinity, Father Eck founded in 1851, the Sodality of the Blessed Virgin Mary, the first in New England. A year later the Sodality was divided into two separate groups for young men and young women.

For almost one hundred years now the Sodalities have been pillars of true Catholic spirit and life among the boys and girls of the parish, giving them in addition to a deep and solid religious inspiration, many facilities for cultural and social training. 13

The purposes of the Sodality: absolute loyalty to the 

Catholic Church, sanctification of self, active apostleship among others, charity and zeal, consecration to Mary for life-these ideals have helped from the beginning to inspire the whole parish with characteristic traits of the spiritual life, most beautiful and consoling: a tender, filial devotion to the Blessed Mother, a great spirit of charity and of love for the missions, a strong bond of loyalty, cooperation and affectionate union between people and clergy.

THE CHRISTMAS PARISH 

Holy Trinity enjoys the special honor of having been the center from where many features of our present 

American Christmas customs rådiated into Boston, New

England and the rest of the country. 

In New England, Christmas was hardly celebrated at all until the middle of the last century, except as a religious observance of the Catholic divine service. The Puritans who abolished Christmas in England, also succeeded in keeping its religious celebration from the American public and home. Not until the German Catholic immigrants came over in larger numbers, about 1840, did 

Christmas in New England begin its glorious ascent as a common holiday. The Germans brought their Christmas trees with them, their cribs, their hymns and carols. 

They were gradually joined in their popular celebration of Christmas by the Irish Catholics and, later on, also by the Protestant population.

At Holy Trinity and among its people, Christmas trees were used for the first time in New England. The solemn service of Holy Night drew large crowds of Bostonians to our church. From here also spread the custom of sending Christmas greeting cards. The first religious Christ- 14

mas play in New England (a shepherd's procession and adoration) was enacted by the school children of Holy

Trinity Church on December 25, 1851. It attracted such public attention and interest that it had to be repeated two weeks later.

AN ABORTIVE PROJECT 

About 1850, the congregation of Holy Trinity received a considerable increase of immigrants so that the need of a new and larger church was soon felt. 

Fr. Eck went to work with great zeal, preparing the erection of a new church to be dedicated to the Immaculate Conception of Mary. According to his plans it was to be the largest and most beautiful church in the United States, a magnificent structure, in Gothic style, with two steeples. The building was actually begun in 1853 at an excellent site on the corner of Tremont and East Canton Streets.

The project, however, was too ambitious and altogether beyond the resources of the small congregation. Money had to be borrowed, all sorts of difficulties arose and finally Fr. Eck's health broke down completely. In 1854 he was called back to Europe. It was then decided to discontinue work on the partly finished structure. The architect received the plot of ground and the building material in payment of his claim.

After this well-meant but unfortunate attempt at building a new church the succeeding pastors had to face a large debt and a variety of complications with a discouraged flock. They consequently remained only for a short time: Fr. Ernest Reiter (1854-1856), Fr. John

Cattani (1856-1858), Fr. Norbert Steinbacher (1858-1859). 

15

QUICK RECOVERY 

In 1859 the Rev. Ernest Reiter, S. J., returned to Holy 

Trinity, now to stay as pastor for eleven years. During his term of office he accomplished great things in the parish, at first under very trying circumstances. In a few years he paid off the debts and acquired many pieces of liturgical equipment. He also bought ground on Shawmut 

Avenue, opposite the church, and collected over $100,000.00 in preparation for the building of a new and larger church. 

Under his able administration the spiritual and financial conditions of the parish were soon excellent again. But his project of erecting a new church had to be postponed because of the impending municipal work of the "reconstruction and level-raising of Suffolk Street District." 

Before this public task was carried out, Fr. Reiter received a call from his superiors to help in the establishment of the new Buffalo Mission of the Society of Jesus. 

He left in July, 1870, and the parishioners deeply regretted his departure.

BOSTON SYMPHONY ORCHESTRA 

During the upheavals of the European Revolution of 1848, a large number of prominent German musicians came to America. A group of these men, residing in 

New England, formed the Germania Orchestra, which, through its Catholic members, was incorporated in Holy Trinity parish. The orchestra played in our church at solemn occasions and cooperated with the Holy Trinity singing societies in producing many concerts.

In 1859, Carl Zerrahn opened the Boston Philharmonic concerts with his musicians of the Germania Orchestra, and these same artists eventually became the nucleus of the great Boston Symphony Orchestra (1881). 16

THE BELLS OF HOLY TRINITY 

The successor of Fr. Eck, Rev. Ernest Reiter, S. J., acquired a set of historic bells historic because of their venerable age and the unique manner they came into possession of Holy Trinity.

During the Civil War, General Benjamin F. Butler, former Governor of Massachusetts, was Military Commander of New Orleans after its occupation by the Union troops. Displeased with the unfriendly attitude of a certain section of the city, he punished them by confiscating the old bells of their parish church. These bells were shipped to Boston and offered for sale. Fr. Reiter bought them in 1863 for $1500.00 and put them in the tower of 

Holy Trinity Church. 

RAISING THE RECTORY 

The gigantic project of raising the Suffolk Street district was finally started in 1870, the ground level of a large section of the South End being elevated ten feet. 

Hundreds of men worked day and night, little freight trains hustled to and fro bringing immense amounts of gravel, stones and soil. New streets were being built, others abandoned. Whole houses were turned about or moved down the streets on rollers while other houses were being raised on the spot.

Since Holy Trinity Church could not be moved or raised, the basement was abandoned and filled up to the rising street level. The large stone building of the rectory, however, was raised ten feet. It took three months to achieve this remarkable feat of engineering. After the foundations had been removed, the house resting on iron screws was slowly elevated inch by inch until it reached the height of the new street level. Then a new foundation was built underneath. All during this work the priests lived in the house, following their usual daily routine. 17

THE NEW CHURCH 

Rev. James Simeon, S. J., the successor of Fr. Reiter, built the new Holy Trinity Church on the corner of Cobb Street and Shawmut Avenue. The architect was John H. 

Keely from Brooklyn, N. Y. The cornerstone was laid by 

Bishop (later Archbishop) Williams on November 10, 1872 -a day memorable in the annals of the city because of the great "Boston Fire" which had broken out the evening before and destroyed a large part of the down-town business section. Fr. Reiter had come from Buffalo to preach the sermon at the laying of the cornerstone. 

Five years later, on May 27, 1877, the feast of the Holy

Trinity, the church was dedicated by Archbishop Williams, the sermon being preached by the famous Jesuit missionary, Rev. Francis X. Weninger, from Cincinnati, Ohio.

Holy Trinity Church is a Gothic structure in plain but tasteful style, built of Roxbury puddingstone with trimmings of Maine granite. Its tall and graceful steeple was a landmark of the South End until 1938 when it had to be removed after being badly damaged by the hurricane. 

Mention should be made of the excellent large organ in the upper church. It has 2880 pipes and 54 stops. Even now after seventy years it is according to outstanding experts, one of the best organs in New England. The upper and lower church contain beautiful altars, statues and stained glass windows, the work of prominent artists. 

The pulpit in the lower church is the one which had served in the old church. 

Holy Trinity Church has a seating capacity of 1200, the lower church of 700. The cost of the building was $250,000.00, including the price of the land and of the new rectory adjoining the church. 18

EARLY PASTORS OF THE NEW CHURCH 

Fr. Simeon returned to Europe in 1877, and his place was taken by Rev. Francis X. Nopper, S. J. No other priest remained connected so long with Holy Trinity Church. He was pastor for fifteen years (1877-1892) and curate for eleven years. The greatest work which he accomplished and the enduring memorial of his pastorate is the St. Francis' Home in Roxbury (grade school, orphan asylum and old folks' home) of which more will be said in a following chapter.

His two immediate successors did not remain long: 

Fr. Nicholas Greisch, S. J. (1892-1893), and Fr. Charles 

Gudenus, S. J. (1893-1896), who started a third parish school in South Boston.

The next pastor was Rev. John B. Jutz, S. J. (1896-1906). 

Previous to his coming to Boston he had been for ten years a missionary among the Sioux Indians in South Dakota. 

It was during his pastorship, 1899, that the monthly magazine of the parish was founded. On the occasion of his Silver Jubilee of the priesthood, in 1903, the parishioners donated the solid marble altar in the lower church. 

Fr. Edmund Sturm, S. J., was pastor from 1906 until his death in 1910. He erected the marble altar of the 

Sacred Heart in the lower church. His successor, Fr. 

Joseph Faber, S. J., built the new school house in Roxbury, put stained glass windows in the lower church, a marble communion railing and new stations in the upper church. In 1918 he was recalled to Buffalo. 

The pastorate of Fr. John B. Schmandt, S. J., lasted only a few months: he died in October, 1918, a victim of the influenza then raging in Boston. Rev. Bernard C.

Cohausz, S. J., (1918-1921), reorganized the life of the parish after the war, brought the parishioners together again and effected a strong revival of devotional services. 21

RECENT PASTORS 

Fr. Cohausz was succeeded by Rev. Charles P. Gisler, S.J. (1921-1940). For almost twenty years Fr. Gisler labored with unremitting zeal for Holy Trinity, highly venerated among the parishioners as a pious, fervent and fatherly pastor. He added many devotions, increased the number of services, introduced the 11.15 Mass on Sundays. 

In 1923 he established a new convent on Shawmut Avenue for the Sisters teaching at Holy Trinity. Exhausted from his many religious and charitable tasks especially his daily sick calls to distant sections of Greater Boston he died in December, 1940, deeply mourned by all. 

Under Rev. Henry M. Brock, S. J. (1941-1943), the new shrine of Mary Immaculate was erected replacing the old Lourdes Grotto. The lower church was cleaned and redecorated and a loud speaker system installed in the upper church.

In May, 1943, Fr. Brock was succeeded by Rev. Francis 

X. Weiser, S. J., the present pastor, who immediately started to prepare the church and parish for the worthy celebration of the one-hundredth anniversary. 

Nearly every one of the pastors has left some permanent, visible memorial of his activity in Holy Trinity parish and dear memories in the hearts of the parishioners. There has been complete harmony between the people and the Jesuit Fathers and nothing has disturbed the peace existing among the parishioners. On the contrary, pastors and people have worked together, zealously and loyally, for the church, the schools, the charitable institutions, and for the particular interests of the many societies. 22

EARLY CURATES 

During the past eighty years, many Jesuit Fathers (as curates of Holy Trinity) have assisted the pastors with great charity and zeal. Special mention is being made here of a few of them because of some outstanding features of their activities. 

Rev. Hugo Praessar, S. J., a fervent apostle of children and youth, founded the great social organization of the parish, the Casino Club, in 1869. Rev. Joseph Krieg, S. J. (1883-1892), a scholarly and devout priest, directed the Young Ladies' Sodality organizing it into a very efficient unit of Catholic Action. 

Rev. Alexander de Ascheberg, S. J. (1894-1901), was director of the Young Men's Sodality and exercised a lasting influence on the young people. His zeal was apparent from the fact that as soon as a young man missed Sodality meeting twice, Fr. Ascheberg appeared in his home, whether it was West Roxbury, Dedham, or any other outlying district, and inquired about the reason of the absence. 

He also went to West Roxbury every week to give the children of the parishioners catechetical instructions. 

Rev. John P. Schleuter, S. J., worked in Holy Trinity 

Parish for twenty-six years (between 1886 and 1918). 

He was a very active man even in old age, most zealous and kindly, and always ready to go on distant sick calls.

He successfully directed the Young Ladies' Sodality. For years he was editor of the parish magazine and wrote and translated several books. He also was one of the 

Fathers who made regular missionary excursions to settlements outside of Boston. 

Rev. Robert Swickerath, S. J. (1904-1906), founded the

Debating Society for young men in 1906. He was a very successful lecturer on many occasions and a beloved 23

preacher, not only during his curateship but also from 1923 to 1941 as regular guest preacher at the 11.15 Mass on Sundays.

RECENT CURATES 

Rev. Henry J. Nelles, S. J. (1909-1914 and 1918-1925), was a great organizer of social affairs, and an able musical director. He founded the Holy Trinity "Choral and Symphony Society." It gave a number of remarkable concerts, particularly in Jordan Hall, attracting considerable attention among the music-loving public of Boston. 

Rev. Joseph J. Keller, S. J. (1916-1942), who retained from his missionary experience in Jamaica, B. W. I., a great love and practical interest for the work of aiding the missions, founded in 1925 the "Little Flower Mission ary Club" of Greater Boston. Living at St. Francis' Home in Roxbury, he inspired and supervised with loving care the liturgical and devotional services at the Home Chapel. 

Rev. Henry C. MacLeod, S. J. (1936-1942), also a former missionary in Jamaica, directed with great success the Young Men's Sodality, and volunteered in 1942 as a chaplain in the U. S. Navy. Commissioned as a Lieutenant, senior grade, he was appointed chaplain in the Naval Amphibious Force and is at present serving on the battlefields of the South Pacific.

A word should also be said about a member of the Society of Jesus, who, though not a priest, did noble work at Holy Trinity and gained the sincere affection of the parishioners: Brother Caspar Menke, S. J. (1850-1894). 

He served forty-four years, humbly and faithfully, as janitor and sexton to the great edification of all. In the early days of the school he also acted as teacher, con- 24

ducting the boys' classes for a considerable time. On the occasion of his Golden Jubilee as a Jesuit in 1894, the parish gave him a great reception in Casino Hall. A few months later he died having spent his whole life as a religious in the service of the parish. He was the only 

Jesuit lay brother at Holy Trinity.

MISSIONARY EXCURSIONS 

The Jesuit Fathers did not confine their activities to Holy Trinity Parish but were willing to attend in other places of New England to Catholics who had no priest to minister to them in their own language. In earlier days regular visits were made to Worcester, Holyoke, Clinton, New Bedford, Norwood and Providence.

During the early part of this century the Fathers regularly attended to a rather large colony of Catholics from 

Germany, Austria, Hungary and Switzerland, who had settled in Adams and neighboring towns in the Berkshire 

Hills. Fr. Robert Swickerath for over twenty years made two annual visits, about Christmas and Easter, to that colony, preaching sermons and hearing the confessions of the old and the sick. In two missions, 1906 and 1926, he succeeded in bringing back to the practice of religion a good number of people who had been indifferent and careless for many years; some had never been to a church in this country.

The experience derived from such missionary excursions proved irrefutably that, at least in the beginning, it is necessary to have priests who thoroughly understand the language of new immigrants. 25.

FIRST NOVENA FOR PEACE 

In September, 1939, three weeks after the outbreak of 

World War II, a perpetual novena to Mary Immaculate was started at Holy Trinity Church with special prayers for peace. It was the first novena for peace, inaugurated during the present war, in the United States. The novena services are held every Friday, at noon and in the evening.

Schools and Institutions CENTENNIAL OF THE PARISH SCHOOL 

Within the archdiocese of Boston there has been an impressive growth of Catholic schools, especially during the last few decades. The great number and splendid condition of parish schools in Boston are a well known and publicly acknowledged fact. 

In this proud record of education Holy Trinity holds the unique honor of having established the first parochial school in New England which had a continuous existence up to the present day.* A few months after the parish was founded the Pastor, Rev. Gerhard Plathe, opened a school for boys and girls in the basement of the church. 

Later the classes were held in different houses on Shaw. mut Avenue, and in 1874 the present school was built. 

The teachers at Holy Trinity Parish School were first laymen and women (1844-1859), then the Sisters of Notre Dame (1859-1913) and the Sisters of St. Francis (from 

1913). For the past hundred years this school has done its work without interruption, training thousands of boys and girls and helping them to become good citizens and good Catholics. * See Centennial Number of the "Pilot," March 8, 1930: 

"A Century of Education" by Richard J. Quinlan. 

29

ORPHAN ASYLUM 

As early as 1860, Fr. Reiter had founded the "St. 

Joseph's Society for the Assistance of Widows, Orphans and the Poor." As a result of the splendid work of this organization the idea took root among the priests and people that it was desirable to have a special home for the orphans and the aged women of the parish. In 1888 a suitable place was bought on Fulda Street, Roxbury, consisting of a mansion and a large tract of land. In 

1890, a new big building was added, and Archbishop 

Williams dedicated it on the feast of St. Joseph, March 19, 1891.

The orphanage has been from the beginning in charge of the Sisters of St. Francis, whose mother-house is at 

Glen Riddle, Pennsylvania. For more than fifty years an average of thirty orphans have been living at the home. 

The total number of children who received their education and training at the orphan asylum is about five hundred. 

HOME FOR AGED WOMEN 

Together with the orphan asylum, in 1891 a home for aged women of the parish was opened in Roxbury. During most of the time this home has sheltered between eight and twelve old ladies, lovingly cared for by the Sisters of St. Francis.

There is something attractive, almost fascinating, in the idea of such a parish institution. Orphans and aged women are not sent away from the parish but remain in it, being attended to by nuns attached to the parish under the supervision and friendly care of the parish clergy. 

This removes much of the purely institutional character, provides a family atmosphere, and entitles it in a special sense to the sweet name of "Home." 30

PARISH SCHOOL IN ROXBURY 

Fr. Nopper founded also a school in Roxbury for the orphans of St. Francis' Home and the children of the parishioners living in the neighborhood. This "Holy Trinity 

School, Roxbury” was opened in 1891. It soon had over one hundred pupils and has grown steadily to the present number of 280. Within the last twenty years many nonparishioners from the neighborhood have also entrusted their children to our school. Teachers are the Sisters of 

St. Francis.

In 1913, a new spacious school building was erected on the grounds of St. Francis' Home. Besides the class rooms, it contains a large hall for school meetings and parish parties.

PARISH SCHOOL IN SOUTH BOSTON 

Since the parishioners of Holy Trinity were scattered over a wide area, a third parish school was opened in 1895 on F Street, South Boston. The teachers were Sisters of Notre Dame, until 1913 when the Sisters of St. Francis took over. The building was a large mansion which had been adapted to the purposes of a school. 

Holy Trinity School in South Boston existed for thirty years. It was discontinued in 1927, and the building was sold.

HOLY TRINITY HIGH SCHOOL 

In 1919, a Junior High School, commercial course, was opened by the Sisters of St. Francis in Roxbury, for the girls of Holy Trinity Parish. This Junior High School had an average number of forty pupils and existed for 31

fifteen years, until 1933, when it was replaced by a complete four-year high school with college and business courses.

Holy Trinity High School at present has 75 pupils. It enjoys the reputation of being one of the best girls' high schools in Boston. The students have their own library, glee club, orchestra and a growing alumnae association. 

SPECIAL FEATURES OF EDUCATION 

The personal interest of the resident Jesuits in education is evidenced by the fact that in the earlier days of the school at various times, when the place of the male teacher was vacant, one of the fathers taught classes in the boys' school. Thus the Pastor, Fr. Reiter, though overburdened with other work, taught school for several months in 1863 until overexertion broke down his health. 

Another feature of the schools of Holy Trinity deserves special mention. The fathers personally have been conducting religious instruction all through the years since 1848. Each priest has his special classes and regular hours for this important work. 

One of the results of Catholic training in parochial schools is the great number of vocations to the priesthood and to religious life. Holy Trinity has a high number of such vocations, both men and women. 

In order to keep up the old schoolday friendships, the men formed in 1898 the Holy Trinity Schoolboys' Association, to which was added in 1922 the Holy Trinity Schoolgirls' Association, and in 1935 the Alumnae Association of Holy Trinity High School. Every year a social is held, there is a general Communion, and a banquet at which a few hundred former pupils are present. It is a source 32

of great joy when old schoolmates, now scattered all over greater Boston, meet at such reunions. These Associations also are giving help to the present generation of our pupils by establishing scholarships for poor graduates of the parish schools.

LANGUAGE OF TEACHING 

During the last century the morning session was taught in English, the afternoon periods in German. But with the increasing “Americanization” of immigrants the use of the German language in school soon became less important. From the end of the last century till 1918 only a period of half an hour was devoted to the study of German language. Since 1918 all classes are conducted in English only.

The teaching of catechism and religious instruction in general has been given in all the schools of Holy Trinity Parish, from early times, in English an interesting exception to the usual tradition of other national parishes. 

The priests and teachers of Holy Trinity were convinced the children should be familiar with religious terms in English so that when they leave their own parish, they easily and freely can join other parishes, make their confession in English, derive benefit from the sermons and be able to read Catholic literature in English. 

The correctness of the principle cannot be doubted by anyone. "Unwise as it would be to force a change of language in catechetical teaching, it would be equally injudicious to artifically retard the natural development." 

(The Catholic Encyclopedia, 1919.)

Organizations and Activities 

Holy Trinity Parish is fortunate in having an exceptional number of splendid organizations for various practical purposes of religious and social life. This feature of our parish has constantly attracted the attention and admiration of outsiders. It has received special mention in the "History of the Catholic Church in the New Eng land States" (Boston, 1899, Vol. I, Page 121). 

CONFRATERNITIES OF MARY 

The first Jesuit pastor, Rev. Gustave Eck, S. J., established in 1848 two great societies of spiritual purpose which are still in existence and constitute the center of devotional life among the married members of the parish – the confraternities of the Holy Rosary for men and for women. Daily prayers, frequent Communion, various charitable activities, special instructions and services are their main features. They have been a source of untold blessings for the families of the parish. At the turn of the century they numbered over a thousand members. 

Their present combined membership is five hundred. 

SODALITIES OF OUR LADY 

Under the pastorship of Fr. Eck in 1851, the Sodalities for Young Men and Young Ladies were founded at Holy Trinity.

A short list of their regular activities will show the immense influence which they exercise in forming the 35

younger generation: monthly general Communion, special devotions every two weeks, instruction and spiritual guidance, daily prayer of the rosary, a special personal devotion to Mary, organized work in Catholic Action, support of Catholic Missions, charity drives, lectures on religious and cultural subjects, library and apostolate of the press, sewing circle, debating society, dramatic club, auxiliary of the American Red Cross, military chaplains' aid, sports and outings, Sodality club house, Sodality rest home, socials and parties. 

The Sodalities attained their highest membership of over one thousand in 1904. The present membership is 

220 young men (140 of whom are in the Armed Forces) and 280 young ladies.

The confraternity of men and the Young Men's Sodality together constitute our parish group of The Holy Name Society.

THE HOLY CHILDHOOD 

This association of children was introduced in 1914 by His Eminence, Cardinal O'Connell, in all parochial schools of the archdiocese of Boston. At Holy Trinity, however, it had existed since 1849, being one of the oldest parish organizations.

The children pray for the Missions and make small but regular contributions for the "buying" of pagan infants and their baptism and Christian education. This beautiful work of mercy has a special appeal to the hearts of children and is peculiarly fitted to plant in them the seed of true Christian charity.

At Holy Trinity some special features are attached to the Holy Childhood according to the old traditions of the parish. Every child is entered at baptism into the Holy 

Childhood Association, receiving a certificate of member- 36

ship. Our parish has headed for many years the contributions for the Holy Childhood, although the number of school children is comparatively small. The annual Holy 

Childhood procession, on the feast of the Holy Family, is one of the most attractive ceremonies. In former years this procession was called the “Shepherd's Procession" as the children marched through the church dressed as shepherds and shepherdesses a lovely relic of popular medieval piety.

The altar boys are pupils of our grade schools. 

The Holy Trinity USHERS' CLUB, founded in 1903, consists of single and married men who have devoted themselves to the service of ushering both in the upper and lower church and in the public chapel at Roxbury. With loyal self-sacrifice they come faithfully from a great distance to all the services. Besides their work in church and their monthly meeting, the Ushers also conduct social and charitable activities.
