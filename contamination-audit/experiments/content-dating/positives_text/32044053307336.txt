Foreword 

-0 The number of slave laborers in the Soviet Union and its satellites runs into the millions and represents the most extreme violation of human rights on the face of the earth today. The United 

States, recognizing that peace can be built only on freedom and the respect of the rights of man, has a responsibility for bringing before the world the conditions of forced labor which exist in the 

Soviet Union.

The United States, acting toward this end, initiated moves that led to the creation of a factfinding body in the United Nations under the Economic and Social Council and the International Labor Organization to explore the conditions of slave labor in the Soviet Union and elsewhere. 

It backed this group, known as the Ad Hoc Committee on Forced Labor, with extensive documentation' on the slave-labor system in the Soviet Union which it had gathered from a vast number of sources and in many areas. 

In waging the Campaign of Truth, the United States is making known to the millions of free workers the world over the nature of the Soviet slave-labor system. It is doing so in order to answer the Soviet assertions designed to show the Soviet Union as the champion of the working man.

Both the American Federation of Labor and the Congress of Industrial Organizations, working through the International Confederation of

Free Trade-Unions, have joined in this task of documenting and telling the world of the Soviet slave-labor system. They have done so recognizing that the most powerful free trade-union movements in even the strongest of the free nations are in danger as long as millions of laborers are being enslaved and that the advances made over the centuries are being imperiled with this new assault on the rights of man.

The study refers to forced labor in a narrow sense of the concept, namely to compulsory work performed by the inmates of prisons, "labor-colonies," and "corrective labor camps" (the Soviet terms for concentration camps) in or near their place of detention.

It does not examine the many forms of coercion which exist in the totalitarian Soviet Union and create conditions more or less akin to forced labor (e.g. compulsory inductions into the vocational training system, obligatory assignments for skilled workers and students, the job freeze under the decree of June 26, 1940, the corvée on peasants, compulsory work at a low wage in punishment for infringements of factory discipline, exile to remote places where working opportunities may be limited to a single mine or farm).

The paper attests to the following facts: 

1. Forced labor has been continuous throughout the existence of the Soviet system. The first concentration camps were organized a few months after the Bolshevik Revolution; since then they have grown into a vast institution. One-third of a century after its inception the Government of the U.S.S.R. still relies on forced labor and concentration camps. 

2. The exact number of prisoners is a Soviet state secret. Even the most conservative calculations are far above what should be the prison population of the U.S.S.R. if the per capita figures of countries outside the Communist pale or even of Tsarist Russia were used as a yardstick. 

3. Common criminals are a small minority among the forced laborers, and the camp administration allows them to dominate or even terrorize the other prisoners. These other prisoners are (a) political offenders; (b) people apprehended not because of any offense but because they were suspected of a lack of sympathy with the regime (such as relatives of political offenders, "bourgeois" or "kulaks" and their families); and (c) people who committed minor offenses or derelictions (infractions of factory discipline, petty black market operations, etc.) which in any humane society would call for disciplinary measures or a fine or, at most, a few days in prison. iii

4. Forced labor in the U.S.S.R. is a punishment applied either in judicial proceedings based on Soviet criminal law (with its vaguely defined "counterrevolutionary crimes") or under administrative procedure. Victims of administrative incarceration have no court trial at all because they are not necessarily charged with commission of any illegal act. 

5. While in Soviet theory penal institutions have the purpose to reeducate their inmates, in reality they are places of brutal punishment characterized by an arbitrary camp regime, overwork, inadequate shelter, a hunger diet, insufficient clothing, and lack of medical care. These conditions have continued through the decades. 

6. Forced labor is a significant feature of the 

Soviet economy. This is clearly revealed by the 

Soviet Economic Plan for 1941. 

The Plan, classified by the Soviet authorities to prevent disclosure, presents official data on the contribution of forced labor to economic activities planned for that year. For instance, more than 

14 percent of the capital construction was to be the work of forced labor; no other People's Commissariat listed in the plan has such a high share. 

The police share in the production of industrial timber and firewood was planned at 12 percent, of railroad ties 22.5 percent, of chrome ore 40.5 percent, etc. There exists, incidentally, evidence that forced labor is playing an important part in the so-called Great Construction Works of Communism, a program for building canals, irrigation, and hydroelectric projects currently under way. 

7. It may be assumed that in general forced labor has been used because the Government had in its hands large numbers of "undesirable" ele ments on whom it wished to inflict punishment whom it wanted to "liquidate," but whom it could exploit in the meantime for some economic pur pose. Even so, the presumption need not be ruled out that, in practice-if not in principle-people have been arrested because of the demand for forced labor. The vast police empire must have a natural inclination to maintain and even expand its activities. Its leaders are probably eager to lay their hands on interesting projects, and the next step is to round up or retain the necessary number of prisoners. There are enough laws and decrees and their provisions are flexible enough to increase the number of forced laborers simply by insisting on a more severe and comprehensive enforcement policy. In such a case, minor infractions which might otherwise have gone unnoticed will lead to long forced labor terms, and unscrupulous agents of the judicial and police systems might even frame innocent people in order to curry favor in the eyes of their superiors. The dangerous combination of judicial and police power with "big business" in one single administration-the MVD/MGB-is one of the reasons for the magnitude of the Soviet forced labor system. 

8. It should be noted that forced labor as an establishment of great economic importance has followed the Soviet flag. It is well known that the countries in the Soviet sphere of influence are being patterned after the Soviet model and that the Soviet forced labor system is one of the institutions which have been copied. iv

I. Forced Labor in the Soviet Union A. Character and Scope of Forced Labor in the U. S. S. R. 

Forced labor has been almost from the time of the October Revolution a constant characteristic of Soviet society. In seizing power the Bolsheviks were ambitious to destroy the old elite and the old institutions and to create a new society with institutions more to their liking. Use of forced labor was related to both ambitions, since it could be used to dispose of undesirable persons and perform some of the work needed to create a new society. 

In the first decade of rule the new Communist elite turned this weapon principally against the dispossessed elite lingering on from the former regime. In the succeeding period of construction of Stalinist socialism the rulers exploited for forced labor principally groups which the regime claimed to represent, the workers, peasants, and the new intelligentsia. 

The repression, including forced labor, introduced by the Red Terror in 1918—even if in response to opposition violence-exceeded in scale anything which had been known in Tsarist Russia. 

Nevertheless, the propagandists employed by Party and Government claimed that this was a temporary phenomenon which would disappear with the liquidation of opposing classes and the achievement of socialism. In fact, however, the opposite occurred. Use of forced labor against the remnants of the aristocracy and bourgeoisie was as nothing compared to the use of forced labor later against the groups supposedly allied with the Bolsheviks.

Forced labor took on mass dimensions after 1929 when ambitions of the ruling group collided with desires of the masses. The elite wanted to preserve and to extend its power. It was ambitious to increase rapidly and at all costs the power and resources within the grasp of the Soviet state while eliminating opposition to its policies within and without the ranks of the Communist Party. The peoples of the U. S. S. R. wanted the better life. they had been promised, in particular release from tyrannical rule and an increase of their material well-being. The regime sought to increase industrial output quickly, especially in heavy industry, and to secure control of agricultural output. A policy of mass deprivation and mass expropriation followed. The resistance engendered by this policy provided great numbers of candidates for repression. Beyond any repression of dissidents, the regime used wholesale forced labor as a weapon against entire economic and ethnic groups which the rulers decided to destroy. The Soviet leaders sought to derive maximum economic benefit from these victims by organizing their labor power to serve the economic program of the state. 

The Soviet Government was unwilling to employ economic incentives potent enough to attract sufficient free labor to the undeveloped areas of the Soviet Union, areas thinly populated and difficult to exploit. Consequently, the Government concentrated the largest masses of forced laborers in the remote areas of Siberia, Central Asia, and the Far North. Not all persons subjected to forced labor in confinement were sent to these distant camps, however. Many camps and colonies were located in and near the well-populated areas. Especially for "short-timers" the nearer camps could, with little waste of time and transportation facilities, usefully supplement the supply of free labor in various parts of the U. S. S. R. 

In addition, they served as an everyday reminder to the people of the need for conformity. 

B. Degrees of Involuntary Labor 

In a society as tightly controlled as the Soviet Union it may be asked if there is a valid distinction between forced labor and free. Much coercion is embodied in the relationship between the allpowerful Soviet state and the individual employee. 

A different kind of coercion finds expression in the relationship between the state and the farmer. 1

Compulsion or coercion of labor takes various forms. (1) Youths, often from farms, are drafted for training in labor reserve schools and bound afterwards, like graduates of higher schools, to work for 3 or 4 years on assigned jobs. (2) 

Workers may not leave jobs without permission, and contrariwise, certain qualified workers may be sent on obligatory assignment to other jobs in other localities. (3) Farmers are required to work a certain number of days each year in repairing roads and the like. This survival of the medieval corvée system has disappeared in most European countries but not in the U. S. S. R. (4) For infringement of certain rules workers may be sentenced to a type of involuntary labor with reduction of 25 percent in wages, but they remain either at their normal place of employment, or at least outside the barbed-wire enclaves. (5)

Workers, peasants, and intellectuals may be exiled to remote places inside the U. S. S. R. where working opportunities are limited to a single factory or mine. Such exiles become in effect forced laborers. (6) If not exiled to a specific place, Soviet citizens may be banished from their home towns and forced to find residence and work elsewhere. 

Although all of these relationships involve a degree of compulsion sufficient to merit a description as forced labor, they are not of primary concern in the following text. They are discussed only as they bear upon forced labor proper. 

Forced labor is a punishment meted out to those who have offended the powers that be or are considered as potential offenders. Forced laborers are persons confined for political or economic reasons in prisons, labor colonies, or concentration camps (in present-day Soviet terminology, corrective-labor camps) and compelled to work in or near the place of confinement.

Included in the great number of forced laborers are both those sentenced by courts through regular or special courts and those sentenced by administrative order. These groups are not treated separately in the present text for the simple reason that the Soviet authorities lump them together in forced labor camps. Certain distinguishing characteristics should be noted, however. Investigation of such "crimes" as those listed in article 58 of the Soviet criminal codes is in the hands of the Ministry of State Security (MGB), an offshoot of the Ministry of Internal Affairs (MVD).

The most important component of the MGB is the so-called political police, which has existed almost from the beginning of the Soviet regime and the bearer of the "glorious tradition," to use phrase from Soviet propaganda, of the Chel the OGPU, and the NKVD. The political poli can decide after completing an investigati whether or not the suspect should be turned ov to the courts for prosecution. If the decision against court prosecution, the police agencies c then, without even the semblance of a trial, se tence the victim by administrative fiat to a 5-ye term at forced labor. If the decision is to pros cute in court, no great uncertainty arises as to t danger of the victim's being released. Judg are sufficiently well indoctrinated politically; t defendants' protections are weak; and procedur are so adjusted that a verdict of guilty can be e pected. Then the victim once more enters th grasp of the political police, who administer th entire system of forced labor.

D. Eyewitness Accounts of Soviet Forced Labor Before World War II forced labor in the 

U. S. S. R. had already assumed enormous proportions. Because of Soviet censorship and tight border controls there were few witnesses who were in a position to testify to the human consequences of the system. The war changed all this. Vast dislocations of population affected Russians, Ukrainians, Azerbaijani, and other nationalities of the Soviet Union, as well as Polish citizens, former residents of the Baltic States, and, of course, Germans. Many of the Soviet citizens who had been abroad refused to return at the war's end, choosing the uncertainties of life in other countries in preference to the cruel certainties of life under a concentration-camp regime. 

They were justly apprehensive of Soviet measures of "re-education" following their stay abroad.

This "re-education" did in fact include large doses of forced labor.

Among the displaced persons were many who had served terms in Soviet labor camps. They had a story to tell which vividly portrayed conditions in forced labor camps up to the war. Poles who had experienced similar conditions in the early years of the war brought the story more nearly up to date when their testimony was made available, as in the notable book entitled Dark 

Side of the Moon.

The reconsolidation of Soviet authority has now dried up the main stream of eyewitness accounts. 

Nevertheless, enough Germans (ex-PW's sentenced to forced labor camps) have managed to leave the U. S. S. R. recently to show that the forced labor system continues to operate in the postwar years in the same old way.

E. Estimates of Size of Forced Labor Group 

Neither reticent Soviet references to the forced labor system nor survivors' accounts offer a means of estimating the number of forced laborers. To fill this gap various students of Soviet affairs have attempted to estimate, on the basis of various statistics, the forced labor population. It is natural that these estimates should vary widely, but part of the discrepancies results from differences not only in the period considered but also in the definition of the group to be estimated. The definition may include-in addition to the basic group of inmates of "corrective labor" camps-forced laborers interned in colonies, exiles, persons punished by forced labor at their place of work, and prisoners-of-war.

Dallin and Nikolaevsky in their book, Forced Labor in Soviet Russia, pass review on various estimates of the number of prisoners running up to 20 million persons and even higher. Their own guess ranges from 8 to 12 million forced laborers.

In 1948, N. S. Timasheff computed the number of forced laborers in 1937.2 He based his estimate on the election returns of that year. Calculating that the population on January 1, 1938, was 167 million, Timasheff estimated that adults of more than 18 years constituted about 58 percent of this total. Since only 56 percent of the population, or 94 million, registered for the 1937 election, Timasheff concluded that 3.3 million missing adults had been deprived of electoral rights. Of these a certain number, possibly one million, were either insane, feeble-minded, or ordinary criminals. The remainder were in prison camps.

An attempt was made by a research member of the Russian Research Center of Harvard University to find the number of forced laborers by making a detailed breakdown of the population as of January 1, 1939, by major occupational groups. 

Forced laborers become the residual category.

For 1940 Harry Schwartz estimated the number of forced laborers on the basis of the gap between payroll figures issued by the Central Accounting Administration and the "inclusive payroll for the economy as a whole." G. Social Effects of the Forced Labor System 

Forced labor on the tremendous scale on which it exists in the U.S.S.R. brings terrible conse quences in its wake.

Those most seriously affected are, of course, the victims themselves, persons confined for shorter

or longer times to forced labor camps. Their lives are likely to be, in Hobbes' phrase, "nasty, mean, brutish, and short." Work is burdensome and for long hours. Skills frequently become rusty. 

Work conditions are hazardous to health, and hence illness comes often. Inadequacy of medical facilities delays or prevents recuperation. 

Human relations deteriorate in the unnatural society of the camps. Even if the victim survives his camp existence and returns to free society, he may be handicapped in rehabilitating himself either by physical or psychical defects or by the stigma attached to his name. 

The forced labor system brutalizes others besides the victims. The keepers and guardians responsible for guarding the unfortunate inmates suffer a blunting of moral fibers and display tendencies to degeneration. 

The system makes fear routine in the population. It shuts mouths which should speak freely and inspires the utmost caution and servility.

By its existence on a vast scale the forced labor system makes necessary the continued elevation of the political police and their continued penetration into the innermost recesses of everyday life. Since the role of the police is in turn dependent in part on the size of the forced labor establishment, a vicious rhythm is created which continues to exert deleterious effects on Soviet society.

II. The History of Forced Labor in the U.S.S.R. 

A. Forced Labor Constant in Soviet Society

Forced labor has been a constant feature of Soviet society from the early days of the civilwar concentration camps to the present period of large-scale economic enterprises maintained and operated by prison labor. In the first years of the regime the Bolsheviks through proclamations promising reform of prisoners attempted to obscure the seamy reality of this institution. In recent years the Soviet authorities by their almost complete silence on forced labor have attempted to enforce secrecy about the hundreds of camps and millions of prisoners. Nevertheless, there is ample evidence not only of the economic and political significance of forced labor in the Soviet Union but also of the inhumanities which have accompanied its application through 34 years of 

Communist rule.

At every stage of Soviet development the forced labor system has been adapted to changing political and economic needs. The use of forced labor as a means of political control has been constant, but the groups most seriously affected have changed in accordance with shifting emphases in political warfare. The economic significance of forced labor has varied with changes in economic programs.

B. Forced Labor in the Period of 

War Communism, 1918-1921 

Forced labor camps were first opened in the fall of 1918 with the institution of the "red terror." Political repression of the former ruling groups, the raison d'être of forced labor in the. first decade of Soviet rule, was presented as a temporary measure directed against an enemy class destined to destruction. In this period forced labor was on a small scale, with perhaps less than 100,000 in camps in 1921. This number was particularly small compared with the large-scale operations in subsequent years, when the same repressive measure was directed against workers, peasants, and the revolutionary intelligentsia, the purported mainstay of the regime. 

Nevertheless, it was in this early period, 19181928, that the forced labor system became entrenched in Soviet society. In the first months of the revolution the Bolsheviks organized a special political police agency, the Cheka, which has continued to exist under other names to the present. This police agency played a key role in the operation of forced labor camps, partly because it possessed the power to sentence victims to exile or to forced labor camps without any judicial procedure, by administrative decree only. The labor performed by the victims was in the early years of only incidental value to the state, since little attempt was made to organize it on a profitable basis. This was also true of the other widely heralded "progressive penal institutions," such as labor colonies or corrective labor houses, to which errant citizens of the working class were sent. 

C. Forced Labor in the NEP Period 

1921-1928 Concentration or forced labor camps continued to exist all through the period of the New Economic Policy (NEP), 1921–1928, along with the system of penal institutions for nonpolitical criminals and the work projects organized for those sentenced to forced labor without incarceration. 

Although the NEP period represented a considerable relaxation from the stringent persecution

applied during the first years of Bolshevik rule, there was little essential improvement in the status of the political prisoner. The repression carried out earlier by the Cheka found its continuation in the activities of the GPU-OGPU. 

It was in this early period that the authorities formulated the legal basis of the forced labor system. One such principle involved the previously mentioned acceptance of extrajudicial methods for sentencing to forced labor, and control of forced labor camps by the political police.

The criminal law codes instituted during NEP fixed forced labor as a standard penalty. These codes, typified by that of the R.S.F.S.R. (1922; revised in 1926), distinguished between forced labor with confinement and forced labor without confinement. The former involves incarceration in a labor camp, or colony, and the latter compulsory performance at a reduced rate of pay in either the person's regular job or some other assignment. These varieties of forced labor have been perpetuated to the present day. 

In addition to establishing norms of the criminal law, Soviet authorities in this early period promulgated the first corrective labor code. This was issued in the R.S.F.S.R. in 1924. It was followed by codes of the other republics. These statutes established a detailed set of rules for persons condemned to all types of forced labor, and provided for a system of social stratification in Soviet penal institutions in which criminal elements were considered the elite. 

D. The Early Plan Period, 1929–1934 

With the end of NEP and the resumption of the "socialist offensive" in 1929, forced labor assumed new importance. In the earlier years forced labor was aimed at former "exploiting" classes. In the period of collectivization the farmers who held small tracts of land and a few head of livestock became the chief victims. Five million of these peasants with their families were eliminated, as Molotov acknowledged, i.e., were uprooted and transported to remote regions of the U.S.S.R. 

The Five Year Plans were inaugurated at this time to bring rapid industrialization to Soviet society. The greatly expanded economic activity of the state was attended by a substantial increase in the population of the forced labor camps. By 

1931 the number of inmates in both prisons and corrective labor camps is estimated to have been almost 2 million for the R.S.F.S.R. and the 

Ukraine alone."

The economic exploitation of Soviet prisoners by the OGPU and its successor, the NKVD, became more obvious as the years passed. Soviet figures relating to several projects employing prison labor were published during the 1930's. 

They indicate that the U.S.S.R. employed more forced laborers on each of several projects than the total number of prisoners forced to perform heavy labor in any year under the Tsar. According to a Soviet source, the highest point of repression under the old regime was reached in 1913, when 33,000 convicts were engaged in penal labor. 

In an attempt to refute Western charges about Soviet forced labor, Molotov reported to the All- Union Congress of Soviets on March 8, 1931, that there were "about 60,000" persons performing corrective labor on three highways, a railway and the White Sea-Baltic Canal.

In actual fact, a much greater number of forced laborers was employed on these projects, which constituted only a small portion of the total forced labor activity. In 1933, upon completion of the canal alone, about 72,000 of the prisoners who had worked on the project were freed or received shortened terms by governmental decree. Similar decrees in 1937 released 55,000 prisoners who worked on the Moscow-Volga Canal and 10,000 who worked on double-tracking the Karymskoye-Khabarovsk railway.

The period of the First Five Year Plan was important for developments in the legal basis of forced labor, which since that time has remained substantially unchanged. Both the 1930 statute on corrective labor camps and the 1933 R.S.F.S.R. 

Corrective Labor Code (see p. 8), which regulates all other types of forced labor, are still in effect. 

In addition, a 1930 law of the R.S.F.S.R. introduced into the Soviet judicial system the principle of exile at forced labor, hitherto applied only administratively by the OGPU. III. Forced Labor in the Soviet Legal System 

A.

Sentencing With and Without 

Court Procedure 

Forced labor is an inherent part of the Soviet legal system. It is firmly based on Soviet legislation, on criminal codes, and on administrative regulations. Over 30 years of experience have allowed the Soviet authorities to weave the system of forced labor into the legal fabric of Soviet society. In fact, laws defining forced labor as a penalty and establishing forced labor camps offer impeccable testimony from the Soviet side as to the existence and importance of the forced labor system.

Forced labor is a punishment applied in both criminal judicial proceedings based on the codes of Soviet criminal law (or uncodified criminal laws) and in administrative proceedings where no violation of law is charged. Each serves as a kind of conveyor transporting victims from free society to forced labor camps. Political prisoners may go by either route to camp. 

If sentenced under judicial procedure, victims are charged with violation of a certain article of the criminal code or some statute. The criminal laws include many provisions under which the prosecutors can bring into court persons accused of political offenses. These offenses, so far as charges have any basis in fact, are usually not direct expressions of political opposition to the regime. 

They are usually marginal expressions of lack of enthusiasm for the regime, such as telling jokes on government leaders. 

If sentenced under administrative procedure, victims merely have to be ruled "socially dangerous" by the Soviet police agency. They have no court trial at all, because they are not necessarily charged with commission of any illegal act. The entire matter is within the jurisdiction of the police, who apply the power of administrative in- carceration on a far more extensive basis than was ever true of the Tsarist police.

The police agency is crucial in the entire process. 

Having full power to "investigate," the police can decide after investigation and with assent of the prosecutors whether or not to have a court trial.

A court trial is then a brief interlude in the victim's career with the police during which another agency of the government, the court, rules on his fate. Following sentencing he is again under police jurisdiction as political prisoner.

B. Criminal Legislation Regarding Forced Labor 

Criminal legislation of the U.S.S.R. and its constituent republics institutes forced labor as a punishment and defines the "crimes" for which this penalty may be inflicted.

The criminal codes now in effect in most republics of the U.S.S.R. date back to the NEP period, when forced labor became a constituent part of the formal legal structure. The prin- cipal code, that of the R.S.F.S.R., was enacted in 1922 and revised in 1926. It defined ten types of punishment, of which two concerned forced labor. These provided for (1) "deprivation of freedom" (i.e., incarceration) with or without strict isolation and (2) forced labor without incarceration. The distinction expressed in the formula "with or without strict isolation" appeared in the basic U.S.S.R. criminal legislation, the socalled "Basic Principles of Criminal Legislation of the U.S.S.R. and the Union Republics," enacted in 1924.10 [It was reformulated about 1929 into a distinction between camps "in remote localities of the U.S.S.R." and "general places of con-

C. Forced Labor and Political Offenses 

Soviet laws include numerous provisions which can be and are used as the basis for incarceration of political prisoners. In the codes of criminal law now in effect, for instance, there is the wellknown article 58, which defines a series of offenses called counterrevolutionary. These include vaguely phrased crimes such as aid to the "international bourgeoisie," propaganda directed toward the "weakening of Soviet power," struggle against the working class and revolutionary movement, and sabotage. Article 76 prohibits "public insulting of official representatives." Article 122 punishes those who inculcate minors with religious beliefs in state or private schools. Other laws, not included in the codes, define offenses of a semipolitical nature. 

Many of these crimes are so loosely phrased and interpreted that they can catch many political victims. In addition, the codes still allow courts to apply "repression by analogy," that is, according to those articles dealing with related crimes.12 (The Nazis followed the Soviet example by introducing into the German Criminal Code the principle of analogy.) D. Terms of Forced Labor 

Administrative exile and forced labor applied by police agencies is for terms up to 5 years, though nothing prevents the MVD from adding 

5-year extensions if the officials consider the victim still "socially dangerous" or perhaps more useful in the economic enterprise of MVD than in other economic activities.

The usual terms of forced labor at confinement under criminal law range upward from sentences of 1 year to 10 years. Since 1937 a sentence of 25 years of deprivation of freedom has been possible for punishment of such crimes as espionage, wrecking, and "diversionary" activities. In 1947 other offenses were added to the 25-year category." 

There is a correlation between length of term and place of confinement. Sentences to deprivation of freedom for 3 years and above are served as a rule in the remote areas. Those sentenced to less than 3 years usually serve their term in the general places of confinement. The "Basic Principles" specify that the latter category of prisoners shall be confined as a general rule in "corrective labor colonies." 14

Labor Camps The 

Bolshevik penal policy envisaged no classless society, even in the prison camp society. Corrective-labor codes in both the 1920's and 1930's established distinct categories of prisoners. early code singled out from the mass of prisoners professional criminals and non-proletarians who committed crimes as a result of class orientation. Emphasis on class increased during the collectivization drive of the early 1930's, when the peasants resisting loss of their farmland and inventory were branded "kulaks” and packed off to forced labor.

A little known but very important statute of this period illustrates the philosophy regarding categories of prisoners. This is the statute of

The law classified all prisoners into three categories: (1) workers (first offenders) sentenced for terms of 5 years or less and not for counterrevolutionary crimes; (2) workers as above sentenced to terms over 5 years; and (3) all non-working class elements, plus workers sentenced for counterrevolutionary crimes. Prisoners of the first category are required to spend not less than 6 months in the initial regime, those of the second category, not less than one year, and those of the third category, not less than two years. Employment of the prisoners is divided into three main classifications: 

1. General work. 

2. Work in institutions, enterprises, trades, logging operations, etc. 

3. Work in the administrative-economic apparatus of the camp.

H. Forced Labor Without Confinement

Although main interest centers on the legal basis of forced labor camps, forced labor without confinement should also be mentioned. Like forced labor in camps, forced labor without confinement has its roots well back in Soviet history. The codes of criminal law now in effect institutionalized the penalty in the 1920's and it has been persistently applied since that time.

At the present time forced labor without incarceration is applied as a general rule only to sentences of one year or less. The R.S.F.S.R. Corrective Labor Code of 1933 specified that forced labor without incarceration shall be the basic measure of corrective labor action applied to convicted individuals who are not required to be put in isolation (isolation here in the broader sense of separation from normal society). The police using extrajudicial procedures, as well as the courts through judicial proceedings, can sentence persons to forced labor without confinement.

Under the code, corrective labor without incarceration is divided into two distinct forms. In either case a fixed sum of money is deducted from the pay of the victim up to a legal maximum of 

25 percent. 

1. Sentences up to 6 months to be served as a rule at place of work. 

2. Sentences over 6 months to be served either at place of work or on work organized by corrective labor organs. Collective farmers as a rule are to serve this category outside their farms. 

A person not permanently employed usually serves his sentence at a colony of the MVD. 16

I. Exile and Banishment 

Exile and banishment are punishments intimately related to the whole forced labor system. 

Like katorga (hard labor), they are reinstitutions of practices from Tsarist days loudly condemned earlier as evidences of barbarism. In 

Tsarist Russia, however, exile and banishment were applied in a relatively small number of cases (17,000 as the maximum in 1907), whereas the Communists have developed these methods of police control to an extent unknown before in Russia or anywhere else in the world. Both are applied by police agencies without trial as well as by court proceedings. The new techniques were perfected in programs of mass deportations carried out during the collectivization period of the 1930's and were later applied with ruthless energy and careful organization to the population of the Baltic States in 1939-40 and to various Soviet minorities in ensuing years. 

1. BANISHMENT 

Banishment is the removal of a convicted individual from a given locality without obligatory settlement in a specified locality. In essence this means that one who is banished may reside in any place not forbidden to him by his sentence. Banishment, which may be applied for 5 years in a court proceeding or extrajudicially, is of two types: 

1. Banishment from a given place without any express limitations in selection of a new place of residence. 

2. Banishment with limitations on alternative residence expressed in the prohibition. 

2. EXILE 

Exile is the removal of a convicted individual from a given locality with forced settlement in a specified locality. In general it applies to those charged with more serious offenses. Exile may be decreed with or without forced labor. Exile in extrajudicial procedure with no reference to lawbreaking can be applied to those considered "socially dangerous" by the MVD's Special Conference for terms up to five years. In court proceedings the crimes which may be punished with exile are listed in article 36 of the R.S.F.S.R. .

Criminal Code and include the more serious acts defined by articles 58 and 59. Simple exile is applied by the courts in terms of one to five years. 

Exile can also be applied by the courts for terms up to 10 years with forced labor for the entire sentence or for part of it. Forced labor in exile is regulated by the rules established under the corrective labor code of 1933, which specifies that such individuals shall perform this labor in the following ways: a. For hire in state, cooperative, and public enterprises and institutions on the basis of contracts between these enterprises and institutions and the corrective labor institutions. b. In enterprises especially organized for this purpose by the corrective labor institutions. c. In mass work organized by contract between the corrective labor institutions and state and cooperative organs. d. In colonies for mass work. 

3. APPLICATION OF EXILE AND BANISH- MENT 

Not only the police but also the courts have wide latitude in sending into exile or banishing persons considered "socially dangerous." The

R.S.F.S.R. Criminal Code of 1922, largely reenacted in the code of 1926 now in effect, declared in article 49 that persons recognized by a court as "socially dangerous" by reason of criminal activity or connections could be forbidden to reside in specified localities for periods up to three years. The "Basic Principles" of U.S.S.R. 

Criminal Legislation (1924, still in effect) declared that exile and banishment "shall be applied by the courts in respect to individuals recognized as socially dangerous by reason of their criminal activity or their connection with the criminal environment in a specified locality." The same law stated that both exile and banishment might be applied by the courts without legislative justification and even to persons acquitted in a criminal proceeding: "These measures may be applied by the courts on the initiative of organs of the prosecution to the designated category of individuals (those recognized socially dangerous) regardless of whether they have been subjected to criminal prosecution for commission of a specified crime, and also in such case when they are acquitted by the courts 17

of the charge of commission of a specified crime, but are recognized as socially dangerous.' "20

From 1929 to the present, the maximum sentence to exile has been ten years. In addition, since 1929 courts have had the power to institute a supplementary punishment of exile for individuals sentenced to corrective labor camps in remote localities of the U.S.S.R. Such individuals, after completing their term in the camps, may be required to settle in the camp area for a certain period of time, during which they are deprived of the right of freely selecting their place of residence.

J. Soviet Mass Deportations and 

Forced Labor 

In addition to individual selection of forced labor candidates, the Soviet authorities have resorted at times to mass deportation, particularly in areas newly added to Soviet territory or in areas where widespread disaffection with Soviet rule on the part of entire peoples found expression in wartime. The authorities not only arrested, deported, and broke up these groups, but sent a great many of the victims to forced labor. 

Soviet methods of arrest and exile are well documented by captured Soviet files on the deportations from the Baltic States during 1939-41. 

Lists drawn up by the People's Commissariat of 

State Security (NKGB) provide for the arrest of a wide variety of individuals, both for political and criminal reasons. The political suspects run all the way from Trotskyites to Free Masons and relatives of those who have escaped abroad. Those individuals who were not shot were transported to a large variety of points throughout the Soviet 

Union. A police document of June 13, 1941, listed some of these, which included localities in the area of Karaganda, Krasnoyarsk, Tomsk, Gorki, and others.

IV. Administration of Forced Labor Camps A. Historical Development 

From the earliest days the organization and maintenance of the forced labor camps was largely in the hands of the Cheka and the NKVD. With the advent of the Red Terror and the corresponding increase in the camp population, a Central Administration of Forced Labor Camps was set up in the NKVD.23 This was charged with responsibility for general administration of all camps on the territory of the R.S.F.S.R. In the early 1920's the NKVD assumed even greater responsibilities in this field. The 1922 law on the structure and organization of the NKVD established a new Main Administration of Forced Labor, which was given the responsibility for establishment and maintenance of forced labor camps and other places for serving out forced labor.24 A short time after this, on July 25, 1922, a decree of the R.S.F.S.R. Council of People's 

Commissars ordered that a single administration be formed from the Justice Commissariat's Cen tral Corrective Labor Section and the NKVD's Main Administration of Forced Labor. This ad ministration was created on October 12, 1922, as the Main Administration of Places of Detention 

(GUMZ) of the NKVD. In other republics similar administrations were formed under the local NKVD's. 

All places of detention continued under the control of GUMZ until 1930, the year that the old NKVD's (of the republics) were abolished. A significant exception concerned the camps for political prisoners maintained by the OGPU. As we have seen, substantial changes occurred in Soviet penal institutions around the end of NEP which were reflected in the statute of April 7, 1930, "R.S.F.S.R. Laws, 1919, 12: 124. 

24 R.S F.S.R. Laws, 1922, 33: 386. on the corrective labor camps under the jurisdiction of the OGPU. Sometime after this a central 

Main Administration, known as GULAG, was set up to run these camps. The statute liquidating the NKVD in December 1930 ordered the transfer of all other places of detention to the People's Commissariats of Justice of the Union Republics. 

The latter were also given charge of organization of exiles at forced labor and of forced labor without incarceration.25 To administer these institutions, a new administration of the Justice Commissariats was formed, entitled the Main Administra tion of Corrective Labor Institutions (GUITU).

C. Administration of Camps: 

Recent Developments 

Since 1934 the procedures for control of forced labor camps and other penal institutions have undergone no basic change. During the purge period of the middle and late 1930's the scope of GULAG's responsibilities and its administrative structure expanded considerably as the NKVD increased its economic functions. This trend continued into the following decade. GULAG was revealed by the 1941 Plan to be one of the largest single entrepreneurs in the Soviet Union. 

Since the end of the war, the activities of the NKVD (MVD) in the construction field appear to have been curtailed, although it still retains responsibility for a series of important economic undertakings. There are unfortunately few means of determining the effect of this shift in functions on the operations and structure of GULAG. Soviet sources in recent years speak little of the U.S.S.R.'s forced labor system and hardly at all of the institutions which administer it. At the present time the control of penal institutions is definitely among those functions handled by the MVD on a centralized basis.29 This alone would indicate that penal labor continues to form an integral unit in Soviet planning, and is undoubtedly still a factor of great importance to the development of Soviet economy.

E. Current Camp Administration 

Administrations of the individual camps follow the pattern of organization generally accepted in the U.S.S.R., an organization into sections very similar to the central structure. The chief of each camp usually has under his control the following sections: 1. Administration 

2. Production 

3. Third (or investigative) section 

4. Accounting and control 

5. Finance 

6. Technical supplies 

7. General supplies 

8. Cultural-educational 

9. Medical

The chief production unit in the corrective labor camp system is the camp center (lagpunkt), headed by a chief assisted by the chiefs of the production and administration sections. 

Among the various sections, the third one plays a very special role. It is vested with broad powers and establishes measures of reprisal against guilty prisoners. Formerly it had even the power 21

of carrying out death sentences. There are secret collaborators (seksoty) in the section's network; it is directly responsible to Moscow for the political condition of the camp, and represents therefore the MGB within the MVD. The chief of the third section is only nominally responsible to the chief of the camp.

Apparently as a solution to the complex problem of administering justice among increasing numbers of camp inmates, a Special Camp Court system was established by a decree of the Presidium of the Supreme Soviet on December 30, 1944.

A Camp Court was set up in every Soviet penal institution and was primarily concerned with offenses by inmates, though it may also have had jurisdiction over civilian MVD workers. Militarized MVD personnel were specifically excluded from the competence of the camp court. The importance and size of the Special Camp Court system was attested to by the creation of complementary subdivisions in the U.S.S.R. Supreme 

Court, Ministry of Justice, and Office of the Prosecutor General.

Prisoners are employed in intra-camp administration in all large corrective labor camps. At one time they are reported to have occupied the posts of chiefs of camp administration sections and chiefs of secondary camp centers. Since the mid-1930's the tendency to appoint prisoners, especially political prisoners, to responsible offices has diminished. It is believed that already at the beginning of the war period the majority of chiefs of secondary camp centers were voluntary NKVD workers.

Like Soviet administration on the outside, labor camps suffer from bureaucracy. In the lagpunkty, there are reportedly innumerable technicians who fill the offices with their staffs. Most administrative duties fulfilled by the prisoners are performed at present by ordinary criminals in conformity with the dictates of Bolshevik class discrimination. This state of affairs is naturally not conducive to honesty in administration, and graft in the camps is reported to run to considerable proportions. 22

V. Economic Significance of the Soviet Forced Labor System A. Use of Forced Labor to Implement Plans 

Forced labor is an important feature of the Soviet economy. According to a statement by 

V. M. Molotov, at that time Chairman of the

Council of People's Commissars, dated March 8, 1931, the labor of prisoners is used "in certain municipal and road operations. 

We have done this in the past, we are doing it now, and we shall do it in the future. It is in the interest of society." Because of its magnitude and intensive exploitation forced labor has become a factor of sufficient economic importance to be included in Soviet economic plans. Already under the first Five Year Plan, in 1929, the Soviet Government realized the economic potentialities of forced labor: "The Five Year Plan required tasks involving a great demand for unskilled labor. 

Local conditions sometimes present serious obstacles to the recruitment of labor. It is here that the places of confinement, having at their disposal excess labor in great quantities [italics added] can come to the assistance of those economic enterprises which experience a labor shortage." From 1930 on planning agencies have formally incorporated work performed by prison labor into the Five Year Plans, and a new agency (GULAG) which is still in existence was established in the OGPU to administer the network of labor camps. The latest official Soviet evidence on planned use of forced labor is contained in the Economic Plan for 1941, which gives information on the extent of production activities on the program of the Commissariat of 

Internal Affairs (NKVD, since 1946 MVD).

In 1941 the NKVD, according to official Soviet statistics, was to contribute 1.2 percent of the industrial production of the Soviet Union (in 1926/1927 prices) but this figure understated the importance of NKVD activities for a number of reasons. (1) The figure does not include the output of gold, and it should be noted that the U.S.S.R. is the second largest gold producer in the world and that the police apparatus seems to provide most of the Soviet gold output. (2) Forced labor is employed mainly in extractive and other basic industries, the output of which in relation to that of manufacturing industries is undervalued by the prevailing price system. Even so, the planned output of the 

NKVD was higher than that of the Commissariats for Construction Materials, for the Paper 

Industry, and for the Fishing Industry. (3) The percentage share of the NKVD in total output does not take into account the product of camp inmates working for enterprises of other ministries, to whom they are contracted out by the MVD. This farming out business seems to have assumed large proportions. (4) The NKVD share, finally, fails to disclose the importance of the NKVD as a supplier of labor. Since the productive activities of the 

NKVD are concentrated in labor-intensive branches of the economy, its labor input per unit of output is greater than the average for the economy as a whole.

The NKVD, on the other hand, employed some free labor in industry. The numbers involved, however, were planned to be small, compared to the NKVD's total activities, namely in the neighborhood of 80,000 (see Economic Plan for 1941, p. 545, Suppl. 262–263).

The productive role of the MVD is more clearly pointed up by the extent of its activities in specific sectors of the economy. In 1941, the NKVD was assigned to greater volume of construction than 23

any other ministry. Next in importance was the utilization of forced labor in mining and lumbering.

There is evidence that forced labor is playing an important part in the so-called Great Construction Works of Communism, a 7-year program for building waterways, irrigation, and hydroelectric projects. Soviet propaganda boasts that completion of this program in such a short time can be undertaken only under a planned economy in a Socialist country. It has carefully avoided any statement, however, on the great extent to which the success of this undertaking depends upon the availability of a large and highly mobile army of forced laborers. An official announcement of the role of MVD in the construction of these projects has, of course, not been made, but the director of design and research work on all phases of the construction program, S. Ya. Zhuk, as well as many of the chief engineers, has a long service record with the MVD. Zhuk, in particular, played an important role in the construction of both the White Sea Canal and the Moscow- Volga Canal, and is at the present the chief of 

GIDROPROYEKT, the MVD hydraulic design administration. This is sufficient indication of the MVD's participation. The MVD and its predecessors have ample experience in the construction of hydro-projects and waterways, since they were in charge of the White Sea-Baltic "Stalin Canal" (1933), the Moscow-Volga Canal (1937), and the reconstruction of the Stalin Canal in 1946.

Inmates of the MVD's corrective labor camps are, in the main, assigned to unskilled and heavy manual tasks in construction, timber, and extraactive industries. C. Productivity of Forced Labor 

There is widespread belief that the Soviet forced labor system has been established primarily for economic reasons, i.e., as a source of exceedingly cheap labor. Thus it has been asserted that the police does not undertake its many economic activities because it has large numbers of prisoners at hand, but the other way around, that it plans its economic activities first and then arrests the appropriate number of people under this or that pretext.

The advantage of cheap labor means in this connection that the difference between the value of what the prisoners produce and the cost of their upkeep plus depreciation is larger than in the case of free labor. It is well known that throughout its existence the Soviet regime has followed the principle of maximizing investment and military outlay at the expense of consumption. 

But though the consumption of the prison population has been kept at or even below the subsistence level, it does not follow that their toil has yielded the largest possible increment of funds for investment or military purposes.

First of all, the productivity of forced labor is much lower than that of free men. This does not necessarily apply to individual cases. Given long working hours, the calamitous tie-in between output and food ration, and the threat of punishment, a healthy man in a prison camp may, for instance, cut the same amount of lumber over a period of time as in a camp for free workers. But there are numerous circumstances which lessen the productivity of forced labor far below the level of free labor. 

1. Forced labor has the considerable overhead cost of the police apparatus from its top administration down to the guards watching the prisoners. 

2. All reports agree that the prisoners spend an inordinate amount of time in jail and on transports from camp to camp. There are examples of prisoners with relatively short terms (six months to two years) who spent only one or two months working in a camp. It is reported that in recent years efforts have been made to streamline the exploitation of the prisoners by cutting down on long stays in jail and long hauls between places of detention. 

3. Though there have been efforts to utilize the skill of the prisoners, their work in the camps results, as a rule, in an appalling waste of talent and skill and this in a country which has a scarcity of skilled labor. People trained at a considerable cost to the community as electricians, machinists, doctors, etc., are employed as common laborers. 

4. The camp administration is frequently inefficient and the herding together of political prisoners with common criminals of the worst type reduces the productivity of the camps in general. 

5. Under the camp conditions described in this report a large portion of the prisoners are unable to work because of punitive measures such as beating or solitary confinement and, most important, because of undernourishment, disease, and accidents.

It must finally be added that a forced labor system of such a magnitude as in the Soviet world must, necessarily, lower the productivity even of the free population because of the anguish it causes the relatives and friends of the prisoners and because of the ill effects on the health, morale, and training of former prisoners. 

This leads to a second consideration. Human beings are a costly resource because in their youth they consume without producing. If their lives are shortened either because they perish in their prime in some jail or because the prison term shortens their life after their release, it is the nation as a whole that loses. In economic terms the depreciation rate on the investment in forced labor must be high. This consideration holds, of course, only for the native population. Prisoners of war, who were brought up in a foreign country and after release become a social charge in a foreign country because of ill health acquired during their detention, are in a different category. 

Under these circumstances it is highly doubtful whether the forced laborers' net product available for investment and public consumption (such as military use) is larger than that of an equal number of free workers. But even if it should be larger, it would be simple to reduce private consumption of the nation as a whole through taxation and loans by a corresponding amount. An infinitesimal reduction in the consumption of the free population would bring about the same relation between private consumption and investment (plus public consumption) that is now achieved through the starvation of several million prisoners surrounded by a large unproductive police apparatus.

This is even true of problematical projects carried out in certain remote areas which, for per- 28

fectly sound economic reasons, have been left uninhabited until recently. If the Government, for strategic, prestige, or other non-economic reasons, feels compelled to develop those wastes, a sound method would be to offer wage and other incentives to free workers and tax the population as a whole correspondingly, i. e. reduce the consumption of the large bulk of the nation somewhat in order to allow the prisoners a higher standard of living. 

It is not known whether such considerations have ever entered the decisions of the Kremlin. It may be assumed that in general forced labor has been used because the Government had on its hands large numbers of "undesirable" elements on whom it wished to inflict punishment, whom it wanted to "liquidate," but whom it could exploit in the meantime for some economic purpose. Even so the presumption need not be ruled out that in practice-if not in principle-people have been arrested because of the demand for forced labor. 

The vast police empire must have a natural inclination to maintain and even expand its activities. Its leaders are probably eager to lay their hands on interesting projects and the next step is to round up or retain the necessary number of prisoners. There are enough laws and decrees and their provisions are flexible enough to increase the camp population simply by insisting on a more severe and comprehensive enforcement policy. In such a case minor infractions which might otherwise have gone unnoticed will lead to long forced labor terms, and unscrupulous agents of the judicial and police systems will frame innocent people in order to curry favor in the eyes of their superiors. The dangerous combination of judicial and police power with "big business" in one single administration—the MVD-is one of the reasons for the magnitude of the Soviet forced labor system. 

It is useful in any discussion of the way in which political arrests are made in the Soviet Union to distinguish between arrests of isolated cases and mass arrests. Each is conducted in a rather distinctive manner and under different circumstances. Individual arrests are made day in and day out, while mass arrests are sporadic, fitful, associated with some great occurrence or directed against some special class, locality, or nationality. The wholesale arrests made after the assassination of Sergei Kirov in December 1934, the great drive against the so-called "kulaks" in the early 1930's and the mass deportations from the Baltic States during and after the war are notable examples of such mass arrests deportations. and 1 29

VI. Arrest of Suspects A. Individual Arrests 

Arrests are made on the basis of warrants issued by the state prosecutors. The actual process of arresting usually takes place late at night or in the small hours of the morning at the home of the accused. Whether the persons under arrest may take with them personal necessities depends in good measure upon the whim and humor of the arresting officers, though they will desperately need these items in the prison and in transit to a labor camp, in view of the niggardly and negligent attitude of prison and camp officials. B. Mass Arrests 

Mass arrests differ from individual arrests in that the persons arrested often are not even given the semblance of a judicial hearing but are placed into trains immediately after arrest and dispatched to a labor camp. They are often prejudged guilty merely because they belong to a specific group or a class about which the Soviet Union has doubts rather than because of any specific hostile acts committed against the Soviet Union. Such mass arrests and deportations occurred in the area of eastern Poland occupied by the Soviets in accordance with the Nazi-Soviet Pact of 1939 after the Nazis had attacked Poland. 

Esther Lea Witkowska tells of such a deportation which took place from Brest-Litovsk. In the spring of 1940 individual arrests were made by Soviet authorities of refugees who refused to accept Soviet citizenship and to take Soviet passports. Soon these individual arrests were replaced by mass arrests and deportations. Witkowska, who was herself caught up in these mass arrests, describes them as follows: "The raid took place at night. When darkness fell, detachments of NKVD, militia and Red Army soldiers, in cars and trucks, would appear on the streets. They would cut off whole blocks, round up the refugees and take them directly to the railway station where cattle cars were already waiting for them. "On June 29, 1940, I found myself in such a car with 24 other prisoners. There were men, women, and children, all packed together in terrible congestion and unbearable filth, but it was even worse and even more crowded in the other cars." 19 39 After waiting in the station for two days without food or drink the train started out on July 1 for its destination, the town of Degtlarka in the Ural Mountains. The journey took 11 days and was effected under the most appalling conditions for the prisoners. Upon arrival they were set to work at forced labor in the five copper mines located at Degtlarka.

VII. Investigation and Trial 

Extensive possibilities for the prosecution of socalled "counterrevolutionary" crimes are afforded by the Soviet legal system. Not only is the general understanding of such crimes more comprehensive than that of political crimes in many other states, but the judicial criteria for a wide variety of punishable offenses are established only in very loose terms. In addition there is a broad general definition of the term "counterrevolutionary crime" which includes, among other points, "any action directed... towards weakening the power" of the Soviet Government, of its "external security," and of "the basic economic, political and national conquests of the proletarian revolution." 45 Article 59 of the Code also sets up an even broader group definition of "crimes against the administration." Soviet legal organs may be assumed to find adequate justification for their sentences in such statutes. Furthermore, all actions not punishable under law may be handled by the principal of extrajudicial procedure, which prescribes the imposition of severe terms of punishment on individuals merely "recognized as being socially dangerous."

A. The Preliminary Investigation 

Following his arrest, the accused undergoes a period of preliminary investigation which may be conducted by a variety of police organs. For the more serious type of crime, including the charge of counterrevolutionary activity, the investigation is conducted exclusively by detachments of the 

Ministry of State Security (MGB). The accused is given no opportunity to be represented by counsel during the investigation, and his sole defense during this period consists of what he is able to prove in his own exoneration. Theoretically, under Soviet law, the lack of a defense counsel is 

45 R. S. F. S. R. Criminal Code (1926, revised), art. 58. 

compensated for by the actions of the investigator, who is supposed to give equal weight to evidence for as well as against the accused. A leading Soviet legal theorist has stated recently: "The principle of objectivity of the preliminary investigation in Soviet criminal procedure consists in the fact that the investigator, while carrying out his accusatory function . . . at the same time defends the rights and the legal interests of the accused. He not only offers the accused the chance to defend himself, but is obligated to investigate all circumstances favorable to the accused, collect and verify all proofs exonerating the accused or extenuating his responsibility." 

In actuality, such solicitous concern for the rights of the accused does not exist, since it would require the wisdom of a Solomon to carry on the functions of an accuser and defender simultaneously. All survivors' accounts agree that the investigator concentrates exclusively on proving the accused guilty of the crime with which he is charged. One competent observer who underwent the experience of a Soviet interrogation, has reported: "The commissars in the Lubianka have, in my opinion, three main aims to begin with. First, they try to get to know the prisoner and to assess his intelligence, character, and powers of resistance, as well as his weaknesses and ambitions. No detail of his private and family life is too insignificant for them. "Secondly, they try to work on his imagination and to weaken his spirit by causing continual changes between the extremes of hope and despair. B. Rules of Criminal Procedure According to the R.S.F.S.R. Criminal Pro cedural Code (art. 104), the prosecutor or the local 

People's Court must be informed of an arrest within 24 hours after its occurrence and must either approve or reject this action within a further 48 hours. C. The Trial 

The trial and sentencing of persons condemned to forced labor is conducted either through the established court system or by extrajudicial procedure in the Special Conference of the MVD.

Court trials do not follow the pattern of those in Western countries where the accused is guaranteed adequate opportunity to defend himself, but appear to be intended largely as a legalistic device. to confirm the results of the preliminary investigation. VIII. Detention and Transportation of Persons Sentenced to Forced Labor 

Persons sentenced to forced labor usually spend a few weeks or months in a transit prison. Here their lot becomes somewhat easier as the Soviet Union differs from other countries in that accused persons are generally treated worse before trial and sentence than after. The hideous nights of interrogation or of waiting sleeplessly for the call to the interrogation even if the latter does not actually take place finally come to an end. Persons are generally not taken back to their cells after sentencing but are transferred to other sections of the prison to await transport.52 Their lot while waiting for shipment to a camp may be improved in that authorities sometimes allow the receipt of parcels by the prisoners 53 just prior to their dispatch to the slave labor camps. 

Prisoners are forwarded to the camps via train, truck, barge, ship or by foot with the typical journey utilizing some combination of these means of transport. It is carried out by the transportation section of GULAG and the convoys are accompanied by special MVD convoy units. Conditions while en route by any one of these means are frightful and belong to the most difficult experiences the prisoner has to undergo during his prison term. The most important means of remanding prisoners is by train and it is this means which will be first considered.

IX. Camp Life A. Political and Cultural-Educational Work in the Camps 

Officially the Soviet authorities regard even the political prisoners as being salvageable for reintegration into Soviet society. The Large Soviet 

Encyclopedia, vol. 29, p. 601, carries a typical statement in this regard: "... Soviet corrective labor policy cannot have any dealings . . . with the wholesale denial of the possibility of reeducating criminals from among class-hostile elements.

Such statements fit in with the Soviet propaganda contention that Soviet penal institutions do not exist simply to punish the criminals, as in "bourgeois countries," but to reeducate him and prepare him for a new and better life as a fullfledged citizen.75 Other Soviet statements make it patently clear that such institutions exist to exploit fully the labor of its inmates. The great object of this labor is, via a system of compulsion, to crush "class-hostile elements." The same encyclopedia article quoted goes on to say: "On the other hand, the view is also incorrect which regards corrective labor establishments as purely educational or even purely economic establishments and glosses over the elements of compulsion in the work of corrective labor establishments and in this way slips into a denial of the class question in the carrying out of corrective labor policy and into a refusal to carry out the tasks of crushing hostile elements and corruptive elements."

The picture of camp life given by eyewitnesses bears out fully that camp authorities are much A leading example of this view is the book by A. J. 

Averbakh, titled expressively From Crime to Labor, published in 1936 with a preface by Andrei Vyshinsky. "A process," writes Vyshinsky in the preface, "which is pursued each day, each hour, in the fire of a creative enthusiasm which burns faults and old vices, which purifies men of their old errings and which fortifies them for a new life of happy and gay labor." more interested in extracting the last full measure of the workers' strength in the accomplishment of camp labor than in political, educational, or cultural work. The way in which this program works is summarized by one former inmate: "In all camps there are cultural education departments.

B. Class Distinctions 

Though most camps contain both political and criminal prisoners and representatives of all classes and occupations, Soviet penal policy both in statute and theory and in actual practice makes a sharp differentiation among them as to treatment. One of the earliest differentiations made was with regard to those who came from the proletariat and the peasantry and those who came from other classes. Soviet laws and juridical writing make it clear that the first two categories of prisoners receive preferential treatment. Thus, for example, if one looks at the "Statute on Cor rective-Labor Camps" (Polozhenie ob Ispravi telno-Trudovikh Lageryakh) of April 7, 1930, one finds the following provision in the section entitled "cultural-educational work": 82a

Early laws of the Soviet period provided for the "possibility of liberation before their term of inmates who, during their isolation from society, had paid for their past crimes and had distinguished themselves by personal application.' 

The order of the Cheka, No. 10 of January 8, 1921, however, marking the difference between worker and bourgeois, told prison officials "not to apply liberation before expiration of sentence to the bourgeoisie."

Closely paralleling the difference made between persons coming from a peasant or a worker background and those from the bourgeoisie, is the differentiation made between persons convicted or sent to camps for "counterrevolutionary" crimes and common criminals. Soviet sources make it amply clear that criminals are superior to counterrevolutionaries. This is a direct reversal of the Tsarist policy of regarding political prisoners as being superior in status to common criminals. All accounts of former labor camp inmates agree that the criminals are rarely punished for their depredations. The faith placed in the criminal class as hostile to political prisoners is amply justified. 

Thus another Pole reports that the criminals were intensely hostile to foreigners; while robbing and assaulting them, they called their victims bourgeois, fascists, and capitalists. 86 Accounts all agree that criminals in the camps do little work. Most are able to secure some very sort of job in the administration, guard, culturaleducational section, etc. In some camps they do not go to work at all but sit around the barracks playing cards. When sent to a work site, they resort to bribes, etc., in order to have the brigadier put them down in the record as having completed the norm. Moreover, on the job they monopolize the good tools. They are able to get away without doing any work, or very little, by connivance with the camp authorities, in many cases criminals like themselves. Then too many camp authorities fear the criminals who form a tightly knit group. 

C. Resistance

One is struck by the fact, in any extensive perusal of literature dealing with the problem of 

85 Ibid., p. 195. 

86 Anders Collection (R4289). forced labor based on the experiences of eyewitnesses, by the almost complete absence of cases of overt, mass resistance to the camp authorities. 

One looks in vain for many instances of riot by the prisoners or of armed resistance to the authorities. 

In accounting for this it should be first pointed out that the camp authorities possess in the criminal class a section on whom it can always rely to act against the other inmates. Criminals are usually not segregated from the other prisoners but are mixed in with the political inmates, among whom they form a hard core. The political prisoners, on the other hand, find it difficult to combine. The usual camp population is composed of many nationalities and many different strata of society. Thus one source states that in his camp there were present Turks, Chinese, Uzbeks, and Armenians mixed together with Russians and Poles. It would be difficult to effect any combination in such a welter of nationalities, social groups, and interests. The criminals, on the other hand, are banded together by a way of life and by long experience in labor camps. 87 Equally important is the physical undermining of the average prisoner's health which makes him think little beyond the next crust of bread. The long hours of brutal labor, slow starvation, and miserable living conditions in general soon weaken his system and his resolve so that any thought of resistance becomes impossible. Many prisoners are nothing else but horses of toil, as one source describes them. Such prisoners lose all semblance of humanity and become completely unbalanced. 

The concepts of family and country have no appeal for them. Hunting for bread fills their whole life and the spirit of revolt is completely missing. 88 As for the other prisoners the most that can be expected from them is refusal to work or occasionally a hunger strike. Such modified resistance is usually met with stern and cruel counteractions. "Discipline [in the camps]" Averbakh asserts, "is as stern as it can be made." 89 And the accounts of eyewitnesses show that this is no idle boast.

D. Graft and Influence 

The iron discipline and brutish conditions of the camps are only modified by the practice of "blat" and "tufta." Blat is prevalent in all of the

Soviet Union and may be loosely translated as "pull." Blat in the camp means the by-passing of regulations due to influence, gifts, and "personal relations" (compassion or friendship). To the political prisoner blat may be a life-saver as it may mean his transference from the slow death in the mines or forests to some easier work such as carpentry or even a job in the camp administration. 

Eyewitnesses have related how they used blat to make their lot easier. Closely associated with blat is "tufta," which may be translated as "padding."

Norms which are completed only 70 to 80 percent are listed being 100 percent completed. The distance which prisoners have to carry gravel, timber, and other products is listed as being twice the distance. Hills to be dynamited are reported twice as large as they are in actuality, etc. Often it is only because of tufta that inmates were able to receive food for the full norm. An example of the way in which this system works is related by one source in the following words: 66 because the amount of food depended on the work performed, the foremen willingly accepted bribes of underwear, clothing and other objects and with the help of this system raised the [reports of], norms of work [performed] to the actually unattainable 100 percent, [we] received better food and larger portions of bread.⁰2 

Bribery is also resorted to in order to receive preferential work assignments. Not a few inmates owe their survival because they possessed a good garment of some article in order to bribe someone in the administration to give them a work assignment other than the brutal work of the forest or mine.

X. Treatment of Inmates A. Shelter 

The adequacy of and type of shelter provided prisoners varies with the part of the Soviet Union to which the prisoner is sent, the type of economic activity the camp is performing, the age of the camp and the status and job of the prisoner. As a general rule, though, it may be stated that housing is grossly inadequate for the needs of the prisoner.

The most common habitation of the permanent type is in barracks within a rectangular-shaped enclosed area surrounded by barbed wire. At each corner armed guards are posted in lookout towers although, if the camp is large, these towers may be also placed in additional points along the enclosure. In some camps the barbed wire is replaced by a wooden stockade. The standard dimensions of barracks are: 36 meters long, 5 meters wide, and 2 to 2.5 meters high. Barracks are usually made of wood with an earthen floor. Inside, the barrack is divided into 2 rows of sleeping shelves built along the walls and separated by a corridor of about 1 meter wide running down the center. Sleeping shelves are built in either two or three decks and each shelf accommodates up to 4 persons. These barracks are commonly built of freshly-cut timber so that the barracks are always moist inside. The humidity is a constant scourge of the prisoners as most of the camps are located in the north where rains are frequent and the ground is often swampy.

Barracks contain one or two stoves but these are usually inefficient and only heat up the area immediately around the stove. Most of the prisoners receive little benefit as only a few of the hundreds of persons in each barrack are able to crowd around the stoves. Moreover the habitual criminal class (urki) will usually appropriate these places so that the non-criminal prisoner will have no opportunity to dry his clothes.93 Finally 03 

Anders Collection (R4289). there is the difficulty of fuel procurement to keep the stoves going. The situation reported by one former inmate in the camp where he worked may be cited in this regard. In the fenced-off camp yard not a tree stump was left. Though prisoner gangs were sent to bring firewood for the kitchen, the bakery, and the bath barracks, no detail was provided for wood for the barracks. Their liv ing quarters could be heated only by wood brought individually by prisoners returning from work and the latter were usually too tired to carry any." 

In other camps the barrack orderly has the responsibility of fetching wood for the stoves. 

Prisoners sleep on wooden shelves and often are crammed in a frightful fashion. One former inmate states that in his barrack men were so crowded that the men on each sleeping shelf had all to sleep on one side, and all had to turn on the other side at the same time.95

Officially prisoners are provided with an adequate supply of bedding. (See paragraphs 91-92 of the Ukhta-Pechora Regulations.) In actuality inmates sleep on bare boards or on their clothing while bedding consisting of straw sacks and covers is generally issued only to forced laborers of the privileged sort (office workers, administrative employees, specialists). In some camps even the provision of shelves is not made and prisoners are forced to sleep on the bare floor or in the corridor." 

Many of the barracks have no windows while those that have them have rags stuffed in them in winter to keep the cold out as the glass is usually missing. In actual practice the prisoners care little if their barracks have transparent windows in them or not as they leave the barracks before daylight and return only after dark. Some barracks are provided with dim electric bulbs while others use kerosene lamps. "Ibid. (6166).

The vitamin content of food has an important relation to the worker's efficiency. That the vitamin content in Soviet rations is very low is shown by the necessity to provide an "anti-scurvy" ration in paragraph 36 of the Ukhta-Pechora regulations.

The total caloric content of this supplementary ration is 10,151 calories or a daily ration of 339 calories. But in order to obtain such supplementary dishes (as distinguished from a supplementary bread ration), the worker must complete at least 100 percent of the norm.

Paragraph 15 provides a daily ration of 3,808 calories for the most highly qualified of the technical personnel. This ration is reserved for the social elite of the camp, one of the categories listed in paragraph 15. This list includes engineers and technicians, skilled workers in production offices and sections of administration, managers of state farms, managers of electric stations, etc. 

Paragraph 28 of the regulations provides that "Prisoners who have been submitted, in a disciplinary manner, to penal confinement without permission to work; prisoners forbidden to work; and prisoners held without permission to work in penal confinement in the capacity of being under investigation for a crime newly committed in the prison-camp, consume according to the penal norms" a total of 21,481 calories for the month or a daily ration equal to 716 calories. This, of course, is starvation.

Thus, the official Soviet regulations themselves show the inadequacy of the prisoners' diet, assuming that these regulations are actually carried out in practice. The latter, however, is a gratuitous assumption, as the many reports of former inmates testify. Moreover, even if these regulations are carried out in practice important qualifications would have to be made. 

To proceed with the latter point first. What emerges from the great body of eyewitness accounts is the poor quality of food that prisoners receive, which lowers in food value the little they do receive. The bread is commonly very low in quality, clayey in consistency, and containing much water which adds to its weight (bread rations are distributed according to weight). The fish is often spoiled while the quality of other ingredients is also often of a very low quality. 

Another important qualification to be made is that a person unfamiliar with camp conditions might easily think that the additional rations coming with fulfillment and overfulfillment of the norm is easy of attainment and that the average worker performs this regularly. In point of fact this is far from the truth. A common complaint voiced in the accounts of former prisoners is that the norms of work which are set are too high. One source states that none of the prisoners in his group was able to fulfill the norm.8 Another states that the brigades in his camp accomplished not more than 20 to 30 percent. Soviet corrective labor camp legislation sets up three categories based on class derivation. The

E. Family Separation 

When a political prisoner begins his trip to a forced labor camp it is very unlikely that he will see his family for a long time. If the prisoner is married and his or her spouse is condemned to prison at the same time, then separation is the order of the day. Usually husband and wife are not only sent to different camps but also to regions far from one another.39 If a woman has children they are taken away from her, and usually to a I place where she is not allowed to go. 

In theory every prisoner has the right to receive one visitor a year. In practice most of the prisoners have to wait three and as much as five years before they can utilize this right. The procedure to be followed is that when a year has elapsed from the moment of arrest a prisoner is free to present a written request for a visit to the Third Section of the camp, together with a letter from one of his family that makes it quite clear that one of them wished to see him. The third essential is a certificate of good behavior both at work and in the barracks from the camp authorities. The latter is given only if the inmate has worked throughout the whole year fulfilling his full norm. 

The second item in the application is no easy thing for the prisoner to produce either. If the tie to the prisoner is not one of blood but of mar- 37

XI. Disposition of Inmates A. Death Rate 

In Soviet prisons and concentration camps death often claims the prisoner before his or her term is up, though the sentence may be for 3 or 5 years only. Under the prevailing conditions the prisoner's health is rapidly undermined through constant undernourishment, chronic overexhaustion due to the backbreaking work and the long hours demanded, inadequate clothing and housing, and the compulsion to work in bitter cold and intense heat. All of these factors, coupled with wretched medical facilities, give rise to a frightful death rate among prisoners, a fact mentioned in all eyewitness accounts of former slave laborers. Speaking of the conditions in the Kotlas Camp, located in northern Russia, Jules Margoline states: "During a half year in the year 1945, there died in the 5th corpus of the camp hospital in Camp Kotlas, where I was at the time, 15 out of 50 sick persons. They died as a result of dropsy, heart disease and other diseases which come as a result of continued malnutrition. 1956

B. Release

Even when a prisoner manages to live through his sentence this does not mean necessarily that he is automatically released; rather it is dependent upon a favorable conjuncture of several events. 

One of the most important factors in determining whether an individual will be released or not is whether his scheduled release coincides with a period of comparative leniency or severity in the treatment of prisoners. Thus the rates of release have differed markedly in the history of Soviet slave labor. In the early days of the slave labor camps a prisoner could even obtain release before the end of his term by doing good work. A prisoner, whether political or criminal, by consistently fulfilling his norms, could obtain two and even three years remission of sentence for every year of work. Gennadi Khomyakov, for example, accumulated two years in this manner and was thus released that much before the end of his term. 66 

Moreover, on the occasion of the completion of important construction works such as the White Sea and Moscow-Volga Canals thousands and even tens of thousands of prisoners were released while others had their terms shortened." C. Reintegration Into Civilian Life 

The lot of the prisoner is indeed difficult. He is unable to pick up in civilian life where he left off for the simple reason that little remains of his former life. He is often not allowed to reside in his former place of residence, as has already been mentioned. If a man's wife has been condemned with him it is difficult for the couple to rejoin each other inasmuch as it is a policy of the Soviet authorities to send man and wife not only to different camps but to different regions in addition. 

If the wife and children manage to remain out of prison his situation may be no better, for they may have abandoned him in the meantime. XII. Forced Labor in the Soviet Sphere of Influence A. Forced Labor Follows the Soviet 

Flag

It is a well-known fact that the countries in the Soviet sphere of influence are being patterned after the Soviet model in every manner possible and that the Soviet forced labor system is one of the institutions which have been copied. In Eastern Europe as well as in China laws and decrees have been issued which correspond closely to Soviet rules and regulations, e.g. by introducing forced labor at the place of work, administrative sentencing, pronunciation of groups of persons who approximate the "socially dangerous elements" and "counter-revolutionaries" of the Soviet legal system, and the mingling of common criminals with political prisoners. Soviet model and satellite practice differ in only one respect. The 

Soviet Union does not export forced laborers, as the so-called People's Democracies do. The satellite countries possess camps of their own but have also furnished prison laborers to the labor camps in the U.S.S.R. In the case of the European satellites the largest contingent has been the Volksdeutsche or ethnic Germans, together with German POW's and former soldiers. Abundant examples exist showing that these persons were sent into the Soviet Union under the same conditions that obtain among other forced laborers. 

In the case of China rumors persist that Chinese forced laborers have been sent to Siberia to work. 

B. The Legal Background 

Satellite legal systems, as in other aspects of the national life, are faithful copies of the Soviet model. They integrate in a similar fashion the repression of possible dissent by assigning forced labor for the accomplishment of their economic plans. 

1. CZECHOSLOVAKIA 

Czechoslovak laws mention with perfect candor a system of "forced labor" to be applied to arrested persons without the usual phrases about "re-education" or "communities for educational labor." Under the law of October 25, 1948, all forced labor camps are placed under the Ministry of the Interior. Persons are sentenced in an administrative procedure by so-called People's Committees of which there is one for every province. These Committees may condemn persons for up to 3 years of forced labor if they "threaten the establishment of the people's democratic order or economic life."

On July 12, 1950, a new criminal and procedural code was put into practice which provided for the application of forced labor sentences either by sentence of a court or by a People's Committee. In a certain variety of cases, however, jurisdiction is placed exclusively in the hands of the People's Committees. These offenses include offenses against the present economic order, economic planning, economic operations, against the public authorities, and against the general safety. 

2. BULGARIA 

Bulgarian statutes provide not only for the establishing of forced labor camps and colonies, but also for correctional labor without confinement.

This follows the pattern in the Soviet Union by providing for deductions from the wages of the person so sentenced while he works at his usual place of work (see Sec. 22 of the New Criminal Code of February 13, 1951). The Bulgarian forced 

67

labor system legally dates back to two decree laws issued on January 20, 1945. These regulations remained in effect, with a few changes, until March. 

25, 1948, when they were replaced by the law on the People's Militia. Under this law the Minister of the Interior may condemn any person to forced labor for one year who has shown an "anti-popular" attitude as well as malefactors such as blackmailers, swindlers, procurers, prostitutes, gamblers, and others. The law of March 25, 1948, also prescribes the "internment (of politically dangerous persons) in a new place of residence," that is, banishment to some remote place. 

Still another Bulgarian law, dating back to April 30, 1946, provides for the setting up of a special series of forced labor camps for those "who have taken to loafing and vagrancy and spend most of their time in saloons, coffee houses, bars, pastry shops, and the like." On May 9, 1949, this law was broadened to include persons "fit for work who do not perform socially useful work." In language very similar to Soviet penal legislation it provided, in section 9, that "appropriate measures shall be taken for their systematic enlightenment and re-education." 

3. OTHER EASTERN EUROPEAN SATEL- LITES 

Other Soviet satellites (Poland, Hungary, Rumania, and Albania) have legal enactments providing for forced labor that differ little from the examples cited. In Hungary, however, not only have the usual Communist laws been enacted but laws dating back to the days before the First World War, and further elaborated under the Horthy regime, have been kept in force. Both 

Poland and Rumania provide, in their law codes, for the punishment of persons who commit "offenses detrimental to the economic and social life. of the country." The Rumanian Criminal Code states flatly that if "harm to the economic, social, or political structure" is caused by the omission of an act, the person is "to be considered as dangerous to society." A further provision of the law is that "actions which are considered dangerous for society can be punished even when they are not expressly prohibited by law." 

4. COMMUNIST CHINA 

The basic legislation for the Chinese system of forced labor is contained in a February 20, 1951, decree of the Chinese Communist Government providing for death or life imprisonment for a variety of "counterrevolutionary" crimes including "collusion with imperialism," "disturbing the market or undermining finances at the order of the enemy" and "conducting counterrevolutionary propaganda and agitation and the fabricating and spreading of rumors."

Article 16 of the 1951 decree provides: "Other crimes with counterrevolutionary purposes, but not provided for in these regulations, may be punished according to a similar offense set forth in these regulations."

Article 18 provides for ex post facto application: "The provisions in these regulations are applicable to counterrevolutionary criminals who offended before the promulgation of these regulations." 

C. Economic Significance 

The system of forced labor in the Soviet sphere of influence is important both in numbers of persons confined and in economic tasks performed. 

The number of forced laborers in the Eastern European orbit alone has been estimated at about 1 million persons. Even more people seem to have been arrested in China.

The gamut of tasks performed by forced laborers in all satellite countries is quite large and extends from complex industrial processes to the crudest forms of manual labor. In Bulgaria forced labor camps, numbering at least 35, furnish labor for the following industries: coal mining, copper mining, dam construction, electric power stations, railroad construction, mountain roads and mountain passes, general road construction, vegetable gardening, and general farming. 

In Czechoslovakia forced labor camps are numbered at 179 at the lowest figure. In addition to such tasks as those performed in Bulgaria, forced labor is being used in uranium mining, stone quarries, airfield construction, a variety of factory operations, even including the making of costume jewelry, and work on fortifications. 

In Hungary the camps number 79 and engage in a similar variety of activities, in addition to gold mining, the making of prefabricated house parts, and the construction of ammunition fac tories. 68

In Poland the list of confirmed camps numbers 

21 with four others still unconfirmed. The range of activity includes fertilizer plant work, construction of various industrial plants, and harbor construction.

In Rumania a large portion of the forced labor supply is engaged in work on the Danube-Black Sea Canal. In addition, labor is engaged in pipeline construction, railroad extension, road con- =struction, and military construction.

As to China an idea may be gained of the extent of forced labor enterprises from an official Chinese 

News Agency Dispatch from Pei-P’ing describing the operation of the Ch'ingho Farm which has been in existence since March 1950. Up till that date the forced laborers there built eight new villages, excavated one and a half million cubic metres of ditches, and erected two power-driven pumping stations. The expected rice crop for 1952 from this operation is calculated to yield 15 million catties (nearly 20,000 tons). The conditions of work at the farm are as brutal as those on Soviet enterprises. "For instance," the dispatch goes on to say, "when ditches were dug last spring, water in the ditches froze, but some criminals stood digging barefooted in the ice and moving as much as 34 cubic meters a day."
