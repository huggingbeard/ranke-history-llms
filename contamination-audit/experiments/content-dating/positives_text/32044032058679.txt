LTHOUGH for purposes of the income tax, taxpayers are chiefly classified into corporations, and individuals, partnerships form a third distinct group, and estates and trusts, a fourth. 

The peculiar status under the income tax laws of the latter two groups is the subject of this discussion. As the treatment accorded to partnerships and to estates and trusts is distinct they will be considered separately. 

PARTNERSHIPS METHOD OF TAXING INCOME 

The partnership itself, as a separate legal entity, is not subject to income tax; the "individuals carrying on business in partnership" are liable for income tax "only in their individual capacity." This means that each member in a partnership is taxed separately on his distributive share of the "net income" of the partnership of which he is a member along with his other income. Whether or not actual distribution is made to him is immaterial.³

The partnership, however, as such is required to file a return (Form 1065) which must set forth the partnership income and each partner's distributive share of that income. The return is required even though the firm has no net income for the taxable year." It must be made for the taxable year of the partnership, that is, for its annual accounting period (fiscal year or calendar year, as the case may be) irrespective of the taxable years of the partners. Likewise, the partnership's income is computed on the cash or accrual method of accounting employed by it, regardless of the accounting methods employed by the partners in reporting their individual incomes."

PARTNERSHIPS COMPUTATION OF NET INCOME 

The net income of the partnership is computed "in the same manner and on the same basis as the net income of an individual”, but with the following modifications:

Segregation of Capital Gains and Losses. The partnership is required to segregate its capital gains and losses. These do not enter into the computation of the partnership net income but are required to be reported by the partners in their individual returns as part of their individual capital gains and losses." 

Computation of Ordinary Net Income. After excluding its capital gains and losses, the partnership must compute (1) its ordinary net income which consists of the excess of its gross income over deductions, or (2) its ordinary net loss which consists of the excess of its deductions over the gross income.10 Each partner is required to include in his individual return his distributive share of the ordinary net income, or the ordinary net loss of the partnership so computed.¹¹ 11 In the computation of its ordinary net income or net loss, the partnership is denied the deduction for charitable contributions allowed by Section 23 (o) of the Code, but each partner is allowed to deduct his distributive portion of such contributions in his individual return.12 Similarly, the credits against net income provided in Section 25 of the Code are not applicable to partnerships as such, but each partner is entitled, in addition to the credits allowed to him under Section 25, to a credit against normal tax of (1) his proportionate share of partially exempt interest received by the partnership, and (2) his proportionate share of the credit for interest on tax-free covenant bonds received by the partnership.13 He is also allowed as a credit his proportionate share of income, war-profits, and excess-profits taxes imposed by foreign countries or possessions of the United States, to the extent provided in Section 131 of the Code.¹4

243 Salaries paid to partners, and interest on capital contributions, are not deductible in computing the net income of the partnership, but are treated as part of the distribution of partnership profits.15

For the purpose of computing the earned income credit¹6 against net income, a partner is entitled to treat a proper part of his distributive share of the partnership net income as earned income. Such part may not exceed a reasonable allowance for personal services actually rendered in the partnership business.¹ 

Where both capital and personal services are income-producing factors, as in the case of a mercantile or manufacturing business, this allowance may not exceed 20% of the partner's share of the net profits.18 However, if the occupation is professional the 20% limitation is not applicable.19

The benefit of the deduction for net operating losses provided by Sections 23(s) and 122 of the Code (the net operating loss carry-back and carry-over provisions) is not allowed to the partnership, but is allowed to the members thereof in accordance O with detailed Regulations prescribed by the Commissioner.20 

In case the taxable year of a partner is different from that of the partnership, the share of the partnership's income to be reported by a partner is based upon the partnership income for its taxable year ending within the partner's taxable year." 21 Contrary to the general treatment outlined above, a partner is denied the benefit of the deduction for amortization of emergency partnership facilities allowed by Section 23(t) of the Code, but the partnership is allowed the deduction on its return.22

As we have seen, payments to the decedent's estate give rise to no difficult income tax problem if they represent merely his investment in the partnership plus his distributive share of partnership income to the date of his death.48 However, where following the death of a partner payments are made to his estate pursuant to the partnership or other agreement at other than the face value of his interest in the partnership, such payments raise problems affecting the income tax of the decedent's estate, the decedent's estate tax, and the income tax of the surviving partners. The effect in each of these instances depends upon whether the payments represent a distribution of partnership "income" as such or represent consideration paid by the surviving partners in "purchase" of the decedent's interest. If the payments made to the estate are characterized as "purchase" payments, the tax consequences are as follows: 

(1) The right of the estate to such payments must be valued, and such value included in the deceased partner's estate tax return.¹º (2) The payments received by the deceased partner's estate are not taxable as income to it except to the extent that they exceed the value included in the estate tax return.50 51 (3) Such payments are taxable to the surviving partners as part of the partnership income. Consequently the surviving partners pay an income tax on payments which they actually never receive. Such payments are deemed to be additional capital contributions by them to the partnership.

249 On the other hand, if the payments are characterized as the estate's share of partnership "income" as such, the estate is in effect a "partner", and the tax consequences are as follows: (1) The right of the estate to such payments must be valued, and such value included in the deceased partner's estate tax return.52 (2) The payments received by the estate are taxable as income to it,53 but it is allowed a deduction for the increase in the estate tax of decedent caused by the inclusion in his gross estate of the value of the right to receive such payments.54 (3) The surviving partners are not taxable on the portion of the partnership income representing such payments.55 

No categorical answer can be given as to when payments made by the partnership to the estate of a deceased partner will be considered "income" payments and when "purchase" payments. In dealing with this question the exact facts of a case are particularly important, as is also a careful examination of the partnership agreement in order to determine whether amounts paid pursuant thereto are a return of the decedent's capital interest and a payment of income as such, or a purchase of the decedent's capital interest by the surviving partners. In this connection it seems appropriate to observe that no draftsman of a partnership agreement should fail to give careful consideration to this problem in connection with the type of provision to be inserted for the liquidation of the interest of a partner in the event of his death during the term of the partnership.

PARTNERSHIPS ORGANIZATIONS QUALIFYING AS PARTNERSHIPS 

"Partnership" or "Association"? The Codes defines the terms "partnership" and partner” as follows:

The term "partnership" includes a syndicate, group, pool, joint venture, or other unincorporated organization, through or by means of which any business, financial operation, or venture is carried on, and which is not, within the meaning of this title, a trust or estate or a corporation; and the term "partner" includes a member in such a syndicate, group, pool, joint venture, or organization. 

The corresponding provision of the Regulations points out that for purposes of income taxation certain organizations are classified as "associations" taxable as corporations even though their members may be subject to personal liability as partners under local law.57 The test is declared to be as follows:

If an organization is not interrupted by the death of a member or by a change in ownership of a participating interest during the agreed period of its existence, and its management is centralized in one or more persons in their representative capacities, such an organization is an association, taxable as a corporation. 

The Regulations also provide that a limited partnership, although organized under the Uniform Limited Partnership Act which has been adopted in a number of states, may be either an "association" taxable as a corporation or a partnership, depending upon the characteristics already referred to. 

What then is an "association"? As described in the Regulations58:

The term "association” is not used in the Internal Revenue Code in any narrow or technical sense. It includes any organization, created for the transaction of designated affairs, or the attainment of some object, which, like a corporation, continues notwithstanding that its members or participants change, and the affairs of which, like corporate affairs, are conducted by a single individual, a committee, a board, or some other group, acting in a representative capacity. It is immaterial whether such organization is created by an agreement, a declaration of trust, a statute, or otherwise. It includes a voluntary association, a joint-stock association or company, a "business" trust, a “Massachusetts" trust, a "common law" trust, an "investment” trust (whether

EXTRAORDINARY TRUSTS 

The procedure which has been discussed above for the taxation of the income of estates and trusts to the fiduciary or to the beneficiary applies broadly to estates in the process of administration and to ordinary trusts in which the grantor has parted with all interest in the corpus and the income therefrom. That procedure is not applicable to revocable and other trusts which fall within Sections 166 and 167 of the Code, nor to trusts the income of which is taxable to the grantor under Section 22(a) of the Code because the grantor is considered as remaining in substance the owner for tax purposes. The discussion in this section deals with trusts of this character, that is, trusts the income of which is required to be included in the income of the grantor (1) for the reason that the grantor is deemed, for tax purposes, (not to have parted with the corpus of the trust, either (a) because he has retained a power to revest the corpus in himself within the meaning of Section 166, or (b) because, despite the existence of the trust, he is considered as remaining in substance the owner of the corpus and hence taxable on the income thereof under Section 22(a), or (2) for the reason that the income was, or might be, distributed to, or held for the benefit of, the grantor under 

Section 167. 

Prior to 1924, there were no specific provisions in the federal law for the taxation of the income of revocable trusts, or trusts the income of which might be distributed to or held for future distribution to the grantor. In that year the Treasury submitted to Congress two new subdivisions for this purpose,188 explaining as the reason therefor, that "trusts have been used to evade taxes by means of provisions allowing the distribution of the income to the grantor for its use for his benefit" and that the use of revocable trusts by grantors constituted "nothing but an assign- 

185 Merchants National Bank of Boston v. Commissioner, 64 S. Ct. 108, November 15, 1943. 

186§ 219 (j) and (k) of the Bill (H.R. 6715) which ultimately became the 

Revenue Act of 1924.

ment of the right to receive future income."187 The proposed subdivisions subsequently were enacted and, as amended, presently comprise Sections 166 and 167 of the Code. By their terms Sec tions 166 and 167 require the income to be taxed to the grantor only if he either has a power to revest the corpus in himself or to receive the income of the trust. "Because of the complexity and number of combinations of trust powers and character of beneficial interests inherent in the trust device,"188 considerable difficulty, therefore, was experienced in applying the two Sections to close the door of tax avoidance. A step in this direction was made in the application to cases which fell outside the strict limits of Sections 166 and 167 of the "satisfaction of obligation" or "benefit concept".189 In these cases it was recognized that a valid trust had been created and that under the normal treatment of trust income the income would be taxable to either the trustee or the named beneficiaries; such income was taxed to the grantor, however, on the ground that it was used to satisfy a legal obligation of the grantor and thus used for his benefit. 

More recently the courts have supplemented this concept by what has become known as the "doctrine of the Clifford case", 190 that is, that the income of an inter vivos trust may be taxed to the grantor under the broad provisions of Section 22(a) of the Code (which defines generally what constitutes gross income to the taxpayer) if the grantor has retained such control over the corpus or income of the trust as to be deemed in substance the owner thereof for tax purposes.

Because of the inter-relationship of these concepts in determining who bears the incidence of tax on trust income, it is practically impossible to separate the types of trusts and to state separately the rules which apply to each. Probably no other phase of the income tax law has shown as profound a change in the last several years as that involving the tax liability of creators of inter vivos trusts. Recent judicial decisions have not only undermined materially the importance of formalism in the creation of trusts, but have injected into trust problems general concepts which affect profoundly interfamily transfers or settlements of property and income and the drafting of trust instruments. In this field of tax law it is particularly important not to ignore the theory behind the concepts announced by the Supreme Court, or to rely unduly upon the strict adherence to the letter of the statute or the technical right of beneficiaries to income or corpus under local law. 

Application of Section 166 

Section 166 of the Code provides that where at any time the power to revest in the grantor title to any part of the corpus of a trust is vested (1) in the grantor, either alone or in conjunction with any person not having a substantial adverse interest in the disposition of such part of the corpus or income therefrom; or (2) in any person not having a substantial adverse interest in the disposition of such part of the corpus or the income therefrom, then the income of such part of the trust shall be included in computing the net income of the grantor. 

It should be noted that the statute does not refer to the actual revesting of the corpus in the particular taxable year, but provides simply that the power to revest must exist and be in the grantor during the taxable year. Therefore, the existence of the power to revest is the test of taxability under this Section rather than its exercise. Since the Section requires for its application that there be a "power to revest" title to the corpus in the grantor, it does not apply to cases where the corpus will revert to the grantor upon the expiration of a term without the exercise of any

power,191 although the income in such cases, if other factors are also present, may be taxed to the grantor under Section 22(a).192 "Power to Revest." A "power to revest" is defined in the Regulations as a "power or ability to retake the trust corpus to the grantor's own use" whether "effected by means of a power to revoke, to terminate, to alter or amend, or to appoint."193 Thus, the device or mechanics by which a power to revest may be exercised is immaterial in determining the taxability of the income to the grantor. For example, it has been held that where the grantor reserved the power to purchase the trust corpus for a nominal consideration, the trust was revocable to the extent of the value of the property which could be reacquired over the amount to be paid therefor by the grantor because the grantor had the right "to revest" in himself title to the corpus on making the required payment.194 Similarly, the reservation of broad powers of sale under which the grantor might buy from or sell to the trust at his own price, has been held to be substantially equivalent to a power to revest.195 The grantor also may be taxable on the income of a trust where he reserves the power to change the trust in any manner and there is no limitation upon his right to name beneficiaries.196

The income is likewise taxable to the grantor where a power to terminate the trust is reserved through the retention of a power to appoint an individual other than the grantor to exercise the right to terminate the trust in whole or in part.197

Application of Section 22(a) 

Apart from Sections 166 and 167 which specifically tax trust income to the grantor, every trust must be considered in the light of the broad definition of taxable income contained in Section

A common trust fund is a fund maintained by a bank252 for the collective investment of moneys contributed thereto by the bank in its capacity as trustee, executor, administrator or guardian in accordance with the rules and regulations of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System.25 A common trust fund is accorded special treatment in order to avoid the possibility that such funds might be taxed as associations.254 They are taxed in a manner similar to that of partnerships, each participant therein reporting its share of the net income of the fund, computed in the same manner and on the same basis as individuals, except for the segregation of capital gains and losses and partially exempt interest, and the disallowance of the deduction for charitable, etc. contributions.255 Every bank maintaining a common trust fund must file a return showing gross income, deductions, and the proportionate share of each participant therein in much the same manner as a partnership.256
