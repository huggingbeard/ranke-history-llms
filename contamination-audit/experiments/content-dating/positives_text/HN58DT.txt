To THE OFFICERS, TEACHERS, ALUMNI OF THE EPISCOPAL THEOLOGICAL SCHOOL AND TO OTHER FRIENDS 

Having known more or less intimately the Founder of the School, every one of its Officers, Teachers and Graduates, being also its oldest living alumnus, I am on this 

May 30th, 1940, my ninetieth birthday, sending you this

Sketch of the History of the School in which my father and I have taken deep interest from its beginnings. This story goes to you not only as a bit of local history, but also as a narrative of one of the many happy episodes of my favored life. It calls for no acknowledgment. 

Yours faithfully, William Lawrence

strong,

THE PAST 

HEN I was a Harvard freshman, seventy-three years ago, I roomed in the front of the old Brattle House facing Brattle Street. Time and again, as I looked out of the window, I saw walking by an old gentleman, handsome, and cheery, accompanying him a man about thirty years of age with a scholarly look, and three young men, evidently students. Under their five hats walked the whole Episcopal Theological School that was in residence. Two other teachers moved to Cambridge later. These three students made up the first-year class; the second-year class had no students. I had heard my father say that a Boston businessman, Benjamin Tyler Reed, had pledged one hundred thousand dollars to found in Cambridge a school for the education of young men for the ministry of the Episcopal Church and that a lawyer, Edward S. Rand, had the securities in his safe, and that several other laymen were to be Trustees. I knew also that the Rev. Dr. Francis W. Wharton, Rector of St. Paul's 

Church, Brookline, was active in advising Mr. Reed.

With this said I have mentioned everything I knew at that time relating to the Episcopal Theological School except that the Trustees had rented for the School two frame houses next to Mt. Auburn Cemetery and that skeptics [5]

averred that the School would soon move into the Cemetery.

Some six years before this the Rev. Dr. John S. Stone, the “old gentleman," had preceded Dr. Francis W. Wharton as Rector of St. Paul's Church, Brookline. Dr. Stone was the pastor of my younger boyhood, hence I knew him as intimately as a boy can know a man some sixty-five years his senior. In his prime he had been rector of large parishes, a leading preacher (to a boy a very dull preacher) of the Evangelical school. He had, however, to the end of his life a youthful, cheery and kindly temper. He was a warm friend of our family, his boys had been my playmates. The young professor, Alexander V. G. Allen, I did not then know. But the Rev. Dr. Wharton who had not yet taken a professorship was at the time of my entering college the Rector of St. Paul's, Brookline. He was the pastor of my later boyhood. He, too, was a friend of our family and with his large round body and falsetto voice was an interesting, lovable and able man. A few years before this he was one of the most brilliant young members of the Philadelphia bar. The death of his wife and his religious temperament drove him into the ministry; after passing a year at the seminary in Kenyon College, Gambier, Ohio, he went directly to the rectorship of St. Paul's, Brookline. His active mind, his evangelical spirit and his legal and political interests made him immediately a force in the Massachusetts Diocesan Convention.

It was he, I am quite sure, who suggested to Mr. Reed [6]

the founding of the School, who framed its Charter and who, with two or three laymen, saw the Charter and the organization of the School through and into action. 

Dr. Wharton soon accepted the chair of Ecclesiastical 

Polity, Homiletics, and Pastoral Care, also Liturgics, for he could lecture in an interesting way upon almost any subject, though his manner of lecturing was often as much to the amusement as to the edification of the students. He continued his interest in international law, of which he was a leading exponent, and wrote many volumes of legal commentaries which had a remarkable sale. Upon retiring from the professorship in 1882 he became counsel of the State Department in Washington under Secretary Bayard. He retained throughout his Washington life his interest in the School and his religious faith and habits. 

These laymen, Benjamin T. Reed, Edward S. Rand, Robert C. Winthrop, John P. Putnam, Amos A. Lawrence, and James S. Amory, with Dr. Wharton, were the Founders of the School. They were all of them friends of the 

Bishop but being somewhat fearful of Episcopal authority did not ask his counsel until the organization was well assured. They were careful to talk but little with the clergy. They were laymen of strength and of a determination to found a school for a vigorous and effective ministry of the Church. They were the Trustees and were beholden neither to the Diocese nor the Bishop, but were bound in loyalty to the Church, her Creeds and Laws. 

They held the power to appoint the professors. The [7]

Trustees have been laymen ever since. It was a bold experiment, unique in Theological Education and has worked well. 

Certain conditions have been essential. The laymen have been men of good judgment and of common sense, with of course loyalty to the Church and the provisions of their Charter, conscientious in giving of their best in time and thought to their responsibilities as Trustees. They have held themselves responsible or have seen to it that others were responsible for the financial and administrative functions of the School. In the selection of the professors and other teachers they have not presumed to be experts in theology but, like good jurymen, have consulted experts and others whose knowledge might be of value, have weighed the evidence of their advisers and without personal or theological prejudice have made their decisions. They have shown courage; for instance when the matter of the site of the School was in question there was, on the part of some timid clergymen and others, a reasonable doubt as to whether the School should be placed in immediate proximity to the College. How about the Unitarian influences? for at that time Harvard College was theologically, though not by its charter, a Unitarian institution. Would such a site prejudice the Church throughout the country against the School, as indeed it did to a large degree for some years? Nevertheless, these lay 

Trustees, with the advice of some strong clergymen, had such confidence in the faith and the historic life of the [8]

Church that they did place it, weak as it was in scholars, numbers and wealth, where it now stands. 

Brattle Street, Cambridge, used to be one of the most beautiful and dignified streets in the whole country. Colonial mansions, great elms and a winding road opened vista after vista. Along the dirt sidewalks (for brick walks were anathema in old Cambridge), James Russell 

Lowell, John Bartlett of "Familiar Quotations," Alexander Graham Bell, Charles F. Choate, several other lawyers well known in their day, Colonel Thomas Wentworth Higginson, Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, John 

Fiske, Governor Russell, William Roscoe Thayer, Horace

E. Scudder, and college professors greeted each other as they walked towards Harvard Square in the mornings, and in the afternoons as they walked back towards Mount 

Auburn. Dr. Morrill Wyman, with his long, curly hair, wise and beloved, drove along in his buggy while John 

Holmes, more witty than his brother Oliver, cracked jokes with everyone. In "Craigie House," General Washington with his wife, Martha, had lived during parts of 1775 and 1776, and here was his headquarters. In 1867 it was the home of Mr. Longfellow. About five minutes walk towards the College stood the elm under which Washington took command of the American army; between these two, Craigie House and the elm, bordering upon Brattle Street, was a small field dignified by a massive walnut-butternut tree which tradition said had sprung from a nut brought by a colonist from England. Indeed, [9]

Professor Charles Sargent once told me that there were only two other such trees known in this country. 

Old Cambridge was then the real Cambridge. There was a grace of living, a neighborliness, a simplicity and a sedateness which we associate with the best of New England.

This small field, half-way between Craigie House and the Elm, eight minutes walk from the College, was selected by the Trustees as the site of the Episcopal Theological School, near enough to the College to feel its atmosphere of scholarship, distant enough to keep its own individuality. The field was bought and the School escaped from Mount Auburn and its cemetery.

In order that there might be no question among the people of Cambridge as to the purpose of the School, the Chapel was built first, and so planned as to command a view up and down Brattle Street on the corner of what is now Mason Street, which leads towards the College. 

St. John's Memorial Chapel was the gift of Robert M. 

Mason, a merchant of Boston, in memory of his wife, his brother, and other members of his family; a stone church with spire, of English Gothic, suggestive of England's rural life. As one enters the Chapel door, he faces a large tablet, cut into which are several names of the Mason family, so many names, in fact, as to arouse the interest of Dean Gray's small son, George. While his father was preaching a rather dull sermon the boy studied the names, spelling out each one; then pressing nearer his mother he [ 10 ]

whispered, "Mother, the Mason family seem to have been very unfortunate, they all died."

The Chapel is the heart of the School. The House of 

Prayer and daily worship prompted Mr. Longfellow to write:

I stand beneath the tree whose branches shade 

Thy western windows, Chapel of St. John, And hear its leaves repeat their benison,

On him whose hands thy stones memorial laid. 

Not only tongues of the Apostle teach 

Lessons of love and light, but these expanding

And sheltering boughs with all their leaves implore, And say in accents clear as human speech: "The peace of God that passeth understanding 

Be and abide with you forevermore.'

There followed the Dormitory, Lawrence Hall, given by my father, who was then the junior partner of Robert 

Mason, of the firm of Mason, Lawrence & Company.

Here again we note the personal sentiment and spiritual motive, for my father's religious faith was founded upon a sense of gratitude for many mercies, and he himself framed the inscriptions in Latin and English. Over the entrance to the Hall, cut into the stone, is the sentence, IN MEMORIAM SUMMAE.

Friendship and admiration of the generous character of the founder prompted the friends of Benjamin T. Reed to build Reed Hall for lectures. 

A cousin of two of the Trustees, John Appleton Burnham, gave the Dining Hall, and in 1911 a Trustee, John Gordon Wright, built the Library. Much later, in 1892, the friends of Robert C. Winthrop, who was for many [12]

years the President of the Board of Trustees, joined in erecting Winthrop Hall. Such traditions and expressions of sentiment enhance the spiritual value of an institution. 

The history of the School may be best sketched through the careers of the five Deans.

Dr. John S. Stone, the first Dean of the School, served from 1867 to 1876, nine years, during which an average of only three students graduated each year. He was a scholarly man, though not a scholar. He was a strong, pious, human pastor who treated the boys as if of the family, and who gave them a high conception of the calling of the ministry, and of their work as preachers and pastors. 

Dr. Wharton, in a somewhat desultory way, lectured upon his various subjects. But, as I have said, whatever subject he took up, he was bound to be stimulating and suggestive.

The real scholars and teachers were two. Young Alexander V. G. Allen became one of the most interesting and illuminating teachers in Cambridge. Through his treatment of Ecclesiastical History, he took up the Theology, the Philosophy, and Organization, the Ritual and the Creeds, of the historic Church and so interwove them with the thought, philosophy, and social life of men as to influence deeply the thought of the Church and its relation to life. I recall that when Archbishop Davidson, upon his visit in 1904, was about to give an address in St. John's 

Chapel Dr. Allen came into the Gray Memorial Room and I introduced him to the Archbishop. After a few [13]

words Dr. Allen modestly turned away and I said to the 

Archbishop, "That is Professor Allen who has written a book on Church History called "The Continuity of Christian Thought," at which the Archbishop exclaimed, “Is that 'Continuity Allen""? and he immediately sought

Allen out, and thanked him for what the book had done for him. Dr. Allen did not drive the students but made the subject in hand so interesting, referring to so much literature, that they found themselves engrossed in their work.

The other teacher was Peter H. Steenstra, Professor of the Literature and Interpretation of the Old Testament. 

He was a Dutchman, born in Dongjum, a Baptist, who had lately entered the ministry of our Church, a truthlover if there ever was one. His courage, his openness of mind, his interpretation of the Old Testament which under German scholarship was changing every day, was almost frightening at times. Juniors from various colleges and simple Sunday School teachings were turned loose in what was to them, and would have been to their pastors, a wild field of heresy. But in a year the Middlers had gained their bearings, and when they graduated they knew what young people were thinking and could meet them on their own ground.

Steenstra grew every month of his years of service. One afternoon, as we were passing from a Matriculation service to the dinner, I saw Professor Steenstra breaking away from the company and going home. I called to him to [14]

come back to the dinner. He refused, saying, "I must go and write my lecture for tomorrow." "Why don't you give your last year's lecture?" I asked. "Because I don't believe it." So he went home and the next morning his class had the latest and the best of Biblical Criticism. 

During the first twenty years the financial stress was very great. In spite of meager salaries, (Dr. Allen's salary was $1200) the strictest economies, and the income from the Reed Foundation, there were continual deficits and the Trustees were again and again almost forced to dismiss the students and close the doors. Year after year, however, Mr. James S. Amory (grandfather of Robert

Amory) took his hat in hand and with loyalty and courage in his heart went in and out of the offices of his Boston friends collecting some $10,000 or more each year, while my father, who was Treasurer, advanced from his own pocket the monthly payment of salaries in expectation of Mr. Amory's successful solicitations. I became familiar with my father's remark in the evening after a Trustee meeting, "I do not see how the School is going to hold out any longer." The Chapel, Reed Hall, and Lawrence Hall already built required upkeep; there were no tuition fees and some student aid was necessary. 

Doctor Stone, the Dean, was of generous disposition but was not a good administrator. When Mr. Reed died and his will was published it was found, as was to be expected, that while he had left a legacy to the School he had bequeathed to his widow a life interest in the income of [15]

most of his estate which upon her death would go to the School. Mrs. Reed was a second wife, some thirty or forty years younger than her husband, bright and attractive. Dean Stone soon made a call of condolence upon the young and bereaved widow. As he entered the drawingroom she greeted him warmly with the words, “Oh, Doctor, don't you wish that I were dead, too?" At which the handsome old gentleman straightened himself and exclaimed, "Oh, Queen, live forever!" Indeed, there were times when the Trustees thought that she might. However, with mutual loyalty and skilful teaching the solicitation for help went faithfully on and when Mrs. Reed died she left the School a legacy from her own estate. 

After his retirement at the age of eighty-one, Dr. Stone continued to live in his house at the corner of Mason Street and Phillips Place. In the first ten years there were thirty graduates, an average of three a year. The School had been well established, it was like a comfortable quiet family, excellent teaching had been sustained. Every morning and afternoon teachers and students met in the Chapel for prayers and occasional addresses. The modest 

School was pleasantly patronized or quietly ignored by the intellectual aristocracy of Harvard. On Sunday mornings the Chapel held a congregation of Cambridge people including one or two Harvard professors and a transept full of Harvard students. The Dean usually preached one of his old parish sermons.

One man, hitherto unmentioned, placed the School on [16]

the map of Cambridge. In 1869 Phillips Brooks came from Philadelphia to his native city of Boston as Rector of Trinity Church. As a boy he had sat under the preaching of Doctor Stone when he was Rector of St. Paul's 

Church, Boston. How natural it was, therefore, that Dean 

Stone should ask him to preach at St. John's Chapel. At that time I was a Junior at Harvard and an officer of the St. Paul's Society of students. It was arranged that Mr. 

Brooks should be asked to preach on one Sunday evening a month to the Harvard students and the people of Cambridge. A fellow student and I went into town and gave 

Mr. Brooks the invitation; he was at the time only thirtyfive years old, unknown to the rank and file of students and Cambridge people, but after his first sermon he became known. The Chapel was overcrowded and I collected money enough to buy fifty camp chairs to place in the aisles. From 1870 to 1875 he preached in the Chapel one Sunday evening each month, usually repeating the sermon that he had given at Trinity in the morning. 

After the retirement of Dean Stone Phillips Brooks was called into service by Harvard. 

In 1877 the Trustees were faced with the problem of the selection of the second Dean. George Zabriskie Gray, thirty-eight years old, an active rector of a parish just outside New York City, of Dutch Reform stock, of business inheritance, and a student in Theology and kindred subjects, was elected and entered upon his duties. 

To the Cambridge people he was evidently a New [17]

Yorker and he never really understood the provincial temper of Cambridge and Harvard at that time. He built and owned the Deanery, which after his death was given his widow to the School in her husband's memory. 

The Dean and Mrs. Gray soon made their home a center of warm and generous hospitality, winning the affection of those who took the trouble to know them. To the students the Deanery was a second home and the Dean's impulsive autocratic manner backed by a tender heart made up an interesting personality. Theology, Homiletics, and Pastoral Care were the subjects of his teaching, his chief interest being Theology, though he used to complain to me that Dr. Allen in his Church History lectures overlapped into Theology in a way that embarrassed him. 

He was a wide reader, he had soaked himself in the writings of the German liberals and at the same time held his inherited evangelical temper. He was not a scholar but a reader and he threw his somewhat undigested opinions and conclusions at the students, sometimes in such a way as to confuse or stun them, and when they asked Dr. Allen what the Dean meant he was unable to tell them.

Besides gaining the affections of the students and organizing the School Dean Gray performed one service which of itself places him high in the history of the School. 

During the preceding twenty-five or thirty years since Darwin and others had opened up the secrets of Nature, and the theory of Evolution had become a commonplace, universities and professional schools had responded by so [18]

altering their systems of teaching that students were encouraged to think for themselves, to weigh facts and by reasoning and freedom to gain independence of judgment and personal convictions. The search for truth had become the touchstone of reality in education. Meanwhile, the schools of theology were pursuing the old method of textbook instruction, of conforming to authority and accepting implicitly what the professors taught their pupils. 

In his reports of 1886 and 1887 Dean Gray broke away from these traditions and in the name of the School challenged the old system in a most eloquent and convincing way. "This, then," he wrote, " is what the School stands for, candid, advanced, unpartisan, manly preparation for the ministry of this historic Church. Nothing else will long be tolerated by the present growing spirit of the Church, no other method will be submitted to by these young men who have tasted the mental culture and activity of our own region-none other is feasible in the presence of a great university, where men have learned to think for themselves, to distinguish between divine revelation and human opinion and to appreciate the rich birthright of freedom that is theirs who belong to our historic Communion." 

We of today have no conception of the revolutionary note here struck; it was a challenge to every theological school and to the prevailing temper of the Church. It set this School to the fore, at the same time, however, it created throughout a large part of the Church a distrust [19]

of our loyalty to the Faith and led many bishops and conservative people to blacklist the School and its teachings. 

In this leadership Dean Gray did his great work. He retired on account of his health and went back to New 

York to die in 1889.

On January 1, 1884, I entered upon my duties as a professor in the School and assistant to the Dean in certain features of his work. The teaching of Homiletics and Pastoral Care was my school duty besides alternating with the Dean in the Sunday morning sermon in the Chapel. 

I had also the pastoral care of some two hundred Harvard students who had given their names to the Dean of the University as worshippers in St. John's Chapel. I also had a student Bible Class every Sunday in the College yard and the responsibility of a Sunday School of the children of the Chapel congregation, and undefined duties in assisting the Dean in the administration of the School. Upon the death of Mr. Amory I was given the duty of soliciting money to meet deficits. With great timidity I began work, for no trace of scholarship was ever found upon me. Until my senior year at Harvard I had never really studied, at school and college I was always in the last half of the class. 

I matured late and here I was at thirty-three years of age a teacher of men only a few years younger than myself. Of

Pastoral Care I could speak from experience, for during eight years I had worked with and practically lived with the working people of a large mill city. Of Homiletics I had had in preaching in my Lawrence parish for eight [20]

years the experience of very limited success and frequent failure. The spiritual care of Harvard students brought happy friendships and often meager results. And when in three years I was made Vice Dean, and upon Dean Gray's retirement, at the end of four years in the School, I was made Dean, I wondered then and have never ceased to wonder why. Sometimes I have thought that because I was not a scholar nor concentrated upon one subject, they assumed that I would have a free and unbiassed oversight of all subjects and their limitations. 

This thought arises from the fact that within two years after I became Dean I discovered much wastage in the imperfect correlation of the subjects, causing listlessness among many of the students who were compelled to study subjects which did not interest them and for which they had no use. Hence I persuaded the Faculty to make such adjustments, and to offer to the students such election of subjects as would kindle their interest and because of their own choice challenge their concentration. This was, I think, the first break in a traditional system of preparation for the ministry which later was followed in other schools and after a few years appeared in the Canons of the Church. Again I have the impression that standing between the theological teachers and the practical work of the ministry I connected the two in the minds of the candidates and while spiritualizing the practical duties I made more real the practical and human relations of Theology. 

By my own example in taking lessons in voice culture [21]

and reading the service I induced some of the professors to do the same and thus aroused the students to their duty in reading and preaching acceptably the Word of God. 

The scholastic standards were raised for admission, indeed they were at that time higher than those of Harvard's professional schools. There came, too, during my service a rich increase in scholastic and spiritual power through the entrance into the Faculty of younger men: Henry Nash, Max Kellner, and Edward Drown. 

For twenty-seven happy years my home with my dear wife and seven children stood between "Craigie House " and "Lawrence Hall"; in front was a beautiful lawn, in the rear, until Winthrop Hall was built, was the tennis court where Professor Drown and I used to challenge and beat any two selected students. As I sat at my desk I could look across Brattle Street, Longfellow Park, the Charles River, with its salty meadows and haystacks, to the Brookline hills. Down Brattle Street, before breakfast, President and Mrs. Eliot on their bicycles passed, to be followed an hour later by Dean Briggs on the "double quick' to the College Chapel. Now and again tug-boats and schooners passed up or down the river, the masts moving across the meadows as in Holland or the Fens of Lincolnshire. 99 While the challenge of Dean Gray and the spirit of the School reached a somewhat limited circle of theological schools and teachers, there was during the years of my residence one influence which reached throughout the [22]

Church and nation: the preaching and personality of Phillips Brooks. Whatever might be said in criticism of his so-called heresies, his supposed sympathy with Unitarianism, and his emphasis upon loyalty to the truth, there was a general admiration of the man, of his eloquence and his Christlike human spirit. He took pains to show his interest in the School and loyalty to its teachings; young men from all over the country turned to him for advice as to their calling and caught from him an enthusiasm for the School. In his only Diocesan Convention address as 

Bishop he launched out in an eloquent description of the 

School at Cambridge. 

With all this said, however, the support of the School and the attraction to it of adequate students was a difficult task; as compared to other schools the standards of scholarship were high, so was the cost of living in Cambridge. 

Scholarships and financial aid were very meager. A heavier handicap than any or all of these things was the suspicion throughout the Church that the teaching was tainted with heresy and that candidates for the ministry would in the freedom of their thought be liable to turn out disloyal to the Church. Of all the Bishops there was only one, Bishop Clark, of Rhode Island, who wished his candidates to come to Cambridge; even Bishop Paddock of this Diocese was saddened if any candidate of his entered the School; many Bishops simply forbade their candidates coming. While this attitude shut some young men out of the School it whetted the spirit of adventure in others and [23]

young men from a distance brought strong pressure on their Bishops to transfer them to this Diocese and to give an unwilling consent. Called to be Bishop, after somewhat over eight years in the service of the School, I left with great regret, with deep humility at the inadequacy of my work.

In the selection of the fourth Dean the Trustees turned to the Rev. George Hodges, Rector of Calvary Church, Pittsburgh. Dr. Hodges was born in Rome, N. Y., and at thirty-seven years of age entered upon his duties early in 1894.

Pittsburgh was a great industrial city; Calvary Church was a great parish. The reputation of the Rector as a leader in Social Service had gone far and wide. Kingsley 

House, so named by Dr. Hodges, was the latest and best type of Civic Social Service springing from a religious motive. Hence the fact of his election as Dean of a theological school signified that the Trustees and Faculty were determined to bring Theology and the ministry of the Church into closer relation with the practical life of the day and incorporate into the preparation for the ministry a more sympathetic and intelligent leadership. While the new Dean laid emphasis upon this phase of education he had by nature and habit a remarkable talent in the vigorous and interesting expression of his aims. With exceptional felicity he wrote and preached upon these and kindred subjects. The School library lists thirty-one books written while he was Dean. By addresses and lectures [24]

throughout the country he interested the people and prompted them to see here a theological school which, educating its students along these lines, was bound to lead their parishes into social and public service. This change of emphasis drew the attention of the School's critics away from the dread of doctrinal heresies. The thorough teaching which the professors had given the students in the past years had sent out through the Church and Mission Field young men who were winning commendation for their character and ability. So that the very Bishops who used to be critical, now seeing the results of the School, wrote back to Cambridge in commendation and asked for more graduates. In his personal relations with the students, as indeed with everyone, Dean Hodges, by his simplicity, his humility, and affectionate spirit won all hearts. Under his instruction the preaching of the young graduates became more direct and practical, and their parishes entered more sympathetically into the every-day life of the people. 

With an increase in the number of teachers, the Dean became more closely incorporated with the Faculty, and the School as a whole took on a more organic form, while the spiritual life of the students was cultivated by Chapel Prayers and other services, quiet days and personal counsel.

In 1913, President Lowell suggested through Dean Hodges and Professor Washburn a closer affiliation between the School and the University, whereby the students of the one should enjoy the privileges of the other [25]

without payment beyond that of the tuition fee of each institution. This plan was confirmed by the authorities of both, and marked the beginning of the reciprocal arrangement which has now been in effective operation for twenty-seven years.

By the death of George Hodges in 1919 the School lost an efficient Dean and a friend beloved of all who had been associated with him.

For the fifth Dean, the Trustees now turned to the Faculty and elected Henry B. Washburn, of the Class of 

1894, fifty years old, a scholar, and Professor of Ecclesiastical History since 1908. He had an intimate knowledge of the School, and enjoyed the confidence of all who knew him.

As the growth of the Faculty had made a larger income necessary, the Trustees and the Dean asked me to lead a campaign for an addition to the endowment. Friends and graduates responded to the amount of one million dollars which has given the School a back log of confidence as well as of income.

By tradition and the terms of the Charter, the Dean and Faculty have always worked in close cooperation. 

During these twenty years of Dean Washburn's deanship they have seen the School rise in numbers from twenty, the year after the war, to sixty-six, six years ago, and sixtyone today; the members of the Faculty increase from five to eight, and the part-time instructors from five to nine. 

They have introduced a system of tutorials and general [26]

examinations which shifts the attention from marks in individual examinations to the student's mastery of permanent principles. They have reduced the number of lectures practically one-third, laying increasing emphasis on conferences for small groups and individual work. 

They have (the first school to do it) introduced the clinical and case system into the teaching of Pastoral Care. They have, in Homiletics, brought the students under the instruction of five or six men (some of other Communions) instead of under one. They have greatly enlarged the elective system, so that now practically one-third of the work is elective. They have taken increasing advantage of the Harvard affiliation, and have had active part in teaching certain Harvard courses. The School has given its professors a full year of absence after six years of teaching, or a half-year after three that it be used profitably. years, on full pay, expecting

As Dean Washburn retires at the end of this term under the conditions of an age limitation of service, which he himself, with the approval of the Faculty, proposed to the Trustees, we all who believe in the School may well feel gratified that from the day of its foundation until its next Commencement it has steadily moved up in standards of scholastic work, in character, and in full loyalty to the Faith and the Master. This School has served 

Christ's Church in sending throughout the world able and devoted ministers of His Word. [27]

T TODAY HE real test of the work of an educational institution is in the quality, character and standing of its graduates; an imperfect test at best, but a real one.

The School has now 555 living Alumni. Leadership in the Church cannot be finally determined by office; however, the men who are selected for leadership in each Diocese by laity and clergy are the Bishops. Of these 555 

Alumni there are now twenty-seven living Bishops, and we must realize that only one-third of the Alumni are yet fifty-five years old. In other words, of every twenty graduates, one is a Bishop. As to the students, there are now sixty-one, all but three Bachelor of Arts or Science, the third largest number in the history of the School; and the 

Junior class is the largest that ever entered.

As to its physical plant, that is reasonably good; and we must remember that the students have all the privileges of Harvard's athletic plant and its medical care, its libraries, and other resources.

In these facts we have notes of satisfaction and opportunity.

As to its finances. Its income last year was $65,000; its expenses $81,000; its deficit $16,000.

The fundamental purpose of a theological school is of [28]

course the training of men for the ministry by teachers and other leaders. The chief item of expense is the salaries. 

These must be sufficient and the number of teachers adequate to enable them to do their work well. While the salaries are sufficient for careful and economical living, they are not by any means equal to the salaries paid by Harvard; and our teachers living in the same community have about the same conditions of living expense. 

Here, however, we come to the nub of the problem. 

The School should have at its lowest quota for its several subjects eight resident teachers. Upon the retirement of Dean Washburn in June there will be only six full-time teachers. For several years there has not been a professor in Homiletics and Pastoral Care, nor teachers in several other essential subjects, the work in these subjects being taken by other professors or neighboring rectors. It is an unsatisfactory, if not a perilous condition, for a theological school which, if it exists at all, must hold high its standards of work and scholarship. 

Placing the number and quality of the present student body beside these facts about the professors, the condition of the School is a challenge to its friends, and indeed to the whole Church. The situation is serious and demands our best endeavors.

As I write this, the Rev. Dr. Angus Dun has been elected the sixth Dean.

Dr. Dun is a graduate of Yale University and of this 

School in 1917. He has studied in Germany, England [29]

and Scotland, has served as a pastor, and for twenty years has taught in our School. He is in the prime of life. As a scholar and administrator he has gained the confidence and affection of all. Of deep spiritual character and loyalty to the Church, he will lead the School on to higher planes of thought and devotion. [ 30 ]

THE FUTURE 

HE whole world is living in an era of bewilderment. 

Seventy-three years ago the great and ancient churches of Russia and Rome were the bulwarks of Christianity, withstanding the pagan and barbaric hordes of the East; for centuries they had the monopoly of Christian faith, teaching and worship of their peoples. Today the children and children's children of these churches have risen up against them, and are teaching their children to hate the name of Christ. Millions of boys and girls are being trained, and well trained, to despise the Christ and follow pagan ideals. Whatever one may say of the causes of this reaction, racial, national, or social, whether ambition, worldliness, bigotry or lust for power by ecclesiastics or the rich, the fact is that these churches have failed in holding the loyalty of their people to Christ. "But," we say in a confident and smug voice, "such things cannot happen here. We are a democracy, with liberty of person and of speech, we are self-governed." The phrase "Eternal vigilance is the price of liberty," is still true, and it is that vigilance which the Church above all other educational institutions, because it is training young men in faith and character, should encourage. We Americans [ 31 ]

may not be liable to brutal outbreaks and mob rule, but my question is as to whether we are as well safeguarded as we think. The propagandists of paganism and of unsound government are all about us. Have we the intelligence and will to withstand them?

I believe that what society needs is a deepening of the sense of responsibility in personal life, a clearer intelligence as to morals and religion, and a stability of character which will stem the rush of attractive paganism or brutal mass force when and if it comes. Since we have become aware of the closing of our frontier, the disappearance of our pioneers, the realization that America has come of age, have we not, all of us, become a bit timid in our outlook? The slogan of half a century ago was "Go West." 

Abraham Lincoln sets the note. Now while we admire

Lincoln as a symbol of courageous America, our slogans are liable to be, "Safety first," "Thrift is hopeless; borrow and take your chances." But what has this to do with the 

Episcopal Theological School? 

I believe that this country needs as never before men who are dedicated and intelligently consecrated to God and the service of their fellow men; who have such faith in a living God that they unconsciously connect religious faith with the every-day tests of life, and who are yet human enough to be in the closest and most sympathetic relations with all sorts of people. And I believe that this 

School is sending out such men, and should send out more. 

We need more of the heroic in life, and in many of our [32]

graduates we have it, often unheard of and unsung, in little country parishes, in great cities, in foreign lands. Let me sketch for you one example.

At the 1914 Commencement of our School eleven Sen iors came to the chancel rail for their Bachelor's degree.

At the request of the President of the Trustees I presented the diplomas to each one, among them William Roberts.

He was soon ordained and took his diploma with him to China, where after learning the Chinese language he became a teacher in St. John's University, and later had charge of the missionary work at Nanking. He was born in Virginia and married a young woman from Massachusetts, daughter of an Alumnus of this School, a real American couple. Over two years ago, having been appointed a Bishop, he stood ready for consecration on St. Andrew's 

Day, 1937. The ruthless attack of Japan was near its height and made the consecration in his own church impossible, so it was held in the English Cathedral in Shanghai. Almost a thousand people gathered, Chinese, Americans, and English; but his own clergy and most of his friends were unable to get there. With great difficulty three Bishops were gotten together: his aged predecessor 

Bishop Graves, who had served forty-four years, an English Bishop and a Chinese Bishop; the service was in the 

Chinese language. As the three Bishops laid their hands upon his head the drone of airplanes sounded overhead; the hymn "Jesus calls us o'er the tumult" was sung; the recessional hymn "Jesus shall reign where e'er the sun 99 [33]

was finished and the Bishop standing at the Cathedral door looking over the people realized that of his diocese and churches two-thirds had been destroyed, and one-third was in imminent danger. During these two years he has been going in and out, traveling with great difficulty, cheering the people, comforting them, helping them to rebuild homes and churches. To them there has been no sensational courage, no romantic chivalry, simply the work and reward of a missionary. To us it is all a vivid illustration of Christian heroism. The motto of the School is lived out in China, “ Veritas et Vita." 66 

If old men do not dream dreams, young men will not see visions. 

May I set before you my dream of the School five, twenty-five, seventy-five years hence. 

1. A School as it now is, but with steadily rising standards of thought and consecration, with ever increasing numbers of devoted teachers, and with graduates moving out into the parishes and missions. 

2. There are infinite riches in our search for fresh revelations of a living God. Am I right in saying that while the literature of science and philosophy has increased greatly in this last half century, the scholarly literature of religion and of the interpretation of the Christian Faith has shrunk? This School should have scholars of such erudition, imagination and spiritual vision as will, while building up the faith of future leaders, send literature to [34]

interpret for the whole people faith in a living God, and the Glory of Him who is the Truth. 

3. Our Church is an organization; it is given practical things to do. As economic, social and religious conditions change, a Theological School should be a leader in helping the officers and people of the Church in meeting them. 

4. Every graduate, whatever his office in the Church may be, will have a heart sympathetic with the people; his love will go out to them all, and especially to those who are burdened and heavy laden. He will be a good shepherd who knows his sheep. Whether his work is known or unknown, sung or unsung, he will strive to bring to all faith, charity and peace.

A harassed world beckons us on to supreme faith and devotion in the name of our Master, Jesus Christ. [35]
