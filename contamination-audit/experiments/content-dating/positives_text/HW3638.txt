Preface THE WOMEN'S LEAGUE of the United Synagogue is happy to present this, their "Jewish Home Beautiful," to the Jewish women of America. For many years it has been our hope to publish a book of this kind. 

The dramatic version of The Jewish Home Beautiful was originally presented by Mrs. Althea O. Silverman of Hartford in 1932, with Table Exhibits that appeared in the Women's 

League Outlook in 1933.

The narrative version of The Jewish Home Beautiful prepared by Mrs. Betty D. Greenberg was presented at the National Convention of the Women's League at the Ritz-Carlton 

Hotel in Atlantic City in May 1940. Most of the photographs of the tables in this book were taken at that time. 

The dramatic version by Mrs. Althea O. Silverman was presented at the Joint Sisterhood Assembly in the Temple of Religion at the World's Fair in September 1940. 

We are happy to have the privilege of publishing both these versions. 

We also wish to express our thanks to Mr. Seymour Kaplan for his beautiful illustrations, and to Mrs. Alexander Marx for her very careful and conscientious reading of the proof.

Foreword to Third Edition 

The publishers and authors view the third printing of our Jewish Home Beautiful with pardonable pride. 

The original purpose of this publication was to make available to our sisterhoods two beautiful pageants which we had found useful for organization work. 

We are happy to find that thousands of Jewish women throughout the United States and Canada, and as far away as England, Australia and South Africa have wanted this portrayal of the symbolism and beauty of our festivals for themselves. 

What is even more gratifying, hundreds of men and women in the armed services have found this book an answer to their desire for contact with the Jewish way of life. 

National Women's League of the United Synagogue is humbly grateful that it has been granted the privilege to be of service to those who are giving so much for the very ideals which our holidays symbolize.

Introduction 

Living as a Jewess is more than a matter of faith, knowledge or observance. To live as a Jewess, a woman must have something of the artist in her. She must have an appreciation for things beautiful and a desire to create those beautiful things herself. The widely-held opinion that our ancestors were not artistically inclined is now being rapidly revised. Examples of impressive Jewish art are being discovered in increasing numbers. But the finest artistic achievements of our people were not intended for museums; they were created to beautify the Jewish home and ennoble every Jewish life. 

The beauty of the Jewish home resembles the ever-changing beauties of nature. God did not paint one sunset and hang it permanently in the sky. He paints a new sunset daily. Thus too do the beauties of the Jewish home pass through an endless cycle of repetition but not of exact duplication. Our mothers evolved new and beautiful settings and culinary masterpieces for the various days and seasons of the Jewish year. And just as today does not automatically inherit the brilliant array of yesterday's sunset, so the Jewish home of today does not and cannot automatically inherit the beauties of the Jewish home of yesterday. The Jewish woman today, guided by the memories and traditions of yesterday, must herself create new glory and new beauty for the Jewish home of today. 

The pageant of the Jewish Home Beautiful is not presented as a museum piece, as something to admire and then to forget, or merely to recall in conversation. It's purpose is rather to urge every mother in Israel to assume her role as artist, and on every 13

festival, Sabbath and holiday, to make her home and her family table a thing of beauty as precious and as elevating as anything painted on canvas or chiseled in stone. 

These beautiful settings of tables covered with the finest in linen, silver and food, reflect the painstaking love with which all of us should approach our task. Nothing less than the best of our effort and of our possessions should satisfy us. A little skill and love and understanding can transform the humblest surroundings into a sanctuary more holy and beautiful than the house decorated elaborately, but without love and intelligence and religious warmth.

Jewish mothers of today have not lost their desire to introduce beautiful pageantry into their homes. But they have turned to strange sources for their inspiration. The attractive settings offered by our large department stores and women's magazines for Valentine's Day, Hallowe'en, Christmas and other non Jewish festive days have won the hearts of many of our women who either through lack of knowledge or of imagination have failed to explore the possibilities of our own traditions. In the following pages we have offered a few suggestions which we hope will inspire Jewish women to a deeper search of their own treasure house. For it is well for every mother to remember that the ethical and historical lessons associated with the holidays, have been as deeply impressed upon many a youthful mind by memories of unique foods and symbolic ceremonies round the family table, as by books and teachers. 

It lies within the power of every Jewish woman, with the expenditure of just a little effort, to transform whatever habi tation she may occupy into a JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL.

Narrative Version Betty Davis Greenberg

I. Reader t was the Englishman's boast that his home was his castle. 

The outlawed, persecuted Jew could not make his home his castle; but he did more than that. He made his home his sanctuary. Because God had a great share in his little house, the 

Jew succeeded in transforming it into a great home. Because 

God was to be worshiped "b'hadrat kodesh," in the beauty of holiness, the Jewish home was to be holy and beautiful. "This is my God and I will beautify Him," Moses and the children of Israel sang with their first breath of freedom. And later generations marveled at the seeming irreverence of the sentiment. How can God be beautified? Our Rabbis gave the answer. Beautify Him, they taught, through the performance of Mitzvot, with the lighting of candles, with a Lulav and an Ethrog, and with a fine Sukkah. Glorify Him, in short, with a beautiful Jewish home. Hence the Jewish woman gladly took upon herself the performance of duties, humble in themselves, but exalted to the high status of Mitzvot because they were dedicated to the glory of God. Thus she succeeded in elevating her household tasks to the dignity of sacred rites upon a holy altar. Together with the Jewish father, she created that sanctuary which has been our pride through all the ages, the Jewish Home. The faith, love and understanding which permeated family relationships, also created foods, symbols and ceremonies, all of which gave to the Jewish home its distinctive. 

Jewish character. "The wisdom of woman buildeth the home."

Whether that home be a crude hut or a rich mansion, it is within the power of the Jewish woman to beautify it with the beauty of holiness.

As the beauties of nature vary with the cycle of the seasons, so too do the changes in the calendar, together with the historic 17

experiences of our people, form the background for the rich pageantry of the Jewish home. 

In the autumn, when decay temporarily overtakes the physical world, we open our year with the great Holy Days, testifying to our faith in the indestructibility of the spirit, and in the God-given power to renew our spiritual lives. These aweinspiring days head the procession of Sabbaths, Festivals and feast days annually recalling events which forged Jewish destiny, and molded Jewish thought and Jewish life. 

These outstanding days of the Jewish year will now pass in rapid procession before us. As we gaze upon the beauty of this scene, and listen to well-known and beloved melodies, may our minds dwell upon the deeper and more permanent significance of that which is here enacted. We shall then better understand what enabled Israel to weather the pitiless blasts of the storms of the past. We shall then realize that no matter what the circumstances may be, we can make Judaism a thing of joy and beauty for ourselves and for our children. 18

OSH HASHANAH 

Reader: Daily, during the month of

Elul, the shrill notes of the Shofar call the Jew to prepare himself for the solemn days ahead. The ten days of repentance begin with Rosh Hashanah on the first and second days of Tishri. Through impartial self-criticism and sincere repentance we hope to make ourselves worthy of the mercy of our Creator. 

Rosh Hashanah is primarily the Yom Teruah, the day of the sounding of the Shofar. Its blasts remind us that this is the 

Yom Hazikaron, the day of memorial, and the Yom Ha Din, the day of judgment. On this day "everything is remembered from the beginning" and justice, tempered with mercy, is meted out to all.

In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, the mother provides for the 

Kiddush special round Ḥallot which are symbolic of life without end. They symbolize her prayer for a year of uninterrupted health and happiness for her dear ones. The first night the meal begins with an apple dipped in honey, and a special prayer is recited appealing for a sweet and pleasant year. For the second night mother has sought out a fruit which the family had not eaten since the fruit was last in season. It is dipped in honey, and the Sheheḥeyanu blessing is said over it. At no time during the holiday are any bitter or sour foods included in the menu. 

Special sweets and dainties are reserved for these days. The beautiful custom of sending greetings and good wishes to friends and relatives is not neglected, for the Jewish greeting is more than a greeting-it is a blessing. "L'shanah tova tikatev"-May you be inscribed for a happy year! (Choir or cantor sings-"BeRosh Hashanah Yikatevun" from the Rosh Hashanah ritual, or "Avinu Malkenu") 21

UKKOT 

Reader: As soon as Yom Kippur is over, the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL 

bestirs itself for the celebration of the festival of Sukkot which comes on the fifteenth day of Tishri. A Sukkah is a hut or a booth, and Sukkot is the Feast of Huts or Tabernacles, commemorating God's watchful care over the Israelites as they dwelt in frail huts during their journey through the wilderness. In the days of the Temple, Sukkot was the great festival of thanksgiving. Having completed the harvesting of the crop, the grateful people made a pilgrimage to the Temple to thank 

God for His bounties.

In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, young and old are busily and gaily engaged in building a Sukkah, and in transforming the bare hut into a bower of sweet-smelling leaves and flowers and hanging fruits. During the first seven days of the holiday, the family take all their meals in the Sukkah. The sun shines through the leafy branches overhead by day, and at night the friendly stars twinkle approval as the happy family sit around the table and lustily and joyfully sing their praises to God for 

His manifold blessings.

On Sukkot also, every morning, with the exception of the Sabbath, and before partaking of any food, the blessing over the Lulav and the Ethrog is said. Four kinds of vegetation are used in this ceremony: the citron, the myrtle, the palm and the willow. These are symbolic of the entire realm of vegetation which has been blessed by God and for which we are giving Him thanks. The Lulav and the Ethrog are waved in the direction of the four points of the compass, then upward and downward, thereby acknowledging the sovereignty of God over the whole universe. 22

The week of festivity and rejoicing reaches its climax on the last day of the festival. On this day, Simhat Torah, the annual reading of the Five Books of Moses is completed and at once again begun. This is truly an occasion for much joy and celebration in the synagogue. The children, carrying Jewish flags and led by the men bearing the Sifre Torah, join in the processions around the synagogue. The house of God resounds with the songs of praise and rejoicing. This spirit of joy and cheer characterizes all our festivals. Indeed, one of the great sources of strength for the Jew has undoubtedly been the fact that to be cheerful and happy was considered a religious duty of prime importance. (Choir sings "Sisu Vesimḥu Besimḥat Torah") 23

ANUKKAH 

Reader: Hanukkah, coming on the twenty-fifth day of Kislev, gives us the finest opportunity for bringing the joy of Judaism into our homes. For eight days the JEWISH HOME BEAUTI- 

FUL is bright with candle lights and gay with parties and the exchange of gifts. The characteristic ceremony of Hanukkah is the kindling of the lights in the Hanukkah Menorah and the singing of the blessings and appropriate songs. (singing of the blessings by Cantor.)

In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, the children are presented with Hanukkah Gelt. Every night, after the lighting of the candles, there is the added fun of searching for the little surprise gift which has been hidden, perhaps by the prophet Elijah, somewhere about the house. The special dish for the 

Hanukkah party is the Lotke, and most of the games played are based on the Dreidel. The letters on its four sides explain the significance of the Feast of Lights-Nun, Gimel, Hey, Shin -Ness Gadol Hayah Shom-"A great miracle took place in those days."

In celebrating Hanukkah we commemorate not so much the military victory of the Maccabees over the Syrian hosts, as the cleansing and rededication of the Temple after it had been defiled by the heathens. The miracle of the little cruse of oil lasting for eight days is the miracle of the little Jewish nation which outlived all its powerful enemies. (Cantor sings "Die Hanukkah Liḥtelaḥ") 24

P URIM 

Reader: Of all the holidays, Purim, which is celebrated on the 14th day of Adar, is the closest to the feminine heart. The central figure of the Purim story is a woman, Queen Esther. Both the Cinderella-like manner in which she rose to fame and power, and the courageous manner in which she saved her people, satisfy the dramatic urge in every woman's soul. The Purim tradition of sending Sheloah Manot, gifts of food to friends and relatives and to the poor, is essentially womanly. Nor should one minimize a woman's delight in exhibiting her culinary abilities at the large family dinner, or Seudah, held on Purim night. In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, masquerade, hilarity and merry making are the order of the day. For the Seudah, besides all the customary delicacies of a banquet, mother has prepared Kreplach and Homontaschen-Haman's pockets-small tricornered cakes filled with poppy seeds or prune jelly.

The story of Purim is told in the Book of Esther, the Megillah or Scroll, which is read on the eve of the holiday. The children are permitted to bring gragors or noise makers into the synagogue that night, and to use them whenever the name of the wicked Haman occurs in the reading. The word Purim means lots, and it is so called, because that was the manner in which Haman chose the day when all the Jewish people were to be destroyed. But his evil intentions were frustrated as were the intentions of all our enemies throughout the ages. 

Purim is the jubilant expression of our solemn faith in the downfall of all tyrants, past, present and future. "In every generation have there arisen those who would destroy us, but the 

Holy One, blessed be He, hath delivered us from their hands." (Choir or cantor sings "Haynt is Purim Brieder") 27

P ESAḤ 

Reader: Pesaḥ is the Festival of

Spring. The historic occasion which it commemorates parallels in the realm of the spirit the marvelous transformations which spring brings into the realm of nature. For Pesaḥ tells of the rebirth of a nation, the redemption from slavery to freedom, the restoration to the disinherited among men of their God-given rights to life and liberty. It is the Independence Day of the Jewish people. The importance that the Jew attaches to liberty can be judged by the honor and the dignity which he has given to this Festival. For weeks ahead, he prepares himself and his home for its coming, and when it arrives, he celebrates it with a long and beautiful religious service around the family dinner table. 

Pesaḥ is so called because of the Biblical account regarding the angel of Death who passed over the homes of the Israelites when the first born of Egypt were slain. It begins on the evening of the 14th day of Nisan, and lasts for eight days. The essential of all Pesaḥ preparations is the removal of leaven of every type from the home. By leaven or Hometz is meant any substance that causes fermentation. Hometz includes, among other things, every kind of cereal, baking powder and yeast.

The JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL is thoroughly cleansed of all 

Hometz. All the exciting preparations for the Sedorim to take place on the first and second nights, have been completed. The three basic symbols, the roasted lamb bone representing the Pascal sacrifice, the Matzot and the Moror are on the table, as are also the Haroset, the celery, the egg, the salt water, and the cup of Elijah. At each setting are a cup of wine and an Haggadah containing the special Pesaḥ ritual and recounting in appropriate prayer and song, the inspiring story of the redemp- 28

HABUOT 

Reader: The sixth day of Sivan occurring at the end of May or the beginning of June, ushers in the Festival of Shabuot in the season of fragrant blossoms and budding greens. In Biblical days the agricultural aspects of this Festival were expressed by the elaborate ceremony of offering the first fruits of the harvest before the altar in the Temple. In modern Palestine, this significance. of the holiday is again prominent. Haifa is the goal of the pilgrimage, and all the colonies of the Emek bring the first fruits of their harvest as a gift to the Jewish National Fund. Associated also with this holiday is the lovely pastoral idyl of Ruth, Naomi and Boaz.

Traditionally Shabuot commemorates the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai. That is the dominating theme in the celebration of the holiday today. It is thus the most appropriate time for the ceremony of confirmation which has been introduced in many synagogues throughout the land.

The JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, as well as the synagogue, is fragrant with blossoms and flowers. Mother faithfully observes the custom of serving milk foods on the evenings of this Festival, for it is both sensible and beautiful; sensible because of the warm season of the year, and beautiful because of the association with the Torah. The whole of the first night of Shabuot was devoted by many to the study of the Torah, and these zealous students would be served coffee, milk and cake to help them keep awake at their studies. Like Sukkot and Simhat 

Torah, Shabuot illustrates the balance which Judaism always seeks to maintain between heaven and earth, between the blessings of the soil and the aspirations of the spirit. (Cantor sings "Salenu"-a Palestinian harvest song.) 32

HABBAT 

Reader: It has well been said that "more than Israel has kept the Sabbath, the Sabbath has kept Israel."

It has preserved the dignity and selfrespect of the Jew through centuries of oppression and darkness. Six days he may have toiled at the most menial tasks, but on the seventh day he was commanded to rest and to devote his mind to things spiritual. However mean his surroundings, for one day out of seven the Jew rose above them. 

He became a king in his little domain; he welcomed the holy day in a royal manner, with the finest of food and clothing that he possessed. And he was well rewarded, for the holy day in return sanctified and ennobled him.

The woman plays a large part in impressing the Sabbath spirit upon her family. In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, the elaborate preparations on Friday, the extra cooking, the special dishes, the more than usual cleanliness of the home, and the beauty of the table appointments, leave no doubt in anyone's mind that a great day is approaching. The woman is privileged to be the first to usher in the Shabbat as she lights her candles, a quarter of an hour before sunset, and says the benediction over them. Before partaking of the evening meal on Friday, and the noonday meal on Saturday, Kiddush is recited by the father of the house. The Kiddush expresses the holiness of the day, and our gratitude to God for the blessings that it brought to Israel. (Kiddush is sung.)

The two loaves of Shabbat bread, or Ḥallot, are symbolic of the double portion of manna supplied to the Israelites for the Shabbat in the wilderness, and of the two-fold commandment- "Remember and observe the Sabbath." 35

"Remember the Sabbath"-keep the seventh day as a day of physical rest and relaxation. "Observe the Sabbath"-keep it holy. Therein lies the vital significance of the day. Physical relaxation is not sufficient. The day must be rich in spiritual achievement. (Cantor or choir sings "Veshomru”)

We take leave of the Shabbat as we would of a precious guest. 

We are loathe to part with it. Through the lingering twilight, we hold on a bit longer to the inner peace and calm of this day of rest. In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, it is the magic hour of story-telling to the younger children, and of intimate talk with the older children. And when night finally closes in, there is one more beautiful ceremony of farewell to the honored guest. 

By the flare of the braided Habdalah candle, father chants the

Habdalah over the wine and the fragrant spices, and the family join in the prayerful song of Hamabdil, grateful for the respite of the Shabbat, and hopeful for a week of blessed labor ahead. (“Hamabdil” is sung by choir.)

Production Notes 

This pageant is very effective for a Mothers and Daughters Day program. A mother and her married daughter, symbolizing the transmission of the tradition from one generation to another, act as hostesses at each table. The mother lights the candles and the daughter reads the description of the holiday represented by their table. This arrangement may be preferred to having one reader for the whole pageant since it enables a larger number of women to participate in the program. 

The tables are arranged in a semi-circle or in a U-shape at the front of the auditorium, on a raised platform so that they can be seen by the audience. The Reader is stationed at a high reading desk with a light to the right rear of the Pesaḥ table. The soloists and the choir may be seated in front of the Pesaḥ table. 

The chairman should request the audience to refrain from applause until the very end of the pageant. 

As the reader starts, the auditorium is darkened, and remains so until she is through with the introduction. The spotlight is then focused upon the Rosh Hashanah table. The mother lights the candles while the daughter or the reader begins to describe the holiday. As the reader mentions the various ceremonial objects, the mother raises them from her table to exhibit to the audience. The spotlight is then shifted to the next table and the same procedure is followed.

The musical selections should be short and appropriate to the holiday. While the musical numbers are rendered the spotlight may be shifted to the performers.

OSH HASHANAH 

Reader: In the autumn, after the solemn month of Elul when the Shofar has sounded each morning its clarion call, Tishri ushers in the New 

Year. For the Jew, each of the first two days is a Yom Hazikaron, a Day 'of Remembrance, when "everything is remembered from the beginning." Each day is a Yom Teruah, a day for the Blowing of the Shofar. According to tradition, on Rose Hashanah the world was created, and on the birthday of the world, God passes judgment on every living creature. 

On this Yom Hadin, the Jew considers his past deeds and resolves in his heart to start afresh and make a new beginning in the new year.

In the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL, the mother bakes round 

Hallot that are symbolic of life without end, a complete year in which there will be no break or interruption. Every meal is begun with honey for sweetness, that no bitter moment come in the new year. In the woman's ears echoes the sound of the 

Shofar, the Shofar that heralds the dawn of another year and summons man to his duties. OM KIPPUR 

Reader: On the tenth day of the month of Tishri is Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the Jewish year. On this day of solemnity and awe, there is a spiritual exultation that transcends the physical. On this one day of the year, the Jew endeavors to approximate a complete spiritual life. By dispensing with the material needs of food and drink, he hopes to acquire an exalted mood which will persist throughout the year to influence his thought and actions.

On the eve of Yom Kippur, after ten days of penitence in which she and her household have looked into their ways and have sought to make reparations for the wrongs committed in the year gone by, the Jewish woman dons garments of white for the Kol Nidre service at the Synagogue. Her family has been blessed by a patriarchal grandfather, and her JEWISH

HOME BEAUTIFUL is sanctified with a spirit of holiness that pervades each nook and corner. The Memorial candle stands high and her prayer book is open to a passage which tells that "Repentance, Prayer and Righteousness Avert the Evil Decree." (When Reader says, "The Jewish woman dons garments of white," a woman, dressed in white, walks slowly to Yom

Kippur table. She holds a tall, lighted, white Memorial candle which she places on the table. She lights the two candles in the candlesticks on the table, raises her hands to her forehead and blesses candles silently. When she has finished, an organ off-stage plays softly the Kol Nidre melody as a background for the verse which the woman recites slowly and with feeling.) 44

Music in the Home Althea Osber Silverman

Music in the Home

Many elements combine to make a Jewish home. Ceremonies, art objects, books, prayers,—all have their place and each one its own purpose to fulfill. Foremost among these factors is music, which, from time immemorial, has been recognized as an important influence on the human heart. Music creates the atmosphere of the home and helps to make it truly beautiful. 

As in ancient Israel, so in our homes today, every joyous occasion, every festival, every ceremony is celebrated with music and song. Our prayers are not recited. They are chanted, each with its own characteristic intonation. The Kiddush, the Habdalah, the Grace, the Blessing of the Hanukkah candles,—who, once having heard them, can ever forget?

Queen Sabbath is ushered in with song, and after the day is ended and the sun gone down in the west, she departs in all her glory on wings of parting songs. And after that, song begins anew, to greet the work-day week.

For the Sabbath, special zemirot are chosen. The family, gathered around the table for the festive Sabbath meal, sings hymns and psalms of thanksgiving for the blessings of the Sabbath day, for life, for rest, for peace. Before the Grace at the end of the meal, a psalm is chanted in gratitude for the return of the Jew to Zion, and when there are three or more adults present around the table, short verses of the Grace are sung to tuneful melodies.

On Ḥanukkah, after the kindling of the lights, and on Purim when the Megillah has been read, the Jewish home resounds with singing because the Jewish heart is glad.

It is at the Seder Service on Passover that music lends its greatest enchantment. Far into the night there is singing and 77

rejoicing until the eyelids close in sheer exhaustion. Thus, do song and music make the Jewish home beautiful!

Jewish children all through the ages have been nurtured on folk-songs that expressed the hopes and yearnings of their people. They were lulled to sleep, not with rustic, pastoral lays, not with lithesome, merry ditties, not with songs of war and patriotism, of animals or pleasures, nor were they cradled with warm, glowing love ballads that soothed the hearts of other children the world over. Into their small, pink ears, Jewish mothers crooned desires and hopes, dreams and yearnings that have sprung from the traditions of Israel. 

And in the JEWISH HOME BEAUTIFUL the mother still sings the old folk-songs which she has heard her own mother sing,songs of Torah, of learning, of duty. She sings of the little kid that stands under her child's cradle,—the little kid that went a-bartering with raisins and almonds. "Vos is die beste schoire?" she asks and then proceeds to tell her child that he will learn Torah, that he will go to Hebrew School, write books and remain an upright and pious Jew. 

The Jewish mother dreams of the day when her child will grow to manhood. "When you grow older," she sings, “you will be a scholar. You will make your parents happy and proud.

It is therefore not becoming for you to fret and cry." 

And when her child is older and ready to enter school, the mother inspires him with the atmosphere and importance of the Ḥeder, the Hebrew School. She sings "Auf'n Pripitchek," that encourages him to study diligently. "Every beginning is difficult," she tells him in song. "Fortunate is he who knows the Torah. What more does a Jew desire?" Later, in another folk-song, she impresses upon her child his duties as a Jew. "Whatever we are, we are," she sings, "but we are always Jews. 

Whatever we do, first of all, we pray; whatever we study, we study especially the Torah. We give alms, we rest on the Sabbath, we bless the Hanukkah lights, we eat unleavened bread, 78

we fast on Yom Kippur, we sit in the Sukkah." All his obligations she teaches him through song!

Now Jewish mothers have new folk-songs to sing. Out of the land of Israel, out of the land of their fathers, have come new Jewish folk-songs, songs of planting and reaping, of harvesting and cutting, of building and creating. Special Holiday Foods and Delicacies Betty Davis Greenberg

Foreword

There is a story told of an Emperor who frequently joined the family of a great Rabbi at their Sabbath meal. He was particularly fond of the gefilte fish that was served on that occasion. 

One day the Emperor asked that his cook be given the recipe. 

The next time they met, the Emperor, rather displeased, accused the Rabbi's cook of having supplied a false recipe. The royal cooks had faithfully followed all the instructions and yet the fish dish that they produced did not have the same delicious taste as had the fish which he ate in the Rabbi's home. Whereupon the Rabbi explained: "Your Majesty, neither your cooks nor the recipe are to blame. The Jews possess an unusual ingredient, a singular spice which, when added to the fish, gives it that unique flavor of which you are so fond. That spice is called the Sabbath."

Our maternal ancestors seem to have understood the sound psychological principle illustrated in the above anecdote. 

Whether it was wisdom or necessity that directed their action, the fact remains that certain choice delicacies were not permitted to become commonplace by everyday use, but were reserved to enhance the holiday meal. And in return, the joy and sanctity of the Sabbath and the holidays imparted an unusual spice, a unique flavor to the foods that were eaten thereon. In time, each holiday acquired its own characteristic delicacy. The 

Yom-tovdige spirit was a complex made up of Yom-tovdige food as well as synagogue attendance, Yom-tovdige clothes, games, songs, and leisure. How well we recall our youthful appreciation of the savory odors that filled the house Erev- Yom-tov. Even as we entered the front door, the familiar aroma of some beloved dish reminded the most forgetful amongst us of the approaching holiday. There was no mistaking the sweet syrupy smell of teiglach boiling in honey and the spicy odor of 87

honey cake just out of the oven, the day before Rosh Hashanah; the peppery pungency of gefilte fish every Friday; the crisp slightly oniony smell of frying potato lotkes on Ḥanukkah; the rich buttery aroma of blintzes and raised coffee cake on Shabuot; and the sour wine-like smell of Pesaḥ borsht. The mere mention of these names arouses nostalgic and mouthwatering memories.

Our mothers brought to their preparations for a holiday or a Sabbath the same love and fervor and joyful anticipation that characterized their performance of sacred rites, or their preparations for welcoming some honored guest. It is not surprising therefore that the very foods themselves acquired a dignity, bordering upon sanctity, which elevated them to the status of religious traditions in the home. 

Will we be wise enough to recognize the importance of these traditions and to hand them down intact to future generations? 

It is not as difficult as some may imagine. All we need to remember is that by the beautiful expedient of surrounding certain foods with the halo of religious associations and with the magic charm of "once in a while," our mothers were able to preserve these traditions for us down through the ages. It is an old Jewish cooking secret, that one should never abuse a delicacy by serving it too frequently. Let us cherish that secret as we turn now to familiarize ourselves with some of these treasured recipes gathered from the culinary lore of our people. 888

While it is true that on Pesah it is quite within the law to make the usual borsht out of fresh beets and lemon juice, yet, by so doing, we are depriving ourselves and our families of the benefits of a good old tradition that has preserved for us an ambrosial dish. GEFILTE FISH 

3 lbs. fish (pike and white fish or pike and carp) 2 onions 1 egg cup water (approximately) 2 tbsp. matzoh meal or bread crumbs 

1 tsp. salt 

1 tsp. pepper a few dashes of cinnamon 

1 stalk celery 1 carrot 

1 potato (if desired)

If there is any one particular food that might lay claim to being the Jewish national dish, gefilte fish is that food. This may be due to the fact that since it is associated with the Shabbat, it appears on our menus more frequently than do most of the other distinctly Jewish dishes. But the greatest factors making for its popularity are its intrinsically delectable qualities. 

Clean and wash the fish thoroughly, salt and place in refrigerator until ready to prepare. Either leave the fish whole or cut it into two-inch slices, as preferred. If the fish is left whole, do not remove the head or the tail. In either case, remove the flesh with the bones, leaving the skin intact. Now clear the flesh from the bones. 

Put the fish and one onion through the food chopper and then into a wooden bowl. Add the egg, matzoh meal, seasoning and enough water to make a soft light mixture. Chop until smooth and thoroughly blended. Fill the skin with this mixture. If the fish has been sliced, wet the hands with cold water, form oval cakes out of the mixture and fit them into the bands of skin. Place the bones on the bottom of a heavy kettle. Slice the other onion, the carrot, the potato and the celery, place them on the bones and season all with a dash of salt, pepper and cinnamon. On these place the fish and cover with cold water. Cook rapidly at first, then slowly for about two hours. If the sauce has boiled down completely, add a little water. Allow the fish to cool somewhat before removing from the kettle. Serve warm or cold, garnished with the sliced carrot and, if cold, also with the jellied sauce.
