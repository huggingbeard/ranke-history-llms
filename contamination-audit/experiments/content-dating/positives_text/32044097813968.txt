The Commonwealth of Massachusetts SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

The legislative directive for this report on minimization of damage from future natural disasters in Massachusetts was prompted by the series of heavy disasters suffered over recent years causing heavy loss of life and many millions of dollars of property damage in the Commonwealth. The more serious of these disasters include the Worcester tornado of 1953, Hurricanes Carol and Edna of 1954, and Hurricane and Flood Diane of 1955.

GENERAL DISASTER DATA. 

The report discusses with illustrations the nature and frequency of the various kinds of disasters which have stricken Massachusetts and New England, — (1) hurricanes, (2) floods, (3) tornadoes and (4) other disasters. (1) Hurricanes are destructive winds of at least 75 miles per hour, which rapidly circle counterclockwise around an "eye" (Fig. 1) as they move up the coast from tropical Atlantic waters from midsummer to early autumn (Fig. 2). 

They bring torrential rains. Their range in number is from 

2 to 21 per year, a majority of them well out at sea. Over the past decade they have shown greater frequency in the northern as compared to the southern Atlantic coastal States (Fig. 3); with present meteorological knowledge it is not possible to predict with confidence to what extent this trend will continue. (2) Floods are of two types: the first arises from melting snows and usually develops rather slowly with or without rain; the second type is that of floods caused by sudden excessive rain. The annual rainfall in Massachusetts over the past three years has considerably exceeded that of previous

(3) Tornadoes resemble hurricanes only in that they are "whirls of air about a central point," usually occurring in the spring or early summer in association with thunderstorms. They destroy instantaneously and completely because of winds which have been estimated at 300 to 400 miles per hour. They cut narrow paths averaging about 

300 yards, extending in skipping fashion usually over only some ten miles in distance. There have been 28 tornadoes reported in Massachusetts since 1916, with records going back much farther. Their total damage in lives lost and property damage is, however, dwarfed by the losses connected with the Worcester Tornado of 1953. (4) Other disasters in Massachusetts include damaging winter storms of glaze or snow (four since 1916), and occasional heavy thunderstorms with hail. Earthquakes have occurred in New England, relatively frequently in the St. 

Lawrence River Valley, but damage has been minor.

WEATHER OBSERVATIONS AND FORECASTING. 

Hurricanes are tracked by regular observing stations, by aircraft reconnaissance and by radar observations. The report shows the photograph of a single radar observation of the "eye" of Hurricane Connie of 1955 from a radar station many miles removed (Fig. 4). Unfortunately hurricanes are found to vary their courses erratically as they move forward (Fig. 5); hence, with present forecast techniques, errors of up to 60 miles may occur for each 12-hour advance prediction of where they will strike. This means forecasts 

24 hours in advance cannot be offered with a confidence factor of more than 50 per cent. Much improvement in the near future in this record is very unlikely. 

Flood conditions are watched by regular observing stations, special river observers, and radar weather checks. 

Snow-melt floods may often be anticipated well in advance, but flash floods are only as predictable as the rain storms which produce them.

Tornadoes with their small size and short life greatly increase observation and forecast difficulty. It is reported that they can be predicted as far as 24 hours in advance for total areas of approximately 20,000 square miles within which tornado occurrence is relatively probable. (Area of 

Massachusetts is 8,257 square miles). With radar there is a prospect of more accurate warnings an hour or two in advance of specific existing tornadoes.

Efforts to modify the weather by cloud seeding and other methods have not yet proved their effectiveness, in the view of most meteorologists. Proposals to use thermo-nuclear weapons to dissipate or alter the course of hurricanes are considered of doubtful wisdom because such action might add to the energy of a hurricane and would surely add radio-active contamination.

Weather Bureau facilities in Massachusetts are now extensive and are in the process of being enlarged. Regular surface observations are prepared at Westover Field, Worcester, Hanscom Field, Otis Air Force Base, Nantucket, and Logan Airport, as well as at points in adjacent States. 

Massachusetts gets radar coverage from installations at 

Logan Airport, Windsor Locks, Conn., and Albany, New York. 

Increased Weather Bureau appropriations have brought added personnel to this area, including a sea surge specialist and a research assistant. 

New radar installations are planned for Worcester and

Nantucket. A new teletype circuit connects the Severe

States for tornado warning purposes. Windsor Locks has been designated River Forecast Center. The Weather 

Bureau and local authorities have worked out an elaborate and generally effective local warning system.

FLOOD CONTROL. 

Apart from storm forecasting and warning, little can be done to prevent direct wind damage. But flood damage can be reduced both by flood control (Fig. 6) and by reducing hazardous activities in flood-prone areas (flood plain zoning) and elsewhere.

Flood control programs have long been operated here. 

They include measures to reduce flood flow by retarding run-off, measures to enlarge the water-carrying capacity of channels, and measures to hold off flood waters from certain areas (by dikes, sea walls, etc.). Fortunately, two thirds of the Commonwealth's area is now covered with woodland which is ideal for flood retardation, and no change in this type of present land use is therefore urgently needed. 

Upstream dams, reservoirs, etc., including livestock ponds and control of run-off from swamps and ponds, may still do much to retard flood run-off; planning for such flood control on smaller streams has been stimulated by federal aid for locally initiated projects of this sort. 

Major flood control reservoirs now exist on the Connecticut and Merrimack rivers. Additional undertakings are projected for these systems as well as the Blackstone, Quinebaug and French rivers. Completion of 21 projected new reservoirs on the Connecticut would cost an estimated $130 million and would reduce annual flood damages by an estimated $7 million. Four projects in the French-Quinebaug valleys would cost $14 million and save $0.8 million a year. 

Projected single reservoirs on the Blackstone and Merrimack rivers would also bring substantial savings to downstream communities.

The 1955 floods may lead to an acceleration of federal flood control work. But there is also some evidence of a stricter rather than more liberal federal policy interpreting federal responsibility for flood protection. The basic criterion for federal participation on flood control reservoirs is whether the benefits clearly exceed the costs.

Channel widening, deepening or relocation can often help the run-off of flood waters, but the benefits from such im-

Dikes now protect several down-river cities in Massachusetts, and new projects have been authorized at Lawrence and North Andover on the Merrimack, and at Adams and North Adams on the Hoosic. No other major projects have appeared economically feasible.

The high cost of effective protection against seashore flooding has sharply limited the extension of seawalls, artificial dunes, riprapping, etc., although in certain situations they have proved useful.

PREVENTION OF FLOOD DAMAGE. 

The other approach to flood damage reduction — the elimination of vulnerable uses- involves several possible methods. Where a use, say housing, is peculiarly sensitive to flood hazards, its elimination by public purchase or condemnation may be justified; in practice a property so taken is usually turned over to some non-hazardous public use, such as parks or playgrounds. Where an area has already been ravaged by floods and become blighted it may be redeveloped for better uses under state and federal urban redevelopment legislation. 

If the elimination of existing uses is financially or practically not feasible, further dangerous uses may be prohibited by various exercises of the police power. Massachusetts law now permits zoning to prevent dangerous uses of floodprone areas, and the authority is used. Some Rhode Island communities prohibit residential construction along certain coastal areas.

Subdivision control regulations are also used to prevent or control land development which is inconsistent with health, safety and welfare; so are building code restrictions. 

In Massachusetts the Division of Waterways of the Department of Public Works is charged with preventing

[Feb. 1956. channel encroachment by private or other buildings. But the inspection of dams is left to the counties, which have not always had the skilled specialists to do it properly. 

Some jurisdictions use an "official map" procedure to establish flood clearance lines along streams. 

All of the above preventive measures assume that floodprone areas are correctly and fairly defined. This is an engineering problem which might well justify state assistance to the policing communities..

DISASTER RELIEF. 

Disaster relief, which comes into play after natural disasters have already occurred, is largely outside the scope of this report. But well-organized emergency action may also prevent, as well as ameliorate, damage.

Chapter 639 of the Acts of 1950 gave the handling of "natural" and "enemy-caused" disasters to the Governor and established a state civil defense agency to act as his staff agency for these duties. The Massachusetts Civil Defense 

Agency (MCDA) is now in the process of reorganization, after five years of experience in disaster work. This report summarizes an extended MCDA document prepared for the 

Legislative Research Bureau leading up to recommendations to stimulate better advance readiness for emergencies. The document is on file with the Bureau. 

DISASTER INSURANCE. 

Much interest has also been shown in recent months in the possibility of softening the economic consequences of natural disasters through some form of improved insurance. At present there is no private insurance available against flood damage or rising tidal waters. Various federal proposals for an experimental federal flood insurance program are now before Congress, and are receiving active attention.

Chapter I. Natural Disasters Affecting New England. 

HURRICANES. 

Description of a Hurricane. 

A hurricane is a portion of the atmosphere which is in rapid circular motion counterclockwise about a central region of near calm called the "eye". This type of disturbance is born from midsummer to early autumn in the tropical 

Atlantic Ocean, the Caribbean Sea or the Gulf of Mexico. 

When the whirling winds have attained a speed of 75 m.p.h. or greater, the storm is considered to be of hurricane intensity. The eye of the hurricane, together with the surrounding winds, at first moves slowly westward at a speed of 10 to 15 miles per hour (m.p.h.). Such storms approaching the southeast coast of the United States usually turn northward and then northeastward while the rate of progression increases to sometimes more than 50 m.p.h.

Destructive winds and torrential rain encircle the central eye and extend outward from it for varying distances ranging from about 50 to 200 miles, depending upon the particular storm and the quadrant. The eye itself is roughly circular in shape, with diameter ranging from a few miles to about 50 miles. This structure of a hurricane is illustrated in Fig. 1. Many of the fatalities and much of the property destruction caused by hurricanes result from the inundation caused by the piling up of water along coastal regions which are exposed to onshore hurricane winds. 

In New England, this rise of ocean level, often several feet above normal tides, occurs north and east of the eye of the hurricane.

FLOODS. 

Meteorological Aspects of Floods. 

Two broad classes of flood conditions may be distinguished. First, are the floods associated with snow melt.

These generally develop slowly over periods of days or weeks and migrate slowly downstream. The development of this type of flood may be aggravated or hastened by a spell of

[Feb. several days of unseasonally mild temperature and heavy rain. The snow melt flood is generally experienced in winter or, more frequently, spring. 

The other type of flood is the flash flood produced by sudden excessively heavy rainfall. Thus the meteorological causes of floods may range from the general rainy weather prevailing for days or weeks, as in the case of the Connecticut and Merrimack floods of 1936, to a single spectacular meteorological event, such as Hurricane Diane of 1955. 

Trends in Precipitation.

Since amounts of precipitation are related to the probability of flooding, it is noteworthy that the past three years have brought amounts of rainfall to Massachusetts considerably in excess of amounts observed in the years during several previous decades. These amounts, however, are no higher than those observed in several years during the last half of the nineteenth century. It is not possible to predict with confidence to what extent this trend will continue.

TORNADOES. 

Description of a Tornado. 

Like the hurricane, the tornado is a whirl of air about a central point. There, however, the similarity ceases. The path of the destructive tornado winds has an average width of only about 300 yards, though individual path widths as small as one hundred feet or as broad as one or two miles have been observed. The length of the path of destruction averages about ten miles.

There are many instances in which the tornado aloft has skipped along the countryside striking only single objects or producing destruction only along intermittent segments of its path. The winds of the tornado are much stronger than those of the hurricane, and have been indirectly estimated as high as 300 or 400 miles per hour, so that destruction is usually instantaneous and often virtually complete. The entire tornado vortex moves usually toward the east or northeast at speeds ranging between 5 and 65 miles per hour. 

Tornado Frequencies.

Tornadoes are generally associated with heavy thunderstorms and have been reported in every State of the Union. 

They are observed almost exclusively during the period between late winter and midsummer. Tornadoes occur most frequently in late Spring in the central plains States. Since 

1916, 7,770 individual tornadoes have been observed in the

United States. A great increase in reported numbers of tornadoes in recent years is believed to be due primarily to population increase in sparsely settled areas, and to greater public concern and co-operation in reporting the occurrence of these small and short-lived storms. 

In Massachusetts since 1916, 28 tornadoes have been reported with a loss of 95 lives and property damage in excess of 54 million dollars. About 95 per cent of the above fatalities and damage were caused by the tornado of June 9, 1953, which struck Worcester and vicinity. Tornadoes have been observed in Massachusetts, on the other hand, as early as the middle of the eighteenth century.

OTHER NATURAL DISASTERS. 

Among other natural disasters, winter storms of glaze or snow have caused great damage in New England. Four major damaging storms of this type have been recorded since 1916. Summer thunderstorms also occasionally produce hail and strong winds (aside from tornadoes) which are responsible for some damage. Thunderstorms occur in

Massachusetts on the average about twenty-five days per year, while hail occurrences average about one day per year. 

Earthquakes, though not meteorological in nature, will be mentioned briefly. The St. Lawrence River Valley has been the site of relatively frequent seismic activity. Although quakes have been felt in New England on occasion since 

1775, only minor damage has occurred. True tidal waves, associated with undersea earthquakes, have had no effect upon New England.

HURRICANE OBSERVATIONS. 

Regular Observing Stations. - Hurricanes can be located through use of reports from regular observing stations when the storm center is over land or very near the coast, provided that communications are left intact. At sea, regular meteorological reports from the merchant marine and from Navy and Coast Guard vessels in the vicinity of the hurricane offer means of determining the approximate location of the storm center. Reports near the region of violent winds are generally missing, however, since shipping is warned to avoid these areas. 

Aircraft Reconnaissance. When the hurricane is at sea frequent penetrations into the eye by aircraft of the Air

Force and Navy permit accurate tracking of the progress of the storm and a good indication of the maximum strength of the winds. During the hurricane season of 1955, hurricanes threatening the coastal areas of the United States were under virtually continuous surveillance by reconnaisance aircraft.

Radar Observations. 

Within the past decade radar has been found to be useful in the location of hurricanes. An example of a radar scope photograph of Hurricane Connie of 1955 is shown in Fig. 4. The radar is able to detect through rain and mist, by night and by day, the presence of rain at distances up to 100 miles and sometimes beyond. 

This rain appears bright on the scope. The eye of the storm is identified visually as the small central region of no rain at the center of the spiralling rain bands. 

In Fig. 4, for example, the eye of Hurricane Connie at the time of the observation shown in the radar photograph was found to be about 60 miles west-southwest of the observing site at Cape Hatteras, North Carolina. Sometimes the structure of the rain bands is not sufficiently clear to permit confident location of the eye. Either land-based or airborne radar may be used to track the storm, depending upon its location. It should be possible to obtain information con-

[Feb. cerning the hurricane wind strength by observing the motion of the wind-borne "cells” of rainfall as they whirl about the central eye. 

Application of radar to hurricane tracking has disclosed that the eye as observed on the scope travels in a much more irregular manner than the relatively smooth paths shown above in Fig. 2. Radar positions of Hurricane Ione of 1955, observed from Cape Hatteras, are shown in Fig. 5. It should be noted carefully that while radar may give prompt indication of a major change in the path of the eye, it will also give indications of changes in direction and speed which will be misleading and which subsequent events will show to have been only small irregularities.

Predictability of Hurricanes. 

Since the path of destruction produced by the hurricane is relatively small, the ability to predict its position accurately at some future time is extremely important. Recent studies of the accuracy of hurricane forecasts indicate that with present forecast techniques an average error of about 60 miles in position is incurred for every twelve hours of advance time for which the hurricane forecast is made. 

Thus in forecasting a hurricane twenty-four hours ahead, an average position error of 120 miles must be expected. 

When this accuracy is considered in relation to the size of the path of destruction of a hurricane, it must unfortunately be concluded that at this time forecasts of hurricane conditions 24 hours in advance cannot be offered with a confidence factor of more than about 50 per cent. 

Hurricane positions can be forecast with the electronic computer now being operated jointly by the Weather Bureau, Air Force and Navy. Present indications are that these forecasts are about as accurate as conventional forecasts issued in the past.

OBSERVATIONS OF FLOOD CONDITIONS. 

Observing Stations. 

With snow-melt floods, regular meteorological reporting stations give sufficient information concerning the general weather situation conducive to this type of disaster. Flash floods, on the other hand, may occur over small areas due to locally heavy rain which may not be observed in the relatively coarse network of regular observing stations. 

Observations of river height and rainfall are provided by additional numbers of special river observers. These observations are extremely useful in both types of flood situation, particularly so in the case of flash floods.

Radar Observations. 

The intensity with which rain is falling at any point within the radar scope may be estimated from the brightness of the "echo" portrayed on the scope. The area over which rain is falling may be determined in detail from the area on the scope in which the bright echoes are observed.

Information of this sort is potentially very useful in the prediction of flash flood conditions, which may escape the regular observing network of conventional stations. Radar is of no particular advantage in dealing with the more slowly developing snow-melt floods.

Predictability of Flood Conditions. 

The predictability of flood conditions varies greatly, depending upon the type of flood. With the snow-melt flood,

[Feb. development of the situation is so slow that adequate forecasts can generally be made days, and sometimes weeks, in advance. Flash floods, on the other hand, develop rapidly from locally heavy rain, and can be predicted no farther ahead than the rain itself. In general, present forecast methods do not permit such predictions more than about 24 hours in advance, and often not more than a few hours ahead. Present basic meteorological knowledge does not offer the expectation of substantial improvement in this forecast accuracy in the near future.

OBSERVATION OF TORNADOES. 

Personal Observations. 

Due to the extremely small size and short life of the tornado, this type of storm is very rarely observed by regularly reporting stations. These stations are, however, adequate to determine the general weather conditions in which tornadoes are likely to develop.

Increased citizen interest in and concern for tornadoes has led to identification of many more tornadoes in recent years than previously, and has permitted the Weather Bureau to become aware of their presence more promptly. 

Since speed is of the essence in dealing with tornadoes, these observations are extremely useful when reliable.

Radar Observations. 

Severe thunderstorms, which are sometimes accompanied by tornadoes, can be observed accurately by radar once they have formed. Their path, furthermore, may be followed quite accurately. There have been several instances in which a peculiar configuration of the radar echo has been subsequently identified as a tornado, but direct tornado identification by radar is not yet reliable. Potentially, radar offers a means of identification and of tracking tornadoes immediately upon their formation.

Predictability of Tornadoes.

No method is presently available for the prediction of the precise location and time of occurrence of a particular tornado. It is, however, possible to predict as far as 24 hours in advance the areas of approximately 20,000 square miles within which tornado occurrence is relatively probable. 

Recent Weather Bureau verification figures show that, in the United States in 1955, 32 per cent of all observed tornadoes occurred in previously warned areas, while an additional 30 per cent occurred within 150 miles of the warned area and within two hours of the predicted time interval.

As an alternative method of verification, tornadoes subsequently occurred in 35 per cent of the previously warned areas, while if the region within 150 miles of a warned area and a time two hours from the predicted time is included, tornadoes occurred in 59 per cent of such extended areas. 

Even in warned areas in which tornadoes are observed, the path of destruction is very small in comparison with the size of the warned area.

While there is no immediate prospect of great improvement in accuracy of tornado forecasts 24 hours in advance, there is a prospect of more accurate warning of specific existing tornadoes an hour or two in advance through the use of radar. In the light of present meteorological knowledge, however, radar cannot be used to predict the place or time of the initial appearance of a particular tornado. 

Possibilities of Weather Modification. 

Cloud seeding (the introduction of suitable agents into clouds) has been studied in the United States and elsewhere as a method of enhancing or inhibiting rainfall from various types of clouds, and also as a method of prevention of severe thunderstorms and hail. There is at present no consensus among meteorologists as to the effectiveness of these measures. The bulk of the experimental work has been devoted to stimulation rather than suppression of rainfall, but a limited amount of work performed in connection with hail prevention indicates some degree of success.

[Feb. 

Use of nuclear or thermonuclear weapons in dissipation or alteration of the course of hurricanes has been frequently suggested. There is at present no basis for reliable prediction of the significance of the effects of such measures, or even a prediction of whether the results would be helpful or harmful. Since the effect would quite possibly add to the energy of a hurricane and would surely add radioactive contamination to the other harmful effects of a hurricane, the wisdom of undertaking such experiments with storms which may affect the United States is considered doubtful by most meteorologists.

Tributary Forecasts: Based on known conditions in the basin and reported rainfall processed through rainfallrunoff procedures giving a stage forecast. Issued from hours to days in advance, and revised as later reports of rainfall and stage are received.

Headwater Warnings: Where floodwaters rise practically as the rain falls, these warnings are issued on the spot, based either on rainfall reports collected locally or on forecasts of anticipated local heavy rainfall; issued up to 12 hours in advance, seldom specific as to stage.

Chapter IV. Measures to Reduce Floods. 

INTRODUCTION. 

Forecasts and warnings are almost the only preventive measures which can be taken against wind damage. But floods are another story.¹ Many measures may be taken to minimize damage from this quarter. 

They can be divided into two categories: (1) control of the magnitude and extent of flooding; (2) control of the amount of potential damage in areas subject to flooding. 

The first category includes many devices for retaining flood waters within the banks of rivers, or within the established high waterline of shore areas. The second control category, to be discussed in the following chapter V, includes various procedures which may be used (a) to reduce to a minimum the human activities and man-made structures in probable flood areas, or (b) to reduce the vulnerability of such activities and structures. Put another way, the first category is concerned with measures to reduce floods, the second category with measures to reduce flood damage. 

Measures to reduce floods include: (1) those devices which tend to reduce flood flows by retarding runoff, either by absorption into the ground or by retention storage; (2) those devices which in one way or another enlarge the watercarrying capacity of channels (usually by widening, deepening, straightening or relocating channels, or by a combination of these); and (3) those devices which directly hold back floodwaters from areas to be protected; that is, dikes, levees, flood walls, sea walls or dunes. 

Well-established programs are now operating in most of the above directions, and pressures are being exerted for their extension. Yet the cost of most of these devices is great, and their extent is therefore limited severely by economic considerations. In general, really effective control can be expected only where highly vulnerable areas are to be protected and where large-scale direct benefits can be expected to offset the high costs.

Briefly, the programs under the three headings mentioned above may be summarized as follows:

RETARDATION OF FLOW. 

Ground Cover.

The rate of runoff, and thus usually the magnitude of the flood, is affected by the type of ground cover, though little is known about the precise behavior of different types of cover under varying conditions. Most serious floods are caused by unusually heavy rainfalls, extending over some hours or days. Under such conditions the cover tends to have minimum effect, because it, and the soil beneath it, tend to become saturated fairly rapidly. On the other hand, flash floods in small areas may be significantly affected by the presence or lack of ground cover, since speed of runoff and percentage of absorption are major factors in many such floods.

The Situation. 

In Massachusetts, where about two thirds of the area, and most of the hillsides, are covered with some sort of woodland, existing ground cover is nearly optimum for flood retardation purposes. Hence, except in relatively small areas, little improvement in conditions can be expected from changes in land use practices. For example, the New 

England New York Inter-Agency Committee estimates that in the Connecticut River Valley as a whole "reduction of floodwater damage expected from land treatment measures is estimated at $149,000 annually." This figure probably exceeds the potential benefits in all Massachusetts from such measures. On the other hand, flood conditions could become much worse if there were to be a trend towards clearing, tilling, or the intensive pasture use of lands which are now covered with heavy vegetation. 

There is some speculation as to the effect upon floods of rapid urbanization, where roofs, pavements and lawns replace fields and woods. The effects of urban development can be significant upon the magnitude of flash floods, as

On the other hand, it is doubtful if even large-scale urbanization can affect appreciably floods on the larger tributaries, since the gross area of all urban land in a valley such as the Merrimack, or the Millers or Chicopee, is such a small proportion of the total.

In much of Massachusetts the pattern of settlement is such that urban areas are usually (but not always) "downstream," where rapid run-off may tend to reduce, rather than to accentuate, flood peaks. For example, if heavy rainfall of relatively short duration runs off rapidly from the Springfield urbanized area, it is likely to have passed well downstream before the flood run-offs from upstream areas get to that part of the Connecticut River. On the other hand, similar rapid run-off from the Worcester area, near the headwaters of the Blackstone River, might bring the heaviest flows downstream at the same time that the slower run-offs from wooded areas downstream get to the main stream, and thus the two maximum flows would combine to produce a peak flood in the lower Blackstone River.

The Prospect. 

Wise land use and conservation practices can aid in prohibiting or reducing some types of flood. They cannot be expected to reduce appreciably the magnitude of major floods in Massachusetts.

Upstream Engineering. 

This term is usually applied to the various reservoir and other devices used to control or retard run-off on the smaller tributaries upstream from the main river. Check dams, livestock ponds, small reservoirs, control of run-off from swamps and ponds, come into this category.

The Prospect. 

Programs of this sort undoubtedly offer real opportunities for coping with purely local problems. Collectively, the possibility of their reducing floods perceptibly on major tributaries or main streams is open to question. Thus, co-ordination of programs at the state level, involving at least the Department of Agriculture, the Soil Conservation Service, and the Division of Waterways, and probably the Department of Commerce and the Department of Conservation and Natural Resources, seems essential.

CHANNEL CAPACITY. 

Local Improvements. 

Where channels are naturally, or by acts of man, too small to carry flood flows, increased capacity can often be obtained by widening, deepening or relocating the channel. 

Usually the benefits are chiefly local, but sometimes the increased capacity serves to lower flood heights for many miles upstream.

The Situation. 

In some instances the potential benefits from local channel improvements have been so great as to justify federal participation under the Corps of Engineers flood control program. The proposed Kettle Brook Diversion Tunnel, in 

Worcester, and some of the authorized work on the Hoosic River in Adams and North Adams, and on the Merrimack at Lawrence and North Andover, seem to come under this category. The relative amounts of "Federal benefit," as contrasted to local benefit, impose severe restrictions on the amount of federal aid which can be economically justified for this type of project. Where federal participation is justified, land damages, rights of way, and maintenance costs, at least, are a state or local responsibility. 

The Corps of Engineers, acting on behalf of the Federal Civilian Defense Agency, has responsibility for restoring channels to their original condition in the wake of flood devastation. This clean-up work has resulted in some net improvement to the capacity of channels in flood areas, but this type of improvement is limited to emergency measures. 

Where cleaning-up operations can produce long-range improvements at no extra costs, such improvements are made; but few major benefits are likely to result from this program. 

State and local initiative in improving channel capacities is possible, and has made significant progress, particularly

[Feb. 

after the floods during the 1930's, and since 1951. It has been suggested that more progress might be made in this direction if responsibility for initiation and design of such projects were assigned more specifically to the Division of 

Waterways of the State Department of Public Works. That 

Division now depends principally upon local initiation before projects are undertaken. 

The Prospect. 

Relatively little direct action in the line of channel improvements can be expected under the federal flood control program. In many cases there may be potential improvements of local significance which could be carried out, at reasonable cost-to-benefit ratios, through a more aggressive program of co-operation between state and local agencies, implemented by larger appropriations at the state level, and increasing willingness of local officials to accept their fair share of the costs.

Channel Obstructions. 

Flood heights are frequently accentuated because of clogged channels. Debris, refuse and a variety of unnecessary or poorly designed structures act as partial dams to hold back, and thus peak, flood waters. 

Clogging of channels by dumping or placing of obstructions in channels can be controlled by regulation, as will be discussed below.

Malfunctioning of dams, bridges, culverts and other structures across minor streams have some deleterious effect on flood flows. Most, but possibly not all, such structures are inherited from the remote past, and may reflect former lack of engineering data or lack of engineering skill on which to base adequate designs. Machinery exists for approval, by the State Department of Public Works, of the adequacy of designs of new structures. The present mechanism appears to provide adequate legal machinery; appropriations and assignment of specific responsibility may be needed to discover and rectify the worst of the past mistakes.

DIKES AND FLOOD WALLS. 

Along Rivers. 

Even with maximum programs for storage control and channel improvements, there are likely to be high-value, low-lying areas where floods can be controlled only by dikes or walls.

The Situation. 

The federal program, under the Corps of Engineers, calls for joint federal and local participation in building flood dikes. Usually construction cost is met by the federal government, and state and local interests pay the balance. 

Under these programs the most vulnerable high-value areas along the lower Connecticut, from Northampton to Springfield, have been rendered apparently safe from flooding. 

Similar programs have been completed in some Merrimack 

Valley cities. A project in Lawrence and North Andover, on the Merrimack, has been authorized; and projects at 

Adams and North Adams, on the Hoosic River, are partly completed. More projects, for other highly urban areas, may be needed, but up to the present, no major projects, other than those mentioned, have appeared to be economically justified.

The Prospect. 

The cost of dikes or flood walls, and of attendant pumping stations, is usually great per acre of land protected. 

Hence the value of land and buildings protected has to be high to justify such projects economically. Relatively vacant or low density residential land can ordinarily be protected if its protection is incidental to protection of highvalue industrial or commercial property behind the same flood walls. Chief exception to this generalization is where the flood plain to be protected has great width, as along the 

Mississippi River, but as is almost nowhere the case in Massachusetts.

Along the Coast

Sea walls, bulkheads or dunes are frequently effective in holding back tidalwaves, abnormally high tides, and other potentially destructive aftermaths of coastal storms or hurricanes.

The Situation. 

Federal-aid and local programs have frequently protected shore areas from storm flooding by erecting barriers which will hold back waves and high tides. Whether such devices are justified is usually determined by local cost-to-benefit ratios.

Artificial dunes or stabilization by grass, paving or riprapping of existing dunes are likely to be the most economical measures where existing conditions make such methods possible. Sea walls may be more economical or more efficient where available area or other conditions render dunes impractical. However, the requisite height and weight of such structures make their use practical only where the value of property to be protected is great, or where they can be justified on the basis of erosion control and smallstorm benefits, even though they do not assure protection against really major storms. Inadequate structures may give false illusions of protection, and thus invite even greater disaster; or they may be destroyed in a single storm, with resultant waste of substantial sums of money. 

Adequate structures, along the southern Massachusetts coast, may have to rise as much as 20 feet above the normal high-water mark (the hurricane tide level, not counting waves, has been more than 10 feet above normal high tide at many points on the east coast); they might easily cost several hundred dollars per foot. Hence, unless the value of property to be protected can reasonably be figured in many hundred dollars per foot of shore front, such expensive structures are likely not to be justified.

Groins, low sea walls and low bulkheads may be very effective in preventing shore erosion, in reclaiming land along the shore, or in protecting property from the ravages of less

1956.] 

severe storms, but offer little significant protection against really severe storms or tidal waves. Much of the relatively good work carried on by the state, often with local aid, in recent years, has been in this category. In some cases it seems to be a moot question whether such projects would stand up against a really severe hurricane storm.

The Prospect 

Dunes and sea walls may offer real protection for highvalue areas where the potential benefits justify large, expensive installations. Only where intensive uses are concentrated is it likely that their costs can be justified. Possibly, along most stretches of shore front, more modest structures, helpful in controlling the shore line, and in protecting against yearly storms, are more economically sound, even though they may not afford significant protection at the time of a major disaster.

Chapter V. Steps to prevent Flood Damage. 

The second major approach to disaster protection lies in the taking of steps to prevent, or minimize the extent of, flood damage. Under this general category may be included two classes of measures: (1) those measures designed to reduce the damage by elimination of vulnerable activities and property, and (2) those measures designed to prevent future development of uses which may be subject to damage. 

ELIMINATION OF VULNERABLE USES. 

In many situations it may be of long-range public benefit to limit or eliminate some or all of the uses in a flood-prone area, in order to forestall the possibility of future catastrophe and loss of life

Elimination of Serious Hazards. 

In some situations uses may be so sensitive to flood damage that outright elimination appears desirable. 

The Situation. 

Some types of use, notably housing, are peculiarly sensitive to flood hazards in that damaging floods threaten loss of life as well as property. Though many such uses have already been eliminated by past floods, many have been rebuilt, despite the threat, and some others exist in precarious localities. Despite the fact that the cost of elimination is at least equal to the probable magnitude of flood damage, public purchase or condemnation of such properties may be justified. If such uses are marginal, substandard or relatively undesirable, or if a new use not particularly vulnerable to floods would be desirable in that particular locality, then part of the cost might well be justified on other than flood-protection grounds

Where such direct purchase of flood-prone properties has been practiced it has usually been for a specific public purpose for parks, playgrounds, or other public purposes.

It seems clear, however, that purchase or condemnation for

1956.] 

HOUSE - No. 2727. 

the sole public purpose of eliminating a public hazard a threat to public health, safety and welfare could be justified in extreme situations. 

Whether especially vulnerable properties could be ordered removed under the police powers, without payment of damages, is a matter not yet clearly decided. In some jurisdictions, owners whose properties have been partly damaged by floods have been at least temporarily restrained from restoring them by police power regulation. Such legislation, providing for a six months' moratorium on rebuild-. ing or reconstruction, was submitted to the Special Session of the Connecticut General Assembly in the Fall of 1955, but was not enacted.

Action authorizing state purchase of more than two miles of vulnerable beach frontage was enacted by the Rhode

Island Legislature in 1955 (chapter 3561, Public Laws of 1955); and in the same session legislation was passsed providing state assistance to towns and cities to acquire for public purposes "shore areas endangered by hurricane tides or floods" (chapter 3485, Public Laws of 1955). No such general legislation has been found relating to acquisition of river flood plains.

Redevelopment to Minimize Hazard. 

State and federal urban redevelopment legislation may be used to facilitate redevelopment of flood-damaged areas to uses less vulnerable to flooding.

The Situation. 

Where areas have been partly ravaged by floods, they are rather clearly substandard or blighted under the terms of the federal urban redevelopment sections of the Housing Act of 1954 (administered by the Housing and Home Finance Agency), and apparently also under the Massachusetts Redevelopment Act, chapter 654, Acts of 1945. If such areas qualify for redevelopment proceedings, they can be acquired, redesigned and put to appropriate new use at considerably less direct cost to local governmental units. Ob-

[Feb. viously the new uses cannot be uses which would be vulnerable to flood damage.

Subsequent to the 1955 floods in Connecticut several communities have prepared, and received preliminary federal approval for, redevelopment projects which may involve a net cost of close to $20 million. Under the federal act, two thirds of this cost would be met by the federal government as its contribution to the net cost of redevelopment projects. 

Under state legislation enacted in December, 1955, the state would contribute one half of the remainder, so the community involved would have to pay only one sixth of the net cost of redevelopment projects "made necessary by the floods or the danger of flooding," as the Connecticut 

Flood Recovery Committee puts it.¹

The Prospect. 

Many of those areas subject to most serious flooding can be safeguarded from appreciable flood damage by purchase or condemnation for some public purpose, either under direct action or under redevelopment programs. The benefits of such action may appreciably exceed the costs if the new use meets a public need, or if the areas to be redeveloped were initially substandard or blighted in other respects. 

Where such areas qualify for federal redevelopment aid, the net cost to the town will be much less than the potential flood-damage loss.

PROHIBITION OF VULNERABLE USES. 

Except where the flood hazard is very pronounced, it may be financially or practically infeasible to eliminate the existing uses which are subject to flood damage. However, in many of these areas further development of vulnerable uses can be prevented by various exercises of the police power, and thus the magnitude of potential damage can be held at a minimum. Flood-Plain Zoning. 

By application of zoning measures not appreciably different from standard procedures, areas subject to flooding may be zoned to permit only those types of new uses which are not particularly vulnerable.

The Situation. 

Zoning permits the subdividing of a town or city, by action of its legislative body, into zones, and the designation of what uses may or may not be permitted in each zone. 

According to the Massachusetts Statutes (chapter 40A, section 2) "A zoning ordinance or by-law may provide that lands deemed subject to seasonal or periodic flooding shall not be used for residence or other purposes in such a manner as to endanger the health or safety of the occupants thereof." A similar provision was inserted in the Connecticut zoning enabling statute last Fall; and such provisions are found in other states. In Rhode Island, where the matter was carefully scrutinized a year ago, it was concluded that general zoning powers, though not referring specifically to flood plains, can be used to restrict uses in the flood plains or in low-lying coastal areas. 

Although zoning ordinances incorporating flood plain provisions are in effect in many parts of the country, notably in California and Wisconsin, there have been few court tests of such provisions. In the only significant case, one testing such zoning in Keene, New Hampshire, before a United

States Circuit Court of Appeals, the decision supported flood plain zoning as a proper exercise of the zoning power (41 Fed. (2d) 484).

Since the 1954 hurricane several Rhode Island communities have adopted zoning controls to restrict the types of use along coastal areas. In general, these restrict areas less than ten feet above mean high water to non-building uses, except for some types of non-residential, recreation-oriented buildings; and allow buildings in the area between elevation 10 and elevation 20 only if they have special structural

[Feb. 

and sanitation features, and first-floor levels above elevation 20.¹

In Connecticut, several municipalities are now studying possible flood-plain zoning amendments to existing zoning ordinances. So far as is known, no specific provisions of this sort have yet been enacted in Connecticut or Massachusetts.

Endorsement of flood-plain zoning has been widespread since the 1954 and 1955 hurricanes and floods. Special groups reporting on disaster prevention measures in Rhode 

Island, Connecticut, Tennessee and North Carolina have all made strong recommendations urging adoption of such measures.

The Hoover Commission Task Force Report states as follows: 

There is a growing conviction that owners of land vulnerable to floods do not have a clear right to put these lands to dangerous use and then obtain protection at public expense against their indiscretion. Floodplain zoning . . . could be more accurately termed "sensible adjustment of land use to the flood peril." (Op. cit., Vol. III, p. 1253.)

The Prospect. 

Though zoning cannot be used to reduce appreciably the potential damage to existing developments in areas subject to flooding, it can go a long way toward preventing increased development of vulnerable uses.

Subdivision control powers can be exercised to complement zoning control in prevention of further encroachment into flood plains.

In a few cases the approval of a subdivision is permitted only if areas "subject to overflow, inundation or flood hazard" are clearly indicated on the plat as recorded.

Subdivision regulations can be amended, by local Planning Board action, in such a way as to permit effective prohibition of the development of new subdivisions in flood-prone areas.

Building Code Restrictions. 

A variety of restrictions and regulations can be, and frequently is incorporated in building codes, sanitary codes, etc., to insure that precautionary steps are taken to prevent flood damage. The specific provisions are so numerous and

[Feb. 

common as not to require listing here, but those relating to foundations, to the securing of superstructure to foundations, to the means of disposal of sewage, to sources of water supply, and to elevation of living space in relation to potential water levels, are the ones of most direct relevance here. Application and enforcements of accepted provisions of this type could aid significantly in reducing future damage and threats to health.

Obstructions to Channels. 

Related to the channel widening and straightening measures discussed above are the police control measures to prohibit private building in such ways as to impede stream flow. 

The Situation. 

It is not uncommon to find buildings and other structures so located that they significantly encroach upon river channels. In the case of small streams, such encroachment can measurably increase flood heights and flood damage.

Local ordinances can, apparently, expressly prohibit any such encroachments. For example, a Los Angeles ordinance states that "a person shall not place in the channel or bed of any river, stream, wash or arroyo . . . matter which may impede, retard or change the direction of flow of water."

In Massachusetts, the Division of Waterways of the Department of Public Works has this power to prevent channel encroachment. Hence there is no reason to assume that further encroachments need be expected; and local powers in this direction would probably be superfluous.

Many parts of the country assign to some appropriate state agency responsibility for inspection of dams and ordering of necessary repairs or replacement (as is the case in 

Connecticut and New Hampshire). In Massachusetts this responsibility rests with the county, where the extent of enforcement depends upon the technical ability and available time of members of the county engineering staffs. Though documentation is difficult to find, evidence indicates that assiduous inspection and enforcement of safety precautions

1956.] 

could have prevented failure of various dams during the 1955 floods. The failure of some of these dams added perceptibly to the amount of damage suffered.

As this type of inspection work calls for highly specialized skills, it may well be the type of work that could be handled better by a specialized state agency, such as the Division of Waterways, rather than by a number of different county offices. That state agency is already charged with inspection and approval of culverts, bridges and other similar structures, and thus is already relatively well equipped to handle the problem of dam inspection. 

Some jurisdictions make use of the so-called “official map procedure, or somewhat broader powers than those granted to Massachusetts communities under chapter 41, sections 81E through J, to establish lines along streams within which private structures or other encroachments shall not be allowed, in much the same manner as building or bulkhead lines have traditionally been established. Such lines could, presumably, be used also to prevent encroachment into stream bank areas where dikes, levees and similar improvements are contemplated. However, a Kentucky case which seems to establish something of a precedent found that a similar procedure intended to set aside an area of private land for flood water storage could not be enforced without award for damages. 

Subdivision regulations sometimes provide specifically that drainage easements be dedicated as a condition for plat approval. For example, the Azusa, California, ordinance provides that if a subdivision "is traversed by any major water course channel, stream or creek, the subdivider shall dedicate an adequate right of way for storm drainage purposes, if in the opinion of the (Planning) Commission such dedication is necessary. There is evidence that the term "adequate right of way" has been construed liberally so as to make provision for peak floods. Presumably such easements could be required, where appropriate, to provide for channel widenings or protective works. In a few cases, apparently, the subdivider has been required to build protective works at his own expense.

Channel encroachment can readily be prevented by law, although systematic enforcement may require alert administration. Such enforcement is logically a state concern, because of the highly technical skills involved, and because such encroachments often have inter-town implications. 

When land is being developed, easements, and possibly engineering improvements, needed to accommodate storm runoffs can be required of the developer, by the local municipality; most of the improvements, themselves, will be subject to state approval.

GENERAL OBSERVATIONS. 

All of the above preventive measures are based on the assumptions that flood-prone areas can be defined, and that reasonable decisions can be made as to what areas should be regulated to minimize future flood damage. These assumptions seem to be correct; but the problems inherent in them may be complex.

Delineation of flood-prone areas is not a complicated engineering task, especially where relatively recent major flood characteristics have been recorded. But relatively careful and competent engineering determinations must be made if the appropriate regulations are to be just. In the case of share areas, the process may be the relatively simple one of establishing elevations. Along some of the smaller streams, a complicated set of elevations, or careful mapping of actual areas, may be necessary. In Milwaukee, where much of the responsibility for determining areas subject to flooding has been placed on the developer, frequent benchmarks have been established by the city to help the private surveyor. 

According to the Hoover Commission Task Force, "the engineering problems (involved in establishing flood lines) are no more difficult than those involved in flood control" (Op. cit., Vol. III, p. 1256). Nevertheless, they are undoubtedly beyond the means of many small towns which might well profit by adoption of some of the preventive

1956.] 

HOUSE 57 

No. 2727. measures described above. Hence provision might be made for rendering state assistance in this connection. 

In addition to the engineering problems involved, there are the decisions respecting the size of flood to be planned for and the extent to which such safeguards should be applied. Some places have elected to apply restrictions only to places subject to frequent or periodic floods - say to those floods which may be expected on the order of every ten years, or every fifty years. In other cases restrictions endeavor to protect against the "maximum" flood which can be expected. In most parts of Massachusetts it might be logical to establish lines on the basis of the abnormally high, if not record, floods experienced in 1955. Where land values appear to be high, or where there is already much development which cannot be expected to be eliminated in the near future, more modest requirements may be all that will be accepted as "fair" and practicable.

Then, again, where there is reasonable assurance that engineering protective devices will be provided, the measures to prevent development need not be applied. For example, when it becomes assured that the East Brimfield and Westville dams are to be constructed, then it would be expected that any lines to be established downstream in places like Southbridge should be adjusted to reflect what the Corps of Engineers agree will be the reduced flood peaks, rather than accept the flood peaks recorded in the 1955 flood. 

CONCLUSION. 

Available evidence indicates that much can be accomplished to reduce potential flood damages by the construction of engineering devices for reducing floods. Even though the cost of such devices are great, the benefits in many instances will exceed the costs. The end result would be to reduce the theoretical possibilities of catastrophic loss of life and property way below what they were in 1955, and what they may be in 1956 or any other year.

At the same time, the exercise of planning and police power controls can also greatly reduce the possibilities of

Chapter VI. Disaster Relief. 

Disaster relief, which usually comes into play only after hurricane or other storm damage has occurred, is largely beyond the scope of this report. But it is apparent that well organized emergency action may sometimes not only relieve suffering caused by storm damage, but also mitigate or even prevent that damage. A brief discussion of disaster relief in the Commonwealth is therefore included here. 

DISASTER RELIEF ORGANIZATION. 

Until comparatively recently, emergency relief in natural disasters was left to local authorities and the American Red Cross, with assistance from the state and federal governments as the situation demanded. There was no formal relief set-up.

Chapter 639 of the Acts of 1950, however, gave the Governor sweeping powers to deal with enemy-caused and "natural" disasters. It set up a "division of civil defense" under a director to act as the staff agency of the Governor in carrying out both preparatory and emergency activities in this field. The Massachusetts Civil Defense Agency thus became the co-ordinator for all disaster relief activity in the Commonwealth. 

In 1953 the President, by Executive Order 10427, placed the administration of federal disaster relief under the Federal Civil Defense Administration (transferring it from the 

Administrator of the Housing and Home Finance Agency). 

A co-ordinated relief organization was thereby established extending from the local to the federal level. 

The Massachusetts Civil Defense Agency (MCDA) is itself strictly a co-ordinative organization, utilizing the resources of state agencies, private agencies, institutions, local governments and individuals for disaster action. Its staff is purposely kept small because its function is to assist other agencies of government to carry out their inherent civil defense responsibilities. In Executive Order No. 25, dated 

January 3, 1955, Governor Herter directed all state agencies

1956.] 

to make plans for their own protection in case of disaster, and further directed that certain agencies provide civil defense services directly to the MCDA. 

The staff of MCDA consists of a director, a deputy director, and a score of specialist officers at headquarters level, four area Directors and twenty sector Directors in the field, plus clerical and communications personnel. 

MCDA has been completely reorganized during the past year. Since funds were not available until the middle of 

September, 1955, the implementation of the reorganization plan is still going on. March 1, 1956, has been set as a target date for a minimum state of operational readiness by the reorganized agency. 

The revised plan calls for new, fully equipped Control Centers to serve all MCDA units, for a new Intelligence Section of the MCDA staff, for an improved warning and alerting system, and for many other changes. The plan is spelled out in a special and comprehensive MCDA report prepared for the Legislative Research Bureau,¹ for which grateful acknowledgment is here expressed. Unfortunately space permits presentation below of only a summary of that extended document; it is, however, available for examination at the Research Bureau office.

FUNCTION OF DISASTER RELIEF AGENCIES. 

After five years of operation, including experience with several of the State's most severe natural disasters, MCDA has reached some judgments on the kinds of on-the-spot relief action that may diminish the damage from hurricanes and other natural disasters.

In its special report it notes three kinds of action, preventive, emergency and post-emergency. The first kind is outside MCDA's field of competency (except for advising local industry and business what to do in case natural disasters develop or threaten). But the others are very

Under "emergency" action the report mentions five possible "preparatory" measures: (1) A system of receiving disaster intelligence can be established so that the development and subsequent progress of any disaster conditions can be known immediately to proper authorities. (2) A system must be established for alerting public authorities, the general public, and special groups who may be affected by disaster, and the public must be informed as to the system in specific emergencies. (3) Preventive steps can be taken in some cases, such as evacuation of people and property from threatened areas, providing additional structural protection, etc., removal or protection of vital machinery, and so forth. (4) Plans can be made to mobilize various types of resources person- nel, equipment, supplies which may be of use in event of disaster; these plans can call for "stand-by" or partial mobilization as the threat. increases. (5) The civil defense control center staffs, who would act as the co-ordinating unit at all levels, can be placed on "stand-by" with arrangement for quick mobilization. 

After a disaster has actually begun, emergency action. must concentrate first on saving lives and second on protecting property. The report lists four steps here: (1) Complete and periodic reports (disaster intelligence) as to what is. happening must be sent to the next highest civil defense control unit, as long as disaster conditions exist. (2) Disaster-fighting resources must be mobilized as conditions ap-proach the disaster stage, or as soon as possible following disasters which occur without warning. (A special plan must be made for this contingency, similar to former plans for alerting civil defense resources in waremergency.) (3) Threats to life, property and health must be dealt with by local civil defense forces, with such outside assistance as they may request. through civil defense channels. (4) Life-saving or property-protecting resources which are in short supply must be allocated in the disaster area as "disaster intelligence" indicates is required. This applies to all levels of civil defense organization. 

Under post-emergency action (relief and rehabilitation) the report lists four kinds of undertaking: (1) Those persons affected by disaster must have emergency relief im-mediately, such as food, housing, clothing and identification of survivors..

1956.] 

(2) Disaster-caused threats to public health and safety must be detected and remedied as soon as possible. This may include emergency water supply, emergency lighting, disease control measures, fire prevention, etc. (3) Damaged areas must be protected, movement of the public and other traffic controlled, and other public safety measures taken. (4) Secondary damage - especially interruption of economic activities — can be minimized by civil defense action to speed repair of utilities, re-open businesses and industries, and otherwise assist in restoration of the community. 

Longer-term rehabilitation in the post emergency period is primarily a responsibility of private welfare agencies such as the American Red Cross. But even here civil defense has helped by providing temporary housing and other assistance.

RECOMMENDATIONS OF CIVIL DEFENSE AGENCY. 

Despite its present state of partial organization, the MCDA is convinced that the civil defense system, from federal to state, area, sector and local levels, is potentially the best method of co-ordinating disaster action within the 

Commonwealth to minimize damage from natural disasters.

To achieve that end it has made the following concrete recommendations: (1) That the current reorganization of the MCDA (including state, area and sector levels) be pressed with all possible speed, including staffing, equipping and additional training. (2) That current participation by other state agencies in the coordinated use of resources of the Commonwealth be developed with all possible speed. (3) That the position of the civil defense director as "disaster coordinator" for the political head of government at every level be specifically repeated (recognized) by executive and legislative branches of the government and that the position be established in fact in all localities. (4) That in all disasters requiring assistance in excess of that secured by existing mutual aid procedures, the civil defense "chain of command" be recognized as the single authorized channel for requesting such assistance to avoid duplication of requests. (5) That the local civil defense organization be recognized as the sole channel for public announcement and public information in time of disaster to avoid confusion and conflicting or unconfirmed reports. (6) That local planning for disaster be stimulated and assisted by the

Chapter VII. Disaster Insurance. 

INTRODUCTION. 

Like disaster relief, disaster insurance is chiefly a means of mitigating or repairing the effects of damage after it has occurred. It results, as one expert has put it, in "an improved distribution of the costs of (disasters) over time and place." 1

It therefore touches the subject matter of this report only indirectly.

Much interest has been shown, however, in the development of improved insurance coverage against the effects of disasters, particularly flood disasters, and discussion of the subject has frequently tied it in with preventive measures. 

The disappointment felt by many Massachusetts citizens upon discovering that their Extended Coverage insurance contracts did not cover damage by rising waters in 1954, or by floods in 1955, led to the adoption by the General Court of chapter 80, Resolves of 1955, setting up an unpaid special commission to investigate and study fire insurance policies, with Extended Coverage Endorsement and the $50-deductible clause, as well as special reference to the general problem of waterfront damage. 

In a preliminary report dated January 4, 1956, the Commission stated flatly that "insurance against flood damage, or against rising tidal waters, is for all practical purposes unavailable to owners of real property in Massachusetts." 

After reviewing the losses suffered by insurers offering Extended Coverage after the 1938, 1944 and 1954 hurricanes, the Commission concluded that it would be "worse than futile" for the state to tell the private companies that they must continue such coverage in future.

This view was borne out by private researches of the insurance companies themselves. On the basis of engineering studies after the floods of 1951 in Kansas and Missouri and the more recent inundation of August, 1955, in the northeastern states, the American Insurance Association, in conjunction with the National Association of Insurance Agents, found that "insurance against the peril of flood cannot successfully be written."

FEDERAL DISASTER INSURANCE PROPOSALS. 

Current hopes center chiefly on the possibility of government insurance or reinsurance. The initiative has been left to the federal government, although various schemes call for state participation.

At least four bills were introduced in Congress after the 1955 hurricanes, and comprehensive hearings on them were held in a half-dozen eastern seaboard cities, including Boston, between October 31 and December 19, 1955. (Both the hearings, and an earlier staff report which was prepared to assist the Senate Committee at these hearings are on file with the Legislative Reaserch Bureau). 

On November 11, 1955, the New England Governors approved a resolution (of their Textile Committee) urging a federal program of flood insurance, "the federal government to participate in so far as private companies are unprepared to make flood insurance available."

In his State of the Union Message the President urged the enactment of federal legislaton providing for a disaster insurance corporation, and in his recent budget for the fiscal year 1957 he proposed $100 million of new federal authority for an indemnity and insurance program under which the cost of flood damage would be carried jointly by the individuals protected, the states, and the federal government.

The current administration plan is embodied in Senate, No. 2862, which was introduced by Senator Bush of Connecticut. This bill would provide a 5-year experimental program of flood indemnity and reinsurance. The individual purchasing the insurance would pay 60 per cent of the premium, the participating State 20 per cent, and the federal government 20 per cent. The program would be handled by a special Federal Flood Indemnity Administration in the 

Housing and Home Finance Agency. The maximum initial liability of the program would be $1.9 billion. Reinsurance up to $100 million would be provided in order to encourage private companies to enter the field. 

Another federal proposal, Senate, No. 3137, by Senator

Lehman and a group of Democratic senators, provides for

1956.] 

a $3 billion program of federal flood insurance and reinsurance as well as for a study of the feasibility of extending coverage to other natural disasters. This bill does not make the federal program contingent upon state co-operation. 

It differs from the administration bill in a number of other details.

Early additional hearings on both bills (and others related to them) are scheduled. But the outlook for federal insurance is still not clear. Although there is strong pressure for the adoption of an experimental federal program of flood insurance, many continue to hold with Commissioner
