TWO I LOVE 

Two is not company, and three is more than a crowd-when two girls are in love with the same man. Jerry and Joan Barbour, twins, discover this when they try to save their small inheritance by opening up a boarding house-and then fall in love with the first paying guest!

Complications even more embarrassing follow, when the twins discover that their boarders are not to be discouraged by the inconveniences of an old, neglected house and a slightly unbalanced caretaker. Mrs. Bianca Gorham, the unwanted but selfstyled chaperon; Kevin Mills, psychologist, on leave from the Army; Honey, a child who makes her own recreation by making mischief for others—all announce their intention of becoming "staying" guests. And they make the girls' first business venture thrilling, if not financially successful.

It is only when the last guest to arrive finds herself lying in a freshly-made grave that the twins begin to consider giving up the whole idea. But they cannot so easily solve their difficulties. Their adventures and misadventures continue throughout the long summer, and are recounted with rare freshness and charm by Ruth Rosemary Corby in "Two I Love." 1

Chapter One 

For all the golden sunlight pouring in from the great windows, the room had a slightly dissipated air. 

Chairs and beds were piled high in a confusion of boxes; trunks, partly filled, stood open in the small space between the twin beds and the bureau; closets gaped open and were forlornly empty. Joan Barbour, on her knees before one of the trunks, shook back a mane of straight blonde hair that fell in satiny smoothness to her shoulders, and groaned. "There ought to be a law against buying something unless you throw something away at the same time," she said sharply. She tried again, without success, to close a trunk drawer on five pairs of shoes, when the drawer was built to hold only four pairs. 9

Although her words were apparently addressed to the empty air, they brought a response from underneath the bed. "Mmm-maybe you're right. Joan, darlinghere are those snowshoes we bought last year. Do you think we ought to take them along?" Jerry Barbour backed out from under the bed and held up the four articles in question. Her blue eyes looked into the identical eyes of her twin; her hair, slightly more disheveled, hung in exactly the same golden curtain. 

It was wonderful to have a twin, Jerry often thought -you knew exactly what you looked like without the bother of gazing into a mirror. But she sometimes felt, privately, that Joan's hair was just a little finer than her own, and that no one could fail to appreciate the fact that Joan's nose was a trifle more tilted, her face slightly more oval. 

Joan, having given up the struggle with the trunk drawer for the moment, sank back on her heels and regarded the snowshoes with distaste. "By all means let's take them," she said with false heartiness. "We never did learn to manage them, and just how useful they'll be at a country house in the middle of New York state in the middle of the summer, is a problem. But do let's take them. 

Let's take everything, and just hope that Cousin Walter DeForest's house is bigger than we remember."

"Poor Joan-you're tired!" 

Jerry was instantly all sympathy for her sister.

She was very conscious of the fact that she was three minutes older than her twin and, she felt, a lot more able to cope with life's difficulties. Joan had been a delicate child for the first two years. For the last sixteen, she had not shown any lack of health and vitality, but Jerry could not shake her conviction that Joan had to be watched over. "Why don't you lie down . . ." she began, when suddenly the doorbell rang-sharply and decisively. 

It was Jerry's turn to look dismayed. "Good heavens!" She glanced down at her smudged blue slacks and crumpled shirt. Joan's outfit was the same, but she always managed to look neater, and at the moment, beside Jerry, her costume was perfectly fresh. 99 "I'll bet it's that old fuss-budget, Bianca Gorham,'

Jerry looked at her sister speculatively. "Want a trade?" she inquired in their usual formula. 

Joan looked up with interest. "What?" she demanded.

"Say good-bye to Mrs. Gorham for me, and I'll finish packing your trunk." "It's a deal." Joan scrambled to her feet and ran down the hall. But before she could reach the door, the bell had already rung again-a long, loud sound. "She would!" Joan thought impatiently.

But her face was composed and smiling as she opened the door.

It was Mrs. Gorham, who stood in the hall outside, smartly dressed in a dark blue suit and small sailor hat. She looked, as indeed she was, a quiet, efficient woman who ran her own life with dispatch, and had plenty of time left over to run the lives of her friends. She smiled now at Joan, with a slightly reserved air, intending to convey the fact that she had been kept waiting before the door was opened. "She never smiles, really," Jerry had once commented. "She just shows her teeth." "Won't you come in, Mrs. Gorham?" Joan tried to put a more cordial invitation in her voice than she felt. "Of course. That's why I came." Mrs. Gorham marched briskly into the living room, devoid now of all the charming gadgets that Aunt Mathilde had once loved. For the first time, Joan saw the woman's face soften, as she looked around. But her small eyes blinked rapidly as she lifted her veil and sat on the one straight-backed chair in the room.

"Are you the one I met this morning?" Mrs. Gorham demanded. "I'm Joan," the girl evaded. "Jerry's simply snowed under with the packing. You know we're driving out to Midlands this afternoon." Joan perched on the arm of a sofa, and gave herself up to the inquisition that was coming. Yes, they were going to what had been their cousin's home.

Outside, Kevin and Joan were waiting, and Joan shouted that it was almost train time. Mrs. Gorham pecked at Jerry's cheek, and simpered as Hugh gave her a resounding smack on the lips. The next minute they had piled into the car and Kevin slammed the door. Honey stuck her head out of the window and shouted back: "When you're married, will you have children?" "Yes, dear." Jerry felt her cheeks flame once more.

The car had started and Honey was dangling from the window in an effort to make another remark. "Maybe you'll have a little girl just like me," she screamed. 

Jerry grinned at Hugh, who shouted after them: "Just like you, Honey. But we'll make it twins !"

Abruptly, Honey was yanked back into the car, and the next minute Jerry and Hugh were alone.
