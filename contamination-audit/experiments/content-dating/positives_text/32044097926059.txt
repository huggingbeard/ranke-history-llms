The Author FOREWORD 

The following analysis of certain aspects of contemporary America, the reader should be warned, devotes the whole of the canvas, so to speak, to the ugly hues! 

Such a treatment naturally lends itself to the charge of partiality, if not of actual distortion. 

While I cheerfully concede the one-sidedness of the argument, I must plead the extenuating circumstance that I deemed it superfluous to undertake an enumera tion of what I regard essentially as exceptions to the basic premises on which I have predicated my conclusions. There are, of course, abundant-and sometimes impressive exceptions to almost every generalization. 

Cambridge, Massachusetts 

December, 1938

K. G. D.

To what extent does contemporary America offer a “modus vivendi❞ for sensitively organized persons-for persons who set some store by good breeding and gra cious manners, æsthetic values and cultural standards, and a decent regard for the observance and enforcement of the law?

In the attempt to answer this query, let us consider first the question of manners, in its more specific, if superficial, aspects. Not only is good breeding con spicuous by its absence, but it is actively precluded by a theory of democracy which proclaims the transcendent merits of the principle of equality. While equalitarianism is a plausible enough philosophy when confined to political or juridical questions, such as "natural rights" and the proper jurisdiction of the law, its current interpretation in this country envisages courtesy and deference as the hallmarks of servility. 

This attitude, which regards with disfavor amounting to suspicion any conspicuous evidences of superior cultivation, accounts in no small measure for the fact that American society is virtually devoid of an aristocratic

class in any true sense of the term. The criteria determining such an aristocracy as exists rest essentially upon a monetary basis. In the emphasis on the acquisi tion of wealth is to be found, moreover, the explanation of the most notable exception to the commonly accepted code of manners-namely, the artificial ritual of politeness governing the personnel of the larger, more up-todate business houses, which is motivated, of course, purely by financial expediency. 

Let us survey some of the more obvious symptoms of the phenomenon. A pedestrian or a motorist, for instance, seeking guidance as to his route, accosts a wayfarer. As a rule, he neither greets the person addressed nor deprecates the interruption. One is, indeed, fortunate not to be hailed with a brusque "Hey, Jack! 

How do I get to -?" The proffered information is usually rewarded either with silence or with a cavalier "O.K." or "I get you." Particularly deplorable, it seems to me, is the ignorance of the rudiments of the social amenities exhibited by children in addressing adult strangers. On countless occasions youngsters have stopped me to ask the time of day. The invariable form of the inquiry is, "Mister, what time is it?" I can recall few instances, if any, where the request has been ac companied by even the barest elements of politeness, to say nothing of the hesitation and deference which, in like circumstances, still mark the behavior of provincial 

English children.

Akin to this unmannerliness is that of the general run of store customers, with their boorish "Gimme a loaf of Bond bread" or "I'll take one of these radio tubes." Even an act of pure accommodation, such as supplying one at a drugstore with postage stamps or with change for the telephone, is likely to be demanded, as a right, and accordingly unrequited by an acknowledgment of the favor conferred. Conversely, a purchase frequently fails to elicit any thanks; and formulas of greeting-unless the customer is a familiar patron, or is personally acquainted with the salesman-are prac tically unheard of. I often wonder how so negative a conception of the amenities strikes the French or German visitor, accustomed to such expressions of courtesy as "Bonjour, monsieur. Merci beaucoup"; "Guten Tag. 

Besten Dank"; "Adieu. Danke vielmals"; and their equivalents.

Again, one observes the reluctance of men on this side of the Atlantic-including those who have enjoyed special social advantages to doff their hats as a token of respect in greeting ladies. Deliverymen and workmen ordinarily fail to remove their hats on entering the home of a patron or employer, remaining covered even in the presence of the mistress of the house. The same insouciance applies to business executives as they pass through an office occupied almost exclusively by women. 

And such a mark of deference as the inclination of the body in saluting the fair sex has become as extinct as the proverbial dodo!

Then there is the ubiquitous practice of gum-chewing in public places. Addicts of this habit do not hesitate to subject fellow passengers in crowded streetcars or subway trains to the disgusting spectacle of a wad of chicle displayed on the tip of the tongue, or to the crepitating sounds incident to the rhythmic process of mastication!

Suppose we shift the scene to the theater, cinema parlor, or opera house. A party of theater fans, entering the auditorium after the curtain has risen, brush unceremoniously past those who are already seated, expressing no regret either for requiring them to rise or for intercepting their view of the stage. At the operaduring the ravishing strains, we will say, of the overture to Lohengrin-a group of belated patrons make their noisy irruption, to the accompaniment of swishing garments and a staccato cross-fire of sibilant ejaculations, and, as they take their places, add insult to injury by slamming down their seats with a resounding bang. 

Those seriously intent on following the program, whether it be a "movie" or legitimate drama, are harassedin addition to strident conversation-by intermittent guffaws provoked by horse-play and inanities, risqué allusions, and even pathos and downright tragedy; by the rattle of pop-corn bags; and by similar distractions. 

That the direct responsibility for such incidents rests with a slender minority is beside the point; for the very fact that they generally pass unrebuked serves to indict the entire audience.

Contrast this situation with the decorum which prevailed in the better class of pre-Nazi German and

Austrian theaters! I recall, to cite a typical case, the impressions gleaned during a performance that I once attended at the Hofburg-Theater in Vienna. Just as at the Wagner festivals at Bayreuth those who are not in their places when the overture commences are excluded until the end of the first act, so here latecomers may not enter between the rising and the falling of the curtain. Throughout the whole course of the playsuch was the rapt attention of the audience-one could positively have heard a pin fall! And when the curtain marked the completion of an act, the spectators, instead of rushing to the corridors or lobby to smoke and chatter, remained with barely an exception in their seats. 

So far we have confined our attention, for the most part, to the relatively negative aspects of the question. 

But there is a positive phase, which cannot be ignored without seriously distorting the picture-namely, the intolerance of children and young people toward whatever savors of nonconformity to the mores of the masses. 

It is my custom to carry a green-baize bookbag (inherited from my Harvard days) and, on occasion, to wield a walking-stick. I happen, also, to wear a mustache which is not of the Charlie Chaplin type. As a result of these eccentricities I have repeatedly been hailed with jeers and cat-calls. Indeed, I have more than once found my self the target of sundry more substantial missiles! The

plainest inference to be drawn from these manifestations of rowdyism, I submit, is a want of proper training of the young. Parents-in line with one of the grosser aberrations of the Freudian school of juvenile pedagogy -fail to realize that children, if not actual sadists, are congenitally endowed with the instincts and impulses of primitive man and the higher animals, and that they attain the stature of civilized human beings only through a process of education and discipline which emphasizes the need for restraint and inhibition.

II 

This brings us to the related, though broader and more complex, question of taste and general cultural standards. We may dismiss at the outset any estimates based on statistical data,-concerning such matters as highschool and college enrollment, the multiplicity of public libraries, the lavish endowment of symphony orchestras and grand-opera organizations, and the popularity of week-end motor jaunts into the country districts,—directing our attention, rather, to such qualitative indices as the intellectual and recreational interests of the masses, their reaction to nature, their æsthetic capacity, and the like.

It is highly instructive to note the predilections of the "man on the street" as reflected by his selection of reading-matter from the varied topics purveyed by the daily press. The overwhelming majority of newspaper

readers ignore virtually every feature save the sporting news and the moronic “comic strip." The mental caliber of most of the remainder admits, apparently, only such additional items as domestic or political scandals, murder cases, and serial stories marked by sensationalism or trashy sentimentalism. Taking a wider view of the situation, one may safely affirm that the rank and file are little concerned-aside from the items just enumer ated-with anything except professional sport (shared, for the most part, vicariously), the more blatant, vulgar, or banal radio offerings, the cinema, the species of enter tainment erroneously styled "musical comedy" (for in any true sense it is neither music nor comedy!), jazz and "swing," and "joy-riding."

A partial explanation of this attitude is to be found, I believe, in a phenomenon which in due course we shall examine more closely. Anyone familiar with the more highly civilized European and Oriental peoples is struck by the feverish, almost pathological restlessness that impels the average American, even in his moments of potential leisure, to unflagging physical activity. The instinctive basis of this disposition lies in an irrepressible craving for sensation, partly demanded as an offset to the stultifying effects of a specialized industrial and business routine, and partly inherent in a way of life which, in default of inner resources, of any capacity for detached observation and contemplation, perforce relies solely upon physical stimulation.

Let us now survey in more or less detail some of the principal recreational interests of the public. Conspicu ous in any representative cross section is the texture of our radio programs. Apart from a few admirable Sunday features (including classical music), some notable week-day musical numbers, the distinguished interpreta tions of current events by publicists of the caliber of 

H. V. Kaltenborn, sporadic political pronouncements 

(such as President Roosevelt's "fireside chats"), and occasional book reviews or dramatizations of novels, radio offerings are restricted very largely to a fatuous, vulgar, and not infrequently degenerate potpourri alike insulting to the intelligence and offensive to the most rudimentary æsthetic sense. Probably ninety-five per cent of the "musical" material consists of jazz, whose disintegrating rhythms and bestial noise might well bring the blush of shame to the cheek of the aborigines of the African jungle! 

Incidentally, the institution of broadcasting throws a significant sidelight upon cultural values irrespective of the composition of the programs themselves. I refer to its predominant emphasis upon salesmanship. Leav ing out of account the exaggerated and sometimes spurious claims advanced for many of the products advertised through this medium, what shall be said of its menace to the basic requirements of a well-poised mind and a sound nervous constitution? It would be a truism to point out that the accelerating tempo of commercial 10

and industrial life, and the increasing obsession of business-mindedness, demand recurrent periods of mental and nervous recuperation during which one's thoughts and emotions may be directed along channels as remote as possible from those that claim the energies of the daily vocational routine. Unfortunately, one can no longer elude the insistent appeal of commercialism through a week-end escape to the country; for one is confronted at every turn by the high-power salesmanship of billboards. Similarly, on the streets of our cities not even the advent of nightfall brings any appreciable relief, owing to the impact of illuminated advertisements. And now the final bulwark against the onslaught of "Business first"-the home-has collapsed under the assaults of our radio broadcasters, prophets of the sav ing graces of dental pastes, panaceas ranging from cough remedies to specifics against "the gray dawn of the morn ing after," breakfast foods, cosmetics, cigarettes, et alia!

It is hard to conceive of a more revealing symptom of our sense of values and general philosophy of life than our toleration of so flagrant an invasion of the home, at all hours of the day and night, by the spokesmen of an economic system which finds the raison d'être of human existence in the quest of profits! 

It may be contended that the foregoing strictures ignore the upper strata of society, reflecting merely the predilections of the masses. Let us, accordingly, vary the picture by reference to one or two of the types of 11

entertainment which cater principally to the "intelligentsia," such as symphonic music and travel lectures. 

In the front rank of our orchestral organizations stands the Boston Symphony Orchestra. Though the vast auditorium is regularly packed to capacity during the annual series of Saturday evening concerts, the motive force which accounts for such an assemblage is to be found preeminently in the fact that these concerts are regarded as a sort of "institution," attendance at which is looked upon as a hallmark of social superiority. The absence of genuine discrimination on the part of the audience is all too clearly evinced by their equally stereotyped response to authentic music and to the most revolting specimens of modernistic cacophony. It is further suggested by the average tone of the conversa tion which one overhears in the corridors during the intermission. The interest presumably uppermost in the minds of the patrons either is non-existent or is reflected by casual, perfunctory remarks purely conventional in character. 

Similar testimony is furnished by the triviality and banality that pervade even the better class of travelogues, such as the illustrated lectures of Burton Holmes.

The pictures, to be sure, are on the whole admirable, and the commentary seldom strays beyond the limits imposed by the screen spectacle. But how palpable is the speaker's effort to hold the attention of his hearers by lending a factitious importance to every trace of 12

narrative or personal interest! This solicitude to amuse is becoming increasingly apparent in the technique and substance of the pictures themselves—in the heightened vogue of the "close-up," and the incorporation of mo tion pictures as an essential ingredient of the screen program. As John L. Stoddard, the late dean of Ameri can travel lecturers, pointed out a few years ago (in a conversation with me after his retirement to Meran, in 

South Tyrol), the retrogression of the travelogue in the

States during the past generation is attributable chiefly to the restiveness of audiences called upon to lend concentrated attention to text or pictures unrelieved by a substantial narrative or personal element, or calculated to instruct or to instill appreciation of the finer values of foreign travel.

A fairly reliable test of the cultural capacity both of the rank and file of the population and of the more highly educated strata is afforded by the reactions of sightseers abroad. I have had many opportunities to compare my fellow countrymen in this respect with Europeans, and have been rather painfully impressed by the relatively restricted range of their interests. Whereas the average German, for example, displays the liveliest response to the appeal of the landscape, the flora and fauna, architecture and art, history, geography, geology, and so forth, discussing these topics with the keenest zest and intelligence, the conversation of most of my compatriots, unfortunately, betrays a poverty of 13

inner resources which renders them incapable of appre ciating anything save the more material, superficial, or ephemeral features, such as the hotels and shops, the mechanical equipment, traffic conditions, and the eccentricities or colorfulness of the national manners and customs. 

It is appropriate at this point to turn to the broad field of recreation represented by the world of nature. 

The younger generation, it seems, is rapidly losing its capacity for the appreciation of nature-ironically enough, in almost direct ratio to the growing use of automobiles for excursions into the rural districts! The country, including the mountains, still attracts, to be sure, its quota of lip worshipers. Golf commands a following of robust proportions; "hiking" is still rated,

I believe, as a favorite pastime; and the motor mania has placed us in the forefront of excursionists the world over. But suppose we look a bit beneath the surface and examine the actual determinants of these forms of diversion.

Golf may be safely dismissed as a relatively pure phenomenon of sport, with landscape as a decorative adjunct. For who would deny that the average mind is so constituted that concentration upon a competitive game leaves scant room for æsthetic motivation? As to hiking. Will anyone have the hardihood to declare that the enthusiasm underlying this activity roots primarily in the instinct of communion with nature-in the craving 14

for more intimate acquaintance with its subtle beauties, its varied moods, its complex juxtaposition of detail? Is not the determining motive of those who participate in such enterprises, rather, the instinct of gregariousness, the opportunity for displaying their physical prowess, or the lust for adventure? Several years ago my atten tion was drawn to a symptomatic case-that of a young woman (living in southern California) who, though passionately fond of mountain-climbing, disliked venturing into the wilds alone. For many months, I was informed, she had been unable to indulge her propensities in this direction because she could find no companion of like tastes.

With automobiling we reach the reductio ad absurdum of the claim for landscape appreciation based on mere physical propinquity. I readily concede the occasional advantages to be derived from this means of locomotion. 

Where nature operates, so to speak, in large, more or less undifferentiated units, as in the case of far-flung mountain ranges, broad expanses of woods and plain, sweeping vistas of sea and shoreline, the motorist, proceeding at a moderate rate of speed, may, indeed, satisfactorily embrace the essential features of the view. But let us turn from this relatively restricted sphere to that of the average automobilist, say, of New England.

Until recent years I was in the habit of spending a part of my summer or autumn holidays in Massachu setts, along the North Shore and in the Berkshires. In 15

my rambles through these regions I found myself, as a pedestrian, wholly without a competitor—in fact, a last survivor of an otherwise extinct species. Nay, more. I had begun to entertain some doubt as to my sanity; for 

I observed that a goodly portion of my motor-minded compatriots were unable to repress a sense of amusement at the spectacle afforded by such an "original." Eventu ally, owing to the monopoly of the highways by motor cars,* I was forced to betake myself either to the Canadian Rockies or to Europe, where devotees of the art of walking for health or for pleasure are not yet suspected of belonging to the "lunatic fringe"!

Now how is one to interpret the exclusive vogue of motoring and the concomitant contempt for the plodding pedestrian? I reiterate the question raised in connection with hiking: Is the excursionist primarily or even substantially concerned with the beauty of the landscape? The material obstacle, on the one hand, to its intelligent observation and just appraisal imposed by the conventional means of locomotion and, on the other, the immeasurably superior advantage attached to the leisurely progress of the traveler afoot compel an an swer in the negative. What is the conclusion to which the foregoing data clearly point? It is this. The world of nature, in so far as it concerns the excursionist, represents largely but a framework of space which constitutes an appropriate theater of action-action whose cardinal motives are to be sought principally in gregariousness, rivalry, the love of adventure, and the exhilaration arising from the sense of speed and motion.

III 

The sporadic references to the civilization of the European continent perhaps justify a digression at this point for the sake of assessing a few of the relative cultural advantages of Europe. (The qualifying adjec tive "cultural," I trust, precludes our superlative me chanical equipment and other material conveniences, such as central heating, bathtubs, elevators, and that indispensable asset-ice water!)

The merits of the older civilization from the standpoint of its superior antiquity and historical appeal, as well as its greater wealth of architectural monuments and art treasures, are almost universally conceded. Less stress is placed upon the pervasiveness of the European feeling for beauty and the European sense of repose. 

We have already noted the defacement of our cities and rural districts. The comparative genius of the respective cultures for transmuting raw materials, so to 19

speak, into æsthetic values is aptly illustrated by the following passage from an article in The Nation (of 

June 7, 1933), by Martha Gruening, entitled "American Expatriates." Speaking of the Seine, the Rhone, the 

Neckar, and other Continental streams, she observes: "The Hudson is really. a nobler river than any of them, with greater possibilities of beauty; but in no 

European city that I know could it be disfigured by so much that is ugly, sordid, and chaotic as it is in New York. Lakes and rivers are used commercially abroad, but they remain an intimate and lovely part of the lives of the cities situated on them. What one remembers when one thinks of the Seine is not belching factory chimneys and railroad sidings and billboards, but lovely weathered gray-stone parapets and bridges, trees, and Parisians fishing from the quays and from little row. boats moored in the shadows of the bridges." Elsewhere she exclaims, "One likes to walk in the streets of these 

European cities," and asks, "Is walking in any American city still a pleasure?”

As regards the use of potential leisure. The American, if gainfully employed (and in the States, as the late Elbert Hubbard once reminded an English visitor, such a "leisure class" as we have consists largely of hobos), hastily gulps, during his brief noonday breathing-spell, a cup of coffee and a sandwich, and rushes back to his work, whereas his European cousin spends a comfortable hour or two on the "terrace" of a sidewalk café, or 20

beneath a vine-draped pergola in some secluded open-air restaurant, engaged in urbane conversation (a lost art, by the way, in this country!) over a glass of wine or beer. Is the American really more pressed for time?

On the contrary, he manages to find abundant oppor tunity to indulge the interests closest to his heart, the sole difference being that those interests presuppose, either objectively or subjectively, physical action-the giddy progress of a motor jaunt; vicarious participation in the clash of rival sporting teams, or in the encounter of gunmen in a "Wild West" movie show; the riotous pleasures of a night club or roadhouse; and the like. 

The same general disposition perhaps accounts to some extent for the bleak, inhospitable aspect of our lake-side and river esplanades, in distinction from the gracious reposefulness and mellow beauty of their Old 

World prototypes, such as the charming promenades, overhung by thick-foliaged planes and chestnut trees and bordered by vine-clad seats, which skirt the shores of the Swiss and north-Italian lakes and the banks of many a Continental river.

Similarly, the European knows how to extract the true flavor of the countryside. Just as the relative absence of automobiles makes it possible for pedestrians actually to enjoy a stroll through the city streets (with the exception of some of the metropolises), so also in the country, apart from those regions invaded by American motorists, the opportunities for bicycling, 21

hiking, and equivalent expressions of the Wanderlust grant full scope for the tastes of the connoisseur of nature and the lover of the picturesque in its most varied manifestations.

Finally, a comparison of the two cultures suggests the higher valuation placed by most of the western- European nations on personal privacy, a desideratum scarcely recognized on this side of the Atlantic! The denial of the right of those who, by reason of wealth or other circumstances, are in the limelight to enjoy reasonable immunity from the prying eyes of the public and the inquisitiveness of press reporters has at last been brought home to the national consciousness through the unhappy experience of the Lindberghs, culminating in the abandonment of their native land in quest of an asylum in England. But even the humbler citizenunless he is stout-hearted enough to defy the disappro bation of his neighbors must forgo, for instance, the seclusion afforded by a high garden wall lest such a barrier be regarded as a mark of snobbishness or unsociability.

IV 

Any inquiry into taste and cultural standards would be incomplete without a few observations concerning American speech. (The subject is, of course, far too complex and technical to admit here of more than passing notice.) The progressive deterioration of diction 22

in respect to dignity, purity, copiousness, and general excellence-as revealed by a comparison of eighteenthand twentieth-century letters, sermons, political documents, and the like-is too familiar to call for special emphasis. Moreover, the increasing inroads of slangwhich often robs the vehicle of expression of much of its capacity for communicating definite, clear-cut ideas -are by no means confined to the States, being equally well attested by current English, French, and German usage. But the decadence of speech in this country has now reached the point where simple, basic formulas of long-established currency are being rapidly crowded out by locutions savoring of sheer puerility. Thus, the timehonored "Good-by" is being supplanted by the silly "See you later"; and "Good morning" and "You're welcome" are beginning to give way to the meaningless "Yes, sir."

This trend is doubtless related to the general impov erishment of vocabulary observable among even the graduates of our high schools and colleges, and, indeed, the members of the teaching profession. The paucity of linguistic resources in question is instantly suggested, for example, by the juvenile character of the explanatory footnotes and glossaries provided by editions of English classics designed for school use, as well as by the diction of a not inconsiderable percentage of our textbook writers. A cursory perusal of the latter discloses the 23

disillusioning fact that many of our leading "educators" are notoriously ignorant of the proper meaning of multitudes of the terms which they employ, including words whose signification one would presume to be thor oughly familiar to a high-school sophomore. (Ironically enough, the worst offenders among our academicians seem to belong to the departments of English literature and composition and of pedagogy!)

Almost equally deplorable is the universal slovenli ness of enunciation and articulation. In isolated affirma tions "Yes" has become well-nigh obsolete, having been superseded by "Yeah." Especially striking is the virtual suppression of final mutes. It is no accident that many stenographers spell "statute" the same as "statue," or that in the correspondence of the semi-literate the past tense of weak verbs like "ask" and "beg" is identical with the infinitive; for the orthography employed by such persons is based largely upon the spoken form of the word. It requires but a modicum of introspection to establish the fact that one actually hears only a small proportion of the syllables of the average conversation, eking out the expressed meaning by subconscious deduction from the context. Although this conjectural process may serve tolerably well for ordinary purposes (unless an unfamiliar proper name is in ques tion), it fails lamentably when the subject matter calls for incisive reasoning, where the misapprehension of a given word or phrase may easily befog the entire issue. 24

V The question of noise next calls for specific treatment, as one of the cardinal aspects of American life—a phenomenon which sheds copious light alike upon cur rent manners and standards of taste and upon the prevailing attitude toward law observance and civic rights. 

In the first place, we are justly reputed to be at once a noisy and a noise-loving people-a distinction that formerly belonged preeminently to the Italians. This characterization is supported by a wide range of data. 

Witness the tenacious vogue of jazz, and, among even the élite of the concert hall, the growing popularity of formless, cacophonous tone fabrics whose primary ap peal is to be found in the vigor and insolence of their onslaught upon the ears of the listener. Still more to the point is the wide-spread practice of exploiting to the full the dynamic resources of loud-speakers, as well as the increasing tendency to use the radio to supply a continuous tonal background for the domestic routine and for the transactions of the smaller commercial estab lishments (notably, shoe-shining parlors, barber shops, drugstores, and cafés), and, latterly, as a source of diver sion or a soporific for motorists engaged in threading their way through the maze of metropolitan traffic or seated in cars parked by the roadside! Again, one re calls the testimony of foreigners respecting the tastes of 

American travelers. At Lake Louise, in the heart of the

Canadian Rockies, I have been told that tourists from 25

the States, finding themselves encompassed by the spacious solitude of great mountain sanctuaries, are wont to complain of the depressing effect of the unbroken silence!

Secondly, this matter of noise-paradoxical as the contention may appear on the surface-discloses both the obtuse selfishness and lawlessness of the citizenry and their seemingly spineless acquiescence in the violation of their most essential rights. Consider such inexcusable disturbances as the blaring din of midnight radio programs a medley of raucous jazz and strident vocalization; the penetrating yelps and howls of dogs prowling beneath one's chamber windows during the "wee small hours"; and the persistent honking of motor car horns, on sweltering midsummer nights, apprizing late callers that their automobile is awaiting them.* 

According to the time-honored Anglo-Saxon tradi tion, "one's home is his castle." This principle has here tofore been generally applied through statutes directed against the undue invasion of one's domestic privacy. 

Moreover, the budget of the average home-owner or tenant, as everyone knows, includes a heavy outlay for the privilege of residence in an urban center. Is he not thereby-if for no other reason-entitled to live his normal life, without the distraction of unnecessary as *Anyone unfamiliar with the singular aversion of Americans, since the advent of the motor car, to the use of their legs would assume that they would find it more convenient to alight and ring the doorbell! 26

He may perchance seek a momentary escape from business or financial anxieties by con centrating on a novel or a magazine article, or may wish to devote himself, without unseemly interruptions, to social correspondence. Again, he may be intent on a leisurely interchange of ideas with members of his family. Indeed, there are still a few persons (strange survivals from the pre-war period) who now and then crave an opportunity for reflection!

A truly civilized society, of course, would insisteither through appropriate legislation, adequately enforced, or through the pressure of public opinion-that canines whose propensity to bark is not amenable to training should be kept indoors or else disposed of. 

Above all, it would decree that those who operate radio receiving-sets (save in isolated places) should use ear attachments, or, at least, should reduce the volume to that essential for reasonably clear audition within the limits of the room. Particularly does this apply to hospitals, where patients suffering from grave physical and nervous shock are sometimes driven almost to madness by loud-speakers installed or permitted by the hospital authorities themselves! One such victim, summing up his experience in a letter to the press, recently characterized the radio as "the most refined instrument of torture that has ever been invented."

Neither regard for ordinary decency on the part of the body politic nor a sense of responsibility on the part 27

of the police ensures, however, any appreciable protec tion against the abuses in question. The callousness of the vast majority of the former renders them insensible to the most tactful and reasonable appeals; and the nonchalance of the latter precludes, as a rule, any hope of redress, even where the law actually provides relief, unless the complainant enjoys the advantage of wealth, political pull, conspicuous social prestige, or personal acquaintance with the law-enforcement authorities. 

Let us now analyze in some detail the paradoxical attitude of the victims-that slender and fast-dwindling minority whose nervous organization rebels against such disturbances. What accounts for their apparent supine indifference in the face of these indisputable inroads upon their comfort and peace of mind and (particularly as a result of loss of sleep) upon their health and their business or professional efficiency? This attitude, it seems to me, is less a question of one's immediate reaction to certain definite physical stimuli than of one's response to the total situation conceived in social terms. 

One may experience a keen sense of annoyance; may even, privately, express vehement disapproval. But his reaction seldom goes any farther. Let an apostle of civic reform, supported by a municipal ordinance, approach him with the suggestion that he sign a memorial addressed to the duly constituted authorities. No matter how modest the scope, how scrupulously courteous the tone, of the petition, in nine cases out of ten he declines, 28

on one pretext or another, to become a signatory—nay, perhaps denies the very existence of the grievance itself!

Have the heirs of the glorious traditions of 1776 reversed the order of evolution, and degenerated into invertebrates incapable of asserting their erstwhile inalienable rights? Such a conclusion, I confess, I have found it difficult, at times, to resist. But long observation of our folk psychology has convinced me that the principal explanation is to be sought, rather, in our social philosophy. The dominant note of this creed is license, masquerading, to be sure, in the guise of "liberty,"—that is, complete freedom of action for all who conform to the "mores" and to such statutes (chiefly those applying to felonies) as command prac tically universal respect. In a word, according to this doctrine, -a quasi-anarchistic interpretation of the equalitarianism which lies at the heart of our theory of democracy, - everyone may, within the broad limits permitted by the folkways and the criminal code, follow his bent without let or hindrance, even though in so doing he treads on his neighbors' toes, provided he concédes his neighbors the same privilege!

This conception naturally leads to a further reversal of perspective, in which any protest against civic abuses tends to be regarded as antisocial, the complainant being accordingly branded as a heretic and subjected, as such, to the severest displeasure of his fellows. Two personal experiences may serve to clarify the point. Some years 29

ago, in conjunction with one of the most highly respected members of the community, I headed a petition directed against a practice which violated both the canons of common decency and a specific municipal ordinance. The offender, on getting wind of the pe tition, was seized with virtuous indignation, uttering dire threats of "running out of town" the leading protestants. More recently, having long suffered from the ear piercing outbursts of a vicious dog, I donned my dressing-gown at about 2 A. M., hastened to the scene of the disturbance, and hailed the rabid beast in stentorian tones, in the naïve hope that my remon strances might shame the owner into abating the nuisance. Eventually I succeeded in rousing him. Did he take the dog indoors, or even try to quiet it? Far from it! Instead, a window was raised, and I heard the master declare to a member of his household that someone was disturbing the animal and that he had better summon the police!

VI This widespread disregard for one's fellows suggests a kindred topic: the prevailing unreliability in the matter of appointments and other commitments unenforced by a legal contract - an attitude compounded of indif ference alike to ethical sanctions and to traditional standards of punctilio and good breeding. 30

During the past decade, as a householder resident in one of New England's foremost university centers, I have had dealings with many of the leading merchants, servicemen, and realty operators. In perhaps fifty per cent of such contacts I have found the word of presumably reliable firms the equivalent of a mere "scrap of paper." Thus, a steam fitter is employed to install an instantaneous heater. After the payment of the bill his work proves unsatisfactory. Complaints elicit a tardy and reluctant promise to make good the defect. 

Weeks elapse, and repeated representations produce no material results.

Again, in anticipation of remodeling the house, the appropriate artisans are engaged, in the order of their respective services. One or more fail to appear at the appointed time, with the result that the preëstablished sequence is disrupted and that certain stages of the work are completely paralyzed. The employer, as likely as not, is neither notified in advance nor accorded the courtesy of a post-facto explanation. 

To multiply instances, one may decide to place his home on the market. He makes an arrangement with a leading "realtor" to examine the property at a speci fied hour, incidentally declining interviews with other agents which conflict with this appointment. In addition, he bestirs himself to make the ménage as ship-shape as possible, so that it may produce the most favorable 31

impression. The sequel, in a large percentage of cases, is the same old story of irresponsibility-the repudiation of the sanctity of the plighted word!

VII 

There remains the crucial consideration of the observ ance and enforcement of the law. (A thoroughgoing analysis of the problem-rooting, as it does, in an intricate nexus of historical, economic, political, juridical, and psychological factors, including the traditional overemphasis upon civic rights and liberties, in distinction from civic duties and obligations-obviously lies outside the scope of a non-technical survey such as the one in hand.) The national vice of lawlessness constitutes one of the most alarming and notorious aspects of contemporary American life. Notwithstanding the hypocritical election slogan of "law and order," it is a disease which gnaws at the very vitals of the social organism. It vitiates every stratum of society, involving neighborhood relations; political life (from the lowest ward politician to the most highly placed Federal official); the functioning of the police system; commercial, industrial, and financial operations; and a host of other activities. Indeed, it has achieved for us the invidious distinction of being the most anarchistic and criminally-disposed people on the face of the earth, particularly since the emergence of its two latest phases: kidnaping for ransom - a form of blackmail generally culminating in the 32

murder of the immediate victim-and racketeering, an equally parasitical practice which, if unchecked, will eventually reduce the masses to the virtual status of vassals forced to pay tribute to a twentieth-century reincarnation of the medieval robber barons. Certain portents, happily, suggest that both these nefarious practices are now on the wane. If, on the other hand, this decline proves illusory, even the most confirmed optimist must ere long abandon all hope for the future of our social "order"! 33
