Chapter I EARLY FRIENDS HISTORY 

The England of the seventeenth century was characterized by religious and political strife. Catholics strove to regain lost supremacy. The State church sought both religious and political supremacy-while within its own organization a battle raged between Episcopalians and Presbyterians for control.

Meanwhile the nonconformist sects struggled against dictation by both church and state. When any one of these groups came into power, it was as heartless and overbearing as its predecessors. As a result of these conditions, little companies of persons drew together in different parts of England and Scotland seeking peace, wearied with theological disputation, offended by the High Church clergy and by the rigid external religion of Puritan professors. The way was prepared for the rise of a church that should bear testimony to the spiritual nature of the kingdom of Christ.* Only an able leader was needed. In George Fox was found such a man. 

George Fox was born July, 1624, in Leicestershire, England. As a youth he had a "gravity and stayedness of mind and spirit, unusual in children," and from eleven years of age "knew pureness and righteousness." At nineteen Fox entered a period of three years of spiritual conflicts in which he was often in despair at the wickedness existing in the world at large. Bewildered, he wandered from place to place in search for rest of soul. He sought spiritual help from priests and from the most learned religious men, but they could give him none. One bade him "take tobacco and sing Psalms." His relatives advised him to get married, and some suggested he join an army band. Despairing of human aid. he relied more and more on "walking in solitary places" and reading his Bible. Finally in the year 1647 he emerged from the gloom. In his words: "When all my hopes in men were gone. so that I had nothing outwardly to help me, nor could I tell what to do; then, oh! then I heard a voice which said, "There is one, even Christ Jesus, that can speak to thy condition', and when I heard it, my heart did leap for joy."** 

With this joy in his heart Fox entered his ministry, in 1647, commissioned to bring back to the church the truth that all men may know salvation; not through priests or outward forms, but through the Spirit which would lead them into all truth. He called men from "Forms and shadows" to

The radical truth which he and his followers preached was the bringing of men immediately to Christ.***

Converts were made from the first as he traveled first in the northern counties, and by 1654 over England, Scotland and Ireland. These adherents were like Fox, filled with zeal to spread the glad tidings. In 1654 there were 60 ministers traveling over the land. By 1660 Germany, America, Florence, Mantua, Palestine, Tucanny, Italy, Rome, Turkey, Jerusalem, France, Geneva, Norway, Barbadoes, Bermuda, Antigua, Surinam, Jamaica, and Newfoundland are mentioned as having been visited. 

As the number of converts increased in England they were gathered into groups who were at first called "Children of Light" but later "Friends." The adhesion of Margaret 

Fell, of Swarthmoor Hall, was a wonderful support to the movement. Her house became the headquarters of the missionary band. Traveling ministers often sought her advice and communicated with each other through Swarthmoor Hall.

Accompanying the spiritual teaching of these ministers was the practical testimony against war, oaths, tithes, the refusal to take off their hats to any man, use of the simple address "thee" and "thou," and the acceptance of women ministers. Much suffering was endured in upholding these testimonies for the adherents were persecuted from the beginning. During the period of the Commonwealth and the 

Restoration following it the Friends struggled for their very existence. During the reign of Charles II 13,562 were imprisoned, 198 were transported beyond the seas, and 338 died in prison or of wounds received in violent assaults on their meetings.* These sufferings were endured patiently and nowhere was the fire of spiritual enthusiasm quenched. 

The first recorded visit of Quakers to America was that of Mary Fisher and Ann Austin to Boston in 1656. They were put in prison and their books burned; then they were stripped and subjected to an examination for marks of witches, and finally deported to Barbadoes.** 

Laws were immediately passed against the "cursed sect", but a few kept coming in spite of persecution, so in 1658 a law was passed providing for the death penalty for return after banishment. Four Quakers, William Robinson, Marmaduke 

Stephenson, William Leddra, and Mary. Dyer met this challenge and gave their lives. A restraining order from Charles

Meanwhile other Friends were crossing the Atlantic. 

Elizabeth Harris made converts in Maryland in 1656. Others followed into Maryland, then to Virginia and the Carolinas. Rhode Island and New Netherlands were also entered. In 1672 Fox made his first visit to America, traveling extensively, encouraging the groups, winning new converts, and preaching to the Indians. It was said that the number of the society in Virginia was doubled through George Fox's preaching, many of the prominent colonists being converted.** 

In all these colonies except Rhode Island they suffered much persecution. But with the year 1674 came the first glimpse of a better day. In that year West Jersey was purchased by two Quakers, John Fenwick and Edward Billinge.

Soon the Quaker colonists were coming to a land which they could call their own, free from persecution. In 1681 William

Penn, who had become a Friend in 1667, received from James II the grant of Pennsylvania Colony in settlement of a debt owed his father. Penn set out to make a "holy experiment", a refuge where all persecuted people might find freedom. Quakers began to come in 1681 and soon Pennsylvania constituted the largest Quaker colony in America. 

By the year 1700 Friends meetings had been established in all the colonies. According to Russell, "Friends owned or had recently owned the Jerseys, Pennsylvania and Delaware; they had the controlling influence in Rhode Island and the Carolinas; they had considerable influence in New 

York and in Maryland, and they had won the struggle for toleration in New England and Virginia.”*** This consti tuted the height of their power. Although they grew in number from about 30,000 in 1700 to about 50,000 in 1750, the number of non-Quakers increased more rapidly, and by 1754 the Friends had ceased to control even Pennsylvania. 

The turn of the century had brought the beginning of a new period in the history of Quakerism. As has been pointed out, the first fifty years of existence had been characterized by an aggressive missionary spirit with much suffering from persecution. With the cessation of persecution and great increase in members there came a decline in spiritual life. 

The tendency appeared to run to form rather than "abide in the power and life." Rigid adherence to the rules of ** Thomas, History of the Society of Friends in America, p. 219. 

*** Russell, Elbert, The History of Quakerism, p. 124.

order began to be required. The practice of expulsion for marrying non-members appeared-also the severe rules in regard to dress and language. The system of "birthright membership" became fixed upon the society. "Thus was the Society of Friends changed from a church of believers to a corporation or association of persons some of whom always would be among the unconverted."*

Silence in the worship services became the order, for only "in the silence of all flesh" could God make himself heard. There arose a fear of "creaturely activity" which discouraged any action or speaking except by immediate and definite "leading."

These were the characteristics of the "middle ages" of 

Quakerism,** 1700-1827. The dependence on forms, the lack of doctrinal knowledge and reading of the Bible, and low spiritual life provided a fertile field for the introduction of rationalism that led to the schism of 1827. 

Elias Hicks began preaching about the beginning of the nineteenth century. As early as 1805 Stephen Grellet had "labored with him" about his unsound doctrine. Hicks reasoned that, since there is an inner light in every person that it is sufficient for salvation. Therefore, to him, the coming and work of Jesus Christ in the flesh, the scriptures, and all outward teachings are in no sense essential. He gained a large following by his traveling ministry and aroused the opposition of others. Amid strong personal feelings on both sides a separation was effected in Philadelphia, later spreading to New York, Ohio, Indiana, and Baltimore. The new yearly meetings set up by the followers of Hicks have received the name "Hicksite."

One result of this event was that the orthodox began a more intensive study of their doctrinal foundations. They restated definitely their belief in the doctrines as presented by George Fox and Robert Barclay. A new interest in Bible reading was manifest. A revival of ministerial activity brought a spiritual impetus to the society through the preaching of such men as Stephen Grellet and Joseph John Gurney.

The change in emphasis and new methods adopted led to minor separations of the Conservatives in 1850-1881, called the Wilburite separations. 

In the latter half of the century, Friends experienced a great revival which restored to them the missionary and evangelistic spirit that had characterized the early years. 

Dead churches were revived, new ones established, converts made. Foreign missions were organized and received en- * Thomas, History of the Society of Friends in America, p. 241! ** The term "middle ages of Quakerism" is used by Thomas, Russell, and other

One of the most important characteristics of the history of American Quakerism is pioneering. This fact is of great importance in understanding the history of Kansas Yearly 

Meeting. 

Beginning about 1725 came a general migration of certain Friends from New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and New England southward into Virginia, North and South Carolina, and Georgia. Later in the same century this migration suddenly stopped, probably because of the shifting of the Revolutionary War to the South. They then turned their faces westward. By the time of the Ordinance of 1787 there had been a meeting of Friends established west of the mountains in 

Tennessee. Meanwhile Quakers from New England and

New York began moving westward into Western New York and over into Canada, and a little later into western Pennsylvania.

The event that really touched off large scale westward migration of Quakers was the establishment of the Northwest Territory in 1787, a territory free from slavery. Years before, Friends had forbidden the keeping of slaves by any of their members and had adopted a consistent testimony against it. Meanwhile the institution of slavery was becoming firmly fixed upon the South. The Friends, who were farmers, found it increasingly difficult to compete with the cheap slave labor; and, in addition, they were held in contempt by their slave holding neighbors. The opening up of a free territory offered them a relief of which they were quick to take advantage. Prominent ministers traveled among Friends in South Carolina and Georgia, warning them to escape from slavery and its consequences. 

The results of these influences were almost spectacular. 

Whole meetings from the South moved bodily to the new territory. Within three decades Georgia and South Carolina were stripped of their Quaker population. Virginia Yearly 

Meeting was so weakened that it was "laid down" in 1845. 

North Carolina Yearly Meeting lost a large part of its membership. The movement continued on through the first half of the nineteenth century.

The development in the Ohio valley was phenomenal. 

Monthly meetings and quarterly meetings were established in quick succession in Ohio until 1812 when the Friends in that state were granted a yearly meeting of their own. In 

1806 they had begun to stream into Whitewater River Valley in Indiana and soon thereafter began to fan out over

that state. By 1821 Indiana was granted a yearly meeting to meet at Whitewater.*

Ohio and Indiana were rapidly being filled with settlers so that many of the Friends were not satisfied to remain there while there was other land being opened for settlement in the West. They began to overflow into Illinois and in 

1835 the first ones were appearing in Iowa. In 1858 Western Yearly Meeting was set up, composed of meetings in 

Western Indiana and Illinois. By 1863 Iowa was granted a Yearly Meeting by Indiana. 

Kansas, opened to settlement by the Kansas-Nebraska

Act of 1854, experienced a rapid growth culminating in the establishment of Kansas Yearly Meeting in 1872. In the latter half of the Nineteenth Century the frontier had moved to the far west. Kansas and Iowa had received large numbers from the East and now in turn became springboards. 

They sacrificed many members to the fast growing Quaker communities of California, Oregon, Washington, and Idaho.

Oregon Yearly Meeting was set up in 1893, and California in 1895. The new Friends living in central Colorado and 

Nebraska were granted a Yearly Meeting of their own in 

1908 called Nebraska Yearly Meeting.** 

In a little over a century Friends had spread from coast to coast. They had "grown up with the country". The development of Kansas Yearly Meeting is a part of that larger movement.

Chapter II SHAWNEE MISSION 

The attention of Friends was first turned to Kansas in connection with Indian Missionary activity. From their appearance in America, Quakers had evinced a special interest in the Indians. Josiah Cole and Thomas Thurston preached to the Indians in 1658. George Fox had a special concern for their Christianization, and traveled among them enduring great hardships in 1671-1672.* When William Penn obtained Pennsylvania he had the welfare of the Indians in mind as well as the whites. The celebrated treaty of friendship at Shakamaxon has become a tradition in American History. Many Quaker ministers with "concerns" traveled among the Indians in the eighteenth century. 

With the nineteenth century came a new phase in the Indian work. Baltimore Yearly Meeting established a mission among the tribes near Fort Wayne, Indiana, in 1804.

The work prospered until the troublesome Shawnee chief Tecumseh stirred up Indian trouble in 1810, and the buildings were destroyed in the War of 1812.** In the meantime the Shawnees residing near Wapakoneta, Ohio, were requesting help from the Friends. Making use of funds received from English Friends, the Baltimore Yearly Meeting began a new work among them, building a grist mill, a saw mill and dwellings.*** The War of 1812 closed this mission also, but it was reopened in 1815 under the supervision of Baltimore and the newly organized Ohio Yearly Meeting. The principle purpose of this mission from its beginning was for the civilization and education of the Indians, and definite strides were made toward this goal before the work was brought to an abrupt halt in 1832. 

By 1831 the demand for lands in Ohio forced the government once more to move the Shawnees further west. A treaty was signed in which the government purchased their Ohio land and gave them a tract of land 50 miles square on the Kansas River in Kansas Territory.**** This property was to be shared with the Shawnees who had previously been moved there from Cape Girardeau, Missouri.

These first seventeen years had seen comparatively uninterrupted operation and progress. The farm had been improved so that it was providing most of the food and some of the other necessities. The work was on a good financial basis, with three supporting Yearly Meetings appropriating over $1500 annually for expenses; and the mission seemed prepared for real results in the next 17 years of service. It was not to be so, however, for the ensuing years were characterized by unrest, uncertainties, changes of administration, emigration of Indians, and temporary suspensions.

For several years prior to 1854 the Shawnees had been occasionally upset by rumors that they were to be moved again, to another reservation. The change became an actuality in the treaty of that year, in which they turned over to the government the western part of their reservation, and were given the right to own land individually. Three hundred and twenty acres were leased to the mission by this treaty. In succeeding years the Shawnees were encouraged to move to Oklahoma and many did, in a gradual exodus that lasted seventeen years.

CHAPTER III EARLY SETTLEMENT 

Hardly had the Kansas-Nebraska Act been passed when Quaker families began to turn their faces toward that territory. For nearly twenty years the reports of the Indian Committee on the Shawnee Mission had aroused their interest in that forbidden area, and now it was opened to settlement. 

Good land and generous preemption provisions invited them to come, start life anew, and make their fortune. Besides, in what better way could they help the cause against accursed slavery than to go to Kansas and help hold it for the cause of freedom? Kansas called-they soon heeded. 

George M. Harvey, a son of Henry Harvey, was probably the first Quaker to settle in Kansas. He made his claim on Dragoon Creek in June, 1854, the first settler in that neighborhood. He was followed two months later by his brother, Samuel D. Harvey and family, and Henry Harvey and family. Ira Hadley also entered the territory this same fall, seeking a place to homestead, and Joel Hiatt* settled on an excellent claim near Leavenworth.

But the most significant and important early move for a Friends colony was made by three well known Indiana 

Quakers, Wm. H. Coffin, Eli Wilson, and Benejah W. Hiatt.

These three met at Indiana Yearly Meeting in Richmond, in 

October, 1854, and agreed to go west and examine the new territories. Accordingly, they took the railroad as far as 

Terre Haute, and went from there by stage across Illinois to 

St. Louis where they boarded a river steamer for Westport 

Landing (now Kansas City).

After spending a few enjoyable days at the Friends Mission, the three returned to Westport Landing and secured supplies for a tour of the territory, hoping to find a suitable location for a Quaker settlement. With a Peoria Indian Chief, they proceeded southward about fifty miles to the juncture of the Osage and Pottawatomie Rivers. This seemed to be just the desired location so they staked out claims for each in the choicest places. No Friends settlement was to be established here yet, however, for on the following day Orville C. Brown arrived with a party of twenty-six men and desired to build a town on that location. Coffin related his inner struggle in the following words: "My wife was in the house with our four little children and knew nothing about it and I said nothing. We could expect no favor from such a body of men, composed as they were, of the worst description of border men, of the Jesse James type, and I had little confidence or expectation that a hurried rally of the neighbors would succeed in stopping them, organized as they were. I do not think that I was afraid at that time, being young and excitable; but my education was such I could not, with conscience kill a man; but when I got to reasoning with myself about my duty in the protection of my family, my faith gave way. I had an excellent double-barrelled gun, and I took it outdoors and loaded it heavily with buckshot. It was near bed time; my wife and children soon went to sleep, and I barred the door and set my gun handy, and made up my mind I would shoot any man or set of men that undertook to break in. A cabin, built as they were, of logs at that time, made a pretty good fort; but I could get no sleep, having laid down with my clothes on. Finally, towards midnight I got up, wife and children peacefully sleeping, drew the loads from my gun and put it away; and then, on my knees, I told the Lord all about it and asked His protection; and so, casting all my care on Him, I felt easy, went to bed, was soon asleep, and slept until sun-up the next morning."

The Missourians had never reached their location. It had proved to be one of the many rumors of the day. 

While their policy was to stay quietly at home, open their farms, and mind their own business, yet the peace-loving Quakers could never feel entirely out of danger while living near raiding camps. Shortly thereafter the Wilsons and

Hiatt's life was threatened by a group of pro-slavery men, but his hospitality to them on a cold night saved him. On another occasion, in midsummer of 1856, Wm. Coffin's life was endangered by two armed ruffians. The other Friends had not suffered such constant danger, being further from the Missouri border, and from the scene of strife. CHAPTER IV. 

ENGLISH QUAKERS VISIT KANSAS 

By 1858 the hard-pressed settlers were in need of spiritual encouragement. Isolated from the nearest Friends to the east by several hundred miles, they had been enduring the hardships of cold winters, warfare, and frontier deprivations for three years. During this time no minister had settled among them, nor had any minister visited them. The meetings were held in silence, save for an occasional short exhortation by a member. Therefore, the visit of two English ministers in March and April, 1858, was an important event to them.

Robert Lindsey had begun his ministry in England in 1844. Two years later he made the first of his foreign journeys, a three months visit to Ireland. Later, he and Benjamin 

Seebohm made an extensive tour of North America; following which, with Frederick Mackie, he toured New Zealand,

Australia, and South Africa. After spending a year and a half at home, in 1857 he again felt it his duty to set out on a visit of gospel love to foreign lands. This time his wife accompanied him and they were given a minute to visit "all the isolated families of Friends in the world." Sailing first to the United States, they traveled for over two years in North America. Further family visitation cleared the way for their departure to the next settlement, Le Roy. Their route lay southwestward through the towns of Greely and Hyatt. 

Again they were unable to complete the journey in one day, and found a hospitable frontiersman's cabin for a shelter. 

Two families of Friends resided at Le Roy, where they held a meeting the following day at the house of R. Davis. "Their dwelling is made of rough logs laid one upon another without the interstices being closed; there is no window but an opening for a door, though it is only an opening, the floor is nature's covering and very uneven from the projecting roots of trees: and there is a recess for a fire place, but no chimney. The furniture consists of two beds, two chairs, a few boxes, and mirror, etc. A number of chairs were brought from the neighbours and here in the midst of the woods fifteen persons assembled for the purpose of divine worship. "The Lord owned us in our solitary situation, and counsel and encouragement to some present were given, prayer was also offered for their preservation." 31

Le Roy was situated on the Neosho River, and the route for the next few days lay to the northwest along the valley of this river. In the afternoon following the meeting they rode the fifteen miles to Hampden, where they had a meeting in a school house the next day. Robert Lindsey preached from the text: "All scripture is given by inspiration from 

God, and is profitable for reproof, correction, doctrine, etc."

The frequent reading of these scriptures was encouraged. 

Here at Hampden they especially sought to encourage Perry Mills, who had suffered much during the late political disturbances. 

April 1st they were ready to go to Emporia, thirty-five miles on the Neosho. The road lay through fine rich prairie land and past the town of Autuma. Night closed upon the weary travelers as they neared Emporia and they feared that they would have to travel the remaining distance in the dark. But at last they were cheered by the sight of a light, and a little later found a house, where they were given the best accommodations available. The house being unfinished, there was an abundance of ventilation due to the absence of windows.

Next morning they crossed the Neosho River and came to Emporia, and drove on a few miles west of the town to the Friends settlement where arrangments were made for a meeting the next day. On returning to Emporia they found a "decent Inn" where they enjoyed a day and night of quiet and rest. They esteemed it a great favor to have a comfortable room to themselves. 

The appointed meeting was held in Cutris Hiatt's cabin.

Here met thirty Friends and several neighbors and there "seemed to be great openness in speaking of those things which appertain to our present and eternal welfare." After the meeting Robert Lindsley requested that the Friends remain, when he sought to bring them into a closer relationship with each other. "It seemed that some of them were but little known to each other." They dined at Andrew Hinshaw's, then went back into the town, where they enjoyed another afternoon and night of rest and leisure.

Eight miles on up the Neosho River was the next destination, the home of Thomas and Mary Stanley. These well-known Friends and Indian missionaries had just moved to Kansas the previous fall and had taken a claim four miles west of the present town of Americus, Still living in a temporary one-roomed house, they were already engaging in their own personal missionary work among the Kaw Indians of that vicinity. Here the Lindseys and their faithful companion Benajah Hiatt were given the best lodging the frail 32

house afforded. The walls were of rough boards driven into the ground. The night was windy, "cold and frosty, and owing to the numerous chinks in the walls and roof, it seemed likely that we should suffer from such an unusual stream of fresh air, but we do not seem to have taken cold from the exposure." A meeting was held in the house of a neighbor, where "the room was well filled and the Lord was near." 

The next part of the journey, to Henry Harvey's settlement on Dragoon Creek, was described as "cold, windy, and wet," and they were "glad to find a shelter." Henry Harvey was not at home, but they were welcomed and kindly cared for by his wife, "a delicate woman," and his sons, Here again there were appalled at the inadequate housing, and were "sorry to see the family of such a self-sacrificing friend living in such a humble dwelling with so few of the comforts of life." The meeting in the evening was held among the different branches of Henry Harvey's family. 

Forty miles to the east and north was a settlement called 

Bloomington toward which the faithful horses drew them next day, April 6. Here the appointed meeting was in a school house, and Robert Lindsey had "good service in the ministry, dwelling particularly on the Atonement; the knee was afterwards bowed in prayer." The 25 Friends who had settled around this community seemed in a "lukewarm and benumbed condition", and the visiting ministers labored earnestly to get them to "begin the reasonable and needful duty of holding religious meetings." 

Taking leave of the Edwin Stokes family, with whom they had resided, the travelers continued eastward, crossing the Wakarusa River, and entering the Shawnee Reservation. 

A twenty mile ride brought them to the home of Henry Wilson, who was renting land from the Indians. A meeting was held here on the 8th, with Henry Wilson's family, Levi Woodward and family, and an Indian family present. "The wing of Divine Goodness was felt to spread over us, and we had an interesting season, wherein counsel and close things were spoken." 

The tour of Quakers in Kansas was now complete.

They had visited the different settlements and were ready to journey on. In fact, Sarah Lindsey wrote, they would have been "glad to leave the state." But they could not leave yet. Robert had become increasingly concerned about a lack of unity between some of the Friends at Emporia, and the decision was made to visit them again. 

Faithfully, if somewhat reluctantly, they made the difficult two days journey back to Emporia. Here they vis- 133

ited some other families and held a conference, which settled the matter satisfactorily. They were brought face to face with inconveniences of frontier life when they visited 

Jonathan Wheeler's family, the parents and eight children residing in a one roomed cabin on the open prairie. "The house had no windows and but few of the comforts and conveniences of life within: the bare uneven ground was covered with a little hay Round the sides of the house several trunks of trees enclosed loose hay, which with cross timber, without bed stocks, formed several sleeping places for the night. A large box was used as a table, two or three chairs, and smaller boxes served for seats, a few open shelves held the crockery ware, and a small cupboard contained their stock of books. But in the midst of this humble abode contentment seemed to dwell, and a smile played upon many of the faces around us . We had a meeting with them to satisfaction; many of us would think their lot a hard one, but we had cause to believe that the Son of Peace had taken up His abode in some of their hearts." 

A cold windy ride was the return journey to Lawrence, the ground being covered with snow. With some regret they then parted with Benajah, who left them and returned to his home on Stranger. He had been their driver and faithful companion for three weeks, and had told them much about the recent warfare in those localities. They had safely forded many streams, crossed ravines, and negotiated seldom traveled roads, in order to carry their message to the spiritually hungry settlers.

The public stage conveyed them from Lawrence to the Mission, where they spent fifth day with the Friends and Robert Lindsey preached his last sermon in Kansas. Early on the morning of April 17 they left Kansas City by boat and were again on their way to take their message to other lands. 34

CHAPTER V GROWTH AND ORGANIZATION, 1858-1872 

From the time of the Lindseys' visit to 1872 is a period characterized by growth and organization. This growth was comparatively slow until 1865, due mainly to the drouth of 1860 and to the Civil War, but rapid after that to the formation of the Yearly Meeting. 

In the fall of 1858, Daniel Barker, of North Carolina, and John Barker, of Indiana, carried forward the work started by the Lindseys. They made the second tour of 

Friends settlements, preaching also to many settlers and Indians outside the settlements. An important result of the visits of these ministers was the stimulation of a desire among Friends for the organization of established meetings -a desire which was fulfilled within two years in the three main settlements.

In December, 1857, Whitewater Monthly Meeting, of 

Indiana, received the following request from Springdale: "We, the undersigned members of the Society of Friends and near all members of the above meetings and residents of Kansas Territory, feeling the need of your Christian care and oversight, and wishing to more fully enjoy the privileges of Religious organization, have unitedly come to the conclusion to propose that an established meeting may be set up and held at some point near Stranger Creek in Kansas, as may be most convenient, if in the judgment of Friends such a movement might seem best for the advancement of the cause of truth. "We number at present in this locality nine families of Friends comprising fifty members, and it might be proper to state, that we have been in the habit of meeting together for worship on first days near two years and for several months past have held meetings for worship regularly twice in the week on fourth and first days. "We have also part of the time kept up a school for our children and have one now in session which is attended by an average of near twenty scholars; all of which have been taught by competent Friends. "There are also several other families of Friends in other parts of the Territory who we feel persuaded would rejoice at a meeting being established here, but are not near enough to meet regularly with us. "In conclusion permit us to add that in our weakness and isolated position, we feel to ask your Christian sympathy and prayers for us, and remain in the love of the truth, Your Friends."*

CHAPTER VII WESTWARD MIGRATION 

For eighteen years after the beginning of settlement most of the Friends had been settling in Eastern Kansas, not far from the three original settlements of Springdale, Spring

Grove and Cottonwood-the development of Spring River providing the only notable exception. About the time of the first yearly meeting, however, evidences began to appear of a westward march that was to continue for fifty years, and to add large new areas to the confines of the yearly meeting. 

This westward movement provides a story in itself, in which the hardships of the earliest Springdale settlers were repeated and new ones faced. 

As early as 1869 the first Quakers appeared in Jewel

CHAPTER VIII THE SEPARATION OF 1879 

A discussion of Friends in this area would not be complete without some notice of a "forgotten" group, the Conservative Kansas Yearly Meeting. Set up in 1879 as a result of opposition to the new revival methods, it was always small, and was practically unnoticed among the church groups of Kansas.

Three reasons may be presented for the separation leading to the establishment of this yearly meeting: wide-spread opposition to any change of traditional practices in the Society; the example of previous separations in the Eastespecially the ones of 1877 in Iowa and Western Yearly 

Meetings; and the dynamic leadership of Cyrus W. Harvey.

Cyrus Harvey, the elder of Jesse Harvey's eleven children, was born in Indiana, October 30, 1843. An unusual child, he learned to read at the age of four years, and read all the books in his father's library by the time he was nine. 

At eighteen he entered Earlham College, but enlisted in the Union Army the next year, 1862, and served till the close of the war. Soon after the war he moved to Kansas, settling on Spring River in 1867.* He became a leader in his community, and was recorded a minister. As the evangelistic movement grew he came out as the active leader against it. 

In 1877 he visited almost every meeting in the yearly meetíng, throwing his influence against the revival methods. But he was fighting a losing battle.**

The earliest separations took place in Harvey's home quarterly meeting, Spring River, the stronghold of conservatism in Kansas. CHAPTER IX REVIVAL AND TRANSFORMATION 

Kansas Yearly Meeting was born in the midst of revival. While the important migrations, described in Chapter VII, were taking place, profound changes were coming upon the Society that were to radically transform it. New methods and practices made their appearance in swift succession, and the result was a change from a religious society to a modern religious denomination. 

When the yearly meeting was organized in 1872, all the meetings were of the silent, informal type. Friends would gather at the appointed time, go reverently to their seats and worship in silence. No pastor presided. At the head of the meeting sat the ministers and elders. Perhaps the Spirit would "move" some minister to preach or to pray, but never to sing. Sometimes the hour passed in silence, and the head of the meeting dismissed the congregation by shaking hands with the one nearest him. The monthly business meeting was conducted in the same manner. "At the proper time, explained one member, "down would come the partition separating the men Friends from the women Friends, to deliberate and transact the business of their respective meetings. Frequent messages (were sent) to and fro, until the business was attended to-often long past dinner time for us small boys."* 

It was the revival of 1872-1900 that brought the changes to the modern procedures, a movement which was part of a general revival among Quakers of America, and among other denominations.

Manifestations of the new evangelistic movement began in Indiana as early as 1854. V. K. Stanley noted that the movement was expanded in 1855, under the work of the Committee on General Meetings of Indiana Yearly Meet ings. Social gatherings and two day meetings were held which gradually developed into longer periods.** Conversions became more and more frequent. CHAPTER X OKLAHOMA INDIAN MISSIONS 

Early missionary activity in the area of Kansas Yearly Meeting was among the Indians. From 1857 to 1869 Kansas 

Friends took a major part in the management of the Shawnee Mission, while Thomas and Mary Stanley were engaged in an independent work among the Kaws on the Neosho. In time of drought or prairie fire, the tribe depended greatly on the Stanleys for food and help. Often when a brave wished to go off on a buffalo hunt, he would deposit squaw and children in a tent near the Stanley home. Nothing could hurt them there, for that was "where God stayed."* Sunday afternoons would find Thomas and Mary visiting them in their camps giving spiritual instruction. Also in 1863 to 1866 

Western Yearly Meeting supported a mission work among the Kaws. But all these works were terminated when the 

Shawnees and Kaws were moved to Indian Territory. 

In 1869 Friends' work among the Indians entered a new phase with the inauguration of President Grant's "Quaker Policy." After twenty years of Indian Wars, massacres and pillaging, the government was ready to try other means of pacifying its wards. As part of this new policy, indian agencies in Kansas and Indian Territory were placed in charge of Orthodox Friends. Here were 19,000 

Indians, including some of the wildest and most warlike tribes the Kiowas, Commanches, Apaches, Wichitas, Shawnees, Kickapoos, Potawatomies, Kaws, Osages, Quapaws,

Sacs, Foxes, Cheyennes and Arapahoes. It was a serious undertaking. There were the problems of pacifying wild warriors, the dangers of a treacherous unsettled country, and the encroachings of lawless whites.

To administer this work the yearly meeting early organized the Associated Executive Committee of Friends on 

Indian Affairs with representatives from 9 yearly meetings.

Enoch Hoag was appointed by Grant as Superintendent of the Central Superintendency. Dr. Wm. Nicholson was appointed general agent of the committee.** Friends were appointed agents, and to other positions. This period of the Quaker Policy lasted in its fullest phase, ten years, to 1879. Soon after President Hayes was inaugurated Friends agents began to be withdrawn, and by 1885 all had been relieved. CHAPTER XII EDUCATIONAL ACTIVITIES 

From the time the first Quaker immigrants staked their claims on Kansas soil, the education of their youth has been a matter of great concern. Friends were usually far in advance of their neighbors in providing elementary education in the earliest years, and then a secondary education in later years.

The educational activities fell into three phases: (1) monthly meeting elementary schools, (2) quarterly meeting academies, and (3) college education. The period of monthly meeting elementary schools was 1856-1870, of the academies about 1878-1910, and of higher education 1889 to the present. These periods indicate in outline the gradual growth of interest and raising of educational standards as the yearly meeting matured and kept pace with the progress in education in our nation. 

Monthly Meeting Elementary Schools 

When the first Quakers came to this section it was many years before public schools were established. The monthly meeting schools were therefore set up as pioneer efforts. It was at Springdale that the first one was established in 1856. V. K. Stanley had just arrived in the territory that spring. Having had some education, he was persuaded to start a school in May in the log cabin that was being used as a meeting house. Eighteen pupils from the community at tended. Stanley describes the building: 

"This primitive school house was built of small logs, not hewn, cut from a grove nearby. The cracks were stopped up with split pieces of timber, commonly called 'chinks,' and mortar, composed of mud and prairie grass roots. The building was plastered in good shape for that day. It had one three-light and two two-light windows, the glass being eight by ten inches. The floor was rough and the roof composed of three foot boards riven out of oak timber, brought from Stranger Creek two miles east. CHAPTER XIII INTER-YEARLY MEETING RELATIONSHIP 

From its organization, Kansas Yearly Meeting was closely associated with Iowa, Western and Indiana Yearly Meetings. Ministers frequently travelled from one yearly meeting to another. Occasional trips were made "back 

East" by families to see relatives and old friends. So it was that these yearly meetings looked to each other in a bond of fellowship.

In 1886 the question of use or disuse of ordinances in the church led to an agitation among Friends for the calling of a conference for united action. Indiana proposed such a conference of all yearly meetings in correspondence with London to meet in Richmond in 1887. The conference issued the now well known Richmond Declaration of Faith, with its clear statements of doctrine and definite stand on the ordinance question.* Kansas Friends took an active part in the proceedings and its was William Nicholson who proposed a "conference of yearly meetings with certain delegated powers to meet at stated intervals." No definite action was taken on it, however, until a later conference. 

The second of these quinquennial conferences met in Indianapolis in 1892 and was concerned mainly with the question of the ministry. The trend toward new practices was indicated in a minute which included the statement, "we strongly commend a proper pastoral system."** 

At the Indianapolis Conference of 1897, Kansas with three other yearly meetings requested the establishment of a general organization with some legislative and disciplinary powers. Edmund Stanley suggested a uniform discipline for all the yearly meetings. After favorable consideration, a committee was appointed to work on details of the plan. Three years later it was presented to the yearly meetings and was accepted by all the orthodox yearly meetings except London, Dublin, Philadelphia and Ohio. Kansas

Yearly Meeting adopted it with the understanding that the 

Richmond Declaration of Faith and the letter of George Fox to the Governor of Barbadoes were corporate parts.*** Thus the Five Years Meeting came into being with the first sessions in 1902. The following boards were created: Education, Legislation, Welfare of the Negroes, Evangelistic and Church Extension-and the American Friends Board of For-

CHAPTER XIV OTHER YEARLY MEETING ACTIVITIES 

The work of the yearly meeting in evangelism, missions, and education was supplemented by other activities. In 

1887, the Christian Fellowship Union was organized for the young people. Five years later its name was changed to 

Christian Endeavor, and societies established throughout the yearly meeting. In 1917 the first summer conference was held. This conference has met at various times at Wichita, Hutchinson, Drury, Camp Wood, and Camp Wa Shun Ga, and has grown until in recent years the attendance averaged over 300. The Christian Endeavor Union has always taken an active part in the support of missionary work. 

The Pastors Alliance had its inception as a Bible Institute, held first in 1894 at Emporia, under direction of the Pastoral and Evangelistic Committee of the Yearly Meeting. 

In 1909 most of the features of the present organization were adopted. The Alliance has met in various churches over the yearly meeting since that time.

Throughout its history the yearly meeting has given em phasis to Bible Schools. Attendance at Bible School (Sun day School) has about equalled the Sunday morning worship service. In recent years much attention has been given to 

Daily Vacation Bible Schools as an important addition to

Bible School work. The Bible School Committee cooperates with two other yearly meetings in publishing their own Bible 

School quarterlies. 

Two "testimonies" which have been long associated with the name Friends are Peace and Temperance. Active committees have been maintained on both. In four 

In four wars the young men of the yearly meeting had an opportunity to show in a concrete way their belief in peace, and opposition to war. 

In World War II many served in C. P. S. camps, and others as non-combatants.

In the earlier years, Drusilla Wilson was the outstanding temperance worker, taking a leading part in pushing through the Kansas Prohibition Law of 1881. Mary Sibbit was the leader of the 20th century efforts for temperance. 

In any evaluation of the yearly meeting at present (1946) a study of membership statistics is very revealing.

Dividing the seventy-five years into three periods one finds the first period of twenty-five years a time of steady, sometimes rapid growth. It increased from the 2620 of 1872 to over 11,000 in 1897, an increase of 8500. The second period was one of comparatively stabilized membership in which 88

the reports showed between 11,000 and 12,000. The highest membership was 12,012 in 1910. About 1920 came the beginning of a slow but steady decline-to 9,300 in 1930 and 

8,447 in 1945. 

What were the reasons for this decline? For one thing there was the slowing down of evangelistic work as the Great Revival declined-consequently, fewer requests for membership. Then the large numbers were moving away, especially during the drouth and depression, to Colorado and the far west. Another general population movement took many Friends from the rural areas, where there were Friends churches, to the city where there was probably none. There was a decline of church loyalty. When the 1890 Quaker moved to a new community and found another Quaker family or two, he immediately began holding services in his home, arranged for a Quaker evangelist to come and hold a revival and clamored for the yearly meeting to set up a monthly meeting there. But when the 1940 Quaker moved to a new community, and perhaps found another Quaker family or two, he quietly joined another church (or ceased to attend church anywhere).

Faced with these problems, there were signs that the church was rising to the occasion. Aggresive leaders, backed by the Pastoral and Evangelistic Board, established new churches. Besides the new ones organized in the growing Friendswood Quarterly Meeting, the latest of which was Beaumont, new meetings were set up in Tulsa and Chandler, Oklahoma. Renewed interest in education and mission work was manifested. The Yearly Meeting Bulletin was enlarged and achieved a wider circulation. The church was striving to meet adequately the needs of its day. 68

APPENDIX GLOSSARY OF QUAKER TERMS 

Conservative: The term for the branch of the Quaker 

Church that held fast to the methods and practices of the eighteenth century. 

First Day: Early Friends refused to use the terms Monday, Tuesday, etc., for the days of the week, pointing out that they were taken from pagan terms. They used the simple numerals, "First day", Second day", etc. Similarly, the months were designated as "First month", etc. 

Friends: The name taken from the Scripture: "Ye are my friends, if ye do whatsoever I command you." John 15: 

14, 15.

Meeting for Discipline: The business meeting of the church. 

Meeting for Worship: The regular religious service. 

Meeting House: Early Friends did not call their houses of worship, "churches", for they held that the true church consisted of men and women. They called their meeting places, "meeting houses." 

Minute: When a Friend had a "concern" to visit some metings, he was given a signed letter commending him to that meeting. The letter was an official act of his meeting, and was called a "minute".

Monthly Meeting: An organized local church which conducts its business meetings once each month. In early years it was composed of several "preparative" meetings.

Orthodox: A term denoting those who hold the original belief of the church. Today, the term is associated with the "Progressive" group.

Preparative Meeting: In early years the local organized meetings were called "Preparative" meetings, and several "preparatives" formed a "monthly meeting." In the present system, however, the term "preparative" has been abandoned, and all organized local meetings are "monthly meetings". 90

Progressive: After the separation of 1879, the larger body adopted new methods and practices, and its members were designated as Progressives, as distinguished from Conservatives. 

Quaker: A nickname applied to Friends from its early years.

Its origin has been attributed to a number of sources. 

Quarterly Meeting: A governing body composed of several monthly meetings. Business meetings are held four times each year.

Society: Friends called themselves a religious "society", because they believed that the church included all true 

Christians in the world.

Yearly Meeting: An indepedent governing body composed of the quarterly meetings of a large area. Kansas Yearly Meeting is composed of the quarterly meetings of Kansas, Oklahoma and Texas, with a few meetings in
