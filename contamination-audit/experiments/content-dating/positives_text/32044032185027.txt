AN ACT 

To establish the use of humane methods of slaughter of livestock as a policy of the United States, and for other purposes. ter.

Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That the Congress Humane methfinds that the use of humane methods in the slaughter of livestock ods of slaughprevents needless suffering; results in safer and better working conditions for persons engaged in the slaughtering industry; brings about improvement of products and economies in slaughtering operations; and produces other benefits for producers, processors, and consumers which tend to expedite an orderly flow of livestock and livestock products in interstate and foreign commerce. It is therefore declared to be the policy of the United States that the slaughtering of livestock and the handling of livestock in connection with slaughter shall be carried out only by humane methods. 

SEC. 2. No method of slaughtering or handling in connection with slaughtering shall be deemed to comply with the public policy of the United States unless it is humane. Either of the following two methods of slaughtering and handling are hereby found to be humane: (a) in the case of cattle, calves, horses, mules, sheep, swine, and other livestock, all animals are rendered insensible to pain by a single blow or gunshot or an electrical, chemical or other means that is rapid and effective, before being shackled, hoisted, thrown, cast, or cut; or (b) by slaughtering in accordance with the ritual requirements of the Jewish faith or any other religious faith that prescribes a method of slaughter whereby the animal suffers loss of consciousness by anemia of the brain caused by the simultaneous and instantaneous severance of the carotid arteries with a sharp instrument.

SEC. 3. The public policy declared herein shall be taken into con- Procurement, etc., sideration by all agencies of the Federal Government in connection by U. S. after with all procurement and price support programs and operations and June 30, 1960. after June 30, 1960, no agency or instrumentality of the United States shall contract for or procure any livestock products produced or processed by any slaughterer or processor which in any of its plants or in any plants of any slaughterer or processor with which it is affiliated slaughters or handles in connection with slaughter livestock by any methods other than methods designated and approved by the Secretary of Agriculture (hereinafter referred to as the Secretary) pursuant to section 4 hereof: Provided, That during the period of any national emergency declared by the President or the Congress, the limitations on procurement required by this section may be modified by the President to the extent determined by him to be necessary to meet essential procurement needs during such emergency. For the 72 Stat. 863. purposes of this section a slaughterer or processor shall be deemed to be affiliated with another slaughterer or processor if it controls, is controlled by, or is under common control with, such other slaughterer or processor. SEC. 4. In furtherance of the policy expressed herein the Secretary is authorized and directed- (a) to conduct, assist, and foster research, investigation, and experimentation to develop and determine methods of slaughter and the handling of livestock in connection with slaughter which are practicable with reference to the speed and scope of slaughtering operations and humane with reference to other existing methods and then current scientific knowledge; (b) on or before March 1, 1959, and at such times thereafter as he deems advisable, to designate methods of slaughter and of handling in connection with slaughter which, with respect to each species of livestock, conform to the policy stated herein. If he deems it more effective, the Secretary may make any such designation by designating methods which are not in conformity with such policy. Designations by the Secretary subsequent to 

March 1, 1959, shall become effective for purposes of section 3 hereof 180 days after their publication in the Federal Register; (c) to provide suitable means of identifying the carcasses of animals inspected and passed under the Meat Inspection Act (21 U. S. C. 71 and the following) that have been slaughtered in accordance with the public policy declared herein. Handling in connection with such slaughtering which necessarily accompanies the method of slaughter described in subsection (b) of this section shall be deemed to comply with the public policy specified by this section.

SEC. 5. To assist in implementing the provisions of section 4, the 

Secretary is authorized to establish an advisory committee. The functions of the Advisory Committee shall be to consult with the Secretary and other appropriate officials of the Department of Agriculture and to make recommendations relative to (a) the research authorized in section 4; (b) obtaining the cooperation of the public, producers, farm organizations, industry groups, humane associations, and Federal and State agencies in the furtherance of such research and the adoption of improved methods; and (c) the designations required by section 4. The Committee shall be composed of twelve members, of whom one shall be an officer or employee of the Department of Agriculture designated by the Secretary (who shall serve as Chairman); two shall be representatives of national organizations of slaughterers; one shall be a representative of the trade-union movement engaged in packinghouse work; one shall be a representative of the general public; two shall be representatives of livestock growers; one shall be a representative of the poultry industry; two shall be representatives of national organizations of the humane movement; one shall be a representative of a national professional veterinary organization; and one shall be a person familiar with the requirements of religious faiths with respect to slaughter. The Department of Agriculture shall assist the Committee with such research personnel and facilities as the Department can make available. Committee members other than the Chairman shall not be deemed to be employees of the United States and are not entitled to compensation but the Secretary is authorized to allow their travel expenses and subsistence expenses in connection with their attendance at regular or special meetings of the Committee. The Committee shall meet at least once each year and at the call of the Secretary and shall from time to time submit to the Secretary such reports and recommendations with respect to new or improved methods as it believes should be taken into consideration by him in making the designations required by section 4 and the Secretary shall make all such reports available to the public. 

SEC. 6. Nothing in this Act shall be construed to prohibit, abridge, or in any way hinder the religious freedom of any person or group. Notwithstanding any other provision of this Act, in order to protect freedom of religion, ritual slaughter and the handling or other preparation of livestock for ritual slaughter are exempted from the terms of this Act. For the purposes of this section the term "ritual slaughter" means slaughter in accordance with section 2 (b).

UNITED STATES DEPARTMENT OF AGRICULTURE Washington, November 13, 1958 Statement by Secretary of Agriculture Ezra Taft Benson to the Humane 

Slaughter Advisory Committee, 9 A.M. (EST), Nov. 13, Room 218-A, USDA, Washington, D. C.

It is a distinct pleasure to meet with this group on its initial gathering, and I offer all of you a warm welcome. Each of you has been selected for committee membership because of your individual knowledge or experience in a particular field or as a representative of the public at large. By drawing on this total of knowledge, experience and interest the 

Department hopes to formulate a sound, workable application of the Humane Slaughter Law. Please accept my thanks for your willingness to serve. Yours is a large and important task -- that of reflecting the wishes of the people of this country as expressed by Congress in making the law, and that of advising me and my staff in determining and designating methods of slaughter which are humane.

It may be profitable for us to review briefly some of the history behind the Humane Slaughter Law which was approved by the President August 27, 1958. Traditionally, in this country, interest in the humane slaughter of food animals expressed itself as a part of the total interest in the humane handling of animals that is safeguarded in local anticruelty-to-animal laws. Dedicated members of local humane societies have used these local laws effectively to prevent cruelty in the handling of animals. A bill, introduced on the floor of the Senate April 1, 1955, set in motion legislative action which later resulted in the law which we are here to consider. It changes the traditional pattern with respect to slaughtering of food animals. The law creates a Federal program which is to be administered by this Department. 

The bill introduced April 1, 1955, contained essentially the same features as the current law. It contemplated rendering the animal insensible before slaughter. It recognized an exception in the case of ritualistic slaughter. It also provided for an advisory committee to advise me on questions arising in the administration of the Act. The principal difference is that the current law does not provide criminal penalties, but does prohibit Government purchase of meat products from sources which do not use humane methods of slaughter. It is inevitable that our deliberations in administering this law will be influenced in part at least by its legislative history, as well as the specific terms of the statute.

In considering our responsibilities under this law, we should recognize also that we are dealing with a progressive development in the handling of food animals. As we improve methods of breeding and feeding livestock, it follows that the handling and dispatching of these animals for food purposes must also be improved. As our levels of human relationships are more clearly defined and controlled, our obligations with regard to the treatment of the lower animals also becomes a subject of public concern, and, as such, a subject of public control. 

Years ago the Department recognized the desirability of providing suitable treatment of livestock in transit and in public yards. Regulatory programs such as the Packers and Stockyard's Act and the 28-hour law insured the continuation of proper handling. In conducting our work in the stockyards and packing industry, we have observed that most packers provide shelter, feed, water, adequate space and surroundings for their livestock. These basic comforts are not entirely for economic gain. In the final analysis, all have a direct relationship to the subject of humane handling of food animals. 

With respect to actual slaughter and handling incident to the slaughter of animals, it has long been the practice of the Department to assist USDA 3151-58

wherever possible in the efforts of local jurisdictions in bringing about improvements. However, until the present law was enacted, this responsibility was not included in the Department's functions. We now have this responsibility. 

The Department looks to you for advice in formulating the practical application of the law which now stands on the books. You as a committee are to recommend research to develop and determine methods of slaughter and handling which are practicable and humane. Based upon these findings, it will be your responsibility to assist in obtaining the cooperation of the public, producers, farm organizations, industry groups, humane associations, and Federal and State agencies in the furtherance of research and the adoption of improved methods of slaughter. You are also to counsel with us concerning methods of slaughter which may be designated "humane".

As Secretary of Agriculture, I sincerely hope that a practical application of this law can be worked out which will not only take into account its mechanical requirements, but will also reflect the humanitarian spirit behind it. Members of the Department have been studying and will continue to study and evaluate every possible source of information that is available. The methods to be designated must be humanitarian. They must also be economically acceptable and practical for the producer, packer and consumer. You must realize that in the allotted time between now and March 1, a great deal of work must be done by both committee and the Department. 

Our second target date is July 1, 1960. By that time those packers who propose to sell their products to any governmental agency must conform with the requirements set down on March 1 of next year. Even when our initial goals are reached continued research and development must be undertaken. New and improved methods of slaughter must be considered, evaluated, and either accepted or rejected. Such plans call for an active committee on a continuing basis. 

You, as the committee, have a tremendous task in helping develop a program that is humane, sensible, workable, and at the same time able to withstand review by the courts. You must be convinced that what is decided here is good for all and then help put the decisions into action by the groups you represent.

I believe that your efforts supported by departmental research, study and investigation can produce a humane slaughter program which will be a standard for all civilized countries. 

Again I extend a warm welcome to you and express appreciation for your ready response to the call to serve on this important committee.

Intensive Research Effort Urged by Humane Slaughter Advisory Group: 

Approving current actions and future plans of the U. S. Department of Agriculture relating to the humane slaughter of livestock, the Humane Slaughter Advisory Committee to the Secretary of Agriculture urged intensive research toward ultimate development of several humane slaughter methods at its initial meeting in Washington, Nov. 13.

Committee members also agreed that there is need for more long range, basic research that would establish criteria for measuring pain and insensibility in livestock.

QUESTION - What was the purpose of research on the blood pressure and blood flow in the vertebral arteries of ruminants? ANSWER 

These experiments were designed to study the contribution of the vertebral arteries to the cerebral circulation of sheep and cattle, and what happened in these vessels when the carotid arteries and jugular veins were severed as in the Jewish method of slaughter. We also wanted to determine as nearly as possible when animals become unconscious. 

How was this done?

A Blood pressure and blood flow in the vertebral arteries of sheep and calves were measured both separately and simultaneously under various conditions. For comparative purposes, the blood pressure and blood flow were also measured in the carotid arteries. In addition, observations of the actual slaughter process were made in an attempt to test results with those occurring under actual conditions. 

Q - What animals were used in these experiments?

Sheep and cattle were used.

How much was the blood pressure in the vertebral arteries affected when the main arteries in the animal's neck were cut? 

A Blood pressure dropped sharply, then levelled off. 

Q How long after the animal's throat is cut does blood flow in the vertebral arteries stop?

A - On the average, blood flow stopped in approximately 28 seconds. 

This time was fairly constant in both sheep and calves.

Q. 

How soon did this drop cause unconsciousness?

A - Within two seconds blood pressure was less than one-third of normal in the vertebral artery. We concluded that this level was below the level needed to sustain consciousness in most animals. 

How long after an animal's throat was cut before it made any significant movement?

A - Where this reaction was checked with a stopwatch, the first noticeable movement was a spasmodic twitch of the forelimbs. This occurred in about 17 to 20 seconds after the cut. 

Q - Was this movement a response to pain?

When this response was considered in relation to measurements of blood pressure and blood flow, indications are that it was the first of a series of convulsions due to stimulation of the nervous system caused by lack of blood and not a response to pain. If the animal was reacting to pain of the cut, it should react in one or two seconds time.

Even if the animal was conscious for as long as eight seconds, I don't believe that it could be concluded that it was feeling pain. 

Q - - In your opinion, is the Shechitah, or Jewish method of slaughter, humane?

Based on observation and research, in my opinion the method is humane. The animal's throat is cut with a very sharp knife and the motion is a very quick one. I believe the suddenness of the slaughtering will almost immediately render the animal unconscious.

QUESTION - What problems are encountered in anesthetizing animals for humane slaughter? 

ANSWER They are largely the same as those involved in performing humane animal surgery. Where carbon dioxide is used, for example, there are the problems of inducing anesthesia, maintenance of the anesthesia once it has been produced, determining whether the animal is properly anesthetized, and how to produce a similar or uniform effect in large numbers of animals. 

Q Do animals that are to be anesthetized present special problems?

A - Because any animal, including man, resists being anesthetized to some extent, during the induction phase it is desirable to reduce this initial phase as much as possible by using a minimum of restraint on the animal and by providing a proper high concentration of gas.

An animal shouldn't breathe against resistance because this could lead to suffocation. Neither should it breathe an irritant gas, since this would cause some distress and a tendency for the animal to hold its breath, thus slowing down the process of anesthetization. 

The gas used should also produce its effect rapidly. 

Q - Does the carbon dioxide chamber restrict the animal's breathing? 

A While there is a marked limitation on air, there is no physical restriction on breathing that would cause distress. And since animals are moved through the chamber on an endless belt arrangement and are not tied down or restrained, there would be no distress from this standpoint. 

Does gas administered in this type of chamber produce a rapid effect?

It appears to, and for this reason it can be assumed that it doesn't cause irritation. Otherwise animals would hold their breath.

Q Why is it important to use a quick-acting gas? 

A When gas is used in low concentrations, an animal will go through a long period of narcotic excitement due to partial loss of control of the reflexes. This tends to cause animals to thrash around, which is undesirable from a bruising standpoint. 

Q - Once a hog has gone through the carbon dioxide chamber and been rendered unconscious, how long before it starts to recover? 

A About 20 seconds, which means that the animal must be stuck fairly quickly after emerging.

Q - How far is the animal from complete asphyxiation at the time it leaves the gas chamber? 

A At the level of carbon dioxide normally used, complete asphyxiation would probably require another two or three minutes. 

How can you tell when an animal has been properly anesthetized?

A The electroencephalograph, which detects and records brain waves, can be used to help determine whether an animal has been rendered unconscious. Reflex movements of the eye, breathing, sensitivity of the skin to a cut or prick, relaxation of the jaws and anal sphincter, and sometimes sensation caused by manipulating the ear may be used to decide whether an animal is conscious or not. 

Q - When is an animal considered to be anesthetized? 

Normally, when it is unable to stand, or fails to react to the other tests. However, in light anesthesia, these reflexes vary somewhat. 

Q - Does the gasping of an animal after it has left the gas chamber indicate that the animal is not anesthetized? A No. This is a respiratory reflex due to the excessive build-up of carbon dioxide or the extreme lack of oxygen. It is generally accepted that carbon dioxide in high concentrations is a narcotic agent which depresses the upper centers of the brain. As carbon dioxide builds up in the blood, the animal breathes harder in an attempt to get rid of the carbon dioxide and get more oxygen. 

Q Can other gases be used to produce anesthesia? 

A Yes. Nitrogen is also being studied as a possible agent.

QUESTION 

How can we tell whether an animal is conscious or unconscious, and how can we judge what slaughtering methods will render an animal unconscious with the least disturbance of the senses? ANSWER 

In answering these questions, we must rely to a considerable extent upon our observations of the animal's reactions to the slaughtering procedure. These observations can be classified as either subjective or objective. 

Q - What are subjective observations?

A- 

Subjective observations involve the personal judgment of the observer as he studies a particular behavior reaction. This type of observation has definite limitations since an individual doesn't see the same thing in the same way during a series of observations. 

Another weakness lies in the fact that early observations relative to a certain procedure will bias later observations of that same procedure. An even greater problem lies in the fact that other people can't duplicate these results. For example, even though several people may observe the same thing, reactions to what they actually saw will vary and the account of each will be different from the others. 

Q - What are objective observations?

These are observations based upon a pattern of reaction where a definite value can be applied to the number of times an animal will react in a certain way, or where we can measure in such terms as centimeters, grams or seconds.

Q - Why are objective observations relative to animal behavior so important? 

A It is difficult to observe an animal after it has been stunned and base any conclusions on how it is behaving. For that reason, the use of anatomical and physiological observations of the animal's nervous system are important in assessing consciousness.

Q 

What tools are available for making an objective study of the animal's nervous system?

A The electroencephalograph, used to detect and record brain waves, and the electrocardiograph, used to record heart action, are both valuable in determining as accurately as possible whether the animal is conscious or unconscious. There are other fairly objective methods involving stimulation of a certain part of the animal's body which can be used to determine the levels of anesthesia for surgery or other purposes. 

However, since sometimes these reflexes are gone because the animal's muscles are paralyzed, it is especially important that determination of the state of consciousness be based upon as many observations as possible. 

Q - What are some other methods of determining the state of consciousness? 

A This would include measuring of blood pressure, pulse rate, rate of respiration and by measurement of the adrenal gland secretion. For example, if the blood pressure doesn't increase when an animal is given a standard stimulus, it might be assumed the animal is unconscious. However, in order that the conclusion be as objective as possible, these tools should be used in combination rather than alone. 

Q - What happens when insensibility is caused by concussion, penetration and a combination of the two? 

A Studies of concussion indicate that changes occur in the nervous cells themselves, altering the nervous cell so that it cannot receive or transmit nervous impulses.

Q - What physical factors are involved in rendering an animal unconscious by means of concussion?

A- In terms of presently available equipment, the mushroom or hammer type stunner must generally produce an acceleration type concussion. 

Penetration types of concussion are perhaps more effective because when they penetrate the brain they most certainly will cause a change in intercranial pressure, as well as actually causing brain tissue destruction. 

Q - Does this type of concussion produce unconsciousness?

Unconsciousness is not always produced. For example, a bullet may be fired through portions of the skull and the animal may not notice it at all in terms of lack of consciousness. In other words, the effect depends to a great extent upon the areas to which the instrument is applied and the amount of damage to brain cells.

QUESTION - What types of devices are available for stunning animals for slaughter?

ANSWER 

- There are two distinct types of stunning. One type of instrument stuns but does not penetrate the skull and the other penetrates the skull and goes into the brain. 

What is the hammer or pole-axe method of stunning?

In the past the hammer has been the principal means of stunning cattle. With this method the hammer is swung manually and is used to strike the animal on that portion of the skull where a sharp blow will cause insensibility. 

Q What is the Remington stunner? 

A This is a cartridge-operated device that uses a mushroom-type head. As a triggering mechanism touches the skull the cartridge is exploded and the mushroom head contacts the vulnerable part of the skull. The resulting impact stuns the animal. This type of stunner does not penetrate the skull and does not render the brain inedible from the standpoint of contamination. 

Q Are there other versions of the Remington stunner? 

A Yes. One version is operated similar to the mushroom head stunner except that the mushroom head has been replaced by a penetrating pin about 3 inches long which actually enters the skull and brain. 

A second version uses a combination of the mushroom head and the penetrating pin to produce insensibility. 

Q What is the Schermer stunner? A This is a cylindrical, cartridge-operated penetrating bolt device which was developed in Germany where it has been used for a number of years in stunning livestock. Instead of being fired through contact of the pin with the animal's skull, the Schermer stunner is manually fired by means of a trigger mechanism. 

Q What is the Cash-X pistol? 

A This is a pistol-shaped, manually discharged stunning device which uses a penetrating type of captive bolt propelled by an exploding cartridge.

Q 

What is the Thor air gun? 

A This is a modification of the conventional air hammer. It has a penetrating captive bolt that utilizes compressed air as a propellent. Unlike the other cartridge-operated stunners, which must be reloaded after each firing, the Thor air gun is operated by an air compressor which permits instant refiring. 

Q - Are other stunners available which operate on similar principles?

Yes, other stunners are available.

Q - What other methods are available for immobilizing animals for slaughter? 

A The carbon dioxide chamber is currently being used to anesthetize hogs. 

Q What are the advantages of using carbon dioxide ? 

A This method delivers a limp, anesthetized animal that can be stuck more easily and accurately than a conscious animal that has been shackled and hoisted by its hind leg. 

Q - Is carbon dioxide currently being used by slaughterers in the United States? 

A Yes. Several packers are using this method, and it has been used successfully in Denmark and Holland. 

What other methods are being used to slaughter livestock? 

A Sheep, swine, calves, and goats are generally shackled, hoisted, and suspended head down for sticking and bleeding. This is still the most common method of slaughter for these particular animals. 

Q - Are any animals shot before slaughter? 

A Yes. All horses and a few cattle are rendered unconscious by shooting before bleeding. 

Q Is this method likely to come into common use?

A No. While it is an effective stunning device, it isn't likely to be 

commonly used because of the hazard to employees.

HIGHLIGHTS OF MEETING ON HUMANE SLAUGHTER RESEARCH 

Chicago, December 3, 1958

Representatives of the meat packing industry pledged their full cooperation and support in supplying the U. S. Department of Agriculture with information developed by the industry on the problems of humane slaughter of livestock.

The packers also stated that they intend to continue their own investigations, which are aimed at establishing humane slaughtering methods. 

It was brought out that, under provisions of the Humane Slaughter Law passed by the 85th Congress, the Secretary of Agriculture must designate, by March 1, 1959, methods of slaughter that will be considered humane. Packers whose products may be offered for sale to the 

Federal Government have until July 1, 1960, to put these methods into operation.

The Chicago meeting was called by USDA's Agricultural Research Service and was attended by representatives of humane organizations, major meat packers, and the meat packers' associations, Department of Agriculture representatives, and consultants on special phases of humane slaughter.

Members of the Advisory Committee who attended were T. H. Broecker, Donald MacKenzie, John C. MacFarlane, Rutherford T. Phillips, Dr.

James R. Hay, and Dr. C. D. Van Houweling. All members of the committee were notified that the meeting was to be held and that they would be welcome to attend if convenient although it would not be an official advisory committee function.

Discussion centered around methods of mechanical and electrical stunning and gas anesthetization for the purpose of rendering animals insensible before slaughtering.

In his opening statement to the group, Dr. Van Houweling, speaking as 

Assistant Administrator of the Agricultural Research Service, pointed out that the Department of Agriculture has carefully reviewed current slaughtering methods in this country for the purpose of determining those areas that need further investigation. He also stated: "We are confident that the Department can designate methods of humane slaughter that will lend themselves to adoption by American industry. But how well these methods can be adapted to industry's slaughtering processes will depend in large part on the amount of effort expended by industry itself to anticipate some of the problems and work out solutions as they relate to plant facilities and details in applying the methods."

The slaughtering of sheep and swine with the use of carbon dioxide gas and the handling in connection therewith, in compliance with the provisions contained in this section, are hereby designated and approved as humane methods of slaughtering and handling of such animals under the act. (a) Administration of gas, required effect; handling. (1) The carbon di March 3, 1959 oxide gas shall be administered in a chamber in accordance with this section so as to produce surgical anesthesia in the animals before they are shackled, hoisted, thrown, cast, or cut. The animals shall be exposed to the carbon dioxide gas in a way that will accomplish the anesthesia quickly and calmly, with a minimum of excitement and discomfort to the animals. (2) The driving or conveying of the animals to the carbon dioxide chamber shall be done with a minimum of excitement and discomfort to the animals. 

Delivery of calm animals to the anesthesia chamber is essential since the induction, or early phase, of anesthesia is less violent with docile animals. Among other things this requires that, in driving animals to the anesthesia chamber, electrical equipment be used as little as possible and with the lowest effective voltage. (3) On emergence from the carbon dioxide chamber the animals shall be in a state of surgical anesthesia and shall remain in this condition throughout shackling, sticking and bleeding. Asphyxia or death from any cause shall not be produced in the animals before bleeding. 

General requirements for gas chamber and auxiliary equipment; operator. (1)

The carbon dioxide gas shall be administered in a chamber which accomplishes effective exposure of the animal. Two types of chambers involving the same principle are in common use for carbon dioxide anesthesia. They are the "U" 

Type chamber and the "Straight Line"

Type chamber. Both are based upon the principle that carbon dioxide gas has a higher specific gravity than air. The chambers open at both ends for entry and exit of animals and have a depressed central section. Anesthetizing carbon dioxide concentrations are maintained in the depressed central section of the chamber. Effective anesthetization is produced in this section. Animals are driven from holding pens through a pathway constructed of pipe or other smooth metal onto a continuous conveyor device which moves the animals through the chamber. They are moved onto the conveyor through a manually or mechanically operated gate. Each animal is compartmentalized on the conveyor as he enters the chamber by impellers synchronized with the conveyor. 

Surgically anesthetized animals are moved from the chamber by the same continuous conveyor that carried them into and through the carbon dioxide gas. (ii) Flow of animals into and through the carbon dioxide chamber is dependent on one operator. The operation or stoppage of the conveyor is entirely dependent upon this operator. It is necessary that he be skilled, attentive, and aware of his responsibility. Overdosages and death of animals can be brought about by carelessness of this individual. (2) Special requirements for gas chamber and auxiliary equipment. The ability of anesthetizing equipment to perform with maximum efficiency is dependent on its proper design and efficient mechanical operation. Pathways, compartments, gas chambers, and all other equipment used must be designed to accommodate properly the species of animals being anesthetized. They shall be free from pain-producing restraining devices. Injury of animals must be prevented by the elimination of sharp projections or exposed wheels or gears. 

There shall be no unnecessary holes, spaces or openings where feet or legs of animals may be injured. Impellers or other devices designed to mechanically move or drive animals or otherwise keep them in motion or compartmentalized shall be constructed of flexible or well padded rigid material. Power activated gates designed for constant flow of animals to anesthetizing equipment shall be so fabricated that they will not cause injury. All equipment involved in anesthetizing animals shall be maintained in good repair. 

(3) Gas. Maintenance of a uniform carbon dioxide concentration and distribution in the anesthesia chamber is a. vital aspect of producing surgical anesthesia. This may be assured by reasonably accurate instruments which sample. and analyze carbon dioxide gas concentration within the chamber throughout anesthetizing operations.

Gas concentration shall be maintained uniform so that the degree of anesthesia in exposed animals will be constant. 

Carbon dioxide gas supplied to anesthesia chambers may be from controlled reduction of solid carbon dioxide or from a controlled liquid source. In either case the carbon dioxide shall be supplied at a rate sufficient to anesthetize adequately and uniformly the number of animals passing through the chamber. Sampling of gas for analysis shall be made from a representative place or places within the chamber and on a continuing basis. Gas concentrations and exposure time shall be graphically recorded throughout each day's operation.

Neither carbon dioxide nor atmospheric air used in the anesthesia chambers shall contain noxious or irritating gasses. 

Each day before equipment is used for anesthetizing animals, proper care shall be taken to mix adequately the gas and air within the chamber. All gas producing and control equipment shall be maintained in good repair and all indicators, instruments, and measuring devices must be available for inspection by Division inspectors during anesthetizing operations and at other times. A suitable exhaust system must be provided to eliminate possible overdosages due to mechanical or other failure of equipment. § 180.15 Mechanical; captive bolt.

The latter type is also described as a concussion or mushroom type stunner.

Penetrating instruments on detonation deliver bolts of varying diameters and lengths through the skull and into the brain. Non-penetrating or mushroom stunners on detonation deliver a bolt with a flattened circular head against the external surface of the animal's head over the brain. Diameter of the striking surface of the stunner may vary as conditions require. Unconsciousness is produced immediately by a combination of acceleration concussion and changes in intracranial pressures. A combination instrument utilizing both penetrating and non-penetrating principles is acceptable.
