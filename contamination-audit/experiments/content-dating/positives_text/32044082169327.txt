The History of Acquisition Cost in New York By FRANCIS R. STODDARD Introduction 

Acquisition cost is one of the most important matters affecting the insurance business. It concerns the public as well as all companies, agents and brokers. State Insurance Departments, especially that of 

New York, have given the most careful attention to it. The interest of state insurance supervising officials in acquisition cost springs from a desire on the part of these officials to perform the primary and basic duty of their office, namely, to protect public interest. This is attained by keeping the insurers solvent and at the same time by seeing that the rate charged to the public is not made excessive by too large acquisition costs.

I have tried to give the history of the procedure followed by the various Superintendents of Insurance of the State of New York as they performed their duty of protecting the interests of the citizens of the State of New York through the application of expense control. All of the actions described herein were taken primarily to benefit New York State citizens. The actions, however, have been extended to other jurisdictions and have benefited citizens of other states. 

The results of the actions of the New York Superintendents of Insurance, which are recorded in this history, insofar as New York State is concerned have been excellent. The principle of limitation of acquisition cost is generally accepted by both insurer and producer. In 

New York it is rare to find an insurer or producer rejecting the limitations as they have been established. This is true notwithstanding

the fact that competition for business between companies is exceedingly keen in the state. The natural result of that competition, in the absence of a limitation of acquisition cost, would be a constant and progressive increase in expenditures for acquisition. Serious abuses have been avoided in New York. That this is true should be attributed to the skillful supervision exercised by the Insurance Department of New York. 

It is also a tribute to the company executives who, through the Acquisition Cost Conferences, have supported the acquisition cost program originated by the New York Insurance Department. 

It is advisable that this history be written at this time when the men who originated the Acquisition Cost Conferences and who have been active in making a success of acquisition cost control in New York State are almost all here to give the facts and to verify this account written by me. Now is the proper time to prepare this record for the future.

Chapter I ACQUISITION COST DEVELOPS AS PART OF THE RATING STRUCTURE 

In giving a history of acquisition cost it is important to define what acquisition cost means. The term acquisition cost is used by members of the industry and rating organizations to mean the total production cost which may be said to be the total marketing expense. In the 

National Rules Regarding Acquisition and Field Supervision Cost for 

Casualty Insurance the term is given a special meaning, which, however, applies to that branch of the business only. In referring to acquisition cost herein I shall use the term as it is generally understood in the business which is total marketing expense. 

Next to the pure premium, or that part of the premium dollar which is supposed to pay the loss, acquisition cost is the largest item of expense in the rate. Of course, the agent and broker are entitled to be paid adequately for the valuable services they render but the rights of the insured must also be protected. One of his rights is that none of the premium paid by him shall go for needless waste. In other words, if the agent or broker is paid more than his services are worth, there is a waste and the public is put to a needless expense. In a report of the New York Insurance Department staff to Superintendent Pink dated

November 23, 1942, and published in Volume I of the New York Insurance Reports for 1943, pages 74a to 88a, there is an excellent history of rate regulation. I have taken from it the following: "Prior to 1900, legislation dealing with fire insurance rating had been chiefly in the form of anti-compact laws which had been adopted in a number of other states. The development of the Analytic Schedule and the Universal Schedule provided a means of obtaining legislation which in effect either brought about the repeal of anti-compact laws or superseded them by the recognition of rating and inspection bureaus subject to some measure of supervision by state authorities. The justification for the associations of companies, aside from the savings in expenses, was that the correct application of the schedule would remove to a large degree the possibility of discrimination between risks. involving the same hazards, exposures, and protection against fire. "The first rating law in this state was enacted in 1911 as Section 141 of the Insurance Law. This enactment followed the investigation made by a joint legislative committee commonly referred to as the Merritt Committee. It required associations or bureaus making fire 3

insurance rates to file their rates and schedules with and to be subject to examination by the Superintendent of Insurance. Rates were required to be non-discriminatory and were not to be made contingent upon the placing of all or any part of a risk with members of a particular bureau or association. Provision was made for information to be available to the insured and for an opportunity for appeal to the governing committee of the rating organization. "The law was amended in 1912 and in 1913, extending rate regulation to surety bonds and also giving the Insurance Department supervision over auxiliary organizations assisting underwriters in making and maintaining of rates, establishing rules and standards, etc. (Sections 139 and 140.) “There was no requirement in these early laws for regulation as to reasonableness of rates. The only test was discrimination. It is evident that rates could be non-discriminatory as between risks having the same degree of hazard but at the same time the rates for all risks in a given class could be inadequate or excessive. "During the period from 1909 to 1914 many states enacted laws recognizing rating organizations and including anti-discrimination provisions similar to those contained in the 1911 New York Law. In 

1909 Kansas enacted a rating law which also gave the Superintendent authority to order a change in rates where they appeared to be excessive or inadequate. This power was upheld by the United States. 

Supreme Court in 1914 (German Alliance vs. Lewis, 233 U. S. 389). 

In 1911 Texas passed a law providing for the making of rates by a state board. Texas is the only state wherein fire insurance rates are made by the state. "The rating laws were changed in 1922 following the investigation made by another joint legislative committee, known as the Lockwood Committee. Rate regulation was extended to other lines of insurance. 

The term 'rate' was defined to include all the elements and factors forming the basis for computing the consideration for insurance. 

Rating organizations were required to admit to membership or furnish their service without discrimination to any insurer and were prohibited from adopting rules which would prevent the payment of dividends by mutuals. The Superintendent could review the rates from time to time and order an adjustment when he found that the rates on any class of risks produced an excessive or inadequate profit. Companies were required to file their experience in accordance with classifications approved by the Superintendent. A company could apply to the 

Superintendent for a uniform percentage of deviation from the rates of the rating organization of which it was a member or subscriber. "Marine and transportation risks were exempted under the 1911 law, and this exemption still continues. "The regulation and control of scales of commissions paid to producers are not part of the functions of a rate-making organization. 

Such matters are handled either by voluntary associations of companies or by membership agreements. The top limits of acquisition costs for various lines of business, are, however, taken into consideration as part of the expense elements in the determination of rates."

When the companies first sought to make rates they also sought to control commissions at the same time. In 1891 the Tariff Association of New York was formed, but it was dissolved about May 1898 because of dissentions and mutual charges of bad faith which rocked the fire insurance business of that day to its depths. The Cyclopedia of New 

York in the United States (1898-1899) describes the event as follows: 

"For the next three months (after the dissolution of the Tariff 

Association) chaos reigned in fire insurance rates in New York.

Commissions to brokers ran up at first to 40 per cent. Outstanding business was cancelled and rewritten at almost any rate offered. A number of companies refused to join in the scramble and ceased to do a local business. The demoralization affected the whole country and after the companies had suffered to the extent of about 60 per cent in the premium receipts of the metropolitan district the sobering influence of these losses began to be apparent. . . . 

It was the above trouble which led to the formation of the New 

York Fire Insurance Exchange on March 8, 1899. The attempt of the companies to control their acquisition cost by voluntary agreement was not an entire success. Many companies had no fixed rates of commissions. Many companies paid the commissions which would get or hold business and the basis for commission rates was entirely competitive. Some companies paid more for business than it was worth so as to force weaker companies to meet the competition and to be injured thereby. Some agents and brokers shopped their business around to see which company would pay them the highest commission. 

New companies raided the agency force of their competitors by offering excess commissions, which offers would be met by a general raising of the rates by the older companies. Some companies paid higher acquisition cost in certain States than in others even though the agents and brokers rendered equal service. The number of agents was unlimited and every producer, whether or not a legitimate agent, could command general agent's commissions. Almost all brokers received agents' commissions. The payment of such excess commissions was not for the benefit of insureds, companies or brokers. They were all hurt by it. 

It seemed that some force or influence greater than that of the companies was necessary to make each company obey an acquisition cost agreement made by it. Unless every company obeyed the agreement and paid the same rates of commission, the whole acquisition cost structure might fail because producers might be tempted to send their business to the company offering them the highest commissions. 

The acquisition cost situation was decidedly out of control in New York State at the time that Mr. Emmet became Superintendent of 

Insurance.

Chapter II SUPERINTENDENT EMMET TAKES ACTION 

At first, Insurance Departments hesitated to tell companies what they should pay for the acquisition of business. The relationship between a company and its agent was considered to be personal to them, and state authorities hesitated to dictate what the terms of employment should be. Insurance Departments soon realized, however, that something must be done to eradicate abuses which grew up in the business. 

While an insured in the case of life insurance knew the name of the company with which he was insured, experience showed that in the case of fire companies the insured relied upon the broker or agent to select a company and that the insured practically never knew the name of the company. If he did know the name of the company, it made no impression on him. When the casualty and surety companies developed their business, the same situation existed, namely, the insured left it to the broker or agent to select the company. This arrangement might have been a good one if the broker or agent always selected the best company. Many brokers and agents did so. Many brokers or agents, however, selected the company which paid them the highest commission and a situation developed where companies were in effect bribing a middle man in order to get him to give to them a large volume of business.

In the last analysis the companies did not pay the extra expense except at first. The acquisition cost expense was put into the rate by whatever party made rates and higher acquisition cost would be reflected in higher rates the next year. From the standpoint of the public the situation needed control. Many superintendents or commissioners of insurance avoided the problem.

The State of New York has always been a leader in the field of insurance and it was there that the acquisition cost problem became acute. In 1913 Chapter 816 of the Laws of 1913 was enacted which placed for the first time a workmen's compensation law in effect in New York. There was some question about the legality of its passing so that the entire workmen's compensation law was re-enacted in Chapter 41 of the Laws of 1914 while I was a member of the New York State Assembly and was thus given an opportunity of casting my vote for

this very excellent measure. The law in effect required all employers to take insurance and there was an enormous increase in the premium volume of the casualty companies. At the same time there were advocates of a compulsory state fund which would eliminate private insurers. 

The companies were offering such large commissions for this business. that the cost to the insurer was much more than it should be and the passage of a compulsory state fund act was imminent. New York Superintendent Emmet decided that the matter could no longer be permitted to drift and he thereupon made a decision which furnished the beginning of the control of acquisition cost. 

The situation may best be described in his own words as contained in the Fifty-Fifth Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York, dated February 9, 1914, and published in part I of the 1914 New York Insurance Reports, pages 15 to 19, under the caption "Limitation of the Acquisition and Administration Cost of Liability Insurance:" 

"It is the duty of the Superintendent of Insurance of New York State not only to know, or at least try to know, that the various companies under his supervision are solvent, but also to see that their business is conducted in such a manner that they will remain solvent. 

This is quite as important in the case of companies engaged in the business of liability insurance, as it is in the case of any other kind of insurance organism. A liability policy is supposed to protect the assured not merely against claims which mature during the life of the policy, but also against claims which may mature years after the policy expires as the result of an accident which happened during the policy year. Obviously, therefore, complete protection of the assured requires that the company which issues the policy shall remain solvent not only during the life of the policy, but for a number of years afterward. "With the desire to comply fully with the spirit as well as the letter of the New York law as above outlined, this department has for some time past been keeping watch over a number of matters of administrative detail in connection with the business of these companiesmatters as to which, narrowly construed, the law gave us no specific authority to interfere in. In 1911 it was felt that conditions in the liability field necessitated the sending of a pointed reminder to the various liability companies doing business in New York State that their administrative practices were under close departmental observation. A study of the company statements which were then being rendered to this department showed pretty conclusively that the companies were writing liability business at a premium insufficient to take care of losses and expenses. The expenses chargeable against this class of business, especially those incurred in the acquisition of new business, were excessive. Competition was the basis for underwriting and the same influence was responsible for high acquisition cost. This

condition was brought vividly to our attention during an examination of a liability company in January, 1912. The report on this examination contained recommendations to the effect (1) that the commissions paid by the companies to agents and brokers for the getting of new business should be materially reduced; (2) that investigations should be set in motion into the comparative cost in the long run of pursuing a policy of adjustment as against one of litigation; and (3) that liability rates, which had apparently become inadequate, should be increased. "Conditions improved for a while as a result of departmental activities such as these, but the stress of competition soon forced the situation back to its former unsatisfactory state. The returns for the year 1912 indicated pretty plainly that a continuation of these unsound business methods would imperil the solvency of some of the companies. In spite of this situation, a proposition was made in June, 1913, by the brokers of New York City to the Casualty Insurance

Exchange, providing for an increase in commissions on employers' liability business from 15 per cent to 20 per cent. Our attention was called to this proposed increase, through a notice which appeared in the public press, and we informed the exchange under date of June 10th, that this department most strongly condemned any action which would tend to increase the expenses chargeable against this class of business. I thereupon became convinced, after inquiry and investigation, that the situation was sufficiently acute to warrant action. 

On June 20th, I addressed a letter to all authorized companies, calling for reforms in underwriting methods for a reduction in acquisition and administration expense. This action received the indorsement of the National Convention of Insurance Commissioners, and on September 12th a letter was sent out, fixing October 1st as the date the directions would become effective. A limit of 17½ per cent was placed upon the acquisition expense of workmen's compensation business and 20 per cent for the liability business, covering all lines, and applying to all business written in this country. Immediately, widespread interest in our action was manifested from all parts of the United States. It was generally conceded that the limitation of 17½ per cent for workmen's compensation and of 20 per cent for employers' liability was reasonable and fair. A strong and united protest was made against the application of the 20 per cent limitation to the miscellaneous liability lines. It was pointed out that the premiums covering these lines are, on the average, small, and that these classes of insurance are seldom sought by the purchaser, but require active and earnest solicitation. Statements were made to the effect that the endorsement of our rulings would mean the elimination of certain lines, as far as future business was concerned. The agents in the 

Rocky Mountain district called attention to the enormous size of their territories, which were sparsely populated, and the heavy expenditures necessary for the transaction of the ordinary business of their agencies. "We had early expressed a desire to remedy any injustice that might result on account of the enforcement of our rulings. For the purpose of having a full and free discussion on the various points at issue,

a meeting was called for on December 1st at Chicago and representatives of associations of agents and brokers, company executives and supervising officials were invited to be present. The request that I should call this meeting was made to me by the Insurance Commissioners of the states of Colorado and Utah, who by reason of local conditions were deeply interested in the subject under consideration. Upwards of three hundred agents and company officials from every section of the country were in attendance, as well as Commissioners of Insurance from twenty states. This meeting resulted in a modification of my original directions, as far as they affected. the miscellaneous liability lines and recognition was given to the peculiar conditions in the Rocky Mountain district. "The directions now effective are as follows: "1st. Underwriting to be based upon general statistical experience, modified by the physical and moral hazard of each individual risk, and free from the influences of competition. "2d. Commission on on Workmen's Compensation and Employer's

Liability business in Workmen's Compensation states not to exceed 172% of the premium; in other states commission on Employer's 

Liability business not to exceed 20% of the premium; in all states commission on all liability business other than Workmen's Compensation and Employer's Liability not to exceed 25% of the premium; except that on Employer's Liability business in the Rocky Mountain zone, embracing the states of Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, New 

Mexico, Arizona, Utah and Idaho, a commission not to exceed 25% of the premium may be permitted. "3d. The acquisition expense on Workmen's Compensation and 

Employer's Liability business in Workmen's Compensation states not to exceed 17% of the premium; in other states the acquisition expense on Employer's Liability business not to exceed 20% of the premiums; in all states the acquisition expense on all liability business other than Workmen's Compensation and Employer's Liability, not to exceed 25% of the premium; except that on Employer's Liability business in the Rocky Mountain zone, embracing the states of Montana, Wyoming, Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona, Utah and Idaho, an acquisition expense of not more 25% of the premiums may be permitted. "4th. Administration expense to be minimized. "This action marked a new departure in the history of insurance supervision. We were aware that the liability companies were conducting their business at a loss and that a continuation of these methods would result in disaster and imperil their ability to meet their contract obligations. The means at hand to remedy this situation were availed of and the results sought have been accomplished. All of the companies licensed in this State have complied with our directions and the agents and brokers throughout the country, through their organizations, have accepted and acquiesced in our regulations. The liability business is now conducted upon sounder lines than heretofore, in consequence.

"Personally, however, let me say that I am not, under ordinary circumstances, an advocate of government by ukase or edict. This method should only be used, if at all, in emergency cases or where some grievous injustice has been done. It should never be otherwise regarded than as a mere temporary expedient. Situations calling for permanent treatment should be cured by legislation. The New York 

Insurance Law contains no specific provisions regulating rates and expenses of casualty companies. In the case I have mentioned, the discretionary power of the Superintendent of Insurance proved to be sufficient for the enforcement of reforms the need for which was pretty widely recognized inside the business itself. Perhaps other emergencies can be met successfully, if they should arise, in similar fashion. 

In the long run, though, the proper regulation of the business of workmen's compensation and liability insurance unquestionably requires the enactment of well-considered legislation upon this question of acquisition and administration expenses, as well as upon the other questions of the adequacy of reserves and of rates which I have touched upon in this report."

Superintendent Emmet's action received approval from most of the insurance departments in other states. Insurance commissioners had finally recognized that something must be done to control needlessly mounting acquisition costs. Superintendent Emmet died before he could. see the full effect of his action. He was succeeded as Superintendent of Insurance by Frank Hasbrouck on March 27, 1914. The latter was succeeded on July 1, 1915 by Jesse S. Phillips, who appointed me Second 

Deputy Superintendent of Insurance in charge of the New York City

Office of the New York State Insurance Department on August 1, 1915.

Chapter III SUPERINTENDENT PHILLIPS STRONGLY SUPPORTS THE EMMET RULING 

The action started by Superintendent Emmet spread to other states. 

At a session of the Insurance Commissioners held on September 27, 1916, Commissioner W. K. Chorn of Missouri, introduced the following resolution which is set forth on page 144 of the Proceedings of the National 

Convention of Insurance Commissioners, Forty-Seventh Session, 1916: 

"Be it hereby Resolved, that "Whereas, it is thought that all casualty and surety companies do not operate upon a uniform basis of productive cost for casualty and surety business, and "Whereas, it is thought that the productive cost of securing certain lines of casualty and surety business by certain companies is generally higher than that of other companies writing the same character of insurance in the same territory, and "Whereas, it is thought that certain casualty and surety companies permit their business in one State to cost them more than they allow as productive cost in other States after duly allowing for changed conditions, and "Whereas, the productive cost of all casualty and surety business is an essential element used by actuaries in ascertaining the adequacy of rates, and "Whereas, the rates for said lines of business should not be higher than a reasonable productive cost would warrant, and "Whereas, the Insurance Commissioners of all of the States in the United States are interested in the general welfare of the purchasers of casualty and surety insurance in their territory, and "Whereas, their rule providing for a twenty per cent, (20%) maximum productive cost of all employers liability business has proven. satisfactory and beneficial to both the public and the companies, and "Whereas, it is now thought that the benefits of this ruling should be extended to all lines of casualty and surety business other than employers liability, "Now, therefore, be it Resolved, That the Insurance Commissioners here represented, appoint through its chair or a committee of three (3) to consider the question of placing a maximum productive 11

cost on all lines of casualty and surety business, and report their findings at a meeting of the commissioners to be held at on of December, 1916."

The resolution was referred to the Committee on Fidelity and Surety 

Companies and apparently died there.

When I became Second Deputy Superintendent of Insurance in charge of the New York Office of the New York State Insurance Department, one of the first matters that was brought to my attention was the abuses in connection with acquisition cost. During my term as Deputy 

Superintendent of Insurance under Superintendent Phillips the Department took every measure necessary to sustain the Emmet ruling. Superintendent Phillips went even further than Superintendent Emmet by trying to have the commissions for workmen's compensation business limited by statute to 10%. At the Adjourned Meeting of the Insurance 

Commissioners Convention held on April 18, 1917, he introduced the following resolution with the following remarks which are printed on pages 97 and 98 of the Proceedings of the National Convention of 

Insurance Commissioners, Forty-Eighth Session, 1917, as follows: 

"Resolved, That it is the sense of this Convention that the prevailing acquisition expense in connection with workmen's compensation insurance is excessive and that the opportunity for economy lies chiefly in a reduction in the commission payable for obtaining risks involving large premium payments. "And, be it further Resolved, That this question be referred to the 

Workmen's Compensation Insurance Committee of this Convention. 

for investigation and report." "President Phillips: Mr. President and members of the Convention, as you know, the New York Department has given considerable attention to the question of the compensation rates, and the result was that a rate increase became effective as of March 31. In the investigation it was called to the attention of the committee that the entire expense was about 38 per cent of the premium income, that is, in the State of New York. I think that the average throughout the country is lower than that. There is paid 17 per cent to the agent for commission. It occurs to me that where the State, by legislative enactment, compels its citizens to insure, and that is our law, that it ought not to take very much money to induce that employer to take out insurance. Our statute provides that if he fails to take out compensation insurance he is subject to criminal prosecution. It occurs to me and to those who have made a special study of this question, that it ought not to cost 17½ per cent to have such a risk written. I recommended in the annual report to the Legislature that the statute be amended in our State, limiting the commissions or acquisition cost to 10 per cent. "As I expected, that immediately started a howl by all the agents, and I had a conference at Albany in my office, at which were repre- 12

sentatives of the different agency systems throughout the country, and they claimed that the commission received is not solely for the writing the business, but for the service which they actually render the insured by inspecting and looking over his risk and making certain recommendations by which he can secure a reduced rate. At that conference, which was about two or three weeks ago, I stated that action would be deferred, so far as the State of New York, is concerned, and at that time I received telegrams from various commissioners asking that the matter be deferred until some action could be taken by the Convention. At that conference those attending suggested that probably there might be an investigation of the entire expense question, possibly the other end of the expense question might be considered. That is, that portion of the expense charged at the office for examinations, etc. 

They agreed that during the year they would endeavor to get in touch with a committee representing the managers of the companies and in that way some good might result. "I simply make this statement now, as long as we have this regular standing committee on the question of Workmen's Compensation Rates, that it might be advisable to adopt a resolution that the committee investigate and perhaps report at the September Convention." 

In his address as President, printed in the same volume on pages 111 to 122, Mr. Phillips referred to acquisition cost of compensation insurance as follows: 

"In view of the fact that so many of the larger industrial States have adopted compulsory workmen's compensation laws, and other States will soon follow their example, the question of the amount paid for acquiring this insurance is one of grave importance. "The State of New York, for instance, by statute (and many of the States now have similar statutes), compels every employer of labor to carry compensation insurance, except in such cases where the employer possesses sufficient financial strength to carry his own risk, and of this fact he must satisfy the public authorities. These cases are exceedingly few. The State, therefore, has arbitrarily imposed upon the employer the obligation to insure; if he fails to assume it, he is subject to criminal prosecution. "Under these circumstances, it seems to me that the employer should not require very much persuasion to induce him to carry compensation insurance. In spite of this, 1712 per cent of the premium is paid as a commission to the agent or broker. Heretofore, there seems to have been no concerted action whereby acquisition expenses could be reduced, and no company apparently was willing to take the initiative. "In my report to the New York Legislature of this year, I recommended the enactment of a statute limiting the acquisition expense to an average of 10 per cent of the premiums. This recommendation immediately brought a protest from the various casualty agency associations throughout the country, and, as the result of a conference with the committee representing such associations, I agreed to defer action 13

so far as our Legislature was concerned until next year. 

The question, by an appropriate resolution, was presented at the spring meeting of this convention, and same is now having most careful consideration. The companies and their agents have taken the position that it is not a matter which should concern the insurance departments whether acquisition cost is too high or otherwise, and in various conferences which I have had with their representatives upon the subject, they have pointed to the fact that in the State of New York stock companies do not have a monopoly of the business. This statement is technically true, but it is also true that the business transacted by the stock companies in that State constitutes substantially 80 per cent of all the compensation insurance. It costs the New York employer approximately sixty cents for every dollar of compensation paid to an injured employee and his dependents. This amount, of course, includes administration expenses of all kinds, factory inspections, expenses of claim investigation and adjustment, and total acquisition cost. "It is interesting to note that the expense ratio of the Wisconsin mutuals is 3412 per cent, of which the acquisition expense is a trifle over 7 per cent; the Massachusetts mutuals, 24.2 per cent with an acquisition cost of 1.6 per cent. I may be wrong in my conclusion, but these figures clearly indicate to me that workmen's compensation insurance can be conducted more efficiently and with greater economy than at the present time. The fact that interested parties are opposed to economies does not satisfy the demand of society for a more efficient system. The entire question is one of relative economy, and if properly understood will arouse interest among employers. The agent claims that the employer is entirely satisfied with the present rates, when, as a matter of fact, the employer has very little information upon that subject. The latter simply complains of the excessiveness of the rates in general. It is claimed by the agents that the amount of the commissions paid to them is hardly commensurate with the service which they render to the insured. They contend that they are not only required to write the insurance, but are largely charged with the responsibility of inspecting the risk, and also with the duty of adjusting the claims in the event of loss under the policy. If this is the true situation, then there must be a duplication of effort on the part of the company, for there is maintained within each company, at considerable expense, persons specially charged with the duty of inspecting risks and making adjustments. No doubt the agent renders considerable useful service to the employer, but it is difficult for me to believe that it is necessary to consume 1712 per cent of the premium to convince the employer that it is his duty to fulfill the requirements of the statute; in fact, there should be little or no expense paid out for soliciting business where the insurance is made compulsory by the State. It might, with equal propriety, be argued that every citizen must pay an annual fee to a third person for the purpose of being reminded to observe the criminal statute. It is not my purpose to belittle the usefulness of the agent as an intermediator between the insured and insurer. My criticism is rather directed to the existing schedule of remuneration, 14

which appears to me at least to be excessive for the service demanded under a compulsory system. “A material saving in the acquisition expense could, in my judgment, be accomplished by the adoption of a graduated schedule of remuneration, which would enable producers of small units of business to obtain higher rates of commissions, while the producers of larger units upon which large premiums are collected would be compelled to accept a lower rate, and still yield a return commensurate with the services rendered. A reduction in acquisition cost would have two immediate results: It would discourage a certain class of undesirable agents and brokers, and it would produce substantial reductions in the price paid by the employer for insurance. "Let us hope that the representatives of the companies, and of the agents, with the co-operation of the insurance commissioners will be able to devise a plan whereby a substantial reduction in the total acquisition cost can be effected without the necessity of legislation. We must, however, not lose sight of the fact that compulsory workmen's compensation is entirely in a different class than the other lines of insurance. The State, by statute, has forced it upon the employer, and it is the duty of the State authorities to aid in securing such insurance at the lowest possible expense."

Meanwhile abuses relating to acquisition costs had been prevalent in other lines. The Insurance Department tried to get the companies to keep the situation under control but with little success. Superintendent 

Phillips resigned and I was appointed to succeed him as Superintendent of Insurance on December 1, 1921.

Chapter IV SUPERINTENDENT STODDARD FORCES THE ORGANIZATION OF THE CASUALTY ACQUISITION COST CONFERENCE 

When I became Superintendent of Insurance, one of the worst difficulties confronting me was the matter of acquisition cost. Every possible abuse seemed to be present. The companies met of their own volition in many efforts to control the situation but always without success. At that time there were many leaders in the insurance field who were both powerful and temperamental. The meetings of the companies were noted for violent attacks by certain company executives on each other.

By 1922 five separate national organizations issuing rules controlling acquisition cost for one or more types of casualty and surety insurance were in existence. These organizations only partly succeeded in controlling acquisition cost.

It was not necessary for me to take any action in relation to acquisition cost until October 1922, when one of the leading companies offered a series of extra bonuses for burglary business. The situation was acute. The other companies threatened to equal or surpass in liberality the bonus offer of the first company unless the Department acted at once. I, as Superintendent of Insurance, immediately summoned a conference of representatives of the casualty and surety companies, which met on October 16th, 1922. At that meeting I warned the companies that if they did not regulate the cost of acquisition and other causes of differences, the situation would give rise to publicity and to a possible legislative investigation led perhaps by radicals, who would work for their own aggrandizement and not for the good of the insurance business. I said that I did not wish the State to tell the companies what they ought to do. I said that the insurance business should regulate itself. I urged the companies to "put their house in order." I told the persons present that companies were paying commissions much larger than were generally suspected and that in a case which was brought to my attention the agent's commission in a certain class of business was 65% of the premium paid by the insured. As a result representatives of the casualty and surety companies met on October 24, 1922, at the office of 16

the National Bureau of Casualty and Surety Underwriters, at which meeting those present pledged that steps would be taken to eliminate all excesssive acquisition cost in casualty and surety lines. Chapter VII SUPERINTENDENT VAN SCHAICK TAKES VIGOROUS ACTION IN CONNECTION WITH ACQUISITION COST 

Superintendent Conway was succeeded on January 1, 1931, by Thomas F. Behan, who had been Deputy and Acting Superintendent since July 1, 1930. Superintendent Behan died on February 16, 1931.

Superintendent Van Schaick made an address to assembled casualty, surety, fire and marine company executives, agents and brokers, which was released to the papers of May 8, 1931, as follows: "This meeting has been called for the purpose of stating the attitude of the Superintendent of Insurance toward conditions prevalent in your business. We shall discuss undisputed facts concerning which you must have full realization. There has been placed before me by my staff reports which fully justify the imposition of penalties set forth in the law as punishment for evil practices. Specifically, the insurance law provides that insurance companies may adopt premium rates and underwriting rules for their guidance in the transaction of business. These rates and rules, whether adopted individually or by associations of companies, must be filed with the Insurance Department and strictly adhered to, unless thereafter legally modified. It is a function of the Superintendent of Insurance in the matter of rates, to keep them free from unfair discriminations and adequate enough to meet the obligations under insurance contracts without being unreasonable. These duties of the Superintendent can be satisfactorily performed when rates are properly filed and conscientiously observed by all companies, agents and brokers. "When a material part of the insurance business is transacted on an illegal basis through the use of insurance rates and coverages not sanctioned by law, the Superintendent of Insurance is frustrated in attempting to carry out the obligations imposed upon him to maintain a high degree of equality in the costs assessed against the insuring public and in preserving the financial condition of insurance companies. 

It is of course understood that within recent years there has been an 26

increase in the number of insurance companies and in the capitalization of older companies, which has been provocative of vigorous competition in the business. These conditions must not be permitted to result in serious injury to a business whose obligations are as sacred and as vital to the welfare of the nation as is the insurance business. "There is reason to believe that many insurance companies are either actively or passively participating in the practice of writing insurance contracts at premium rates and on bases differing from those which they are obliged to observe both legally and morally. 

Legally, this is so because of the filing of the rates and forms with the Insurance Department under a law which requires the observance of these filings. Morally, because they are invariably adopted by common agreement with their associates and contemplate justice to all assureds and the unstinting service of the company in carrying out its contractual obligations. "It is true that the investigation now in progress could proceed without issuing a warning and the Department could proceed to invoke the penalties in the law against companies which are found to be violating the law. These penalties can be made sufficiently severe to act as a deterrant to the illegal practices. It is probably true, however, that some of those who are taking part in competitive rating are doing so in self-defense in an endeavor to protect their business from the raids of unscrupulous competitors. "Without in any way condoning past offences, the Department proposes to give all insurance companies until June 1, 1931, to make a thorough inspection of their underwriting records for the purpose of correcting all policies that have been improperly written. All policies must be corrected to the proper legal rate of premium and correct basis of coverage as of the inception date of the policy, and any additional premiums due thereunder must not only be billed but every effort made to enforce collection thereof. After June 1, 1931 there will be conducted as thorough an examination of the underwriting practices in the insurance business as the facilities of the Insurance Department will permit. It is likewise the intention to summon before the Department the officials of any company found to be continuing the practices of wilfully violating the provisions of their rate filings, and to invoke against such company such proper penalties as the law provides. "Action in this connection is motivated primarily by an interest in the welfare of the insuring public who are by no means properly served by an insurance business lacking in ethical standards and a sense of responsibility to treat all its policyholders on a basis of equality. "Thus far nothing has been said regarding the abuses to which the charter powers and exemptions enjoyed by marine companies have been put. Cases have come to the attention of the Department where marine companies have apparently exceeded their charter powers and have used the exemptions which were primarily allowed them because of alien competition, to divert business from properly licensed channels. 

It is not necessary at this time to go into the details of these transgressions. For the time being, the marine men are cautioned to carefully 27

study the writing powers conferred upon their companies and not to abuse a proper conception of the English language for the purpose of procuring a misconception of the meaning and intent of the law. It would be inconceivable that the insurance law should provide that one class of insurance company may transact its business subject to regulation of its rates while another class of company writing the same kinds of coverage should enjoy the competitive advantage of freedom from regulation; nor was that the intent of the law. "There is one more condition which gives serious concern. That is the use of unreasonably high proportions of premium income for the purpose of alienating business from competitors. This subject, under the title of Acquisition Costs, was studied last year by the National Convention of Insurance Commissioners. In the concluding paragraph of its report, the Convention committee appointed to study the subject, stated in effect that unless the insurance companies took proper steps to eliminate the wastefulness in acquisition costs, the supervising officials would be met with a demand from the public that legislation be enacted to cure the existing evils. An overpayment of commission to a producer has the same effect upon an insurance company as the illegal cutting of rates. It produces a deficiency in the company's income, so that it cannot meet on a proper basis the obligations assumed under its policy contracts. The result is one or more of the following conditions. A disposition to be niggardly and overtechnical in the adjustment of losses; insufficiency and inefficiency in the quality of service furnished the public; and a diminishing of the security underlying the policies. "In the casualty and surety field the Insurance Department assists the company conferences designed to regulate commissions and other acquisition costs. Undoubtedly some of the companies who fail to give their full, wholehearted co-operation to these self-governing bodies would be the first to cry out against legislation designed to curb existing abuses. They would likewise be the first to complain against the action of the supervising official who would act to reduce premium rates for the purpose of forcing a discontinuance of competitive abuses. 

You are therefore asked to support the activities of your own conferences, in order that state control be avoided. "The fire insurance business has not sought the assistance of the Insurance Department in enforcing inter-company agreements in the matter of acquisition cost. If you see fit to consult the Department, every effort to aid in every reasonable manner will be extended. If, with or without the assistance of the Department, you fail to conduct your business on a proper and economical basis, you will likewise be faced with either serious economic problems in your business or enlarged state control. "In conclusion, each of the groups represented here today, who deal with various phases of the insurance business, is requested to select from its number, representative men who may form a committee for the purpose of dealing intelligently with the problems confronting its particular field. To these committees is offered the assistance of the 

Insurance Department and the Department will be glad to delegate a 28

representative of the Department to participate in the meetings, as an ex officio member. The companies and associations here represented can do much toward improving conditions that exist in the business today. For the present the burden of reform is in your hands. The results which you shall obtain or fail to obtain will accomplish a great deal toward determining the future of the insurance business not only in this state, but elsewhere."

On January 22, 1932, Superintendent Van Schaick delivered an address to casualty and surety executives at the State Office Building in Albany as follows: 

"Acquisition cost control by insurance companies themselves is hanging in the balance. Company control is desirable just so long as the public is safeguarded and no longer. Insurance Companies exist not for themselves but for the public. Since 1922 in the casualty field, and since 1926 in the fidelity and surety field, there has been an abundance of talk and promises as to excessive costs. The result is far from satisfactory. Unless the companies are willing to translate that talk into action it may as well be promptly thrown into the discard with legislative control as the alternative. "Upon taking office as Superintendent of Insurance I called the companies together and asked support by the companies of their respective acquisition cost control conferences. As long as it was a mere generality this suggestion received widespread approval. After a sufficient period I asked the acquisition cost conferences to file with the Department the results of their years of deliberation. This has been done. There was immediately a loud and widespread protest. It was only the mere suggestion of enforcement that completely changed the picture. "Rates are not made by the Department. They are made and filed by the companies. The power to pass upon their adequacy, their reasonableness and any discriminatory features is then with the Department. In passing upon the adequacy of rates the continued solvency of a company is the basic inquiry. Insurance is worthless unless the company is able to pay. "In passing upon the reasonableness of rates this Department must pass upon the reasonableness of every factor making up the rates. 

The two largest factors constituting a rate are loss experience and production cost. To consider one and not the other is an absurdity. 

If the public is to be protected from exorbitant insurance costs some measure of control must be had over production cost which is controllable. "The power of this Department to insist on a limit to production cost is challenged by certain companies. The Department accepts the challenge. If it has not the power, as asserted, its jurisdiction over the reasonableness of rates is an idle gesture. If the law is 36

defective the sooner the courts pass upon it the better. If acquisition cost be variable then a rate adequate today may be inadequate tomorrow. If acquisition cost may change from reasonable to unreasonable overnight the public would be helpless. A rate once approved is expected to continue for a reasonable time. It cannot be reviewed. from day to day. "There has just been promulgated increased rates for automobiles as to personal liability and property damage. Contrary to popular conception this Department did not make those rates. It did approve them as to adequacy and reasonableness in the light of the experience of companies, which experience was before the Department. 

It has made the approval conditional. It looked to the experience of companies as to acquisition cost as reflected by the rules limiting acquisition cost made by the companies themselves, filed with the Department and analyzed by the Department. Its approval was conditioned upon the companies using these rates pledging observance to the acquisition cost rules of the Conference on Acquisition Cost for Casualty Companies. "The custom has grown up for companies in the casualty and surety fields to incorporate their experience as to necessary costs of the acquisition of business in the form of acquisition cost rules. These rules form the best standard yet made for the guidance of state departments. They have received the close study and scrutiny of the National Convention of Insurance Commissioners and its approval. 

They constitute an expression of the best thought and experience extant upon the subject. They will be accepted by the Department as a reasonable basis upon which to found its approval of rates. That does not mean that they cannot be corrected if harsh or erroneous in any particular. The companies who made them must correct them. 

If the Department attempted to do so the companies would be the first to challenge its power and jurisdiction. "It is unnecessary to discuss whether the Department could have compelled this filing. It is sufficient to know that the rules have been filed pursuant to request. The conferences of the companies which made these rules have repeatedly asked the help and cooperation of the Insurance Departments to the end that their rules as to acquisition cost be made effective. The request for filing under Section 140 of the Insurance Law was in response to that appeal for help and cooperation. As a result of the filing this Department has the benefit of the best judgment and experience of the companies and supervising officials which has been accumulated over a long period of time. It follows naturally that this Department will make such use of these rules as is legal and desirable. "It may be clearly understood that henceforth approval of rates by this Department is to be tied to a specified limit of acquisition cost. 

This may be arrived at preferably by the companies themselves and approved by the Department. In other words the pledge as to acquisition cost limit will be a part of the rate filing. It is only in this way that the Department can keep rates within a reasonable limit. 37

In the present state of economic depression the public is more interested than ever before in having insurance rates kept at a reasonable level. Not only is the public entitled to it but it insistingly demands it. "The Superintendent of Insurance is under a statutory duty to report annually to the Legislature the condition of the Insurance Department, the status of the companies under its jurisdiction, together with recommendations for the strengthening of the law and the promoting of the public interest. The Legislature is now in session. 

Such a report is about to be made. The Legislature is entitled to know how successful has been the effort of the companies at selfgovernment in the matter of acquisition cost. It is easy to see that if the companies are going to make good in the matter of their own control over this important factor of the business they must forthwith demonstrate their ability to do so. This is the purpose of this conference. The result of this conference will show whether the years of labor in the casualty and surety fields beginning in 1922 to the present time will end in total failure to succeed. The time has passed when the situation will admit of partial success and partial failure."

In the Seventy-Seventh Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York, dated January 15, 1936, and published in Part I of the 1936 New York Insurance Reports, pages 61 and 65, Superintendent Pink mentioned acquisition cost as follows: 

"ACQUISITION METHODS "General Need of State Control 

"The State exercises a certain degree of control over the methods of acquiring business in part because these methods may have some effect upon the financial condition of the companies. Primarily it is probably because the concept has become widespread that it is a proper function of state supervision to protect the insuring public from unfair treatment and to prevent for the benefit of the industry itself certain practices which are generally regarded as unfair competition. There are those who feel that a greater degree of selfregulation would be desirable in these matters. As a theory it is difficult to assail this point of view. The department is always sympathetic toward voluntary action upon the part of the carriers and progress has been made in this direction in the past few years. For a variety of practical reasons, however, the matter must be controlled by statute and administrative supervision." 

"ACQUISITION COSTS 

"The phrase 'acquisition cost' as used in the fire, casualty and surety fields represents the total funds expended by companies for the production of business and is usually expressed as a ratio in terms of premiums written. This includes the commissions paid to agents and brokers, the expenses of operating production branch offices, salaries and expenses of traveling supervisors, advertising, and other 42

items of expense, in acquiring business. To a degree the term 'acquisition cost' is a misnomer because commissions paid to agents and brokers cover not only their compensation for placing the business but also the service which they render their assureds. In some cases such service is extensive. "In the making of rates acquisition costs of a definite amount are considered as factors. If these allowances are exceeded in actual practice, the result will probably be that the rates will be inadequate, that reductions otherwise warranted will be withheld, or that rates will be increased. These possibilities are so vital that the influence of acquisition costs cannot escape a measure of supervisory control so as to keep them within reason. "Rating organizations, underwriters associations and conferences exist throughout the fire, casualty and surety business for the express purpose of regulating commissions and other acquisition costs paid by members. These voluntary organizations perform a useful function and their importance should not be underrated. If competition should be given a free hand with business being bought by the highest bidder, such chaos would exist that it would become necessary for the Legislature to fix standard rates of allowable commissions. "State Supervision of Acquisition Costs "The Conferences limiting acquisition costs in the casualty and surety fields have solicited the support of State supervisory officials in enforcing the standards they have adopted. The cost regulatory bodies in the fire insurance business operate through agreements and the support of their members with the state authorities watching their progress so that in the event of a breakdown in this voluntary control proper measures may be taken to prevent injury to the insuring public." 

Superintendent Pink soon found that the Insurance Department must take active steps to limit acquisition cost of fire business. While my account primarily concerns casualty and surety acquisition cost, nevertheless the part that the New York Insurance Department took in regulating the New York Fire Insurance Exchange is interesting as showing the fact that the New York Insurance Department considered that one of its duties was the limitation of acquisition cost through organizations which the Department forced the companies to form and to join. Superintendent Pink mentions acquisition cost and methods in the Seventy-Eighth Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York dated February 8, 1937, and published in Part I of the 1937 New York Insurance Reports, pages 50 to 53, as follows: 

"Excess Commission Investigation "One of the most important matters which has engaged the attention of the Insurance Department during the past year has been an attempt 43

to eliminate the practice of paying excess commissions to producers on fire insurance business. In this activity the Department has had the full cooperation of the officers of the New York Fire Insurance Exchange, the company organization which regulates and controls rates of commission payments in New York City. "Although there is no direct statutory power vested in the Insurance Department to regulate commissions paid for the acquisition of insurance business, complaints and rumors received by the Department during the year impressed the Superintendent with the necessity of taking affirmative action as a means of avoiding a chaotic condition in the fire insurance business in New York City. In the spring of 1936 the Superintendent addressed a letter to the Exchange calling attention to the situation which apparently existed and asking the Exchange, as a self-regulatory body for the fire insurance business, to take whatever appropriate steps might be necessary to curb the practice. In this letter it was suggested that unless a solution could be effected the Department would be forced to the conclusion that the fire insurance rates charged in New York were too high, thereby permitting the payment of excess commissions out of extraordinary profits and that the problem might be solved through rate adjustments. "The Exchange immediately pledged its wholehearted cooperation and appointed special counsel to conduct an investigation with a view to instituting disciplinary measures against offending members of the organization. The Department conducted an independent investigation and as a result thereof became convinced that the rumors regarding excess commissions were well founded. Having this first hand information, the Superintendent requested that he be afforded an opportunity to address the members of the Exchange on the subject. 

He appeared before them on June 24, 1936. In his address the 

Superintendent made specific recommendations for a program, part of which called for action by the Exchange and part by the Department, to effect a permanent solution to the problem. His recommendations were as follows: "Suggested Program for the Exchange "The rules of the Exchange provide for the fining of members. 

Experience seems to indicate that the organization should have wider disciplinary weapons and broader powers to deal with misconduct on the part of its members. "The members of the Exchange are now pledged to that body to carry out its rules and regulations. It is suggested that the Exchange prepare a suitable form of additional pledge directly to the Insurance Department to be signed by those member companies and agents who will agree not to pay or accept any commissions in excess of those allowed by the rules of the Exchange. This pledge should be made directly to the Department and should be kept on file in the Insurance Department. "While excess commissions are usually paid by agents, the fault for the present condition cannot be entirely laid upon the shoulders of 44

the agents and brokers. The companies are equally responsible. They have it within their power to refuse representation to any agent or agency which pays excess commissions. The company members of the Exchange should be pledged to terminate and refuse representation to any agent or agency found guilty of paying excess commissions. "The Exchange should promptly notify the Insurance Department of all violations or suspected violations of its rules relating to the payment of excess commissions so that the Department may make independent investigation into the facts and take such disciplinary action as to it may seem best. "In its investigation it has been brought home to the Department that some agents pay a rebate directly to the assured on direct business written by them. This is encouraged by the fact that agents receive not only their agency overwriting but the broker's commission as well. when they do direct business. The Exchange should give consideration to this problem and available means of solving it, such as by a reduction in commissions or direct business of agencies. "Program of the Department "The Department will cooperate to the fullest extent with the Fire Insurance Exchange and its members in their efforts to prevent the payment of excess commissions. "The Department appreciates the difficulty which confronts the Exchange in connection with companies which are not members and have given no pledge with regard to excess commissions. It will therefore use its best efforts to secure the acquiescence of any company outside of the Exchange to the commission rates, adopted by the Exchange. The Department has already made efforts in this direction and has secured satisfactory assurances. "While the Department will not and cannot lawfully condone the acts of any brokers or agents or any company in the payment of excess commissions in the past, its general policy will be not to cite companies, agents or brokers for past offenses unless required to do so by some exceptional circumstance. It will, however, from now on forthwith cite any licensed broker or agent guilty of receiving or paying excess commissions to show cause why his certificate of authority should not be revoked and in case any such citation is issued past infractions of the rules will also be considered. The Department will also promptly take action against any company found guilty of paying or countenancing payment of excess commissions from now on. "In case the measures adopted by the Exchange and by the Department are not effective in curbing the payment of excess commissions appropriate amendments to the Insurance Law will be sought to extend the authority of the Superintendent of Insurance directly over acquisition costs with particular reference to the payment of commissions. 

In case of the failure of the members of the Exchange to comply with the rules and regulations we shall also make a general survey of the effect of excess commission payments on the general fire insurance rate structure to the end that fire insurance rates may be so revised 45

that the payment of excess commissions cannot be continued by any who give serious thought to the effect of their acts upon the financial stability of the companies and the agencies. "Later in the year the Exchange imposed drastic penalties against several member agents found to have violated the rules of the Exchange with respect to the payment of commissions to brokers. A portion of the fines assessed against these agents was suspended by the Exchange when the violators pledged future compliance with the rules. "If such pledges are broken in the future the suspended portion of the penalty will be assessed. Meanwhile the matter of the power of the Insurance Department in connection with acquisition cost came to the fore in connection with a refusal of Superintendent Pink to renew the license of the Northwestern National Insurance Company, which was organized under the laws of the State of Wisconsin, to do business in the State of New York. 

In the year 1939 Superintendent Pink gave to the company a provisional license under which the company was authorized to do business in New York until April 30, 1940. The conditional renewal of the 

1939 license provided in substance that in the event the company did not discontinue the payment of excess commissions within sixty days, its certificate of authority for the year beginning April 30, 1939, and ending April 30, 1940, would be revoked. The company proceeded under Article 78 of the Civil Practice Act to review the act of the Superintendent but because of delay in presenting the question to the Court the full period of time for which the license was issued had terminated and the proceedings were dismissed on the ground that they had become academic. In the Matter of the Application of Northwestern National Insurance Company vs. Pink, 261 App. Div. 944.

Thereafter Superintendent Pink refused to renew the license of the company for the year beginning April 30, 1940. The right to review the action of the Superintendent in refusing to issue a renewal of a license is provided in Sub-division 7 of Section 40 of the New York 

Insurance Law. 

The case was not a strong one because the Northwestern National 

Insurance Company had never joined any Acquisition Cost Conference and had never promised to obey the acquisition cost rules. Notwithstanding the unofficial advice of the members of the State Insurance Board, Superintendent Pink determined to proceed with a test of his powers. The result was that under date of June 27, 1941, the Appellate 

Division of the First Department by a vote of three to two annulled the determination of the Superintendent upon the grounds that the 47

Legislature had granted no direct authority to the Insurance Department over the rates of commissions to be paid to the Insurance brokers. The 

Court held that while the Superintendent stated that he did not wish to fix commissions or to assume any authority not fairly intended by the statute, nevertheless his refusal to issue the license had the result of placing the stamp of approval of the Superintendent of Insurance upon the schedules of commissions which were fixed by the New York Fire Insurance Exchange. In other words, it seemed to the Court that the Superintendent had done something indirectly which he concededly had not the power to do directly. Matter of Northwestern 

National Insurance Company vs. Pink, 262 App. Div. 216. 

The matter went to the Court of Appeals and was argued on March 11, 1940. A decision was not handed down until July 29, 1942, when a divided court, four to three, held that the Superintendent did not have the power to refuse to admit the company upon the grounds stated. The court in effect held that the Superintendent could not prevent a foreign insurer from doing the business of fire insurance in New York unless it adopted the rate of brokerage commissions formulated by the voluntary agreement of the members of the New York 

Fire Insurance Exchange. Matter of Northwestern National Insurance 

Company vs. Pink, 288 New York 359. 

The Northwestern National Insurance Company decision meant in effect that if the Superintendent of Insurance was going to fix commissions either directly or indirectly, such power should be given to him by statute.

Chapter X LIFE INSURANCE EXPENSE LIMITATIONS 

In the foregoing description I have spoken at length concerning casualty and surety acquisition cost limitations imposed by the State of New York and also have referred to limitations on the acquisition cost of fire insurance. I have not referred herein to life insurance. 

In the year 1906, Chapter 325 was enacted into law and became 

Section 97 of the New York Insurance Law. This section provided by statute a limitation of expenses for life insurance companies. The 

Section has been many times amended. At the present time it is Section 

213 of the New York Insurance Law, and places a direct limitation on the expenses of a life insurance company. 

Superintendent Phillips mentions Section 97 in the Sixty-Second Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New 

York dated February 28, 1921, and published in Part I of the 1921 

New York Insurance Reports, page 8, as follows: 

“Limitation of Expense 

"Section 97 of the Insurance Law properly limits the amount which domestic life insurance companies doing business on the participating plan can expend for the acquisition and conduct of their business, no large company being permitted to expend an amount in 'excess of the aggregate amount of the actual loadings upon premiums received. in said year calculated according to the standards adopted by the company under section 84 of the Insurance Law and the present values of the assumed mortality gains' therein mentioned. "The law providing for limitation of expenses was a direct result of the work of the Armstrong Legislative Investigating Committee, and to my mind is the most effective piece of legislation enacted in connection with such investigation. It has eliminated a great many of the abuses existing prior thereto, and has prevented the companies from making exorbitant expenditures in their zeal for new business and their desire to obtain a competitive advantage over their rivals, and nothing should be done to destroy the effectiveness of the provision of the law upon that subject. "The Legislature, however, saw the necessity of making a slight distinction between a small and a large company. It recognized the fact that a small company struggling to build up its business might require a larger margin for expenses than the large and well-established 51

company, and therefore granted to a company having less than $80,000,000 of insurance in force a slight additional percentage upon its expense loadings of the preceding calendar year based upon the amount of insurance such company had in force. During the last few years, there has been a material increase in the necessary expenses of the management of the companies. The price of material and supplies has advanced. There have been additional agency expenses, and in many instances it has been necessary to increase salaries in order to maintain an efficient office organization. For this reason, some of the smaller companies will have considerable difficulty to conduct their business properly and still keep within the expense limitations of the statute. It must be borne in mind that the expense loadings are in the fixed premiums and there is no way in which these premiums can be increased, and there is no other source from which expenses can be paid. It is therefore absolutely necessary that some relief be given to the smaller companies, the growth of which should be encouraged. The situation is not applicable to the large companies. I therefore recommend an amendment to section 97 giving companies having less than $100,000,000 of insurance in force a slightly increased expense margin, so slight, however, that it will not be a violation of the principles underlying the expense limitation law." 

At the meeting of the National Convention of Insurance Commissioners held on September 27, 1928, it was suggested that a comparison of the acquisition cost of the companies subject to the expense limitations of Section 97 and of companies not authorized to do business in New York and consequently not subject to such expense limitations would be made. The information obtained was tabulated. The result showed that the first expense rate of the companies not authorized to do business. in New York was about 57% higher than the expense rate of the companies authorized to do business in New York. Not a single company doing business in New York had a first year expense rate as high as 75.5% which is the average for the companies not authorized to do business in New York.

Superintendent Albert Conway, in the Seventieth Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York dated March 

11, 1929, and published in Part I of the 1929 New York Insurance Reports, states on page 12 that these figures show conclusively that the expense limitations and other provisions of the New York Insurance Law have constituted one of the major factors in controlling acquisition expenses and in the remarkable progress of life insurance since the 

Armstrong investigation. 

Superintendent Pink, in the Seventy-Seventh Annual Report of the

Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York dated January 

15, 1936, and published in Part I of the 1936 New York Insurance 

Reports, says in part on Page 65: 52

"One of the outgrowths of the Armstrong investigation of life insurance conditions more than thirty years ago was legislative control over the expense of acquiring new business. Section 97 of the Insurance Law prescribes in detail the limits within which first year and total expenses must be confined and also prescribes the maximum first. year's and renewal commissions which can be paid to agents." 

The limitations contained in the present Section 213 of the New York Insurance Law are carefully watched and enforced by the New York Insurance Department. Under this control, the life insurance companies in New York State have grown and prospered in volume of business. and in financial stability until today one of them represents the largest accumulation of assets possessed by any private institution in the world. 53

Chapter XI BAIL BOND ACQUISITION COST LIMITATIONS 

Another branch of insurance where the State of New York has controlled acquisition costs is that of bail bonds. When I was Superintendent of Insurance I found an intolerable situation in which the public. suffered greatly from improper rates and excessive charges. I mentioned the abuses in the Sixty-Third Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York, dated March 13, 1922, and published in Part I of the 1922 New York Insurance Reports, pages 16 and 17. The result was a strengthening of the Insurance Law and the adoption of Section 554 of the Code of Criminal Procedure. Super intendent Pink had more trouble, which he describes in the Eighty- 

Second Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance of the State of New York, dated March 17, 1941, and published in Part I of the 1941 

New York Insurance Reports, pages 63a and 64a as follows: 

"Bail Bonds "As a result of an investigation of bail bond underwriting practices made by this Department during 1940, the Towner Rating Bureau filed a flat term rate of $20 per thousand in both Federal and New York State Courts. Previously the Federal Court bail bond rate was $20 per thousand for one year and the New York State Court bail bond rate was $30 per thousand for the term. At the same time the bail bond affidavit used pursuant to the requirements of Section 554-c of the Code of Criminal Procedure was amended to show a breakdown of the amount paid to the surety company as premium and that paid to the attorney-in-fact or bail bond agent as additional compensation.

Bail bond abuses have been especially hard on the very poor. Many a poor woman, agonized because her husband or son has been placed in jail, has given up her life savings for a bail bond when acting under the stress of emotion. If it were not for the strict supervision of bail bond acquisition costs and rates, a situation would soon develop which would furnish a major scandal. 54

Chapter XII APPROVAL OF RATES BY THE INSURANCE DEPARTMENT Section 184, Sub-division 5 of the New York Insurance Law is as follows: 

"Rate filings for workmen's compensation insurance, for motor vehicle insurance required by law, and for surety bonds given in lieu of such required motor vehicle insurance, shall not be made effective by any rating organization or insurer until they have been approved by the Superintendent."

The effect of the foregoing is to cause the Insurance Department not only to approve of the rate filed for workmen's compensation insurance, for motor vehicle insurance and for surety bonds given in lieu of such required motor vehicle insurance, but also to pass on every item which goes.to make the rate including acquisition cost. This is a much greater power than passing merely on the adequacy or reasonableness of a rate or on whether it discriminates. The Department really fixes the acquisition cost. 55

Chapter XIII DUTIES AND POWERS OF THE SUPERINTENDENT OF INSURANCE 

The basic duty of the Superintendent of Insurance is to protect public interest. Under Section 21 of the New York Insurance Law the Super intendent is given the power to prescribe in writing official regulations not inconsistent with the provisions of the Insurance Law affecting any power given to him under the provisions of the law. Also he is empowered to interpret the provisions of the law. 

All the history of the supervision of acquisition cost as hereinbefore related is a record of an attempt on the part of the various superintendents to carry out the basic duty of the Superintendent under the law. 

The Supreme Court of the United States, in the case of O'Gorman & 

Young, Inc. vs. Hartford Fire Insurance Company, 282 U. S. 251, held that the state has the power to regulate and limit acquisition cost, citing as their reason that public interest is involved. The German Alliance 

Insurance Company vs. Lewis, 233 U. S. 389, and LaTourette vs. 

McMaster, 248 U. S. 465, also sustain the proposition that insurance is an essential part of the commercial activities of our people, that it is business impressed with public interest and thereby subject to reasonable regulation by the State. Such regulation is within the police power of the State.

The Superintendents of Insurance of the State of New York have not assumed authority to fix commissions to agents or producers which is a part of the total acquisition cost, but in New York State, under the rating law, authority has been given to the Superintendent of Insurance to pass upon and regulate all factors and elements which form part of a promulgated rate. The authority seems to cure most of the abuses. 

In the State of New York, the power of the Superintendent of Insurance is conveyed in the Insurance Law, which is Chapter 28 of the Consolidated Laws. Article II of the Insurance Law provides for the organization of the Insurance Department. Section 10 of the Article provides that the head of the Department shall be the Superintendent of Insurance, who shall be charged and invested with the rights, powers, and duties as expressed or reasonably implied by the chapter in connection with the business of insurance in New York. 56

There are general powers of the New York Insurance Department which can be applied to influence companies.

For instance, paragraph 

40, subdivision 4 of the New York Insurance Law provides as follows: 

"The Superintendent may issue a license to any insurer to do in this state the kind or kinds of insurance business for which such insurer is qualified under the provisions of this chapter and under its charter .. The Superintendent may refuse to issue or renew any such license if in his judgment such refusal will best promote the interests of the people of this state." 

In the case of a foreign company, Section 40, subdivision 6 of the 

New York Insurance Law, provides as follows: 

"The Superintendent may revoke any license previously issued to any foreign or alien insurer to do business in this State, if after notice. to, and hearing of such insurer, he finds that such insurer has failed to comply with any requirement imposed upon such insurer by the provisions in this chapter and if in his judgment such revocation is reasonably necessary to protect the interest of the people of this State.” 

Section 42, subdivision 2, provides: "The Superintendent shall issue a renewal license to any foreign or alien insurer if he shall be satisfied, by such proof as he may require, that such insurer is not delinquent with respect to any requirement imposed by this chapter and that its continuance in business in this state will not be hazardous or prejudicial to the best interests of the people of the state."

The power of the Superintendent to control acquisition cost under these last three sections is limited by the Northwestern National Insurance Company decision hereinbefore referred to at length. 

The authority of the Superintendent concerning rates and rating organizations is contained in Article VIII of the Insurance Law. Section 

180, Subdivision 1, of the New York Insurance Law, provides that the term "rate" as used in Article VIII shall include, as the context may require, either the consideration to be paid or charged for insurance contracts including guaranty or surety bonds, or the elements and factors forming the basis for the determination or application of the same, or both. Surely the commission paid for business is one of the elements or factors forming the basis for the determination of the rate. Section 180, subdivision 2, of the New York Insurance Law, provides that the making of rates, within the meaning of the article, shall include the making, approving, or maintaining of rates. Section 180, subdivision 3, of the 

New York Insurance Law, provides that the filings made pursuant to section 184 shall be known as rate filings. 57

Section 180, subdivision 6, of the New York Insurance Law, pro vides that every association which formulates rules, establishes standards. or assists in the making of rates for the information or benefit of insurers or rating organizations in the transacting of insurance business on property or risks located in this state, or furnishes information or advice to insureds or prospective insureds, shall be known as a service organization. The Acquisition Cost Conferences are service organizations. 

Section 182 of the New York Insurance Law provides that every service organization shall file with the Superintendent of Insurance a copy of its constitution, articles of agreement, or association, or incorporation, and the by-laws and all amendments thereto, under which it operates or proposes to operate and such other information as the Superintendent may require. 

Section 181 of the New York Insurance Law describes the rating organizations, and Section 183 of the New York Insurance Law describes rate making. Section 184, subdivision 1, states that every rating organization and every authorized insurer shall file with the Superintendent upon his request every rate manual, etc. and all other information concerning the application and calculation of rates made or used by it. The commissions fixed by the Conferences are used by the rating organizations in formulating the rates. 

Section 184, subdivision 4, of the New York Insurance Law, provides that if the Superintendent finds that any rate filings filed with him do not comply with the provisions of the article or that they provide rates or rules which are inadequate, excessive, unfairly discriminatory, or otherwise unreasonable, he may order the same withdrawn. 

Section 184, subdivision 6, provides that an insurer may apply to the Superintendent for approval on its behalf of a uniform percentage decrease or increase in the rates established and published by a rating organization of which it is a member or subscriber. Section 186 of the 

New York Insurance Law provides that the Superintendent may investigate rates to find out whether they are excessive, discriminatory, inadequate or unreasonable. 

While the Northwestern National Insurance Company decision holds that the Superintendent of Insurance has no power to fix commissions, either directly or indirectly, nevertheless whether or not the Superintendent has any right to fix commissions, he certainly can insist that a 58

company live up to its agreements, and that if a company has submitted itself to the jurisdiction of the Conference, it must obey the rules of the Conference, especially in view of the fact that its chief executive has signed a pledge that such rules will be obeyed. While theoretically a company may resign any time from either Conference, nevertheless it is submitted that the company may not deprive such Conference of jurisdiction where jurisdiction has already taken effect merely by sending in its resignation if it does not like what the Conference has ruled. 

On June 5, 1944, the Supreme Court of the United States handed down a decision in the case of the United States of America against South-Eastern Underwriters Association, which held that the business of insurance is commerce between the states. The Polish National 

Alliance decision was also handed down. These decisions upset precedents which had been in effect for seventy-five years. By these decisions insurance became subject to the anti-trust laws. 

Immediately the question arose as to how far the Sherman Act, the 

Clayton Act, the Robinson-Patman Act, and the Federal Trade Commission Act affected insurance and especially those organizations which had been formed to make rates and to control acquisition cost under state statutes.

The decision in the South-Eastern Underwriters Association case only affects transactions of an interstate nature. There is no reason to expect or to assume that the authority of the Insurance Department of New York in its interpretation and application of the laws of the State of New York will be materially affected. 59

Chapter XIV ASSOCIATIONS AND TRADE ORGANIZATIONS 

In 1936 in the Seventy-Seventh Annual Report of the Superintendent of Insurance, Part I, pages 72-73, Superintendent Pink states: "It has been said that no business is as thoroughly organized as the insurance business. Each branch of the business has its own associations and these are national, sectional, state and local in scope." 

As evidenced by the above quotation, the Insurance Department of the State of New York has recognized the existence and operation of various associations and trade organizations. It is concerned with their procedure and their practice to the end that the public interest may be served and that at no time shall public interest be jeopardized. 000
