The Executive Board herewith submits its report for your consideration, During the 1955-56 biennium, the Board has endeavored to concentrate the work of the various committees through the assignment of specific subjects for study. By assigning fewer topics for study and centering attention upon major problems facing South Dakota State government, the Board believes a more thorough job of research and study has resulted. 

Five standing committees and a special Economy and Efficiency Committee were established to carry out the work of the Council. These standing committees are as follows: 

Committee on Agriculture and Conservation Committee on Assessment, Taxation and Finance Committee on Education, Health and Welfare Committee on State and Local Government Committee on Transportation 

The Special Tax Study Committee was appointed in June 1956 to study the theory of general property taxation and its relation to the 1955 county assessment law. The committee recommendations and supporting data are included in detail in the respective committee reports, copies of which have been sent to legislators, interested organizations and public libraries throughout the State. 

Council committee meetings have been held throughout the past two years. 

The Executive Board has held quarterly meetings as required by statute. Meetings have been open to the public.

REVIEW OF STUDIES AND SURVEYS MADE BY LEGISLATIVE RESEARCH COUNCIL DURING 1955-1956 BIENNIUM INTRODUCTION 

The Executive Board, in carrying out its statutory obligations of instigating research and formulating a legislative program, has endeavored to concentrate Council activity upon major points of state-wide interest. At the beginning of the 

1955-56 interim, the Board solicited and received many proposals for study from legislators, state agencies, private organizations and individuals. These were reviewed by the Board and referred to the standing committees. During the interim, additional subjects were assigned for study. A special committee was established in mid-1956 to study the property tax and its relationship to the county assessment act, (Ch. 422) enacted by the 1955 Legislature.

AGRICULTURE AND CONSERVATION 

The Board recognizes that next to her people, the greatest assets of South Dakota are her natural resources. The State should do all that is necessary to insure that these resources are adequately conserved and put to the best possible use. 

Council study during this interim has been directed toward improvement of state services to agriculture and to the development of watershed legislation. 

Primarily, state services to agriculture are channeled through the Department of Agriculture and through the facilities of South Dakota State College. It is important that the services offered agriculture by these agencies do not overlap or duplicate the work of the other. It is also important that essential services be performed at a level commensurate with the need. 

One of the first lines of defense in the preservation of our water and soil resources is through action at the local watershed level. Through the cooperation of governmental agencies on all levels and particularly among residents residing in a local watershed, the Board believes that appropriate steps can be taken to insure that maximum benefits be obtained from our natural resources through sound conservation practices. gram.

ASSESSMENT, TAXATION AND FINANCE 

One of the basic elements of good government is a sound state fiscal pro- The financing of government on all levels is a matter of continuous concern to all legislatures. While it is possible to formulate general policies regarding fiscal matters, it is necessary to examine these policies and their effects periodic-

ally because of a variety of ever-changing factors. Among these factors are the constantly expanding demands for government services, the rising costs of these services and natural shifts in the nature and level of certain tax bases. 

The components of the state tax structure and the allocation of these tax receipts to the many units of government are matters of interest and concern to all citizens. During this interim, the Board has directed that research be done in the fields of state departmental fees and revenues from services and in the reallocation of tax moneys to local governmental subdivisions. Special studies in the area of property taxation involving moneys and credits and the assessment date were directed by this Board. 

These studies are only a part of what should be a continuing study on the part of administrative officials and legislators alike. 

SPECIAL TAX STUDY 

The Board has been seriously concerned with the effects and ramifications of the county assessment law enacted in 1955. The property tax is the backbone of all local government in this State and major changes in this field should be made with great care following forthright and diligent study. 

During the 1956 property assessment period many dramatic changes took place with respect to property taxation. Overall property valuations nearly doubled; there was a shift in the tax burden from personal to real property; many properties, previously assessed at higher percentages of true value than other properties, were assessed at nearly equal percentages of value with all other property thus further shifting the burden of the property tax. Coupled with these considerations was an undercurrent of public dissatisfaction with certain administrative procedures both at the local and state levels. 

In recognition of this situation, the Special Tax Committee was created at the June 25, 1956 meeting of the Board and assigned the study of "the general theory of property taxation including the relationship of the county assessment law to existing tax laws." The Committee held extensive public hearings where representatives of all interested groups as well as individuals were given an opportunity to be heard.

Publication of Assessments 

In considering the report of the Special Tax Committee, the Board received a suggestion of special merit which is not covered in the Special Tax Committee Report. The Board believes that this recommendation should be considered in conjunction with the recommendation of the Special Tax Committee.

The Executive Board recommends that the valuations placed on personal property by the original assessment be published prior to the meeting of the local board of equalization provided that the assessment date is established at an earlier date.

The most diligent efforts and studies of any board of equalization are not a substitute for a good local assessment in any attempt to equalize property values. In many cases equalization board action only magnifies existing inequities in assessed valuations. Sound equalization policies must be practiced on the local level first if equity in property taxation is to be attained. While low valuations tend to lull the taxpayer into a false "sense of security", publication of personal property assessments would give that taxpayer an opportunity to evaluate his assessment in terms of his neighbor's. Early publication of this list would afford the taxpayer greater opportunity for an economical and essentially local appeal through the local board of equalization, if he felt his assessment was high or out of line with similar property. The Executive Board believes that favorable consideration of this proposal would be a major step in arriving at property tax equalization.

The feasibility of this recommendation is predicated upon the establishment of an earlier assessment date, since additional time would be needed to prepare these lists before the local board of equalization meets. 

State Board of Equalization Recommendation Number Eight of the Special Tax Committee recommends that the Executive Board study the composition of the State Board of Equalization. 

The Board recognizes the heavy responsibilities charged to the State Board of Equalization. The present State Board of Equalization is composed entirely of ex-officio members, all of whom have other full-time duties. A study of the organizational structures in other states of inter-county equalization indicates that many approaches to the nature and make-up of these State Boards are possible. 

These approaches vary with the duties assigned the Board, which in turn vary with the basic reasons for state equalization of property values in the many states. 

Due to time limitations, the Executive Board has not been able to make a specific recommendation on this matter. The Board does believe that some changes in the South Dakota State Board of Equalization are necessary to insure that all segments of the State's economy are better represented on the State Board. 

The Executive Board strongly recommends that the 1957 Legislature study the composition of the State Board of Equalization to the end that all segments of the State's economy are better represented.

Moneys and Credits 

Recommendation Number Nine of the Special Tax Committee is that uninvested funds be exempted from the moneys and credits tax. The Committee recognized the legal difficulties in making a reasonable classification of such funds. The Executive Board recommends in the alternative that a flat dollar exemption of moneys and credits be granted.

EDUCATION, HEALTH AND WELFARE 

The problem of educating the young citizens of this State is intertwined among several units of government - the school district, the municipality, the county, and the state. While the state is constitutionally charged with the general responsibility, "to secure to the people the advantages and opportunities of education," the real heart of our educational system is the local school district. However, the severe burden of local taxes in South Dakota, most of which go to support our schools, makes evaluation of our educational system by all citizens especially necessary at this time. 

While the Legislature must provide workable machinery for school district organization, the planning and the decisions must come from the local units of government. If school district organization is worked out in an orderly fashion at a pace consistent with local attitudes, permanent benefits will result to the children of the State at reduced cost to the taxpayer. 

The progress of the South Dakota Indian is a fact in which all citizens can take pride. Many problems remain, however, which are not necessarily peculiar to South Dakota. In many areas the problems of the Indians' assimilation and integration into the existing society are especially great. The problems of sanitation, health, education and direct assistance loom quite large to many Indians. A basic problem in this regard is the fixing of financial responsibility for these services. The obligations of the federal government at present are vague and indistinct. The need for continued interstate cooperation and effort in delineating these responsibilities is apparent. 

STATE AND LOCAL GOVERNMENT 

One of the most vexing problems facing the South Dakota voter is the voter registration system now in use. The confusion which is the inevitable result of multiple registration lists, seriously hampers the complete registration of all eligible voters. The Board believes that changes and improvements can be made in our voter registration law to eliminate this voter confusion and to insure correct and up-to-date voter registration.

Salary problems are a continuous concern to all legislators. It is essential that adequate salaries be paid if the State is to attract and keep the type and quali- 

The ty personnel necessary to perform the functions of economical government. 

Board believes that the Legislature in considering the salary recommendations of the State and Local Government Committee should consider also the salaries of state administrative personnel who are not mentioned specifically in that report. 

TRANSPORTATION 

The advent of the federal highway program enacted in 1956 poses many problems for both the administrative personnel and the legislature of this State. 

The Transportation Committee, which was assigned the study of highway finance, has recommended in summary, that if after further study by the Executive Board, additional taxes are necessary to meet the financial needs of the Department of Highways, that motor fuel taxes be increased. 

The determination of highway needs is complicated by needs in the areas of federal-aid highways, 100% state-constructed highways, and in the special maintenance program. It will be necessary to weigh these needs for their relative importance. Most important in determining the extent of the total highway program for any year is the inflexible limitation of what the State can afford. The 

Board does not know what the status of the General Fund will be following the current year's expenditures. At this time, the Board is not apprized of the budget requests which will be presented to the 1957 Legislature. This is information which will be available to the 1957 Legislature. The Board strongly recommends that matters of highway finance be given the greatest study and consideration by the next Legislature. It is the wish of the Board that the highway program entered into during the next biennium be to the best interests of the State of South Dakota. 

In financing whatever program is adopted, the Board hopes that no additional taxes will be necessary.

Study or the consideration of the adoption of the Uniform Motor Vehicle Code in whole or in part should be premised on the safety features with emphasis on practicability in South Dakota.

EFFICIENCY AND ECONOMY 

The 1955 Legislature appropriated $15,500 for the purpose of making efficiency and economy studies and analyses of the Departments of Health, Highways, Game, Fish and Parks and Public Welfare. The monetary limits placed on the study of each Department were: Department of Game, Fish and Parks $4,000; Department of Health $2500; Department of Highways $4,000; Department of Public Welfare - $5,000. The Executive Board was charged with the duty of making the studies and was empowered to employ consultants. 

---

An overall Efficiency and Economy Committee of thirteen members was appointed from the Legislative Research Council at large to conduct the surveys. 

In keeping with the goals of economy in government the Board employed consultants only in the areas where necessary. Dr. Gaylord Anderson of the University of Minnesota, one of the nation's leading figures in the field of Public Health, was engaged to conduct an efficiency and economy study of the Department of Health giving special emphasis to matters of finance. Dr. Ira Gabrielson of the 

Wildlife Management In stitute, who has had more than thirty years experience with the Federal Fish and Wildlife Service, and who has authored numerous similar surveys, was engaged to conduct an efficiency and economy survey of the Department of Game, Fish and Parks. The Board believes that the efforts and the approach of the Efficiency and Economy Committee to its work has resulted in many worthwhile recommendations at a minimum of cost. A complete financial report of the Efficiency and Economy Committee may be found in Appendix A. 

The Board believes that the work of the Efficiency and Economy Committee has served a number of purposes. Valuable information has been compiled on the administrative and legislative problems of the many departments. Legislative understanding of the programs and problems of the Executive Branch has been increased immeasurably. The effect of these studies on administrators is extremely beneficial as legislative inquiry encourages the departments to take a fresh look at their own operations which in itself has resulted in voluntary administrative changes and improvements resulting in improved efficiency and more economy. 

The Board believes that the 1957 Legislature should consider the establishment of a permanent efficiency and economy committee, either directly through the Legislature or through action by the Legislative Research Council. It is essential that adequate funds be provided this committee regardless of how it is established. 

The reports of the Committee on each of the Departments in question indicated a need for better and more coordinated public relations and publicity programs. The Board strongly recommends that the Legislature consider the establishment of a central state publicity agency to better coordinate the programs of the many departments in this field. Duplication of effort, equipment and personnel is a practice which cannot be tolerated in a well-managed government. New publicity programs and expansion of existing programs are currently being planned in many departments. Whether this expansion is conducted in an orderly, economical fashion or is developed haphazardly at the expense of the taxpayer is a matter of just concern to the Legislature. A central publicity agency should not be considered an addition to present services but an attempt to coordinate and consolidate individual efforts to the end that more can be gained from each publicity dollar.

CORPORATION LAWS 

Among the proposals submitted to the Board for consideration was one which requested study of the revision of the corporate laws of the State. The objective of this proposal was to determine whether the present South Dakota corporation law in any way discourages industry from coming into the State The Board requested that the State Bar Association be requested to make a study of this subject. Studies have been made of the state corporate laws by the Corporation Law Revision Committee and the Judicial Council. 

Both groups favored a revision of the South Dakota Corporate Law. In convention September 

7-8, 1956, the State Bar Association adopted the report of the Corporation Law Revision Committee and instructed that the Committee prepare legislation following the lines of the Model Business Corporations Act to be presented to the 1957 Legislature.

JUDICIAL SYSTEM 

A proposal was submitted to the Board to make a study of the organization of the judicial circuits and such other aspects of the state court system as may be deemed expedient. This proposal was referred to the State Bar Association. The 

Committee on Judicial System made an intensive study of the county courts. It made a recommendation that a proposed constitutional amendment be submitted to the 1957 Legislature which would empower the Supreme Court to combine two or more counties into a county court system. This recommendation was adopted by the State Bar Association. This amendment will be presented to the 1957 Legislature.

CONCLUDING STATEMENT 

The Executive Board wishes to stress that the principal function of the Legislative Research Council is to provide research information which will assist in formulating legislation. The Research Council does not legislate; but rather makes recommendations based upon interim study. 

Besides being responsible for the assignment of proposals to committees for research study, the Executive Board has served as the coordinating body for the purpose of achieving a consistent and realistic legislative program. By having chairmen of the standing committees selected from the Board membership, it has been possible for the Board to be continuously apprized of committee and subcommittee study.

Good government can be achieved only through a thorough knowledge of the facts and the problems which are confronting us. Through the research and study of the Council committees, much information has been obtained, which, it is hoped, will provide a basis for better legislation.

HISTORY AND FUNCTIONS OF THE SOUTH DAKOTA LEGISLATIVE RESEARCH COUNCIL Introduction 

The purpose of the South Dakota Legislative Research Council, as established by the 1951 Legislature, is "to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature."

For a long time legislators and citizens alike have been aware of the difficulties in trying to cope with the problems confronting state government and to pass appropriate legislation within the limits of a sixty-day session. 

The 1951 Legislature was not the first South Dakota Legislature to pass on the advisability of creating a council. Two years after the first council came into existence in Kansas in 1933 a bill was introduced in the South Dakota Senate to provide for a "permanent legislative committee" with authority to function between legislative sessions. Subsequently, in at least four sessions of the Legislature, council bills were introduced. Favorable sentiment increased. Finally, in 1951, retiring Governor George T. Mickelson, and the new Governor, Sigurd Anderson, recommended the establishment of a council. 

The passage of Senate Bill 283, providing for a council, was thus the culmination of careful consideration and thinking by a number of previous Legislatures. 

South Dakota, on March 2, 1951, became the twenty-seventh state to have a council in operation.

Organization South Dakota adopted the Oklahoma pattern of council organization. Every member of the South Dakota Legislature is a member of the Council. Only Oklahoma, Nebraska, and South Dakota have organized their councils this way. In other states, the councils consist of small groups of legislators, with as few as three members in South Carolina, but ordinarily with between ten and twenty members. 

The advantage of the South Dakota system lies in the extent to which the work done represents the thinking and efforts of all interested legislators rather than that of a few.

In South Dakota there are three important agencies concerned with Council activities: (1) The Executive Board, (2) Council committees, and (3) the Research Department.

(1) The Executive Board 

To supervise and coordinate the work of the Council, provision was made for an Executive Board of fifteen members. This Board consists of seven members elected by the House of Representatives, six members elected by the Senate, the Speaker of the House and the President Pro-tempore of the Senate. The 

Board elects its own Chairman and Secretary.

The Executive Board is given the following specific powers in the statute establishing the Council: a. b. c. d. e. to instigate research and collect information concerning the government and general welfare of the state; to investigate and make recommendations concerning important issues of public policy and questions of statewide interest; to prepare a legislative program in the form of bills, or otherwise, as in its opinion the welfare of the state may require, to be presented at the next regular session of the Legislature; to cooperate with the administration in devising means of enforcing the law and improving the effectiveness of administrative methods; to study, inquire, make recommendations and propose bills in any phase or branch of state government so deemed advisable and necessary; f. to appoint and name committees from the members of the State Legislative Research Council and assign to such committee or committees appropriate subjects and projects of whatever character and nature the 

Executive Board deems advisable.

During the 1955-56 biennium, most of the work on the legislative program was carried on through the standing and special committees. The Board outlined various problems to the committees for study, and Board members served as chairmen of the various standing committees. The Board was also responsible for referring specific legislative proposals to committees for consideration.

In addition to supervising and coordinating committee work, the Executive Board was responsible for the employment of research personnel, administration of the budget, and the preparation and distribution of research reports. The Board met quarterly at the State Capitol as required by law. Copies of the minutes of Board meetings were distributed to all legislators.

(2) Council Committees

Standing Committees 

Among the duties of the Executive Board is the appointment of committees. 

In accordance with the law establishing the Council, the Executive Board provided that each legislator was entitled to serve on two committees of his own choice. For the 1955-56 biennium, the Executive Board created the following standing committees: (a) Agriculture and Conservation, (b) Assessment, Taxation and Finance, (c) Education, Health and Welfare, (d) State and Local Government, and (e) Transportation.

The standing committees and their subcommittees of the Council held a total of forty-five meetings. Attendance averaged approximately 70 per cent. 

Special Committees 

The 1955 Legislature charged the Executive Board of the Legislative Research Council with the duty of conducting economy and efficiency studies of four major state departments. These were: Department of Game, Fish and Parks; Department of Health; Department of Highways; and Department of Public Welfare. The 

Executive Board established the Economy and Efficiency Committee to supervise these studies. For purposes of conducting these studies, the Economy and Efficiency Committee was divided into three-member subcommittees, one to study each of the departments. These subcommittees convened a total of eighteen meetings, with attendance at nearly 90 per cent.

A Special Tax Study Committee was established by the Executive Board to study the general theory of taxation and its relationship to the county assessment law. This Committee held three meetings. 

(3) The Research Department 

Basic to good legislation is the assimilation and dissemination of facts and background material relative to the subjects under consideration. The Research 

Department is designed to facilitate this process. During the 1955-56 biennium the 

Research Department consisted of a Director, a Research Assistant, a Secretary and certain part-time clerical help. 

The Council office staff has worked closely with both the standing and special committees during the present biennium. Through close cooperation with administrative departments and civic organizations the staff has been able to gather a wealth of statistical and other factual information for use by the committees. The staff has actively engaged in the analysis of reports and data and conducted several special surveys to aid certain committees. These major surveys were in the fields of elections, property taxation and South Dakota county salaries.

Another major item in the workload of the staff is the handling of administrative detail connected with committee meetings. Committee activity was particularly heavy during this biennium as more than eighty days were spent in committee meetings.

In addition the Department performs spot-research and incidental services for individual legislators. In many respects the Research Department has become the "home office" of all legislators when they visit the Capitol. 

Outgrowths of 1955-56 Council Activities 

(1) Research The purpose of the Council is "to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature." This means that emphasis is placed on study and research. 

Good legislation is based on understanding and the availability of factual information. 

Many facts are not easily obtainable. The Council, through its Research Department, has conducted research on the specific measures recommended to the 1957 Legislature. Reports of all the standing and special committees have been reproduced and made available to the public. 

(2) Legislative Reference 

Through a policy of purchase and exchange of government publications with other state agencies and through cooperation with the State Library Commission, the Department has amassed a wealth of materials and information on governmental problems. This service is of particular importance during the legislative session. 

The Department has files of information on more than one thousand subjects in the field of government. This information is coded and filed for quick and ready access when needed. In addition, the Library Commission, after discussion and consultation with the Research staff, makes many periodicals and other publications of exceptional reference value available to legislators and administrative personnel. 

The Joint Reference Library of the Council of State Governments has one of the largest collections of governmental research materials in the United States. 

These materials are made available on a loan basis to the Research Department. 

In these ways, the Council, with a small staff, has extended the scope and capacity of its reference resources. These materials are not only available to legislators during the session, but are used extensively by administrative officials the year around. Scores of referrals on questions concerning state government are handled by the Research Department each year.

(3) Interstate Cooperation 

In line with the expansion and growth of government at all levels, state government has also expanded greatly. Commensurate with this unheralded expansion new administrative and legislative problems have presented themselves, while old problems have been greatly magnified. Cooperation among the states is one of the most effective means of working toward solution of these problems. The easy exchange of information on means of combating state difficulties and the possibility of the establishment of a "united front" to the federal government on matters of general concern are desirable outgrowths of interstate cooperation. The understanding of mutual problems is one of the essential steps in arriving at uniformity and in the breakdown of economic and legal barriers between states. 

The South Dakota Legislative Research Council has been an active member of the North Central States Conference and has participated in several meetings of that Conference on highway problems. Other interstate meetings have been held on the problems of the Indian.

The National Legislative Service Conference has been of particular benefit to South Dakota Government. This annual conference provides an opportunity for legislative service agencies, such as research councils, bill drafters, legislative reference libraries, and officers of the houses, to meet to exchange ideas and learn new methods for improving the effectiveness of the legislative process. Through cooperation with the Interstate Cooperation Committee, the Council has been represented at the interstate conferences held by the Council of State Governments. The 

Biennial General Assembly of States provides an excellent pre-session review of matters of concern to all states. 

(4) Civic Education and Information 

One of the most important features of the Council policy of considering a legislative program in advance of the legislative session is opportunity given Council members to learn the views of private organizations and citizens and governmental agencies regarding legislative problems. By considering these views the Council can best ascertain the legislative needs, the plausibility of proposed solutions and the special problems involved. All Council meetings are open to the public. More than that, the attendance of citizen groups presumed to be interested in certain legislative problems is actively solicited by the Council. In addition, the Council meetings are announced publicly in the press and receive wide coverage throughout the state. 

During this biennium, two subcommittees, those concerned with Home Rule and Watershed legislation, held regular meetings in communities throughout the state interested in these particular matters. This practice met with strong local approval and, in the opinion of the Council members present, resulted in better considered recommendations better adapted to local conditions.

-. 

This policy has improved public understanding of legislative problems and generated the constructive local thinking which makes the legislators' task much easier. This policy has served to acquaint all interested South Dakota citizens in matters of pending legislation. 

Conclusion 

The Council's first biennial report properly noted that the Legislature has been the neglected branch of state government. While the executive and judicial departments have expanded, measured both by appropriations and size of staffs, assistance to the Legislature has remained meager, failing to keep pace with modern developments.

The establishment of a legislative council in South Dakota represents an attempt to provide additional aid to the Legislature so that it can accomplish more efficiently and more effectively the tasks assigned to it. The bulwark of representative government must lie on the legislative branch of government. The success of the South Dakota Legislative Research Council will depend on the extent to which the Council can demonstrate that South Dakota legislators and citizens are benefited by the contributions it makes to the legislative process and to the welfare of the state.

TEXT OF COUNCIL STATUTE 

(SDC Supp. 55.05A, as amended) 

55.05A01 Council created; scope. There is hereby created a State Legislative Research Council which shall consist of all of the members of the South Dakota Legislature. It shall be the function of the Legislative Research Council to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature and carry out the duties imposed by this chapter. 

55.05A02 Executive Board: membership; vacancies; officers. The State

Legislative Research Council shall have an Executive Board, to be known as the Executive Board of the Legislative Research Council, which shall consist of fifteen members, as follows: six members from the Senate and seven members from the 

House, to be elected by a majority vote by their respective legislative bodies before the close of each and every regular session of the Legislature, and ex officio the president pro tempore of the Senate and the speaker of the House. Each 

Senator and each Representative serving on the Executive Board, including each ex officio member, shall serve until a new Executive Board has been selected at the next regular legislative session; provided, however, that no Senator who is not reelected to the Senate and no Representative who is not reelected to the House of Representatives, shall serve as a member of the Board beyond the closing day of the term to which he was elected. When the Legislature is not in regular session, the number of Senate and House members on the Executive Board shall remain constant, and any vacancy then occurring shall be filled by the remaining Board members of that legislative body affected. The Board shall choose from its own membership a chairman and a secretary of the Board. 

55.05A03 Board; powers and duties. It shall be the duty of the Executive

Board, and such Board has the power and authority (1) to instigate research and collect information concerning the government and general welfare of the state; (2) to investigate and make recommendations concerning important issues of public policy and questions of statewide interest; (3) to prepare a legislative program in the form of bills, or otherwise, as in its opinion the welfare of the state may require, to be presented at the next regular session of the Legislature; (4) to cooperate with the administration in devising means of enforcing the law and improving the effectiveness of administrative methods; (5) to study, inquire, make recommendations and propose bills in any phase or branch of state government so deemed advisable and necessary; (6) to appoint and name committees from the members of the State Legislative Research Council, and assign to such committee or committees appropriate subjects and projects of whatever character and nature the Executive Board deems advisable. Each member of the Council shall be entitled to membership on two standing committees of his choice. 

55.05A04 Meetings of Board; quorum; rules; biennial report. The Executive

Board shall meet as often as may be necessary to perform its duties; provided that, in any event, it shall meet at least once every three months. All regular meetings shall be held at the state capitol. A majority of the members shall constitute a quorum, and a majority thereof shall have authority to act on any matter falling

within the scope of the said Executive Board's duties, powers and authority. The 

Board shall make its own rules and regulations, keep complete minutes of its meetings, and make a biennial report to the net Legislature. 

55.05A05 Board: powers regarding procedure in discharge of duties. In the discharge of any duty herein imposed, the Executive Board or its duly appointed committees shall have authority to administer oaths, require reports, issue subpoenas, compel the attendance of witnesses, and the production of any papers, books, accounts, documents and testimony, and to cause the depositions of witnesses, either residing within or without the state, to be taken in the manner prescribed by law for taking depositions in civil actions in the Circuit Court. 

55.05A06 Compensation and expenses. Members of the State Legislative 

Research Council and members of the Executive Board shall receive as compensation for their services other than that due them as members of the Legislature, the sum of ten dollars for each day's attendance at meetings, and shall be reimbursed for mileage, lodging and meals on the same basis as other state employees. Such claims and expenses are to be paid out of funds appropriated therefor, and on vouchers approved according to rules determined by the Executive Board. 

55.05A07 Board: employment of assistants. The Executive Board is hereby authorized and empowered to appoint a Director of Legislative Research and employ such clerks, assistants and other help and provide such supplies and equipment as may be necessary.

Executive Board of 

The Legislative Research Council RULES OF PROCEDURE 

(1955-1957) Rule 1. Quarterly Meetings. The Executive Board shall meet at least once every three months at the State Capitol. Unless otherwise directed by the Board, meetings shall be held on the fourth Monday in March, June, and September, and the second Monday in December. Special meetings may be called by the 

Chairman. 

Rule 2. Hour of Meeting. Unless otherwise specified by the Board or Executive 

Committee, the Board shall meet at 9 a. m. 

Rule 3. Meeting Place. The Governor's Reception Room and the Senate Chamber shall be official meeting places of the Executive Board. 

Rule 4. Rules of Order. Proceedings of the Board shall be conducted in accordance with Robert's Rules of Order except as specified in these rules. 

Rule 5. Quorum. A majority of the members shall constitute a quorum, and a majority thereof shall have authority to act on any matter falling within the scope of the said Executive Board's duties, powers, and authority. 

Rule 6. Order of Business. The regular order of business shall be: 

(a) Call to Order (b) Roll Call 

(c) Reading and Correction of Minutes (d) Reading of Communications (e) Introduction and Reference of Resolutions and Proposals (f) Consideration of Committee Reports 

(g) Consideration of New Business

Rule 7. Officers. The officers of the Board shall consist of a Chairman and Secretary who shall be elected by the Board. The Chairman and Secretary shall constitute the Executive Committee of the Board. (a) The Chairman or the Secretary shall preside at all meetings of the Executive Board and the Legislative Research Council. They may call special meetings of the Board. (b) The Secretary shall keep a record of all the proceedings of the Executive Board and copies of the proceedings shall be furnished to all members of the Legislative Research Council. The Secretary shall preside at all meetings of the Executive Board when the Chairman is not present.

Rule 8. Powers of the Board. The Board shall have the power and authority (a) to instigate research and collect information concerning the government and general welfare of the State; (b) to investigate and make recommendations concerning important issues of public policy and questions of statewide interest;

· · 

(c) to prepare a legislative program in the form of bills, or otherwise, as in its opinion the welfare of the State may require, to be presented at the next session of the Legislature; (d) to cooperate with the administration in devising means of enforcing the law and improving the effectiveness of administrative methods; (e) to study, inquire, make recommendations and propose bills in any phase or branch of the State government so deemed advisable and necessary; (f) to appoint and name committees from the members of the State Legislative Research Council, and assign to such committee or committees appropriate subjects and projects of whatever character and nature the Executive Board deems advisable.

Rule 9. Standing Committees. The work of the Board shall be carried on primarily through the standing committees. (a) The following standing committees are authorized by the Board: 

1. Agriculture and Conservation 2. Assessment, Taxation and Finance 3. Education, Health and Welfare 4. State and Local Government 5. Transportation 

(b) Each legislator shall be entitled to membership on two standing committees of his choice. (d) (c) Members present of any duly called standing committee or sub-committee shall be fully empowered to act within the scope of their authority. 

Committees may meet at their pleasure, but shall be entitled to per diem and expenses, only when such meetings shall have been authorized and approved by the Executive Board or Executive Committee. (e) Committees shall prepare such biennial reports of their activities to the Executive Board as may be requested by the Executive Board from time to time. (f) Committees are authorized to perform all activities appropriate to the accomplishment of the purposes of the Legislative Research Council Act, subject to the approval of the Executive Board. 

Rule 10. Special Committees. The Executive Board may establish special committees when, in its opinion, such committees are necessary. 

Rule 11. Resolutions; Proposals. Resolutions on any proper subject may be presented in writing to the Executive Board by any member of the Council. This rule shall not apply to resolutions relating to business of the day, or to resolutions for adjournment.

Proposals may be presented to the Executive Board in writing by any member of the Council. Each proposal shall have a descriptive title, bearing the name of the sponsor, and he shall attach thereto any supplementary matter which he believes will be of help to the Council. 

Rule 12. Presentation of Proposals. Proposals so drafted shall be presented to the 

Executive Board in the regular order of business (e) and shall be numbered in order of presentation. When presenting a proposal, a member shall explain it briefly. Other members may ask questions and request interpretations of any provision thereof. A proposal may be submitted when the 

Executive Board is not in session by lodging same with the Director of Legislative Research, by mail or otherwise, at the Council's office in

Pierre, who shall present such proposal to the Chairman of the Executive Board for reference to committee.

Rule 13. Director of Legislative Research. The Board shall appoint a Director of 

Legislative Research who shall be in charge of research activities and who shall perform such other duties as required by the Board. 

The Director of Legislative Research shall be responsible to the Board through the Executive Committee.

The Director of Legislative Research shall be present or represented at all Board and Committee meetings and shall assist the Council and committees in every possible way.

The salary of the Director of Legislative Research shall be determined by the Board.

Rule 14. Other Staff. Necessary clerical help and assistants may be employed by the 

Executive Committee of the Board, upon the recommendation of the Director of Legislative Research.

Rule 15. Budget. A budget shall be prepared and submitted to the Board no later than 

July 1 of each year.

Rule 16. Expenses. All claims and expenses shall be paid on vouchers approved by the Chairman or the Secretary of the Executive Board, or the Director of Legislative Research and submitted to the Secretary of Finance for approval, except that payrolls and non-recurring expenditures specifically authorized by the Board shall be approved by the Chairman or the Secretary of the 

Executive Board. 

Rule 17. Publicity. All meetings of the Board and the committees of the Legislative 

Research Council shall be open to the public. Full publicity shall be given to the proceedings and decisions of the Council. Executive sessions shall only be held on a majority vote of the Board or committee. 

Rule 18. Amendment. These rules of procedure may, by a vote of eight members of the Board, be amended or suspended in any manner not inconsistent with the constitution and laws of the State.
