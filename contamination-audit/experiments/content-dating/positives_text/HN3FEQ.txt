Report of Activities ARMY FIELD FORCES 

1945-1949 LTHOUGH the name and the command status of Army 

A Forces been

Field Forces have been changed twice since its inception, its mission has changed but little. 

As General Headquarters, U. S. Army, from August 1940 

to March 1942, it was charged with "directing and supervising the training of the field forces of the Army within the continental United States."

As Headquarters, Army Ground Forces, until March 1948, its assigned mission was "to provide ground force units properly organized, trained, and equipped for combat conditions.” 

As Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, it is responsible for the training, composition, and equipment policy of the field armies. It supervises, coordinates, and inspects the training of all individuals and units utilized in a field army. It develops organization, defines tactical and technical employment, and prescribes materiel of all field army units. It determines requirements, conducts research, and develops the items of equipment in which field army units have primary interest. 1

THE Planning HE PLANNING activities of Army 

Ground Forces during the period of demobilization were concerned principally with phasing out the greatest wartime army in American history and bringing into being the postwar military establishment. Policies and general plans provided by the War Department (later, the Department of the Army) placed the responsibility of working out the details in these two major spheres of activity on the Commanding General, Army Ground 

Forces. Ground Forces' planners were spe cifically assigned the following functions: 

a. Drafting plans for the defense of the 

United States, in accord with the general plans of the War Department. 

b. Formulating long-range plans for the organization, composition, and training of the ground forces and ground force service units, including the civilian components the National Guard, the Organized Reserve Corps, and the Reserve Officers' Training Corps. 

c. Planning ground force implementation of a Universal Military Training program and its administration under the War Department directive, against the possibility of its enactment by Congress. 

d. Reviewing new developments in foreign armies and their effect on our plans and organization. e. Developing new strategical and tactical doctrines and reviewing old doctrines in the light of new developments. f. Studying the effect of new weapons on our strategical and tactical concepts. g. Determining materiel and personnel requirements, both quantitative and qualitative, in conformance with broad directives of the War Department, by long-range logistical planning, including a review of industrial mobilization plans from a ground forces viewpoint. h. Recommending over-all Army Ground 

Forces long-range policy, and preparing general plans for mobilization of the Army in the event of an emergency. i. Coordinating defense plans of the six continental U. S. Army Areas. 

Other plans, of a classified nature, were required, and there was a continuing necessity of determining which among many pressing requirements came within the realm of possibility in the light of available personnel, funds, and facilities. There was the further necessity of establishing priorities for desirable projects. Planning for the postwar forces has been a continuous process; the strengths, composites, and objectives have had to be changed in accordance with fluctuations in appropriations and amendments to policies.

In a period in which the Army Air Forces was becoming a separate service under unification legislation, there were many problems to be resolved between the province of the air forces and that of the ground

arms. Army Ground Forces made recommendations with respect to air defense which offered material advantages over the prevailing view, since they clearly defined the area of responsibility of fighter aircraft, freed fighter aircraft from defense of points and small vital areas, and permitted the application of the inherent flexibility of air power to over-all air defense, permitted the maximum exploitation of the capabilities of antiaircraft artillery, and provided maximum economy of force.

These recommendations, which were accepted by the Departments of the Army and of the Air Force, stipulated that air defense remain an Air Force responsibility, and that the term be interpreted to mean defense by air, denying the enemy access to air space over friendly territory. It similarly stipulated that antiaircraft defense be a Ground Force responsibility, and that it be defined as all ground-to-air action, having as its purpose the defense of specific objectives against enemy air action, and operating against all forms of enemy air action within effective range of its weapons.

Reorganization of Combat Units URING THE WAR, change and prog ress in unit organization and equipment were continuous, with flexibility in organization dictated by varying conditions in the various theaters. With the end of the war, it was possible to pool the best experience from all theaters, and to make a detailed study and analysis of the lessons to be learned from combat.

Theater, army, corps, and division commanders were asked for recommendations, and battle-experienced officers and men were interviewed. An Infantry Conference at Fort 

Benning, Ga., an Artillery Conference at Fort

Sill, Okla., and an Armored Conference at 

Fort Knox, Ky., produced valuable results.

Hence, entirely new tables of organization and equipment were developed for infantry, armored, and airborne divisions. The mountain division, developed during World War II, and the cavalry division, employed in an infantry role only during World War II, were discontinued. 

The Infantry Division 

Planned on the principle that the best military organization is one which concentrates maximum strength of men and materiel in combat units, the new infantry division, with only 20% more man power, achieves a 68% increase in fire power over the World War II division (see figure 1). 

Addition of weapons and tracked vehicles serves to increase the striking power, flexibility, and maneuverability of the division.

New units in the division are a medium tank battalion, an antiaircraft artillery battalion, and tank, heavy mortar, and medical companies in each of the three infantry regiments. A fourth company and a bridge 4

platoon are added to the engineer battalion.

The antitank and cannon companies are eliminated. 

During World War II one tank battalion was normally attached to each division; the new division includes organically the equivalent of two battalions, armed with the 

M26 medium tank mounting a 90-mm gun. 

To obtain an increase of 50% in field artillery fire power, the number of pieces in each firing battery is increased from four to six, since combat experience indicated that six-gun batteries required almost no increase in overhead.

Detailed changes in organization extend down to the infantry squad, which now consists of nine men instead of twelve.

Combat experience proved that it was difficult for a squad leader to control and direct more than eight other men in battle, and technical developments in weapons indicate greater dispersion in future warfare. 

The new squad consists of a squad leader and his assistant, five riflemen, and an automatic rifleman and his assistant. 

Equipment for the new division is the latest adopted type. Antiaircraft artillery units are to have self-propelled carriages mounting either quadruple caliber .50 machine guns or dual 40-mm guns. The engineer battalion will have bridging material heretofore found only at corps and army levels. The number of light liaison airplanes is increased. Radar equipment, supplied previously to only a few divisions, has been made organic to headquarters batteries of light artillery battalions, to facilitate location of enemy artillery and mortars. Fortytwo 75-mm and eighty-one 57-mm recoilless rifles have been added to the division's armament.

Reorganization of existing infantry units, including reserve components, under the new tables of organization and equipment is now in progress. 

The Armored Division 

Of the Army's sixteen armored divisions in World War II, thirteen had an authorized strength of 10,000 and three a strength of 14,000. 

Regardless of the type of organization, combat experience indicated conclusively that the ratio of infantry to tanks was insufficient. 

To remedy this shortcoming, an infantry battalion was added to the new armored division, and the strength of the existing infantry battalions increased from three to four rifle companies, increasing the total number of rifle companies from nine to sixteen.

Replacing the tank destroyer battalion, which was habitually attached to the armored division in combat, is an an added heavy tank battalion, consisting of three tank companies of four platoons each.

Two additional battalions have been added to the armored division artillery, a 155-mm howitzer battalion to be equipped with eighteen self-propelled howitzers and an antiaircraft battalion with sixty-four self-propelled weapons. 

Other additions to the armored division include a bridge company and an additional line company for the engineer battalion,

and a quartermaster battalion with trucks for added transport facilities. 

As in the infantry division, the armored division infantry squad is reduced in size, in this case to ten men; the tenth man drives the squad's full-track personnel carrier, a change from the half-tracks of World 

War II. 

The Airborne Division 

The airborne division, in general, parallels the organization of the infantry division, consistent with personnel and logistical considerations. 

The division approximates 17,500 men, an increase of about 4,500 over the old division.

Under recent Department of the Army directives certain units, such as the two tank battalions, which were formerly attached to the division, have now become an integral part of it. Parachute and glider elements of the old organization have in general been combined to form a division composed of dual-trained parachute and glider personnel. 

In the future, all personnel of the signal, ordnance, and quartermaster companies and the engineer battalion will be dual qualified. 

Personnel of the 155-mm howitzer battalion, however, are glider qualified only. And as the two tank battalions and the ambulance company of the medical battalion are not now air-transportable, their personnel are not dual qualified.

The division includes organically three infantry regiments, two medium tank battalions, three 105-mm howitzer battalions, one 155-mm howitzer battalion, one reconnaissance company, and one antiaircraft artillery automatic weapons battalion. Service units are similar to those of the infantry division. The medical battalion, band, quartermaster company, and replacement company are identical to those found in the infantry division.

A support company with one platoon of six 90-mm towed antitank guns and two platoons of four 4.2-inch mortars each, has been provided in the infantry regiment in contrast to the regimental tank and heavy mortar companies, infantry division. Three line companies make up the engineer battalion in lieu of the four found in the infantry division. An antiaircraft artillery automatic weapons battalion has been added. 

A division antitank platoon, identical in organization to the platoon of the support company, and a quartermaster parachute maintenance company, make up the balance of the division.

Preparation of detailed T/O&E for the airborne division is in process. However, the 82d and 11th Airborne Divisions are currently organized under similar first draft T/O&E. 

The "Type" Field Army 

In developing a field army organization built around the new infantry and armored divisions, Army Field Forces planners made an analysis of the numbers and types of units comprising the First, Third, Seventh, and Ninth Armies on 1 April 1945, to establish a basis for the number of personnel to be allocated to combat and service units in a "type" field army. Consideration was also given to the composition of the 

Sixth, Eighth, and Tenth Armies.

The Type Field Army is composed of three corps, each corps having three infantry divisions and one armored division. A minimum of service units are provided, again with the objective of concentrating a maximum of men and materiel in combat organizations rather than service units. 

The Type Field Army is organized as a balanced force capable of operating on land masses in the temperate zone. 

It is possible that no future field army will ever be identical with the Type Field Army, but the organization serves a most useful purpose as a basis for all planning of the field forces and for instructional purposes in all Army schools.

The Type Field Army will be reviewed annually to reflect new or revised doctrines and the capabilities of new weapons.

WITH lective Service in June 1948, Army Field Forces undertook responsibility for training a vastly expanded force in the midst of a tense international situation. While speed seemed a paramount requirement, it was realized that too rapid an expansion could have disastrous results. A lowering of training standards could jeopardize not only the prestige of the Army but also the effectiveness of the nation's armed forces. Training, therefore, had to be thoroughly planned and carefully executed, utilizing to the maximum the available trainer personnel and training facilities. 

This program of Army expansion has now leveled off at a strength of 677,000, and having reached this plateau a vantage point is afforded from which to evaluate the training methods used, noting how they may differ from wartime procedures, and appraising their effect on discipline, morale, and training efficiency.

Today the Army's training techniques have been largely standardized. Yet the factors affecting training are by no means the same as they were during World War II, or even in the period between the World Wars, for training is a dynamic, not a static, function. 

A variety of factors impinge on the training program, and constant adjustments must be made.

Among the variable factors at work, public opinion exerts a pervasive, twofold influence. First, the public attitude is a strong determinant of the outlook and attitudes of the men who come direct from civilian life. And second, the Army, as the servant of the people, relies in the final analysis upon public sanction for its acts. Traditionally the American people have been sensitive to compulsory military service. Consequently the public is disposed to examine exhaustively and comment freely on the Army's treatment of selectees.

The condition of public affairs, both national and international, also has its effect. 

When men are not impelled by a state of war or an obvious emergency to exert their utmost they are prone to question more critically the orders and policies governing their military life. Similarly, in a period when civilian employment, wages, and living standards are high, the soldier expects to find comparably favorable conditions in the Army.

The age, education, and civilian background of the present-day soldier are further decisive influences interacting on the training program. In 1938, approximately 10% of the servicemen were twenty-one or under. Today 50% are twenty-one or under and a large number of these are seventeen and eighteen. Many have never been away from home before entering the service. Today's

soldier has an average education of two years of high school-an average much higher than that prevailing in the prewar period. 

While existing conditions have been recognized, the basic aims of Army training are kept in constant focus. The primary mission of Army Field Forces now, as it always has been, is to teach men how to fight. That mission is unchanged by the state of international relations or the public temper. Briefly, it involves: (1) discipline; (2) imparting to the individual a knowledge of what he is doing, why he is doing it, and how it is related to the democratic ideals of our nation; (3) teaching each man the technique of his job; (4) training in leadership; (5) molding individuals into a fighting team.

Teamwork is indispensable to ground combat. The soldier must be made proficient in his own job, so that he may function not only as an individual but also as a member of the team. At the same time he must be given the knowledge, poise, and self-confidence that enable him to function as a team leader. 

The effectiveness of teamwork has been demonstrated repeatedly on the proving ground of battle. Employment of the infantry-artillery team in World War II was responsible, in no small measure, for the devastating effectiveness of ground action.

This concept has been broadened into the infantry-artillery-tank team, initiated in World 

War II and perfected in our postwar training. Teamwork probably received its greatest impetus in the task force principle em ployed so effectively in World War II. 

In the selection and training of officers and noncommissioned officers, the Army has always placed a premium on high quality of leadership. But the team is no more effective than the individuals comprising it, and accordingly, Army Ground-Field Forces has concentrated on the individual as the common denominator of our training effort. 

Fortunately Army Field Forces has had available the findings of the Army's pioneer research and development project in hu man relations—the Universal Military Train ing Experimental Unit at Fort Knox, Ky.

The Experimental Unit put to test the most advanced thinking on military training, and so soundly conceived and so successful was this project that its principles are currently emphasized in all training.

A series of training conferences at Fort 

Knox enabled commanders and key staff officers to study at first hand in the 3d

Armored Division the new methods developed in the UMT Experimental Unit. Confer ences were held for training division personnel who would conduct basic training, and for key personnel of the combat units receiving replacements and fillers. The service schools also sent representatives for indoctrination. 

Orientation teams were sent from Fort

Knox to all training installations, where they conducted demonstrations for instructors. The philosophy of troop handling that they conveyed to the field reflected an awareness of public attitudes and world conditions. Without resort 'to dogma or arbitrary instruction, they sought to stimulate an awareness of the principles by which true leadership is achieved.

Among the principles on which our postwar training philosophy is based, three tenets are paramount: (1) the human approach; (2) recognition and protection of the dignity of the individual; and (3) provision for maximum personal liberty, consistent with the performance of military duties. 

The human approach is manifest in the student-instructor relationship in the training units. There the student learns because he wants to not because he is driven by threats or coercion. Pride in self and pride in unit instill in the soldier a desire to acquit himself with credit, for his own selfesteem as well as for the reputation of his unit. High standards of discipline and performance result from this kind of enlightened leadership, developing in the individual a marked degree of initiative and esprit de corps. The same principle applies in the soldier-leader relationship in combat units. 

As its core, much of today's philosophy of troop handling is based upon a recognition of the dignity of the individual. The military history of this nation is replete with evidence that a man is a better fighter when he retains his individuality, an element of American strength which no totalitarian nation possesses.

Recognizing the fact that the soldier as an individual has his own aspirations, interests, and desires, the good leader manifests a high regard for every soldier in his command. 

He realizes that the morale and efficiency of his troops are frequently more dependent on individual recognition than on material rewards.

A new approach has been made to the problem of moral guidance among troops.

The chaplain has been specifically charged with certain troop welfare functions in addition to providing religious guidance. One of his biggest tasks is the teaching of sex hygiene, with emphasis on continence rather than on prophylaxis.

In all stages of training, unnecessary restrictions on the soldier's personal liberty have been avoided. The average American, it is recognized, chafes under unnecessary restraints, but accepts restrictions without complaint when the necessity is clearly evident. 

Opportunity for participation in constructive off-duty activities is an important corollary of the training program. Emphasis is placed on team sports and athletics. Soldiers are encouraged to participate in hobbies, afterduty classes, and educational programs. 

When neighboring communities are made to feel that they have a stake in the welfare of the men who are being trained nearby, a spirit of cooperation is generated which gives impetus to the training program. Good community relations are reflected in improved soldier morale and heightened training efficiency. On the other hand, inadequate or unfavorable relations with the community, the public, and the family of the soldier can make a disillusioned, disgruntled soldier, lacking in training incentive.

There are a few persons, both within and without the service, who have said that the Army is "going soft" in its training. If by 'going soft" they refer to banning of profanity, encouragement of high standards of morality, 66 10

efforts to stimulate spiritual welfare, and belief in humane treatment of the individual, then Army Field Forces is guilty of the charge and the specifications. But if they mean that Army Field Forces has lowered standards of training, or relaxed insistence on rigid discipline, then they are victims of misinformation, or prejudice, or ignorance of the distinction between intelligent leadership and coercive methods.

There is only one yardstick for measuring the effectiveness of leadership-how well and how willingly men perform for their leaders.

The end product of individual training is a physically tough, hardened, mentally sharpened fighting man. That is the kind of soldier Army Field Forces is producing. He will be an even better fighter than his father or his older brother, for if he must fight he will not only know how, but will also understand why.

On the training ground and in the classroom, Army Field Forces is striving to give the soldier a sense of purpose, to establish his confidence in his leaders, and to attain the true discipline that is the result of volition. The best discipline-self-disciplineexists not only while men are under the eyes of their leaders, but also while they are acting independently, either on or off duty. This voluntary discipline is based upon knowledge, reasoning, sense of duty, and belief in ideals. It comes through willingness, enthusiasm, and cooperation, not through fear of punishment. With it comes intelligent obedience, inspired by an understanding of the objective, by loyalty to a leader, to a team, and above all to a cause. 

Success in battle is dependent on speed and precision, which can be attained only by prompt, unquestioning, enthusiastic obedience. Brevity and conciseness are the keynotes of battlefield orders. The soldier is given as much information as time and other considerations will permit. But when explanations are lacking, the soldier responds unhesitatingly because he has confidence in his leaders. This confidence is a result of the soldier's training-training in which he has been given all of the facts needed to evaluate his leaders' judgment and ability.

In one form or another, good leaders have always used these troop training methods. 

Perfection in leadership is a goal which is never possible of complete attainment. And yet, by relating certain fundamental principles of good leadership to the immediate task at hand, Army Field Forces has succeeded in achieving high-quality leadership on much broader scale than would otherwise be possible.

We have great faith in the American youth. 

We believe he is essentially sound, instinctively decent, and will usually do the right thing if we show him what is right and explain why we think it is right. Our responsibility is not limited to teaching military techniques. 

To make the man a better fighter we must make him a better citizen. We must help him relate the task at hand to the greater goals of our free American society.

As the paramount training objective, this Office set forth a policy: that since fighting are the one important commodity of which there is never enough and for which there is no acceptable substitute, all training 11

must stress that every soldier regardless of assignment has as his primary duty the obligation to fight.

The Training Program The Army Field Forces training program presents a progressive pattern, from the training of recruits through advanced individual training, basic unit training, and advanced unit training to combined arms training, field exercises, and

Paralleling this pattern is the Army Field Forces school system, which provides personnel capable of conducting this training, all the way from enlisted specialists up to staffs and commanders of divisions. maneuvers.

Army Field Forces training policy emphasizes reduction of training requirements to absolute essentials, elimination of all lost motion, wartime standards in peacetime training, and constant improvement of training methods. It calls for development of leadership by providing the maximum opportunity to officers and enlisted men of the lower ranks and grades to participate in training to the limit of their capabilities, both as instructors and as trainees. It demands that personnel become adaptive to field and combat situations by extensive and realistic field training. It requires development of cohesion and control through stress of both vertical and lateral teamwork, and maintains proficiency at the highest possible level by periodic retraining.

Cadre training, to provide key personnel for the activation of new units in any mobilization, is emphasized. Individuals already trained in their primary military occupational specialty are given maximum opportunity for training in related or higher specialties. Concurrently with other training, understudies are trained for all key positions, so that units at all times can provide cadre personnel for like units, from companies to divisions.

Since the mission of each arm and service normally calls for it to work and fight together with other arms and services, all training is conducted with this teamwork in mind. Every opportunity is taken to bring the arms and services together in training. In an infantry division, when the infantry arm has completed its branch training, its supporting arms, including artillery, armor, and engineers, and the appropriate combat support services, are brought in to complete the team.

Realism is achieved in training without sacrifice of essential safety factors. In the postwar program, training in infiltration courses is not authorized, and training in combat in cities and villages and in close combat is conducted without the use of service ammunition. The use of live mines and booby traps is confined to demonstrations only, and training in live demolitions is conducted only in appropriate courses in schools by personnel or units required to use demolitions. Firecrackers and small demolitions are used, however, to simulate battle noise and add realism to training. 

Training inspections are conducted as necessary, but are coordinated and consolidated to prevent duplication and undue interference with troop training time. Reports are held to a minimum, but interchange of helpful information is encouraged. 12

The training week is established at an average of forty hours, distributed over a period of five to five and one half days. 

Sundays and national holidays are observed, and no training is normally scheduled during the Thanksgiving and Christmas holiday seasons, to permit a maximum of personnel to visit their families. 

The training cycle covers a period of forty-two training weeks, and applies not new units but to those which, only to because of turnover of personnel, have received fillers in such numbers as to necessitate the complete retraining of the unit. 

The first phase of the cycle is basic military training, which prepares the individual with no previous military service to receive subsequent advanced training by conditioning him mentally, morally, and physically, and by developing in him elementary skills of general application throughout the Army. Thirteen weeks are devoted to this training, with one additional week added for reception and processing. This phase is accomplished in training divisions, established in lieu of the wartime replacement training centers, and bearing the names of famous wartime divisions for the sake of morale.

The next twenty weeks of the cycle are devoted to advanced individual training and basic and advanced unit training, on a variable schedule depending upon the progress registered. Each of the three phases is followed by a test to verify proficiency. 

Advanced individual training prepares the basically trained soldier to function effectively as a member of a crew, squad, or detachment, and prepares him to be a leader and an instructor. It also provides training in technical specialties.

In basic unit training, individuals are welded into effective and coordinated crews, squads, platoons, companies, and battalions. 

During this period, advanced individual training is continued for men whose technical duties require additional instruction. 

Advanced unit training provides team training in which regiments, groups, separate battalions, and separate companies are combined into coordinated battlefield teams, and perfected in the tactics and techniques of efficient use of men, weapons, and equipment. 

Field exercises and maneuvers, the final nine weeks of the cycle, provide regimental combat teams and larger units with field training in tactical, logistical, intelligence, and administrative functions under simulated combat conditions. Combined arms training, in which a unit of an arm is trained together with a unit or units of other arms and services, is conducted concurrently. 

Intelligence training is continuous through all of these phases.

Units found deficient, as a result of tests, in any phase of training during the cycle. are given additional training until a proper standard is attained. Any phase or portion thereof is repeated as necessary, if repetition is necessary in maintaining proficiency.

All Army Field Forces units, both combat and service type, are given sufficient airmovement training to insure their ability to move by aircraft, with such of their equipment as is air-transportable and necessary to accomplish their mission on landing. The scope of such training includes aerial delivery; flight rules and safety regulations; familiarization with aircraft; characteristics of cargo aircraft and gliders; knowledge of ropes, knots, and special tie-down devices; computation of aircraft balance; the theory and mechanics of loading, lashing, and unloading of equipment; and actual air movement exercises when possible. Officers are trained in all aspects of staff planning appropriate to their level of operation, and in command and staff procedure for air movement, air landing, and resupply by air. The military materiel involved and mockups of the various aircraft, as well as actual planes, are employed in practical work.

The Army Field Forces School System 

In the years between World Wars I and II, with the U. S. Army at minimum strength and scattered in small detachments at a wide number of posts, its officers rarely had an opportunity to command more than a battalion in field exercises or maneuvers. Their tactical competence was refreshed and enhanced in the Army's schools, in which they led-if only on paper-regiments, divisions, corps, and armies. 

That same school system, during World 

War II, was readily capable of the rapid expansion necessary to provide officers, noncommissioned officers, and specialists for an Army numbered in millions. Following the war it was readjusted to meet the needs of the postwar Army, yet retain the progressive features which have made it and now make it one of the most remarkable educational systems, military or civilian, of the world. Some of its outstanding features, and the schools at which they have been developed or are now employed, are as follows:

COMMAND AND GENERAL STAFF COLLEGE 

At the Command and General Staff College, Fort Leavenworth, Kans., attended by five to seven hundred selected officers annually, courses are offered in the four principal general staff specialties—personnel, intelligence, operations, and logistics. All students receive the same instruction in the functioning of the military commander and in the duties which are common to all staff sections, but the class is divided into groups of approximately forty men each for more efficient instruction.

Conferences and exercises, with emphasis on student participation, have largely supplanted the lecture as the principal means of instruction, with increased student interest and learning as the result. The Navy and 

Air Force have cooperated whole-heartedly in the instruction in combined operations; the 1947 and 1948 classes observed Combine I and II, demonstrations of ground-air coordination, at Fort Benning, Ga., and witnessed Navy demonstrations at Coronado, Calif., of the latest amphibious techniques.

The 1949 class observed Combine III at 

Eglin Field, Fla.

For the past three years approximately thirty-six reserve officers who are professional educators in civilian life have served summer tours of active duty at the Command and General Staff College, to keep the college abreast of the latest educational practices in civilian universities, build a nucleus of potential instructors for service schools in time of national emergency, and make 18

available the special skills of individual reserve officers.

As examples of the latter, the head of the speech department of a civilian university has functioned in the instructor training section and as a professor of modern languages in the allied officers' section. A psychiatrist and a psychologist assisted in the writing of a text on military psychology; members of English departments of universities edited Army textbooks. Other qualified educators analyzed the college's instruction in personnel management and supply, and advised in its extension course program. 

The Commandant of the Command and General Staff College is responsible to the Chief, Army Field Forces, for the development of doctrine related to the employment of combined arms, and is authorized to make such visits to other schools as are necessary in fulfilling this responsibility. 

THE INFANTRY SCHOOL 

Courses in air-transportability, pathfinder techniques, tactics and techniques of selfpropelled and recoilless weapons, and maintenance and use of infantry liaison airplanes and counterfire sound-locating sets have been added to the curriculum of The Infantry 

School, Fort Benning, Ga., since the war.

Accelerated development of air transport of troops and equipment in World War II 

and the concept that, in any future war, units must be prepared to utilize aircraft as a normal means of transportation resulted in The Infantry School's course in air-transportability, so successful that it is now included in the instruction at all Army Field Forces schools, as well as given to all field forces units.

The pathfinder course was developed to meet the need experienced during the war for a unit to aid the Air Force in accurately delivering airborne troops and materiel to selected landing and drop zones. Air-transportability and aerial supply developments increased this need, now adequately met by The Infantry School's pathfinder course and the pathfinder platoon of the new airborne division with its specialized equipment. 

Postwar conditions confronting the infantry officer and noncommissioned officer have also required emphasis on instruction in civil affairs and military government, psychology of leadership and human relationships in the military service, and personal adjustment problems involved in the transition from civilian to military life and vice versa.

The regular courses of instruction at The Infantry School have been up-graded from regimental to divisional level, during the postwar period, with the result that this distinguished institution might more properly be called, today, The School of the 

Infantry Division. 

THE ARTILLERY SCHOOL 

The Artillery School, at Fort Sill, Okla., has made outstanding progress in the development of instructional methods. Among the innovations it has introduced are dynamic skits or “psychodramas," used to advantage in courses in which the applicatory stage requires the solution of problems in human relationships, e.g., leadership and 19

personnel management. The instructor pre sents a situation to the class, then selects several students to solve it and others to act out the solution. This is followed by a class discussion, in which related experiences and solutions are covered. Other skits are employed in nontechnical subjects, in which actions and decisions of the class may be discussed and critiqued by the students. 

Radio quiz program technique is successfully employed to stimulate class interest and participation in subjects involving the presentation of considerable factual matter. 

Magnetic tape recorders are used for voice and delivery analysis in student practice teaching periods and instructor rehearsals. 

Forum and tribunal classes are employed to take advantage of student experiences and knowledge. Students participating in roundtable discussions answer questions propounded by other students. Oral reviews or "examination contests" are used to stimulate student interest through competition; the questions used are prepared by the students as well as the instructor. In

The Artillery School has also been outstanding in the development of training aids and teaching devices with a maximum of effectiveness at minimum expense. addition to expanded use of graphic charts and illustrations, "flip boards" were devised to teach subjects in which it is necessary to reveal successive steps or procedures. "Slap-up charts," for classes involving the building up of a map or a machine before the students, are nothing more than a blanket hung against a wall, or over a stand, and cutouts backed with sandpaper, which adhere to the wool of the blanket. The cutouts are added to the blanket piece by piece to build up the map or machine. 

Instructors make sound film strips by writing their own manuscripts in conjunction with the film strip or slides to be presented, then record voice and sound effects on tape recorders. Humorous charts are utilized to drive home important points and furnish diversion in dealing with an otherwise serious subject; other charts are animated with colored ribbons or small electric bulbs. Contact charts, for teaching weapons nomenclature and functioning, have blacked-out areas which outline the equipment, requiring the student to place component parts on the chart in the proper position.

Finding that many students give so much attention to taking notes that they miss out on some of the instruction, The Artillery School furnishes for each class a lithographed, expanded outline of the subject matter, so that students may focus their entire attention on the instructor and use the outlines later in lieu of notes.

The Antiaircraft and Guided Missiles Branch of The Artillery School, at Fort 

Bliss, Tex., conducts the only service school course currently available on guided missiles, and numbers many Navy, Air Force, civilian component, and friendly foreign students in its classes.

The Coast Artillery Corps has been actively cooperating with the Navy in the field of joint instruction since 1927, a collaborative effort which culminated in 1947 in the estab lishment of the first U. S. Naval School of

The academic depart ment of the Army's school supplies one fourth of the instruction, and in practical field operations phases of the course Army and Navy equipment and facilities are used jointly. Equal numbers of Army and Navy officers have completed the course and are now qualified to serve as planners on the staffs of joint harbor defense commands.

THE ARMORED SCHOOL The benefits of the Army Field Forces school system are not restricted to the officers and noncommissioned officers who are detailed or selected to attend the schools. Each graduate is thoroughly trained both in his own specialty and in instructional techniques, so that he may ably instruct others in that specialty and in teaching proficiency as well. Thus the influence of each Army school is multiplied over and over until it permeates all training. 

The Armored School, at Fort Knox, Ky., is outstanding in the effectiveness of its instructor training course. Graduates are furnished special texts which include lesson plans and materials to be used in troop schools when they return to their units. 

During the academic year 1948-1949 The 

Armored School was the first Field Forces school to institute a course designed to improve the ability of its students to absorb as well as to impart instruction, by means of a remedial reading course. This course has now been authorized for all other Army 

Field Forces schools which are able to fit it within the limitations of their personnel, funds, and facilities.

In this course, students learn to read more rapidly, with better comprehension and less fatigue. They practice recognition of five-, seven-, and nine-digit numbers flashed before them for intervals of from 1/25th to 1/100th of a second, with accompanying drill in speed reading on reading rate controllers. 

As a result of this training, 132 Advanced

Class officers made a gain in reading rate from a median of 270 words per minute to 494, with an accompanying increase in comprehension of from 70% to 78%. Members of the Basic Class-younger men, on the average-increased their median reading rate from 338 to 700 words per minute, with comprehension increasing from 82% to 83%.

Other new postwar courses at The Armored School include military psychology and radiological defense. Training aids developed include a "slidograph," which projects an image which may be seen by students in a lighted room, and a communication trainer for radio transmission practice under simulated combat conditions, including battlefield noise. Each student's transmission is recorded so that he may himself hear the errors he has made.

The Armored School has been outstanding in its development of plastic training aids. 

Weapons, electrical switchboards, etc., have been constructed with plastic exteriors which expose their working parts, so that students may clearly see and fully understand their functioning. 21

THE GROUND GENERAL SCHOOL The Ground General School at Fort Riley, Kans., is a postwar addition to the Army 

Field Forces school system. Established in 

1946 by consolidation of the former Cavalry

School and the Intelligence School, it continues those functions of the former which have not been transferred to The Armored School, and all of the activities of the Intelligence School, as well as a number of added responsibilities.

The Ground General School conducts 

"branch immaterial" courses for the basic training of officers of all arms, officer candidate courses, and general and special courses in intelligence, photo interpretation, order of battle, and prisoner of war interrogation. 

The Officers' Basic Course is attended by all newly commissioned Regular Army officers from whatever source during their first year of service. It is designed to instruct officers who have had no previous troop duty in the responsibilities and duties of junior officers, show them their part in the groundsea-air service team, and start them off on their careers a similar professional and personal basis.

Officer candidate courses have been expanded from the wartime thirteen weeks to six months, followed, after commissioning, by an officers' associate basic branch course of thirteen weeks at the arm or service school concerned.

Army Extension Courses 

At all Army Field Forces schools, where appropriate, special associate courses, of shorter duration than the regular courses, are provided for officers and enlisted men of the National Guard and the Organized 

Reserve Corps. To provide instruction for civilian component personnel who are unable to leave their normal employment, the extension course program, suspended during the war, was re-initiated early in 1946 for the purpose of providing members of reserve components with progressive courses of nonresident instruction. 

The Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, is responsible for approving the extension curricula of both the general and the special service schools, as well as for reviewing and approving the individual subcourses. Because much prewar material had been rendered obsolete by changes in doctrine, procedure, and organization, it was necessary to revise completely all of the courses and to publish wholly new material.

This has been a tremendous undertaking, in view of the 1,000-odd subcourses to be prepared. More than half of these subcourses, however, averaging fifteen hours participation time each, have now been processed and approved for publication, and preparation is well in advance of student progress through each series.

The courses have been streamlined and brought up to date by applying techniques learned from large civilian correspondence schools, such as the International Correspon dence School, LaSalle Extension University, 

DeForest Training, Inc., and the University of Wisconsin Extension School. 

Students, of whom there are now nearly 

100,000, are enrolled at their branch schools, where their military education is administered 23

along lines paralleling resident courses of instruction. (See figure 4.) 

Recently the the program has become even more important as a result of use of extension courses as a means by which reserve component officers may earn credit toward maintenance of their active reserve status and qualification for retirement pay.

As additional courses become available, participation is increasing, particularly among enlisted students who are working for commissions. Many Regular Army officers and enlisted men and reserve component personnel on extended active duty have enrolled, to round out their basic education, keep abreast of latest developments and doctrine, and better prepare themselves for resident courses.

Special Course, Command and 

General Staff

Since senior officers in the reserve components usually occupy civilian positions commensurate with their wartime military responsibilities, it is difficult for such officers to attend Army Field Forces schools for resident instruction. For such officers, Army 

Field Forces in 1948 instituted a special 

Command and General Staff Course of three phases, presented at a convenient center or centers in each Army area. Each phase consists of two weeks of instruction, to be given once a year for three successive years. 

Selected civilian component officers act as instructors, assisted by personnel assigned from each Army. Lesson plans, material, and examinations were prepared by the 

Command and General Staff College. Phase 

I was presented with much success in 1948, and Phases I and II concurrently, with similar success, in 1949.

Civilian Schooling for Army 

Field Officers 

To provide a group of combat officers with sufficient training in scientific matters to evaluate technical developments in industry and in the armed forces, Army Field Forces since the war has detailed a limited number of selected officers to civilian universities for one to two years for the purpose of obtaining master's degrees and in some cases the degree of Doctor of Philosophy. 

Such officers have attended and are now attending some of the outstanding universities in the United States, pursuing courses in many scientific and technical fields. 

Upon graduation, they are applying their scientific training in the the furtherance of military requirements, instructing in service schools, acting as liaison officers on research and development projects, training troops in the use and maintenance of new equipment, and serving as members of higher staffs and of Army Field Forces test boards.

Leaders' Courses 

Throughout military history, the main effect upon warfare of the invention of successively more powerful weapons has been increased dispersion of armed forces into smaller coordinated groups, led by a prodigious number of leaders trained to function on their own in harmony with the commander's plan.

After counting out the civilian requirements for the industrial war effort in any future emergency, it became apparent that 24

even the United States would have difficulty in providing enough military leaders for the combat effort required. Accordingly, in 

December 1946, Army Ground Forces began developing its Leaders' Courses. 

These courses had as objectives the discernment and selection of potential leaders early in their military careers, and the development of their leadership qualities through example, instruction, and broadening the scope of their activities. For Leaders'

Course students the training divisions provided ideal opportunities to learn through actual leading of basic trainees. In May 1947, the four training divisions then operating established Leaders' Courses, which were so successful that these courses have since been included at all six of the present training divisions and at the WAC training center.

In addition to developing noncommissioned officer prospects who have developed in turn into officer material through added experience and the medium of the Army 

Officer Candidate School, these Leaders'

Courses have served another very important purpose. As "islands of perfection," in their methods of instruction, administration, barracks, and all that concerns them, they have influenced first the training divisions, and now the Army as a whole.

From November 1947 to April 1949, fifteen air support missions were arranged by Army Ground-Field Forces for schools and units. The majority of these joint exercises were of battalion size and demonstrated the mechanics of close air support, giving students valuable experience in actually coordinating and conducting ground-air op erations. The largest was MESQUITE, con ducted by the 2d Armored Division at 

Camp Hood, Tex. 25

Exercise ASSEMBLY permitted personnel of the Army and Air Force to test their proficiency on an intermediate staff level in an actual field maneuver. In addition, this exercise tested doctrine and provided field training for an airborne division with tactical and logistical air support. The employment of tactical aircraft was in accordance with approved doctrine; however, the 

Air Task Force supported only one division, which therefore had more air support than normal. This exercise presented an opportunity for joint staff work and planning that heretofore had been only simulated. It was recommended that a joint air-ground agency be established and maintained on a permanent basis for the development and improvement of doctrine and to assist in operations and training.

Exercise TARHEEL covered the employment, organization, and technique of operation of the Fire Support Coordination Center 

(FSCC) and included air missions. Various types of air support missions were flown, testing the technique of integrating all types of fire support. The coordination of target marking and air control was demonstrated at different command levels, showing the close coordination necessary between the artillery and the air in close air support. 

Reports were satisfactory and recommendations were again made to establish a joint agency for air-ground work. 

Army Field Forces has recommended to the

Air Force that such beneficial cooperation be continued on an expanded scale, and that a 

Tactical Air Force Headquarters, including a 

Joint Operations Center with necessary Army and Air Force communication units, be established to assist in joint air-ground operations and training. The Air Force has responded by activating such a headquarters at Pope Air Force Base, N. C., in close proximity to the 

V Corps at Fort Bragg, and joint directives have been issued to air and ground headquarters establishing the close relationship necessary for air-ground operations.

In order to continue to advance in the field of close air support of ground units, it is believed that the Army must have primary interest in the close air support phase of air-ground operations. In order to develop doctrine and gain knowledge and experience in the close air support of ground forces, it is necessary that the proper type of aircraft for this type of mission be determined by the Air Force, assembled into air groups, and made available for joint training. Both air and ground forces officers must be trained in joint planning and operations. 

All demonstrations and maneuvers should, whenever possible, include air-ground action in keeping with the basic purpose of the demonstration or maneuver.

If the desired development of doctrine and technique on a mutual cooperation basis does not result from the missions assigned the V Corps and the provisional 

Tactical Air Force Headquarters, a joint airground center should be established in order to keep the doctrine of air-ground operations alive and progressive, and to provide a means for the joint testing and evaluation of new developments in equipment, ideas, and organization. 26

Many problems of air support of ground forces remain to be resolved, the most important of which, from the ground viewpoint, is the matter of decentralization of air support. The Air Force still retains the power of decision as to the attack of appropriate ground targets, and the interested ground commander has the power of recommendation only.

ARCTIC, MOUNTAIN, AND WINTER WARFARE 

TRAINING 

On 1 June 1946, Army Ground Forces established the Mountain Winter Warfare

School and Training Center at Camp Car son, Colo. This school was under Army

Major Advances in Training

LEADERSHIP TRAINING 

A major advance has been made in leadership training. The outstanding Army combat leaders in the history of this nation were pioneers in applied sociology and psychology, but until early 1947 the principles and practices of good leadership, gained by knowledge, education, and experience, were passed on from the leaders to their subordinates primarily by accident of assignment. Those who happened to serve under outstanding leaders were more fortunate and better prepared to handle men than those who served under poor ones. 

The success of the Leaders' Courses and of the UMT Experimental Unit came as a result of codifying and applying the principles and practices of good leadership. These principles and practices are now being made available to all future potential leaders. The 

Army is gradually developing the social skills which help adapt individuals to changing situations and which promote the smooth interpersonal relations that mold individuals into a team-the skills that our better combat leaders have been practicing for over a century. By stressing the social skills in Army training, Army Field Forces has brought them into better balance with the already well-emphasized technical skills, 30

thus assuring teamwork of the highest type for our Army of the future.

THE AGGRESSOR FORCE 

In combat, the United States Army and those of a number of other nations customarily represent friendly lines and forces, on operations and intelligence maps, in blue crayon, and enemy dispositions in red.

Out of this custom developed the practice, in peacetime maneuvers, of designating one force as the "Blues" and the opposing force as the "Reds."

Aside from the obvious connotation which would be attached to this practice in the present postwar period, there were other unsatisfactory aspects to prewar conduct of maneuvers. The troops on each side were identical in uniform, organization, doctrine, equipment, and tactics. Valuable training in field operations and combat tactics was achieved, but intelligence training was largely limited to scouting, patrolling, and observation. The intelligence specialtiesorder of battle, photo interpretation, counterintelligence, psychological warfare, and prisoner of war interrogation-could not be employed to the extent deemed desirable. 

Accordingly, Army Ground Forces developed the idea of a “maneuver enemy," and the basic concept was presented in 1946 to the six Armies in the United States for comment and suggestions, and to the Sixth

Army for a test. This test was Exercise 

OILSKIN, held at Camp Pendleton, Calif., in October 1946; and in it the maneuver enemy, called "Aggressor," amply demonstrated its worth as a training aid. 

After a study of OILSKIN, Army Ground 

Forces directed The Ground General School to organize and develop an "Aggressor" force, and personnel were ordered to Fort Riley, Kans., to form a permanent cadre for use in connection with the practical application of Aggressor Force in the field.

By the summer of 1947 the basic development of Aggressor Force was completed; this consisted of the establishment of a fictitious aggressor nation, its ideology and economic and political history, and the organization (order of battle) of its armed forces. Three field manuals were published, The Maneuver Enemy, Handbook on Aggressor Military Forces, and Aggressor Army 

Order of Battle. They were for use not only in maneuvers but also in joint training, field exercises, command post exercises, and classroom problems in the Army school system, just as similar manuals on the German and Japanese armies were used during the war. 

Field work included the development of doctrine, techniques, and tactics for the use of Aggressor troops. Class X clothing, beyond salvage, was dyed green and, with the addition of colored cloth tabs and adaptation of U. S. insignia, such as the use of the crossed sabres of the cavalry to denote rank rather than branch, provided a distinctive uniform for Aggressor Force. Addition of a wooden "comb" to the U. S. helmet liner gave it a peculiar and distinctive silhouette which further set Aggressor Force apart.

THE TARGET GRID SYSTEM 

Up to the close of World War II, the field artillery employed two methods of conducting observed fire, the battery commander method and the forward observer method. 

Both were accurate and efficient, but both were subject to certain disadvantages as well.

In the battery commander method, the observer had complete control of the mission at all times, calculating his data and sending fire commands directly to the battery. This was the most accurate method, but it could not be efficiently employed, for instance, by an observer in a front-line infantry foxhole, because of the computations involved, nor by a liaison pilot, because of the evasive action which required his attention and the added fact that his own position was constantly changing, making completed computations impossible. To conduct this type of fire field artillery officers well skilled in gunnery techniques were required.

The method almost universally employed in World War II, therefore, was the forward observer method, in which the observer, instead of calling for a certain range and so much deflection right or left, "sensed" the impact of the preceding round or rounds, and reported the distance from the target in yards, over, short, right, or left. This sensing was transmitted to a firedirection center, which converted it to a fire command and conveyed the fire command to the battery.

Only a little less accurate, this method under certain circumstances required the expenditure of more ammunition. Upon the termination of the war, therefore, the General 

Board, U. S. Forces European Theater, rec ommended that experiments be made and study conducted to simplify the observer's procedure in the conduct of fire missions, without sacrificing any of the accuracy which had been provided by the battery commander method.

By 1946, research at The Artillery School had developed a procedure which simplified the computations necessary on the part of the observer, and still permitted him to deal only in terms of yards, which made it possible for any Army, Navy, or Air personnel, and not just artillerymen, to observe and adjust both field artillery fire and naval gunfire. Since a certain amount of trigonometrical computation was still necessary, however, observer personnel still had to be trained for the task. 

In 1948, The Artillery School developed the Target Grid System, commonly referred as "Comanche," which was adopted in 

1949 as the Army's current observed fire method. This system, while only slightly increasing the functions of the fire-direction center, simplifies the task of the observer to such an extent that any individual, trained or not, and of any service, can accurately adjust artillery fire. The observer need not know the location of the battery, nor the battery that of the observer. The only factor which must be reported to the battery is the azimuth-the compass bearing-from the observer to the target, and the location of the target with reference to a known check point. Thereafter the observer adjusts 34

for the battery in terms of yards, reporting the range and deflection changes necessary with regard to his own position.

TRAINING ECONOMIES 

Motion picture films and film strips were among the most effective training aids during World War II, but because of their high production cost have been produced during the postwar period only if a specific problem in training exists, if a movie or film strip is the most suitable aid for the problem, and if the prospective audience is large enough to justify the man-hours and cost of production. 

Within this policy, forty new sound films have been produced to drive home war-learned lessons and supplement the 290 in the 1945 library. Based upon comparative annual showings and the number who see each showing, and upon periodic descriptive ratings from 

Army Field Forces schools and training divisions, revision of a number of the older films is now in progress. The oldest film in the library, Map Reading, dates back to 1931, but had 300 showings in 1948. 

With the closing of many replacement training centers after the war, the useful training aids developed by those centers were shipped to active centers and schools for further use. Descriptions of locally produced training aids have been incorporated in training manuals, and permanent exhibits of representative training aids have been established at each Army Field Forces school.

Many new training devices, particularly valuable to units of the reserve components, have been developed and made available to units in training. One such device is the

This device is useful in teaching the principles of artillery adjustment, and provides this training without the costly expenditure of ammunition.

There has been little centralized production of three-dimensional training aids of the more simple type, since units can often produce items from basic plans which are included in training manuals. Each training division has a training aids shop which is equipped with silk screens, woodworking equipment, material, etc., for development and limited local production of training aids. 

If such aids prove widely useful and can be produced and distributed more economically by a central agency, they are standardized and established in appropriate tables of allowances.

Research and Development HE DEVELOPMENT of equipment to be THE 

utilized in the field army of the future has been an important mission of Army 

Ground-Field Forces during the postwar period. The "user's" viewpoint and interest have been held uppermost in the execution of this responsibility. This policy is based on the doctrine that realistic and exacting field service tests under simulated combat conditions are essential, to avoid adoption of unworthy items which may not fulfill the purpose for which they are issued or which may result in unnecessary sacrifice of life through failure in combat.

Security regulations prevent the publication of details of specific development projects, most of which are themselves classified and cannot be disclosed. For this reason, development work can be described only in general terms. 

Let it suffice to say that hundreds of projects are being currently handled; that the highly important work of insuring that our Army is provided with weapons and equipment superior to any potential enemy's is proceeding expeditiously. At the same time every effort is being made to keep to the minimum the number of items standardized in order to reduce the load on industry. 

During World War II, service tests were conducted by the Airborne, Antiaircraft, Armored, Cavalry, Coast Artillery, Field Artillery, Infantry, Landing Vehicle, Rocket, and Tank 

Destroyer Boards. To place these boards on a more functional basis by grouping related test, research, and development matters, to avoid duplication of effort, and to effect economies in the use of man power and funds, all were discontinued in the fall of 1945, and their functions consolidated in three Army Ground Forces Boards, designated Army Ground Forces Board No. 

1, Fort Bragg, N. C.; Board No. 2, Fort 

Knox, Ky.; and Board No. 3, Fort Benning,

Ga. The following year Board No. 4 was activated at Fort Bliss, Tex.

The mission of each of these boards is to evaluate recommendations for development, prepare military characteristics, and perform user tests for equipment of the field army. Each board makes recommendations for modifications, classification, basis of issue, and replacement of parts and accessories for this equipment. Each board also studies and evaluates foreign developments and and initiates recommendations for development in its field.

The user testing by organized Army Field Forces Boards has proven superior to the prewar system of using only tactical troop units. The relatively few personnel required are more than justified by the greater thoroughness of planning and testing, and especially by the superior coordination and documentation obtained.

Army Field Forces Board No. 1 

Board No. 1 is primarily concerned with airborne equipment, Army light aircraft, com- 37

munication and electronic equipment, heavy weapons not man-transportable or integral with armored vehicles, and associated equipment.

Board No. 1 studies and tests many items of airborne equipment and airborne transport methods, such as safer parachutes, better load containers for parachute drops, improved methods for loading and lashing airborne cargos, and advanced technique for aerial delivery. as In addition, the Board has prepared military characteristics for various types of cargo aircraft and gliders, and assists in an advisory capacity in their development. One forwardlooking project, completed early in 1949, is a large tractor aircraft which would transport detachable compartments known "pods," somewhat in the manner of a tractor and semitrailer van. Such compartments may be loaded separately with men, equipment, supplies, or other cargo, then moved to an airfield and attached to the tractor or carrier aircraft. Pods may be fitted for special operations, by being equipped as shops, hospitals, or similar installations.

In the field of Army aircraft, considerable attention is being devoted to the rotary-wing type. Tests have been completed on two kinds of helicopters which show great promise. In the fixed-wing light aircraft field, projects include cross-wind landing gear, night lighting for air strips, navigational aids and homing devices, and portable all-weather hangars.

In the communication and electronics field, where development is unusually rapid because of the parallel development of civilian and commercial items, the Board has had many projects. Current examples of progress include a system of communication switching which connects field army radios to telephone switchboards; a lightweight, portable teletypewriter complete with power plant, which can be mounted in a jeep; field telephone switchboards of greatly reduced weight but with an increased number of circuits; field wire and cables of reduced weight and greater strength; and a whole new series of lightweight frequency-modulated radio sets which give better transmission and reception and intercommunication between radio nets of all arms.

The trend in field artillery is toward self-propelled and materially improved towedtype mounts for all types of artillery; improved fire control, ranging, survey, and target acquisition equipment; increased accuracy of weapons; and improved ammunition for all weapons. Self-propelled weapons tested and due for testing include heavier calibers as well as the famous 105-mm howitzer. Tests of the 105-mm howitzer on a new lightweight towed carriage, desirable for airborne operations, are under way. Board No. 1 has revised military characteristics for automatic data computing and transmitting systems, surveying and plotting equipment, and methods of obtaining more accurate meteorological information.

Army Field Forces Board No. 2 

Board No. 2 at Fort Knox, Ky., is concerned with the development of all wheeled and tracked vehicles used in the field army, including weapons, ammunition, and firecontrol equipment for armored vehicles. It 38

is also responsible for all special amphibious and oversnow vehicles, and all engineer and medical equipment used in the field army.

From the standpoint of the user, three general factors must be considered in the design of a military vehicle: first, its "fightability" or ability to perform its primary functions; second, the ease with which it may be maintained; and third, its ability to be transported by air.

Experience with the repair of military vehicles during World War II emphasized a definite requirement for maintenance which can be performed quickly and easily under adverse conditions. To bring this requirement to the attention of all agencies concerned and to assist in its solution, Board No. 2 is currently conducting an conducting an “ease of maintenance" program. The results of this program are reflected in the many ease-of-maintenance features now being incorporated in newly developed military vehicles. Some of these features are power package units; quickdisconnect electrical wiring; quick-disconnect and shut-off devices in oil, fuel, and hydraulic lines; lifting eyes in heavy assemblies; accessibility of units for servicing, removal, and replacement; and standardization of hardware.

In the military characteristics for new vehicles, ease of maintenance is considered the next most important characteristic after fightability and utility. It is expected to result in a minimum of deadline time for vehicles and materially reduce the number of replacement vehicles required.

A full-track, completely armored personnel carrier, which will contain all the newly developed automotive components, will re place the half-track of World War II. Wheeled cross-country cargo carriers have been developed to overcome the lack of tactical mobility of present-day motor cargo vehicles, and a new five-ton carrier has already traveled across country in a manner superior to any other wheeled vehicle in its weight class. Also being developed are 22-ton and 4-ton crosscountry carriers. These carriers are not intended to replace all trucks in the Army, but will be used in units to which off-theroad performance is of prime importance. 

Development of tanks is being concentrated on a family of three: light, medium, and heavy. In the tank development program efforts are being made to improve gun performance, i. e., increase armor penetration and accuracy in laying; to improve engines by increasing power and providing the maximum interchangeability of parts; and to provide improved armor protection. 

Regarding engineer equipment, Board No. 

2 has reviewed the entire field of military bridging, and desirable modifications in existing stocks and required new developments are now under way. Increased emphasis is being placed on the field of vehicular-launched assault type bridges. Fixed type bridging in the higher load classifications has been designed for rapid erection and most economical use of materials. Many other significant and improved items of engineer equipment are undergoing development. 39

Army Field Forces Board No. 3 

Board No. 3 at Fort Benning, Ga., is concerned with equipment used by individuals and units of a field army primarily within the following categories: man-transportable weapons 4.5-inch and below, ammunition and fire control equipment therefor, and associated equipment; clothing, equipment, and protective devices for the individual; small-detachment and individual rations and food packets, and field messing facilities; and animal equipment. 

Postwar trends in the small-arms field are toward reduction in the number of types, in weight, and in smoke and flash. Promising progress has been made in the development of a new lightweight machine gun and a lightweight rifle, the latter to replace the present rifle, carbine, and submachine gun. Range and operating efficiency of the sniperscope have been increased. Mortars will have increased range and portability and less weight; rockets increased armor penetration, accuracy, and range. Recoilless rifles will have increased armor penetration with reductions in weight and back-blast. 

The Board has conducted studies to determine military characteristics for the above items as well as for new range finders, individual heating devices, an arctic winter clothing ensemble, a mobile kitchen, a family of tents and shelters for arctic use, a lightweight rainsuit, a family of man-drawn sleds, an all-purpose helmet, a new duffle bag, and an illumination system for command posts.

Representative items which have been tested by Board No. 3 in the postwar period and subsequently standardized include the following: a new sniperscope, a new light mortar, a target practice shell for recoilless rifles, multicolored ground signals, a new caliber .30 tracer cartridge for rifles and carbine, a 3.5-inch rocket launcher and ammunition, a flash hider for the rifle, carbine, and submachine gun, and a link ammunition belt coupler and disconnector for the caliber 30 machine gun. Other items are a new cotton undershirt, a trigger-finger mitten insert for arctic use, a general purpose pocket knife, plastic spoons, a four- to sixman arctic shelter, new kitchen and command post tents, a small tent stove, a new canvas water sterilization bag, and a new combat gas mask and gas mask repair kit. 

The basic design of combat clothing will be on the layer principle, with increases in utility and a decrease in weight. Modifications of present types and new designs will be tested further in arctic and other climatic extremes. Many types of shelters have been tested to determine requirements for individual, half-squad, squad, maintenance, and command tents; all types will accommodate stoves or space heaters. 

In oversnow equipment, the basic design of sleds is under study, with present emphasis on boat type hulls of plastic or metal. Better skis and snowshoes are under development, and a lightweight metal packboard and a lighter rucksack have been tested and approved.

New types of individual and small-detachment rations and food packets are be- 40

ing developed and tested, in an attempt to increase palatability and utility and reduce bulk and weight. Cooking equipment is under development to provide small and compact but versatile kitchen units for use by units of company size and smaller. Kitchenware and tableware of plastic material has been developed.

Loading tests have been conducted and revisions in tables of equipment recommended to enable infantry units to move tactically with the proper loads of ammunition and equipment, in surface vehicles, by pack animal transport, or by air. Pack equipment has been redesigned and modified to reduce weight and increase strength.

Army Field Forces Board No. 4 

Board No. 4 at Fort Bliss, Tex., is concerned with antiaircraft artillery equipment and ammunition, fire control, and accessories therefor; guided missiles and associated equipment, to include launching, handling, servicing, testing, and training; and aerial targets, to include launching and control equipment. 

In the postwar period this Board has submitted more than twenty-seven sets of military characteristics to form the basis for the development of future antiaircraft artillery and guided missile equipment, has tested over fifty-three new items of equipment and over sixteen proposed modifications to existing equipment, and has conducted many related studies, all of which have been submitted in formal reports. 

Some of the results of the activities of the Board are now in the hands of the using troops, notably a self-propelled dual 40mm antiaircraft gun mount and a radio controlled aerial target capable of simulating flight characteristics of conventional aircraft, for use in antiaircraft target practice.

Other items still in the process of being completed include improved fuzes and ammunition, electronic fire control devices for both AAA automatic weapons and guns, and communication requirements for AAA units. 

Some of the test facilities constructed especially for this Board include two Eastman precision phototheodolites, used to determine the precise location of aerial targets, accurate in elevation and azimuth to plus or minus one tenth of a mil; three mobile data recorders which electrically receive and record six elements of data at one-second intervals; one mobile central control station which automatically supplies electrical synchronizing pulses to the 41

data recorders; and a ballistic computer used to determine AAA gun-director firing errors in 1½ to 3½ minutes, depending upon the complexity of the problem.

The Board also has film slide rules which are in principle slide rules with exceedingly long scales. Each scale, on 35-mm motion picture film, is 200 feet long, and the rules have an over-all precision of approximately 

1 part in 20,000. Other equipment includes an automatic plotting board which provides a continuous plot of the present and predicted position of the target being tracked. 

In the conduct of its operations, the Board utilizes the facilities and know-how not only of the technical development agencies of the armed forces, such as nearby 

White Sands Proving Ground, the Ordnance

Research and Development Sub-Office (Roc ket), and Holoman Air Force Base, but also of the most outstanding industrial firms in the United States in this field, such as the 

Bell Telephone Laboratories, Sperry Gyro scope Company, Eastman Kodak, and others.

From the experience and recommendations of the 1st Guided Missile Regiment will come the guided missile units of the future; in fact, many guided missile battalions have already been established in the Organized Reserve Corps in the various Army areas, and graduates of the Guided Missile Course of the Antiaircraft Artillery and Guided Missile

Program Army Ground Forces had three specially organized task forces in operation during

Army Ground Forces studies had shown that American capabilities for combat were weakest in areas having cold climates, par- 42

Winter operations in Europe during World

War II had revealed many deficiencies in equipment, clothing, and cold weather techniques.

In order to overcome these voids in information, equipment, and skills, Army Ground Forces initiated planning in December 1945 for extensive winter tests of equipment. The tests yielded facts and data which enabled the Army to define and demonstrate the urgent need for development of superior equipment for warfare in all types of cold climate on a world-wide basis. The reports submitted by the three task forces are being used as source material for research and planning throughout the Army, by schools, boards, and training and planning agencies.

Personnel Program EDESIGNATION of Headquarters, Army 

Ground Forces, as the Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, in 1948 and removal of this Office from the Department of the 

Army chain of command greatly reduced its functions with respect to operations in personnel matters. However, as the field operating agency of the Department of the Army with regard to training, the Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, continued to be intimately concerned with the formulation and implementation of personnel policies affecting not only the Regular Army, but the 

Organized Reserve Corps, National Guard, and Reserve Officers' Training Corps as well. 

Early in the postwar period, while it retained its command functions, Army Ground Forces was confronted with a dilemma as regards personnel. There was the heavy pressure for rapid demobilization (see figure 

9). The criteria established for discharge made combat-experienced officers and noncommissioned officers eligible first of all, inevitably making heavy inroads on the number of trainer personnel available to Army Ground Forces units, schools, and boards. At the same time, the urgent need of overseas occupation commanders for large numbers of replacements increased the shortage of competent trained personnel. The postwar resumption of the civilian component training program also made heavy personnel demands.

With the expiration of selective service and dependence solely upon recruiting to meet these requirements, the problem became acute. Added to the general difficulties confronting Army Ground Forces was the fact that enlistments in the ground combat arms were not in proportion to their needs, nor comparable to enlistments in the administrative and technical services, the 

Navy, and the Air Force.

A board of general officers was appointed in 1947 by the Commanding General, Army 

Ground Forces, to make a thorough study of the problem of providing adequate enlistment incentives for the ground combat arms. This board's recommendations, submitted to the Department of the Army early in 1948, have now been largely implemented, with the result that the ground combat arms now securing more nearly the type and number of recruits they require, and are enjoying a re-enlistment rate which compares more than favorably with those of the services and the other armed forces. are 

Paramount among these recommendations was one for the initiation of a temporary housing program, calling for the conversion of barracks not required for troop housing into family quarters for enlisted men and junior officers, and the rehabilitation of barracks, mess halls, day rooms, and other buildings into comfortable and adequately furnished accommodations for enlisted men. 45

It was asked that the assignment of enlisted men within units of the combat arms be stabilized by authorization of enlistment or re-enlistment within a specific unit of the ground combat arms, that enlisted men be authorized to volunteer for overseas assignments, and that these requests receive proper consideration and action. Further, it was asked that the length of time after discharge during which an individual could re-enlist in his former grade be increased, and that World War II veterans with satisfactory service be given some latitude in passing recruiting tests. Intelligence Training URING THE WAR the Army established a Military Intelligence Training Center at Camp Ritchie, Md., where 11,000 technical experts and intelligence specialists were trained. Forces directed in October 1945 that an intelligence school be activated at Fort Benning, Ga. In November, the location of the school was changed to Fort Riley, Kans., where it was established as the Intelligence School under the Commandant of the Cavalry School. A year later, it became the

The broad mission of the Intelligence Instruction Division is the training of intelligence officers of division and smaller units, and of officers and enlisted men in the special techniques of aerial photo interpretation, order of battle, and interrogation of prisoners of war. At present, all Army Field Forces schools include suitable intelligence training in their programs, and training programs for Regular Army and civilian component units provide comprehensive guidance for those who conduct intelligence training, which is continuous from basic training through joint operations. Ground-Field Forces since V-J Day have included such varied projects as supply discipline, improving the quality and efficiency of the combat soldier in the interest of greater economy of equipment, master planning of installations, adequate family housing studies, the development of plans for functional arrangement of division type camps, and features of the new Logistical Command. 

A far-reaching project undertaken recently is an intensified program of supply discipline training. Each Army and training division headquarters was visited by a representative of Army Field Forces, who conducted a conference on supply discipline and obtained valuable ideas toward furthering and maintaining this index of efficiency. These ideas were published and distributed to all Armies to assist them in their programs of instruction. Those calling for action beyond the authority of this office and the Army commanders were forwarded to the Department of the Army with recommendations for appropriate action.

Instruction in supply discipline is now included concurrently with other training within the training divisions. Instruction is to be given in all Army Field Forces schools, including the Command and General Staff College, and periodic refresher instruction is to be required from unit and station commanders. This instruction is progressive, starting with the first day of recruit training and following through the levels of noncommissioned and commissioned officer training. A minimum of ten hours is prescribed for basic instruction in supply discipline as such, with appropriate modifications in the advanced and refresher courses.

A training film, The Late Company B, is available in the Film Library, and is being found an effective visual training aid in demonstrating the disastrous effects in combat of poor supply discipline. The script of another film, one of a series contemplated on this subject, is currently in preparation. 

As a test to determine progress in this field, a project now nearing completion will establish a method enabling commanders to measure readily the supply discipline in their commands through the study of normal periodic reports. It would complement, but not replace, visual inspections. One feature of this method is the application of replacement factors to make a comparison between the allowable and the actual replacement of items per one hundred men, using as a basis authorized strength and equipment. 

Available data reveal that, in general, individuals with higher Army General Classification Test scores (though not necessarily the highest) are more reliable as combat soldiers, and less wasteful of property. It follows that a unit made up of the more intelligent individuals will require less re- 50

placement equipment. Furthermore, personnel losses through absence without leave, unnecessary casualties, and action by courtsmartial will be lower, with consequent lowered logistical requirements. Figure 10 compares the physical profile of the infantry division of World War II with that of a division composed of personnel disclosed by study to be desirable from the foregoing points of view. The matter of assignment to combat units of only the latter category of personnel has been brought to the attention of the Department of the Army, where it is being considered.

Continuing efforts have been made in numerous directions to reduce the time required for the execution of tasks other than those of a strictly training nature. To this end this Office, in early 1949, drew the attention of Army commanders to its conclusion that too little attention was being given to the planning and supervision of fatigue and maintenance tasks. It was directed that steps be taken to impress upon unit commanders that such tasks present splendid opportunities to develop leadership qualities and initiative in the lower command echelons, a conception sure to bring greater efficiency of operation. Constructive comments on this directive have provided material which has been incorporated into the draft of the script for a training film on this subject, which has been submitted for preparation.

Under the so-called "Ring Plan," the Secretary of Defense would enter into contracts, directly or through representatives designated by him, to build specified numbers of housing units on post property at each of the posts concerned. These contracts would provide that payment of the cost of construction and profit be made in a fixed number of annual payments, including interest at an appropriate rate on the outstanding balance. 

These contracts would provide, further, that annual payment, maintenance, and operation be met from a fund created by pooling all rental allowances of those occupying this housing. The post commander would control the assignment of this housing, which would be owned by the United States Government. Proposed legislation implementing this plan has been drafted and is now under study by the Bureau of the Budget. 

In a related matter at the request of the 

Director of Logistics, Department of the

Army, this Office devised a functionally arranged division type camp for use in any future emergency. The layout under ideal terrain conditions, together with modifications to accommodate other less convenient terrain, is shown in figure 15. A brief description of this camp follows.

Starting from the premise that a circular layout would permit more economical concentration of a maximum of activities, an over-all functional plan was developed. This was broken down into type activity areas, and finally into subareas providing housing with adjacent motor parks on a battalion basis, all on concentric circles. The area within the inner circle accommodates all headquarters buildings and athletic fields in appropriate relation to their units, together with the facilities common to all units, such as post office, bank, bus station, main theatre, chapel, library, Red Cross, post exchange, and swimming pool. Surrounding this area, in successive concentric circles, divided in general into unit sectors, are: barracks; motor parks provided with storage and maintenance facilities for vehicular and heavy weapons, for communication equipment, and for the vehicles themselves in so far as strictly necessary; drill grounds; and close-in training areas. Beyond these circular areas are small-arms ranges, within easy marching distance of the barracks circle, and artillery ranges. Ranges for self-propelled weapons are located, as far as practicable, downwind from the areas closer in, so that dust from vehicular tracks will not be blown toward the inner areas. This layout has been approved by the Director of Logistics, and has been transmitted by him to The Infantry School for inclusion in courses on mobilization planning.

Successful efforts have been made to reduce the issue of clothing to enlisted men.

With the adoption in 1947 of the postwar type field army, a study was initiated to determine the distance that organization was capable of moving in a theater of operations while sustaining itself with respect to fuels and lubricants. This study was developed from consideration of requirements in these items for the type field army based upon the combat factors given in FM 101-10 as applied to consumption rates published in pertinent technical manuals. In this study it was estimated that 

4,602,000 gallons of gasoline would be required by vehicles of the field army for a movement of the mass a distance of 100 miles, operating under average combat conditions for an extended period, in a temperate zone. Thus the normal organic fuel resources would be exhausted in a move of 

116 miles.

The consequent problem facing communications zone agencies to maintain the fuels and lubricants supply of the type field army leads to the conclusion that both of the following points should be promptly explored: (1) reduction of the number of fuel-consuming items of equipment, and (2) increase in the efficiency of such items of equipment in order to decrease their fuel consumption.

In November 1948, at the direction of the Secretary of the Army, Army Advisory 

Panels in the fields of amphibious, airborne, and air defense operations met at the Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, to define the Department of the Army position on basic doctrine, policies, and procedures on joint matters pertaining to their respective fields. 

Amphibious matters covered included command, communication, fire control, air support, assault, logistical support, and engineer special brigade procedures. Recommendations 

IN 

IN ORDER to increase the efficiency of the Army Field Forces through training and through the development of doctrine, technique, equipment, and facilities, the following measures are recommended: 

1. The maintenance of a high standard of requirements in the awarding of credit points for performance of reserve duty training. Such a policy is believed necessary in the interests of national security; a relaxation, as advocated by some, would adversely affect the training of Organized Reserve Corps units and individuals by lowering the morale of the active participants and by giving a false idea of training requirements to those who try to receive "something for nothing." The intent of the law is to reward active reservists for military training, satisfactorily performed. com- 2. The constant training of reserve manders to assume the full responsibilities which are theirs as commanders of units. 

The inevitable shortages of funds and Regular

Army personnel will have to be compensated for by reservists who assume more of the responsibilities for the training, administration, and supply of their units, if we to have an active, realistic reserve program. are 

3. Continuing joint study to simplify techniques and procedures in the air support of ground forces and to resolve problems of command relationships in the delivery of such support. 

4. Increase of air participation in school demonstrations and unit field exercises. Air participation adds realism to such exercises and shows the mechanics of close air support, including air and ground communication. 

5. Continuing efforts by the Army to induce the Air Force to bring troop carrier strength to a figure more in consonance with airborne requirements. 

6. The institution of measures to relieve the critical shortage of field officers within airborne divisions. 

7. Continuing efforts to reduce the weight and bulk of the equipment of the infantry division, with a view to air-transportability. 

8. Study of the logistical support for a four-division corps airhead, to resolve the controversial question of the possibility of large airborne operations. 

9. Assignment of responsibility for master planning of installations to the Office, Chief, Army Field Forces, in order to make effective use of an existing agency which has participated continuously in this project during the postwar period. 

10. Provision of badly needed family housing on permanent posts under a plan of payment from pooled commutation allowances of construction costs and incidental charges (Ring Plan, first choice; Wherry Plan, second choice). 63

11. Further study of the types and quantities of fuel-consuming equipment in the type field army with respect to over-all fuel consumption and ability to maintain that organization in an active theater of operations. 

12. Consideration of inclusion of one or more logistical commands in the Organized Reserve Corps troop basis for support of the 18-25 Division Program. 

13. Establishment of a joint amphibious training center at Little Creek, Va. The 

Army requirements for large-scale amphibious operations must be given proper consideration. 

14. Continuation of amphibious training of

Army troops on a scale of approximately one divisional-sized amphibious exercise, in conjunction with the Navy and the Air Force, each year. The continuation of instruction in amphibious staff planning at all service schools at the proper levels, and the retention of an engineer special brigade, at reduced strength in the peacetime troop basis, with its station on the east coast of the United 

States, preferably in the Norfolk area. 

15. Further study and test of the tactical uses of the atomic bomb. 

16. Restoration to commanders at all levels of their authority and responsibilities to the end that the Army will have teamwork instead of individual action. During and at the close of every war there is a very strong tendency to take more and more responsibility away from the commanders and place it in the hands of specialists. Since World 

War II this has been evident in the conduct of military justice, food service, career guidance for enlisted men, and career management of officers. While purposes in these plans are excellent and tend to improve the Army, their implementation must become decentralized as responsibilities of the commanders. The intent is often stated by the proponents of these plans that they are the responsibilities of commanders. Actually, the restrictions imposed in their implementation take away the commanders' authority and responsibilities. 

17. The firm establishment and maintenance of high physical and intelligence standards in the selection and assignment of personnel for duty in combat positions. 

18. Enhancement of the prestige of the commander at the noncommissioned level by a return to a system of titles and insignia that will distinguish the noncommissioned leader from the enlisted technician. 

19. Maintenance of Army Field Forces Boards and the Arctic Test Branch at maximum authorized strength; augmentation of Board No. 2 by an engineer test detachment of enlisted men; personnel of Army 

Field Forces Boards be not transferred or reassigned by Army commanders without concurrence of the Chief, Army Field Forces; place the operation and supervision of the four Boards under direct control of this

Office. There appears to be a tendency to continue to reduce the strength of the 

Army Field Forces Boards under the misguided belief that tactical troops can do efficient testing and evaluation of new equipment. Testing expensive and complicated 64

equipment is a science, requiring the full time and thought of experienced personnel. 

The cost of these Boards, with their specially selected officers and well-trained and experienced enlisted test sections, is very small when compared to the savings they are effecting, not only in assuring the acceptance of efficient, well-engineered equipment but also in assuring the rejection of unsuitable equipment which would lead to wasted procurement funds. These Boards as now constituted are Our best insurance against unsound expenditure of development and procurement funds.
