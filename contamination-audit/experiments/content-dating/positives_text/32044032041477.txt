Section II.  

The chain store is not new.

A Chinese merchant, On Lo Cass, is said to have owned a large chain of stores as early as 200 B.C. Evidence was found in the buri city of Pompeii that chain stores existed i ancient Rome. Early in the history of this continen the Hudson Bay Company established a chain of tradin posts that still survives.

In this country the story of the chain store might well be called "The Little Red Store and How I Grew."

It is typified by the growth of the Great 

Atlantic and Pacific Tea Company, established before

Th he Civil War and now oldest in existence. 

The big grocery chain was started when two ew England traders, dealers in harnesses and leather oods, learned of the large profits made on tea and ecided to go into that business.

They started out as a mail-order house selling ea, and then coffee, at cut-rate prices. From the ery beginning they practiced the same buying and elling methods that chain stores use today.

They bought in quantity and they sold in quanity; and whenever they could, they cut out the midlemen completely.

They did not buy tea and coffee by the box or y the carton; they got it by the shipload. As a reult the cost per pound was small compared with what ival independents of that day had to pay. 

And instead of doling out coffee by the sack, or tea by the package, they organized consumer clubs In various cities, where members ordered their tea and coffee in large lots and divided it up among hemselves. Thus the cost of selling the two items was cheaper for the tea company than for the independent grocer.

Since the tea and coffee were sent directly from the mail-order house to the consumer, the only middleman was the expressman who delivered the package. 

To increase the number of sales, the owners advertised widely that consumers could save 30 per cent by buying from them. They openly bragged that they had achieved these lower prices by cutting out six or seven brokers, commission men, and wholesalers. In addition, they criticized the wasteful methods and the excessive profits of their competitors.

This brought violent counter-attacks from the independent grocers of that day. It is probable that had Mr. Huggle been operating his Liberty Grocery at that time, he would have joined the others in accusing the new mail-order house of "stealing

from the local grocers, using tea that had been damaged in shipment, and of seeking monopoly." 

The tea company, however, survived the namecalling, by keeping prices low, advertising that fact to the consumer, and making profits on volume sales. 

The first retail store was opened in 1859 in New York City. It was painted Chinese red--with gol trimmings.

By the beginning of the century there were 200 stores.

In an economic conflict, as in any other kind of a war, the two sides use almost any weapons that come to hand, unless there is some force stronger than either of them to prevent ito The independents, protecting themselves from the invading chains, have already been described as enlisting the support of the law and of public opinion. Independents in the food field began o adapt chain tactics to their own uses. 

The first to do so was a group of grocers in nd around Philadelphia. They organized the Frankford rocery Company, which was the first cooperative asociation of independents. They began by doing their urchasing together. They reasoned that if the chains eceived certain advantages from buying in bulk, there as no reason why they should not duplicate those adantages.

Although the Philadelphia organization is till the largest cooperative group, the movement has rown until now there are more than 25,000 independnt grocers belonging to 150 associations. Most of hese associations have joined one big central buying rganization called the National Retailer-Owned Grocers.

It was not long before middlemen--wholesalers nd jobbers--began to organize their independent cusomers into associations similar to the cooperative ssociations of retailers. Longed to them.

But the idea in back of their organization as the same: to cut down on some of the cost of listribution so that the little retailers could buy heir stocks for less, and therefore be in a better position to compete with the chains. Of course the iddlemen who organized these voluntary groups did not do so entirely from an unselfish point of view. 

Rather, by saving their independent customers they hoped to save themselves.

They have grown faster than the cooperative chains, and now there are about 90,000 independent members. 

In spite of the fact that in many respects cooperative and voluntary groups are similar to regular chains, the fact remains that each member store is still independently owned and operated. Profits go into the pockets of individual storekeepers rather than into the coffers of a large corporation, from there to seep back to whatever stockholders there might be in the form of dividends. Still Gaining Ground? 

Are Chains Are chains still gaining ground at the expense of the independents? The average consumer would probably guess that they are. Yet certain figures, frequen ly quoted, are supposed to prove that this is not so. In 1931, A & P hit an all-time high with 15,757 stores. The number by the middle of 193 had fallen to 10,500. Latest government statistics show that chain stores generally have decreased in number in the last ten years. Independents on the other hand have multiplied. "Then why," asks the puzzled consumer, "shou everyone get so excited about the 'chain-store octopus' that is gobbling up the little independents? I would seem that they are quite able to take care of themselves. 11 

The answer to his dilemma is this: although there are fewer chain stores than there were at the beginning of the depression, they are doing a bigger share of the total retail trade; and although there are more independents than ever before in history, they are competing for a smaller proportion of the consumers' business.

There are several reasons for this, one of the most important being that the grocery chains, owning a third of all the chain stores, have drastically reduced the number of their units. Yet at

he same time they have expanded and enlarged most of heir remaining stores until they actually are doing ore business now than they did when they owned a reater number of stores. It has been said that "the ay to strengthen a chain is to remove its weakest inks."

929 emphasis was placed on 'combination" markets, here meats as well as groceries were sold. 11 The trend away from little stores and toward big ones was further speeded up by the development of che super-markets. Ten years ago there were around

11 combination" markets put together.

Shoe shops have added women's stockings, purses, and gloves; cigar stands sell candy and chewing gum, and in some cases wines and liquors; filling. stations offer widely used automobile accessories such as tires and tubes.

Anti-chain taxes gave further impetus to the trend already begun in the direction of larger stores and fewer of them. For since the taxes generally increased in direct proportion to the number of stores that a company owned, the big chains found it to their advantage to reduce the number of their links--and

quickly too.

When the chain tax passed in Pennsylvania, for example, American Stores Company announced that it would board up more than 200 of its food markets.

The comment of the average consumer at this point might be, "That may be why chains have increased their share of the business at the same time that the number of their stores was decreasing, but why under the sun did independent stores keep multiplying if business for them was getting steadily worse?"

In the first place new independents have opened because of the increase in population. Small neighborhood stores have been developed to service new residential areas. Other independents have spru up to sell new kinds of merchandise. Examples are electric and gas refrigerators and the great variety of radios. Prohibition repeal in 1933 brought back liquor stores, cocktail rooms, and bars.

voluntary chains of independents have shown that it can be done. An employment service for retraining and transferring would probably be helpful. Chains might make a point of adopting the same policy that the Jenkins grocery chain in Canada does. 

That is, drafting displaced wholesalers and independents int their own organization. Certainly, whatever can be done to make adjustments to mass distribution metho easier, should be done.

It is argued by some, of course, that if chains are destroyed, not only would all workers no employed in them go back to work in wholesale house and independent stores, but that other people now w employed would be absorbed as well. The destruction of the chains, however, would not necessarily mean that mass distribution methods would be destroyed. 

It can be predicted, rather confidently, that they would reappear in some similar form. Perhaps ours might become a nation of cooperative and voluntary chains, or a nation of super-markets, or even a nation of consumer cooperatives. The trend is toward mass distribution, just as surely as it was toward mass production more than a century ago.

Then she said that they were taking the control of stores out of the local commun ity. And finally, she capped both of these argumen with the rhetorical question, "Do we want monopolie In the first place we will have to straight out Mrs. Draper's definition of a monopoly. Like most consumers, she has always been a little vague about it, her idea simply being that it was a big, bad business organization that gobbled up little business organizations.

As a matter of fact, a monopoly may be big or little. The essential thing about it is that it has substantial control over the prices in a certai

market, and buyers have no other choice but to pay che prices asked. Since there is no competition, it is obvious that the seller can raise his prices just about as high as he wants to.

In the very early days when Mr. Huggle first started in business, the Liberty Store--it was the Liberty General Store then--was a village monopoly. 

It was the only place where people could buy dairy products and staples. Mr. Huggle also handled hay, grain and feed then. He set his own prices, charging what he thought was fair. Since he had no competition he could get whatever he asked. Monopolies on one kind of goods or another still exist in most of the small towns in America. 

Of course, what Mrs. Draper meant was that after grocery chains had driven out all of the independent stores, and shoe chains had taken over the shoe trade, that there would be monopolies on a national scale, instead of a local scale. 

So far, the only chains that have come anywhere near dominating the field in which they operate are the variety stores, and as has been pointed out this business was started by a chain. Although the first five-and-ten-cent store was opened by one chain,

Woolworth's, it was not long before such competitors as Kress's, Kresge's, and Newberry's entered the field. In addition many independent owners have started variety stores.

In the food and grocery field, where chains do around 40 per cent of the retail business, more than half of this amount is done by three companies-- 

A & P, Safeway and Kroger. At least two of these,

A & P and Safeway, have kept more or less out of each other's territories. Aside from this there are all the outward signs of keen competition between the rival chains as well as between chain and independent stores. Whether the reality of this competition is belied by the large profits they have been making on their investments is a matter on which economists would disagree. 

For although chain groceries make

only from 1.5 to 2.2 per cent profit on sales, their profit on investments is high--17.5 per cent for each of the years from 1929 through 1935. 

If the time should come that any chain or co bination of chains gets control over price and quality in a field of retailing, then action could be taken against them under the anti-trust laws or other means of regulation that would have to be discovered As an alternative safeguard against such a situation, we may look with favor on the growth of consumer cooperatives which in countries like Sweden and England without sacrificing the economies of large-scale operations and straight-line distribution--have done a great deal to keep competition on a healthy and effec tive basis. 

When Mrs. Draper said that chains were drain ing profits out of the little town, and taking the control of stores away from the local community, she was thinking directly of Mr. Draper's experience with the chains. Five years ago, a family clothing chain had established a store in competition with his, and although there seemed to be no danger that he would be forced out of business, still profits had been reduced.

The new store employed local people, except for the manager, who had been brought in from the outside. The other business men resented this, even though if they had stopped to think, they would have known that he came from some other community and as an individual was just as much entitled to the job a one of the home-town boys. What upset them was that he seemed to take no part in community affairs; he said that he was too busy, and that besides he would probably be transferred somewhere else in the next few years.

More recently all of this had been changed, and orders had come from the central office of the chain that all of their local executives henceforth were to cooperate in community undertakings. After that the manager joined the Chamber of Commerce and

contributed to the Community Chest. Even so chariies always had to wait until he could get permission rom the central office before he could make his doation.

The way the central office seemed to run everything about the local store was something else chat annoyed local business men. The general feeling was that it was bad enough for the chains to take the profits from the town, without having them take the 

Leadership too.

On the other hand, although a certain amount of money does flow from a community in the form of chain-store profits to unknown shareholders, that does not prove that the community is hurt by this. 

After all, the money that Mr. Huggle and other independents paid to outside wholesalers, and that the local wholesaler paid to outside manufacturers, went out of town too. At the same time, since chainstore prices were quite a bit lower than independent prices, that still left in the pockets of the man-inthe-street and other local consumers more than the independent storekeeper could have kept in the form of profits; after all, he has to spend most of the money that he takes in to buy new stocks of goods. 

But if a broader meaning is given to the charge, and complaint is made against the centralization of control over vast distributive organizations in the hands of comparatively small groups of bigcity bankers and business men, a momentous problem of local, state, and national importance is raised. 

In the last analysis this is the chain-store problem. 

Admitting their efficiency in getting goods cheaply from producers to consumers we still have reason for grave concern about the consequences of this concentration of economic power. 

Here the chain-store issue merges into a general one which is, perhaps, the paramount problem of present-day economic society and is absorbing the attention of many of the ablest economists, business leaders and public men of the country today. 

To open

up this subject even briefly would take us beyond the scope of the present study, but a recognition of the fundamental character of the problems involved does suggest that an attempt to deal with them through so blind an attack on mere bigness as is represented by the anti-chain store taxes is inadequate and inappropriate. Must not the challenge be met rather in the end by more carefully thought-out modifications of our codes of business law and behavior which will pre serve the advantages of large-scale operations and provide specific safeguards against the abuse of powe by those in whose hands control is centered?

Section VI. ARGUMENT IN A NUTSHELL 

Chain stores have gained such ground in the field of retail distribution that almost a quarter of all consumer purchases are now made from them. If they were to go on growing as fast as they did in the twenties, they would before long dominate the field. 

Anti-chain propaganda presents this as probable. 

Counter-propaganda contends that chains are past their peak and are actually losing ground. Expert opinion suggests: Independents can often give special services and personal attentions which many customers value highly; chain-store methods are not equally applicable in all lines of business nor in all types of localities; their best opportunities have already been exploited; they will grow some more, but the rate will be slowed down; competitors are imitating their methods; consumer cooperatives and super-markets are gaining in importance; progressive independents have a place in the system of distribution they will continue to hold. Consumers, recognizing that their patronage is a deciding factor in the struggle, must carefully consider which stores really give them the best value for their money.
