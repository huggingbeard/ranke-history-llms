THE DEFENSE COORDINATION OF THE PANAMA AND FLORIDA CANALS (A PRELIMINARY STUDY) By HENRY HOLLAND BUCKMAN M. Am. Soc. C. E. 

1. The purposes of this study have been: (a) To examine the Florida Canal as an element in the national defense, and, in so doing, to develop an accurate statistical background for conclusions which might be reached; (b) to relate it to the wartime traffic through the 

Panama Canal; and (c) to determine within reasonable limits of accuracy what dollar value to the Nation may be placed on the project, viewed solely as an item in our defense economy. It was recognized at the outset that many of the factors involved are indeterminable. 

However, many are calculable and may be reduced to more or less approximate values. By rejecting those which are inherently too indefinite, or for the determination of which data and experience are insufficient, it has been possible to arrive at certain minima which may be prudently accepted as representing very approximately monetary values to be yielded by the canal should the United States become engaged in a major war. 

2. The author is indebted for practically all the data used in this examination to the Departments of War, Navy, Commerce, and 

Interior; the United States Maritime Commission; and the Panama

Canal. In particular, the Research and Technical Divisions of the 

Maritime Commission; the Bureau of Foreign and Domestic Commerce of the Department of Commerce; and the office of the Chief of 

Engineers in the War Department have contributed a wealth of data, the detail and accuracy of which are noteworthy, and without which this study would be impossible. Grateful acknowledgment is made to the Navy Department and those naval officers who have contributed so much to the interpretation of the facts gathered, and whose professional opinions have been invaluable. However, the author desires to emphasize the fact that the views expressed herein are his own. He accepts full responsibility for all conditions adopted, and for all summaries and conclusions reached. 

3. In the ultimate analysis, the successful defense of the United 

States is conditioned upon adequate transport between certain geographical areas. This arises from the highly unbalanced areal develepment of our agriculture, industry, and raw material production. 

Under the conditions imposed by modern warfare, no considerable section of the country, taken as a unit, is defensible. Unfortunately, this is especially true of the areas almost certain to bear the brunt of the initial attack; i. e., the Atlantic and Pacific coastal waters and seaboard regions. To mobilize and maintain, under conditions of actual combat, great land or naval forces in either region without highly developed and effective transport between the areas of combat and the areas of supply is impossible. By itself, such a statement 1

may be a military platitude, but in the present instance the degrees and magnitudes obtaining warrant especial concern. 

4. For the purposes of this discussion, the United States may be divided into three geographical areas, A, B, and C. (See sheet 1 of 8.) 

Area A comprises in general the territory east of the Appalachian ranges. This is a consuming area, and in it are concentrated the greater part of our industries. Area B is the Mississippi Valley, extending between the Appalachians and the Rockies. This is essentially a region of agriculture and raw material production, with insufficiently developed industry. Area C is the region west of the 

Rockies. Except for portions of the immediate coastal strip, this is an area of raw material production with comparatively low industrial development. 

5. The probable cases in which the Nation might find itself at war are three. These are: 

I. Theater of war in the West only. 

II. Theater of war in the East only.

III. Theater of war in both East and West. 

The trend of world politics indicates a diminishing probability of I or II, and an increasing probability of III. The continually increasing difficulty of confining a war to two opposing belligerents serves to emphasize this. Therefore, it is clear that any prudent, adequate scheme for our defense must provide for operations on both coasts simultaneously. Prudent planning must also involve certain other assumptions. For a list of the chief of these reference should be had to the accompanying list, "War Conditions Assumed". 

6. It may be taken for granted that for the next several decades at least, the man-power and the industrial development of the United States, if properly coordinated and adequately served by transport, will be sufficient to meet any war needs which now may be reasonably anticipated. However, supplies of raw materials will have to be supplemented by importations from Central and South America. 

This is particularly true of certain strategic materials, notably tin and rubber. In considering the importation of these supplies it is useful to divide Central and South America into four areas, i. e., D, comprising the territory between the Rio Grande and Panama; 

E, the territory tributary to the Caribbean; F, that tributary to the 

Pacific; and G, that tributary to the Atlantic. (See Sheet 1 of 8.) 

7. The fundamental problem of our defense is the provision, maintenance, and safeguard of adequate transport between A, B, and C on the one hand, and between A and D, E, F, and G on the other. 

It must be remembered that practically all supplies and materials destined in finished form for C (even though originating as raw materials in C) must first move to A for processing, and thence to C, being twice subjected to the hazard of enemy attack on the ocean. 

8. By far the most important of the above-named movements are those between B and A and between A and C. The uninterrupted flow of these will prove pivotal. 

9. Of the two last-named movements (at wartime dimensions), a careful study of existing and proximate future facilities indicates that rail, highway, inland waterway, and air transport may be relied upon to provide not more than 60 percent. The remaining 40 percent must move in ocean-going bottoms, as it did in 1917-18, via one or two of three gateways. These gateways are the Panama Canal, the Straits

of Florida (or the Florida Canal), and the Windward Passage. Sound defensive strategy requires that substantially all wartime movement between the Gulf-Caribbean area and the Atlantic be via one gateway. That the gateway selected should be the Straits of Florida (or preferably the Florida Canal) is not questioned by any considerable body of authority. The Navy can substantially close to enemy surface craft all Atlantic openings to the Gulf-Caribbean area. Complete closure is, of course, impossible. Enemy submarines and airplanes, and possibly surface craft will, on occasion, enter the area and, despite our convoy defense, cause the loss of an indeterminate number of our merchant vessels within that area. But it is reasonably certain (and the assumption forms an essential element in our naval strategy) that until and unless our naval forces there have been so reduced by combat or withdrawal for action elsewhere, the Gulf- 

Caribbean area can and will be made safer for our merchantmen than the open waters of the Atlantic. There can be little doubt that by patrolling the Florida-Trinidad line and the use of the Florida Canal for all merchant traffic, some substantial reduction in the sinkings of merchantmen that would otherwise take place in that area would result. While this is not the chief protective function of the 

Florida Canal, it is an important one. Although it cannot be reduced to an arithmetical basis, it must be recognized as a real value. There are other defense values in the canal which are not directly translatable into money savings. It is impossible to reduce to terms of dollars and cents such values as contributions to the prevention of (1) the sinking of troop ships; (2) the interruption or decisive curtailment of the flow of water-borne munitions, supplies, materials, foodstuffs, or fuel; (3) the possible loss of control of the Straits of Florida. 

These are all entirely possible eventualities which prudent defense planning cannot ignore. 

10. The basic fact which gives significance to the strategy laid down in (9) is that all the inter-areal movements named in (7) (except that between A and G) must traverse the Gulf-Caribbean area. The Gulf of Mexico and the Caribbean Sea are really one body of water and must be considered as such. The corollary to this is the well-known maxim that the keys to the Gulf-Caribbean area are the keys to the Americas. Control of the entrances to this area on the east (the 

Straits of Florida, the Windward, Mona, Anegada, and other passages), and on the west (the Panama Canal) is a sine qua non of our defense scheme. 

11. For a schematic representation of the movements hereinabove referred to, reference should be had to sheets 1-6 inclusive. It will be noted that they proceed along five well-defined routes. Routes 

3 and 4 connect A and G. All other areas are connected by routes 

1, 2, and 2a. Along these latter proceeds more than 80 percent of the ocean-borne traffic between all the areas. Of this 80 percent, by far the greater part moves via route 1 and the Straits of Florida, and would move through the Florida Canal. Routes 2 and 2a run normally through the Windward Passage, but in time of war would run via the Straits of Florida (or the Florida Canal), coinciding with route 

1 between a point near the Dry Tortugas and Ambrose Light Ship. 

It thus becomes evident that any advantage or protection to American shipping afforded by the Florida Canal in time of war accrues in some degree, to substantially the entire inter-areal ocean traffic of the Wes-

12. Examination of the accompanying charts (1-7) will show: a. Under the existing situation all of our ocean-going, interareal transport must traverse from 1,000 to 1,200 miles (depending on the route) of open Atlantic water, where it will be especially subject to enemy attack-because it is nearer to possible enemy land bases or mother ships; because the traffic is here more dense; and because the Bahama, West Indian, and Leeward islands offer at least some color of protection to cruising enemy submarines. b. It would be entirely feasible to reroute all traffic so that it would enter and leave the Gulf-Caribbean area via the Florida

Canal, thus shortening the convoyed mileage for all ships for distances ranging from 136 miles for routes 2 and 2a to 504 miles for route 1. These figures represent the advantage in convoyed miles of the Florida Canal over the next best available gateway, i. e., the Straits of Florida. 

13. Those defense values of the Florida Canal which lend themselves to computation may be examined under four main heads. (See sum mary, Table I.) 

I. Reasonably predictable reduction in the number of merchant ships sunk by the enemy, resulting from the savings in convoyed ship-miles afforded by the canal: Table XII develops this value in detail. It should be pointed out that the results arrived at involve a somewhat optimistic view of the enemy's inability to strike at long range. The rate at which the effective operating radii of all mobile weapons used to attack merchantmen (airplanes, submarines, and certain types of surface craft) is increasing is an accelerated one. Chemical and mechanical advance continue to increase the distance at which a given striking effort is effective. Distances which appear today to involve greatly diminished impact of an enemy effort, may well become relatively unimportant in the near future. In any war which was really going against us, these figures would probably represent substantially less than our actual losses. To assume that we may expect losses less than those here estimated would seem to be imprudent.

II. Savings in tanker construction: The figures developed in table VIII represent an inevitable situation. The only theory upon which we might expect to possess a tanker fleet adequate to our war needs is that involving a peacetime fleet of which considerably more than half was devoted to foreign trade, this major portion being available for our Gulf-Atlantic service in war time. For perfectly sound business and economic reasons, no such proportion exists today. The probability of its existing in the future dwindles with the rise in our own consumption and the tendency to conserve our reserves. The only basic solution of the dilemma (and the most sound from an economic point of view) lies in actually reducing by a sufficient amount the exposed

LO water distance between the producing fields and the mean focus of consumption. This, the cutting of the Florida Canal will effect. It may be argued that once the canal is opened, the size of the domestic, peacetime tanker fleet will in due course adjust itself to the shorter distance, and this is probably true. However, by virtue of their arithmetically increased proportion and the reduced exposed distance, the existing average number of American flag tankers in foreign trade and available to the Gulf-Atlantic run in emergency, would serve to sufficiently augment Atlantic seaboard deliveries, and to meet the requirements of the Navy. 

III. Savings in transportation costs: These figures, based on cargo-ton savings developed by the Department of Commerce represent peacetime values. In time of war, the savings would undoubtedly be larger because of wartime values. 

IV. Direct savings to the Navy: Public policy prevents detailed published discussion of certain phases of the savings in this group. 

The figures here set down are the author's minimum estimates, which lay no claim to accuracy nor to Departmental authenticity. 

They have been temporarily adopted to meet the desirability of reducing to some conservative monetary expression the canal values in this group. Even to the uninformed, a little reflection will indicate that only a very considerable degree of optimism can warrant estimates of cost and loss lower than those here assumed.

REMARKS. AS to group IV: Publication of a detailed analysis of the items in group IV does not appear to be in the public interest. All items are necessarily estimates based upon assumptions upon which competent authorities may differ. It is believed, however, that prudent provision for the protection of this enormous traffic through the Straits of Florida in time of war requires, as a minimum, the cost here estimated. This cost averages $105,700,000 per year for the protection of a yearly traffic valued at $4,586,636,000, or 2.3 percent. An inspection of war-risk insurance rates, which are based on experience tables, will emphasize the conservatism of these figures. That the Florida Canal would save the Navy directly more, rather than less, than the total shown in group IV would be a prudent anticipation.

Plan POSSIBLE REMEDIAL STEPS I. Begin immediate construction: 

(a) 96 average tankers, at $1,750,000 each. (b) 45 145,000-barrel tankers, at $3,130,000 each.

Remarks: Such additional construction, if begun at once, would bring our tanker tonnage up to war requirements by the summer of 1942. In the event that war did not come, however, such a fleet would represent unprofitable standby equipment, and might exercise a demoralizing effect on petroleum transportation rates.

Plan II. Immediate completion of the Gulf Intracoastal Canal System and a barge canal across northern Florida, affording a means for barging petroleum from Gulf ports to Atlantic ports. 

Remarks: Such an artery would make possible the delivery to the Atlantic seaboard of sufficient petroleum to offset the deficit in tanker deliveries. Barge trains would not require convoy, and would effect a distribution service not yielded by tankers. This waterway would likewise make possible the barge transport of numerous other necessary bulk_commodities. It would have a peacetime value commensurate with its cost. Barges constructed for emergency petroleum transport could be converted after the war for miscellaneous commodity transport.

Plan III. Completion of the Florida Ship Canal. In addition to serving in the protection of the Gulf-Atlantic, Intercoastal, and South American ship traffic, this plan would effect sufficient savings in time and distance to enable the tanker fleet to make deliveries of all petroleum requirements. The peacetime value of the canal would be greater than its cost, and would increase. Tolls yielded by ships using the canal would in due course repay its cost, while defraying the expense of maintenance and operation

Plan IV. Recommended: This is a combination of plan II and plan III, and comprises the immediate completion of plan II, with temporary locks, and thereafter the enlargement of the barge canal and its reduction to sea level and ship canal dimensions as rapidly as possible. This would yield an emergency transport means by or before the summer of 1942, which would be susceptible of conversion into the more adequate and useful ship canal without consequential loss, by 1945-46.

DERIVATION OF FACTORS INVOLVED 

Under the conditions set forth in the text: 

1. As a minimum base figure for computing the effective destructive effort of an enemy against our merchant ships may be taken the experience of the Allies and neutrals in the present war with Germany.12 

2. The destructive effect of an enemy effort against merchant shipping is (for ordinary magnitudes) inversely proportionate to his mean radius of operation, and directly proportionate to the traffic density of the shipping attacked.³ 

3. With a plurality of enemy submarines to be encountered at unknown points over the entire route, the number of convoyed ships lost will be in direct proportion to the number of miles requiring convoy. 

1 This base is probably low, for the following reasons: (1) Sinkings to date have been at a much smaller rate than during the World War. If the present war continues to a duration of 2 years or more, activity will undoubtedly show an increased average. (2) An enemy who did not have to defend his boundaries against invasion by our military forces would be able to put a far greater portion of his effort into submarine and other naval attack than can Germany with her west wall defense to maintain, and her ports blockaded. 

2 In translating merchant-ship losses actually sustained by the Allies and neutrals into potential losses by the United States, it is necessary to observe: 

1. All ships sunk so far in the present war (to Mar. 2, 1940) have been sunk by either German or Allies' effort (barring a very few possibly sunk accidentally). No Allies' effort (up to Mar. 2, 1940) has been directed to sinking any but German-flag ships. Further, it has been a policy of the Allies to capture instead of to sink any ship, thereby making her an addition to the Allies' merchant fleet, a course not open to Germany. Hence, barring a few accidents, all Allies' and neutral ships sunk, whether by mine, submarine, airplane, or gunfire, have been the result of German effort. Substantially, all neutral ships sunk have been bound to and from the United Kingdom, and must be considered as "Allies"" ships, since their function is the same as that of "Allies" ships. 

2. Whether ships are sunk by mine, submarine, airplane, or surface war-vessel, the sinking is a result of the war effort of the enemy. The proportionate effect of each of these weapons will vary in varying circumstances. Fundamentally, however, in mechanized warfare, the total effective war effort of a given organized population with given economic resources, will inflict on its opponent losses which are chiefly conditioned by (a) distance, (b) the exposures of the opposition, and (c) the defensive effort which the opposition is able to sustain. This is especially true when the opponents are separated by open sea, and when they have comparable total resources. It must be remembered that one of the conditions laid down in the text is that the enemy will be the aggressor in precipitating the potential war in which the United States is here supposed to be engaged. It follows that the enemy will not provoke this war until his potential offensive effort has been developed and organized to meet particularly the exposures and defense of the United States. 

It is therefore reasonable to assume that he will be prepared to use weapons with operating radii appropriate to his objectives. 

3. The question arises as to whether, in translating merchant ship losses in one set of circumstances into another, the ship or the ton shall be the unit transiated. The mathematics of choice and chance leave no doubt that the unknown chance to be determined relates to the sinking of a ship, and not to the size of the ship or her cargo. In an equation of blind chance, the size of the ship cannot in this case act as a modifying factor. Blind chance might be modified, however, by selective effort of the enemy, and this would tend to increase the size of the average ship sunk, especially where the number of ships at hazard is great, and the supply or availability of weapons of the enemy is limited. The theory of probability, therefore, requires that the ship (not the ton) be taken as the unit, and that some intelligent, but necessarily incalculable, allowance be made for the result of possible selective effort by the enemy. 

3 The relations of effective destructive effort to radius of operation and traffic density are not simple. 

They involve the consideration of many factors with regard to which naval experts are not in agreement. 

For very long radii, the effect of length probably increases in geometrical ratio. The effect of traffic density, after very great concentration is reached, probably declines in geometrical ratio. For the magnitudes to which they are here applied, the author believes that the relations set forth in the above equations fairly represent the opinions of a majority of competent authorities, when taking into consideration the advances in and the trends of war mechanism design and operation. 

4 This would not be true with all enemy submarines attacking at one unknown point. Wherever ships are under convoy, it must be assumed that attack is more or less likely, otherwise the convoy is uselessly employed. It is an accepted fact that the Navy will convoy all shipping from Gulf and Caribbean points to Atlantic coastal points. Hence the hazard must be assumed at all points, and must be proportionate to the length of the convoyed route, or to the convoyed ship-miles. Under these circumstances, time, as translated from distance becomes an essential factor in the equation, and modifies it in direct proportion to its magnitude.
