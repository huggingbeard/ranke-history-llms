FOREWORD 

"Whatever the creed, or land be trod,

Man's conscience is the oracle of God." 

-Byron. 

The contents of this book, "Musings of Ohnahah 

Wakonah," is a manifested result of the passionate emotive force of the Spirit to reach our mortal plane.

Such perturbility would have been, not many years ago, called madness, or, incitation to a burning at the stake. 

Ohnahah Wakonah is an Indian Spirit of ancient lineage who, through the unseen and dimly understood power of ethereal vibrations, has manifested his thoughts materially in the identity of Musings of 

Ohnahah Wakonah, in what is facetiously spoken of as "inspired writing.'

He is apparently a realistic personality; an entity visualized; though not in a materialized existence, who, through perturbation of spirit, has relieved his urge through this material channel. 

He is credited with this ethereal power, transmitted through two mortal characters, "Ruth and Ethel," in a more or less disjointed state it is true, but conjugated and co-ordinated by the authorized writer.

The urge to publish these messages is based on the belief that they will appeal to, and benefit in a material and spiritual way, all who read them, young or old.

It is to these people I dedicate this book. 

J. Buren Evans,

The exquisite selections within were chosen. for their beauty, fitness, and moral solicitude from the pearls of wisdom given by the ethereal presence of Oh-nah-ah Wa-kon-ah (Whispering of the North Pines) to his dearly beloved 

Ruth and her friend Ethel.

Musings of Ohnahah 

Wakonah Deep within the hidden shadows of the stately 

Sequoias, among the flickering light shafts that penetrated the moss dripping branches, sat an Indian. 

He sat on a stone of large proportions with abrupt faces that showed the stratification of ages recorded in parallel lines.

Encompassing and dimly through the trees showed precipitous walls; a strata of gravel, cemented by calcium countless years ago; then a layer of clay and sea shells hardened into solid rock by its carbonates and pressure, known to geologists as marl; layer on layer of black limestone; red, orange and yellow tufa, colored by penetrating heat from overlying lava, scarred by the elements during eons of time, yet withal a clear record to he who wishes to read the history of the earth's crust chronicled in indelible lessons. 

There is only the rippling cadence of silvered water flowing over polished quartz to break the silence.

It all bespeaks of great age-but the Indian was not old, though he had reached his majority. His

muscles were sound and pliable beneath his dark skin. His face was serene and calm, with great knowledge-knowledge taught by ages of contact with unseen worlds. In his face glowed the tender love of a father-the compassion of one who sees and understands and forgives the weakness and frailties of his fellow men.

He looked calmly upon a small group of upturned faces at his feet, faces of children with expectant gaze, faces of middle aged men and women with questions waiting on their lips, faces of old men and women with complacent hands and peaceful smiles. 

Each looked with awe upon the figure on the rock, and each felt in his or her obtuse way the presence of incarnated knowledge and intangible soul—a 

Christ here to teach his children. 

When all had seated themselves upon moss covered rocks or grassy knolls, several with pads and pencils upon their knees, the man broke the silence in a quiet voice, and each felt the etheric thought, that the words were individualistic, directed at each of them alone. "Grasp happiness, my friends, as the river sings over the white pebbles, as the moon lightens the tops of the fir trees, the Earth and the Great Eternity are happy-so should you, its children be."

As they listened he continued in soft, modulated tones that seemed to come from the shadows around his still form. 1

The wind of evil is about tonight,

It howls and whistles about the eves, It snuffs the little candle light, And tears to shreds the trembling leaves. 

O Wanderer beware this storm without, Beware this screaming gale,

The gentle spirits are not about, They hide down in the vale. 

The angry wind would rend and tear, Nor does he cease the long night through, But ever searches for those unaware, And one of them might be you. "Never lose faith, always know there is great good to govern all.” "Good thoughts are never lost, they travel out into the Great Space, helping all with whom they come in contact.' 

"Evil thoughts cling close to the earth injuring and destroying."

Home is where the soul is taught, Home is where the hopes are brought, Life is but a little way- 

Eternity is God's own day But the soul lives within its own home door.

Home is the place where our dreams are made, Home is the place our plans are laid, It shelters us from the winter's rain, It keeps us when our hopes attain, The hight of perfect happiness- 

Friendship, love, for ever more. "Prepare a way for yourselves in the doing of kindly deeds, the road will broaden into a great and wonderful highway." "Truth alone can break down the barriers of deceit and wrong." 

"Light abounds, pursue thy earnest quests, do not lose courage, there is a golden glory of sunsets for you all, though it be hidden at present by a cloud." "Thank God that you have eyes with which to see all his creation, ears to hear the music of the spheres, feet to tread upon the highways of beauty, a soul with which to love and worship."

Such a wealth we hold within our mind, Better wealth we need not try to find. "Years are so short, make each precious moment a valued jewel." "Do not fear death. Death is a kindly friend who comes with soothing touch to ease our pains.'

Man was born with fear; this fear, born in our prehistoric ancestors, is now so devastating to the present races. 

Until we can conquer fear, we ourselves, will be conquered.

Fear is a mental state and only as such does it exist, as the prehistoric, beast woman, a cowering, fearsome creature, followed in the shadow of her mate. As time elapsed, affection grew in her heart, first in the devotion to her young, then in the dependence on her mate-thus was love born in the heart of woman before man and thus was confidence born -first conqueror over fear. "Live each day that we may walk bravely and with unhesitating step into another room, through the door which must open to each in our turn.' 

"I will give you a poem and you may call it 'The 

Prisoner', and within it you may find a moral." 

Grey the sky and grey the day,

The rain streams down in an icy spray, The birds and their singing are hushed and away, And the bell tolls its mournful warning. 

The Prisoner paces his gloomy cell, He mumbles a prayer, yet he knows full well

The prayer will end with the tolling knell, For the Prisoner dies this morning.

Death is a wise and gentle friend-compassionate and understanding.

When we weary of this earth and close our eyes in dreamless sleep, Death wakens us into a new life of perfect happiness and beauty.

Then spoke a voice from the small group, "You are growing too serious with us, Ohnahah Wakonah. 

Please give us a lighter poem before the shadows fall." "I will give you a poem which you may call 'Moonlight on the Lake', an Indian love song."

Light to break the blackened darkness, Light to throb with Indian love song. 

Break, Oh, Moon upon the waters, Light to silver all the tree tops, Lash with silver every petal,

Mark a path across the waters, Path to take for Indian lovers 

In their light canoe, swift speeding;

Brighten ever on their journey

Down the great and blackened waters, Never let their journey darken 

Never let their courage falter.

He finished and smiled as a young lad spoke up with childish enthusiasm, "Please, Mr. Ohnahah

Silently the shadows enveloped the figure, and silently the group arose and wound its way out of the shadowed glen. When summer days were at the flood, A little path which wound its way, Into the thicket of a wood.

A path which climbed to bluest hights, Then ran down to the fields again, But alas! I lost my tiny path,

In the mire of winter's rain. "But enough, we should have a lighter strain, and be merry while we can. I am too pensive. What would you?" "Give us another Indian story, or a storm or something," the children chorused as the elders smiled with tolerance. 

To you who know the calling of the sea, To you who know the song of the surf, The great white waves are music. 

The tides tell endless tales. 

The shifting sands, like singing harp strings, The sea, like a great bowl of melody, Gently calls the sailor to its heart. "The thought of storms and battles serves only to agitate the mind and equals it to the lower beasts --but I will give you a storm, though I am not pleased to thus serve you," he replied, then began in his modulated voice: 

High on the top of the great snow peak

The Storm King dwells; 

Sitting in his palace of ice, He broods darkly 

Wondering where next to strike.

He sees a great and beauteous ocean; 

It lies calm and sleeping in the sun. 

The Storm King is angered. 

He calls forth the great treacherous North Wind. 

Blow! Blow! My noble ally; 

Disturb the calm and sleeping sea! 

Toss the waves to mountains of white spray! 

Clutch with your giant hands 

The frail white sailed ship!

Break it upon the gray rocks! 

Toss it splintered upon the seething shore! 

Blow the trumpets of your wrath 

Upon the ears of the drowning! 

Scream! Scream! Oh, my great commander 

That they may know the Storm King reigns.

The Indian paused and slowly shook his head, his long black hair swaying. "More,” cried a lad, clapping his hands, "I like storms.' 

"Again I say, storms like evil thoughts harass the serenity of the soul, but I will finish with the storm if I am able." 

And when the sun refused his glory, Then the Storm King lashed to fury 

All the waves upon the water, He beat with ever deepening fury, Till the sky broke into terror.

Loud and deep resounding thunder, Breaking flash on flash of lightning,

Wild with crash of falling timber. 

Never were the heavens stricken 

With such long imprisoned passion. 

Wave on wave unloosed its anger 

Beat the shore with white, clenched fingers 

Clasped the roots of gnarled hemlocks

He stopped, wiping his forehead with a trembling hand-his listeners cringed with faces pallid before his agitated features. 

A little girl was crying in frightened tones and a little boy was staring in awed silence, startled at the outburst he had helped provoke. "That is enough," the Indian said. "I would rather tell you of the beauties of life which you overlook in your mad rush for physical pleasures, so please do not ask for storms again. "Tomorrow eve I will tell you more, Ohnahah 

Wakonah, is too exhausted now to continue. Do not feel offended-tomorrow I will meet you again," and his "Goodnight, friends", faded into the silent shadows. 

Over the glade the rolling clouds were wreathing in silent contest as the little group gathered in meek trepidation. "Today, my friends, the Great Arbitrator looks with compassion upon the trees and his loved ones who dwell in the shadows of the fern. They lay upon the hot earth and chant the rain song 

Of their Fathers. "Oh, Great Spirit of the mountains and plains, Bring upon thy poor children the cool and gentle rains, Oh, make the streams to flow once more, And grass to spread in the fields, Desert thy children never more, Oh, Great Spirit! "Some people worship one God, some another, but each one is right in that his God is the perfect conception. 

"The rain is here—go—and leave the trees, the flowers, and the birds to their fill of nature's blessings. Tomorrow afternoon we will have another meeting." 

The moon, a disk of silver, Slips behind the broken hills, Soon the lights will appear 

Like soft round globes in windows.

The birds hush their songs, To ruffle their feathers in drowsiness,

The cricket begins his lusty chirping, In the distant meadows. 

Night comes with her basket of dreams— 

The earth sleeps. "Good night."

Those who looked over their shoulders, could see 

Ohnahah Wakonah standing dimly in the shadows, his head thrown back-his bosom bared to the cooling rain.
