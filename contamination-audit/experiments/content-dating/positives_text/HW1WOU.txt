INTRODUCTION 

AMOUS in the history of the Church shall remain the extraordinary Jubilee Year which the Supreme Pontiff 

Pius XI, of blessed memory, proclaimed in the Apostolic Constitution, Quod nuper, of January 6, 1933, to commemorate the nineteenth centenary of the Divine Redemption of mankind. In a second Apostolic Constitution, Quod superiore anno, of April 2, 1934, the Jubilee was extended for one year to the entire world. It was during this jubilee period that the great 

Pontiff gave exceptional impetus to the Causes of beatification and canonization of many Servants of God. Pius XI was wont to indicate them to the faithful as the choicest fruits of Divine Redemption, the great examples of virtue, the leaders of souls in the way of sanctity, the unswerving guardians of nations, both by the effective memory of their accomplishments in life and by their protection of the world from their heavenly abode close by the throne of God. 

The Vatican Basilica had not often witnessed such numerous and solemn ceremonies. The number of saints and blessed proclaimed during the Pontificate of Pius XI constitutes of itself an abundant Calendar, replete with examples of sanctity for every social rank and every mode of life. 

THE GLORY OF NATIONS 

Not only individuals and religious communities were interested in these events, but entire nations, as their fellowcountrymen were raised to sublime glory. The lives of the saints and blessed were entwined with the history of their 1

birthplaces, of their fatherlands. Heroic and extraordinary achievements in the fertile fields of piety, charity, social welfare and education were recalled. Saints, as great heroes and geniuses, eventually belong to all mankind, rather than to particular localities, but it is natural that a brighter ray of their glory should illuminate the land of their birth.

In general the Servants of God honored by Pius XI belonged to European nations; and to some it seemed rather presumptuous that a young nation should seek to have its own processes of beatification and canonization. Yet holiness is of all times and places: among the new saints have been some who lived in our own age and were personally known to many among the living. Cannot the United States of America then have its saints also? In truth, on June 21, 1925, Pope Pius XI proclaimed blessed the eight Jesuit Martyrs, and only five years later (June 29, 1930) the same Pontiff pronounced them saints.

These saints, of the period of exploration and colonization of America three centuries ago, were French by birth, but it was in this land that they practised sanctity, and here they terminated their mortal existence.

Sanctity is one of the characteristic marks of the Church, serving to distinguish it throughout the world. Nor can it be otherwise in the United States of America, where Christianity in a relatively short time has attained singular success. 

The first American diocese was constituted and the first bishop appointed only in 1789. Yet today the nation counts more than one hundred dioceses, and well over twenty million Catholics.

It seems timely and useful to undertake investigations for the purpose of bringing to general knowledge the names

HEROES OF SANCTITY IN AMERICA 

This thought and the wish to promote such Causes have inspired this collection of brief accounts of the Servants of God of America. Encouraged by the example of various Promoters who have for many years labored zealously for such causes, I corresponded with the Sacred Congregation of Rites, which is the competent office of the Holy See in such matters. 

Subsequent investigation revealed that the number of Causes of beatification for the United States of America is quite large, and that some are proceeding very successfully. 

The present sketches have the sole purpose of blazing the way. The collection is not complete - it is merely a first attempt, that may be further developed by specialists. However, it may arouse in many a sense of pleasant surprise to see such a lengthy list of Servants of God associated with America.

There are indeed many heroes of sanctity among the pioneers of the Gospel in this land among the Jesuits, Franciscans, Dominicans and others who came from diverse nations to preach the word of God. 

At Aquia, Virginia, for example, on the Washington Highway, we find a tablet bearing several names below the inscription: "This tablet is inscribed to the memory of the heroic 

Jesuit missionaries who, coming from Spain to bring Christ's Gospel to the Indians of this Aquia region, erected nearby in 

1570, A. D., the first Christian temple in our northern land,

Our Lady of Ajacan." These martyrs were eight in number: 

Father Juan Baptista de Segura, Brothers Pedro Linares, Ga-

briel Gomez and Sancho Zeballos, and a scholastic, Cristobal

Redondo, Father Luis de Quiros and two other scholastics, Baptista Méndez and Gabriel de Solis. While the bodies of the last three were burned by their slayers immediately after their martyrdom, and the ashes scattered, those of the first five received burial not far from Fredericksburg, Virginia. These 

Jesuit missionaries had previously exercised the sacred ministry in Florida and North Carolina, and upon their arrival near the Potomac in 1570, they constructed a chapel known in history as "the Log Chapel of the Rappahannock," at the present site of Fredericksburg. Hence they were called "the Martyrs of the Rappahannock."¹ 

Five years earlier Father Martinez, the first Jesuit martyr of the American continent, had been killed in Florida.2 How many other missionaries are recorded in historical documents, men and women who professed the faith and practised virtue in heroic degree!³ Many great men and women have labored with evident sanctity for Christ and His Church in the United States. Their zealous works and heroic virtues are now relatively unknown. However, students of historical research are examining this field of sanctity in America and we have reason to believe that their studies will bring to our knowledge many great champions of religion.

The Church, before declaring one of her children a saint, exacts a long, detailed and strict investigation, described according to ecclesiastical traditions by Pope Benedict XIV in De Canonizatione Sanctorum. The Code of Canon Law also outlines this procedure in a section entitled De Causis Beatificationis Servorum Dei et Canonizationis Beatorum (Canons 19992141). The examination is not one for which chosen souls have prepared themselves in the hope of passing it. The Church employs it after their death, in order to prove that the Christian virtues were practised by them in heroic degree. 

Sanctity is the love of God, by which the soul "treats with God lovingly about His most lovable goodness, to unite and join itself with this same goodness."4 Sanctity is love of Christ, Whose doctrine and precepts one seeks to understand and to practise in such a manner that the soul becomes invested in them and in Jesus Christ Himself: "Put ye on therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, the bowels of mercy, benignity, humility, modesty, patience" (Col. 3:12); "For as many of you as have been baptized in Christ, have put on Christ” (Gal. 

3:27). Frequently sanctity is a love of suffering, in the sense that pleasure must be spiritualized, and the soul must be the mistress of the body and the senses.

Sanctity is a gigantic edifice, because it touches the heavens; through it we are raised to a participation in the Divine life. Nevertheless the work of a saint is on lower levels. He must acknowledge his own lowliness and build upon it with a profound conviction of his complete insufficiency. This is

It is hard for the world to grasp this concept; but the saint sees it clearly and without effort. This is because the saint looks to Divinity, and in this contemplation, deepened by love, perceives the peaks of greatness and perfection: the eternal, infinite, ineffable God is revealed to him, and in comparison to God, he cannot but realize the limitation and finiteness of his own being. Even those reputed by the world as great appear small to the eye that has turned its gaze upon God. From this conviction spiritual ascents begin. "Whosoever therefore shall humble himself as this little child, he is the greater in the kingdom of heaven" (Matt. 18:4).

GIVING THE SOUL TO GOD 

Self-sanctification is nothing more than "giving the soul to God." In Christianity, to give the soul to God is an obvious duty. But it is not so in paganism. The giving of the soul to 

God means separating it from those inordinate affections to which it has adhered with every fiber of its being, removing it from the domination of the animal instincts, from the insatiable egoistic appetites and from the pagan philosophy and morality which heretofore pervaded it. Human strength alone must regard this as an inconceivable task. Speaking with reference to history at the period of Christianity's rise, when long custom had immersed the soul in error and corruption, had surrounded it with the atmosphere emanating from circuses, immoral theatres and contaminated places, it seemed impossible to give it to God. Even if we do not accept literally all the tales of degeneration in the Roman world related with scathing satire by Juvenal, Martial, Apuleius and Petronius, it is well established that paganism glorified vice in its own divinities. It is true that

the pagan world possessed some sense of lofty social values: fortitude, a spirit of heroism in conquests, juridical sense, grandiose concepts of government, sentiments of philanthropy, admiration for certain moral virtues, even the most difficult, such as virginity. On examination, however, the values were not great. The distribution of grain and foodstuffs to the populace is certainly not to be compared to the works of charity, hospitals, leper colonies, orphan asylums, homes for the abandoned, blind and handicapped, which Christianity scattered throughout the world constantly, laboriously and prolifically not by the haughty munificence of a proud emperor, but through the heroic personal sacrifice of thousands of generous souls dedicated to the weak and afflicted. What were the numbers of the Vestal Virgins in comparison with the endless choirs of Virgins who follow Christ? — and what a radical difference in their motives and attainments! Even examples of martyrdom have been claimed outside the pale of Christianity. But to be a true martyr, it is not sufficient to suffer violence. The fakirs of India give themselves to death in the frenzies of a false mysticism; and often those killed for their devotion to philosophical conviction have been urged on by pride and contempt of others. What matters is not suffering, but the motive and manner of suffering, the dispositions of the soul rather than those of the body. A true martyr does not die with hatred, scorn and pride in his heart, but, sustained by the spirit of fortitude and truth, he submerges his own personality, pardons his persecutors, and desires that they may be enlightened by the same heavenly ray and the same spirit of truth. Neither hatred nor wrath incites him; sweetly and lovingly he bears witness by his suffering to Christ's teachings and Divinity.

Among those outside the true Church in good faith, it is possible that God may raise up martyrs. But such are indeed rare gleanings in comparison with the rich and boundless harvest of martyrs within the Church, from St. Stephen the Deacon to those of our own day: martyrs inspired and comforted by the Spirit of God, not by human ideologies. 

SANCTITY AND THE NATURAL ORDER 

Sanctity is adaptable to all temperaments and dispositions of human nature. It takes man as he is, with his gifts, qualifications and defects, both personal and hereditary. This is quite evident, for the saints are human and have therefore their defects as well as their talents. Grace, according to theological principles, does not eliminate nor destroy nature, but elevates and perfects it."

Cheerful is the temperament of St. Philip Neri and Felix of Cantalice; severe that of St. Leonard of Port Maurice; ardent that of St. Teresa of Avila; poetic and even passionate that of St. Francis of Assisi; brilliant that of St. Augustine; intellectual and calm that of St. Thomas Aquinas. A saint may have the spirit of a soldier and a leader of armies, as Ignatius of Loyola, Louis of France, Joan of Arc; he may be a builder of civilization, as Leo the Great and Gregory the Great, or a militant reformer as Gregory VII; he may even be possessed of a sharp temper, as St. Jerome. The ranks of the saintly legion hold also humble folk, workingmen, peasants, tender children and the illiterate. Despite poverty of physical energies or of intellectual endowments, the personality of a saint towers in heroic virtue, particularly the theological virtues of Faith, Hope

Saints differ from one another, but whatever their differences, they have in common a transcendent vision of existence, and they all fix life's center of gravity beyond the confines of the lowly world, in the supernatural order - not out of scepticism, stoicism or any other human conviction, but because in the wisdom of their piety they perceive and contemplate the world and its vicissitudes in relation to things eternal, "sub specie aeternitatis"; they understand fully that "the fashion of this world passeth away" (1 Cor. 7:31).

Saints remain men. Consequently, like every mortal being, they feel the weight of the flesh. But grace comes to elevate human nature and its actions. Its advent is marked by an abundance of spiritual, supernatural gifts that penetrate natural endowments in a varied assortment of vocations. Paul of Tarsus is an ardent man, raging with wrath and breathing fire. The grace of the Lord does not change his temperament, but utilizes that same fiery ardor to win nations to God, to resist and defy every sort of danger and storm. Benedict of Nursia, filled with lofty and noble concepts of Roman law, employs his disposition and excellent qualifications in generous correspondence with divine grace, and so bequeaths to posterity the wise legislation that transforms medieval society through the work of learned religious, heralds of a new civilization, teachers of faith and science.

The paths of sanctity are marvelous and manifold. Over them some journey to distant lands, as Francis Xavier; others linger within the narrow walls of a schoolroom for the education of youth in the fear of God, as Joseph Calasanctius, John Baptist de la Salle, John Bosco and Mother Seton; others carry peace and benediction to the suffering in hospital wards, as Camillus of Lellis. Bellarmine. On royal thrones sanctity guides the scepter of

Stephen of Hungary and Edward the Confessor. Family life and domestic duties are the background of the saintly ascent of Frances of Rome and Anna Maria Taigi. The care of a sacristy wins the crown of sainthood for Gerard Maiella. Alphonsus 

Rodriguez manifests heroic love of God and neighbor in the opening and closing of a monastery door. The wild life of a 

North American forest is destined by Providence as the sanctuary for holy Cateri Tekakwitha. "The Spirit breatheth where 

He will" (John 3:8), and "The weak things of the world hath

Sanctity, "a burning and a shining light" (John 5:35), is a difficult program for followers of the world, and at times they prefer to deny and combat it. Mind-enslaving prejudices are frequently the cause of this hostile attitude. In truth, sanctity is the enemy of passion and carries on an unending struggle against moral disorder and excessive attachment to worldly interests and material things. The world, limiting itself to the speculative discussion of sanctity, will never understand its nature. Virtue must be lived and practised to be understood. "The phenomenon of the saint" has even found chapters in the treatises of pseudo-scientific pathologists. The saints, champions of living, active virtue, the true immortals of human history, radiating God's finest gifts, are made to appear the victims of high-sounding psychoses. Imagine such men as Augustine of Hippo, Thomas of Aquin, John Chrysostom, the holy Doctors of the Church, Popes and Bishops, men learned in every branch of knowledge, like the philosophers Justin, Severinus Boethius and the jurists Raymond of Peñafort and Contardo Ferrini, being diagnosed as neurasthenics, victims of

Their lives are a narrative of fortitude and wisdom; their writings are monuments that will outlive the earthen vessels into which man has poured his puny philosophies and theories; the splendor of their eternal glory will shed its beneficent rays over a world that can never be truly illuminated by the sporadic flashes from the human intellect that often divert attention from the constant light of the all-knowing God.

Our Calendars and Martyrologies, the Passiones and Acta Sanctorum, the volumes of the Bollandists and other writers, enumerate thousands upon thousands of the saints of God. 

Divine Economy has given them to mankind that their lives and works, in every land and every age, may lend proof to the truth and sanctity of the faith they practised so valiantly and died for with such unflinching steadfastness. Such abundant good fruits cannot come from a decayed tree. "A good tree cannot bring forth evil fruit, neither can an evil tree bring forth good fruit" (Matt. 7:18).

THE CHURCH, MOTHER OF SAINTS 

The Divine Founder of the Church willed that it be One, Holy, Catholic and Apostolic. These are the characteristics by which we discern it. It is evident from its very founding by 

Jesus Christ that unity and apostolicity are essential marks of His Church. Catholicity and sanctity are equally necessary. 

Even if we grant that catholicity and holiness are found to some extent elsewhere, they are never known in the same vast, supernatural and miraculous manner as in the Church. 

Its Founder and His doctrine are the source of all sanctity. 

Holy are the end and purpose assigned to the Church, namely the eternal salvation of men; holy are the means it employs,

the sacraments, the truths of faith and morality, whence flow effusively gifts, virtues and special endowments in those who constitute the Church. The ninth article of the Creed unites two phrases: "I believe in... the holy Catholic Church [and in] the Communion of Saints," because the one is the sequel of the other.

With such precious means of sanctification in the Church, we should not marvel at sanctity, but rather at the great numbers of the faithful who neglect these means. The world and its distractions are the barriers which keep us from the straight path to sanctity. Despite the divine invitation to all men, "Be you therefore perfect, as also your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matt. 5:48), it is patent that on this earth the grain is mixed with the chaff, the wheat with the cockle, the wise virgins with the foolish, the faithful servants with the unfaithful. The seed of grace, despite its germinating power to produce a hundredfold, often yields but sixtyfold or thirtyfold, and sometimes nothing. The explanation lies in the dissipation and indifference of souls that live on superficialities and are moved by sentimentality rather than conviction. Wealth, pleasure and passion choke and stifle the divine seed. "The concupiscence of the flesh, and the concupiscence of the eyes, and the pride of life" (1 John 2:16) give birth to negligence, weakness and moral servitude. Yet even this mixture of good and bad may be providential, for it paves the way to conversions: "If then any be in Christ a new creature, the old things are passed away, behold all things are made new" (2 Cor. 5:17). So is rendered more vigorous the godliness of the faithful, and stronger their virtue.

It is not our purpose here to discuss the problem of evil. 

Rather, contemplating sanctity diffused among all peoples, of

13 every age, of every kind of temperament, character and mode of life, we sense in that assemblage the irrefutable proof of the possibility and the reality of sanctity in all nations. 

THE TESTIMONY OF THE SAINTS 

After having manifested His divine Revelation, our Lord promised to His Apostles the Spirit of Truth that proceeds from the Father: He shall give testimony of Christ, and "you shall give testimony," proclaimed the Divine Master (John 

15:26, 27). The testimony to be given of Jesus is therefore twofold: the divine, by the Spirit of Truth; the human, by His

Apostles, disciples and other followers. 

The Revelation of Christ was given by His life, doctrine, prophecies and miracles, and especially by His glorious Resurrection. So it was made credible, and even manifest. The 

Apostles, who had passed their days with Him, were to testify to His life and actions. Human intelligence had to be convinced of the truth through this preaching of the chief witnesses of the life of Jesus Christ; human reason, moved by the grace of the Holy Ghost and by the inner voice of the soul, wherein God has implanted the light of His truth, would then submit obediently to faith, and live in its brightness, purity and sanctity. The Apostles gave this testimony, as appears from the Acts of the Apostles, and multitudes were converted, even unto martyrdom and death.

The Church perpetuates this twofold testimony, human and divine. Through divine tradition deposited in the Church, by means of historical documents bequeathed by the Apostles and Evangelists, the world can and must agree in the fact of divine Revelation, in an act of "reasonable service" (Rom. 12:

1), while the grace of the Holy Ghost, working in souls, moves and draws the will to submit to the doctrines of Jesus Christ. 

From the beginning of the Church, the testimony of the Man-God has been propagated, often by heroic means. Men who knew and understood Christ resisted pagan laws condemned by Him. Their testimony was glorious, and they are called the witnesses par excellence, the martyrs (µáo̟tv5, or "witness"), whose number is legion. From the very beginning of the Church the soil of all nations was hallowed by their sacrifice.

In the first three centuries of the nascent Church, testimony to Christ was given especially in the shedding of blood. 

It was fearless, yet calm and serene. If fanaticism arose in some, the Church itself ordered prudence and disapproved temerity. Prudence is rooted in justice and charity, while temerity springs from presumption and may easily lead to defection. With a few unavoidable exceptions due to the contagious impulse of the multitude, or to the inexperience and enthusiasm of youth, the martyrs are models of serenity and moderation. The martyr Polycarp exhorts the faithful not to surrender spontaneously to their persecutors, not to provoke the pagans, nor to outrage their worship, nor much less to destroy their temples and idols. St. Paul did not overthrow any of the altars of Athens.

Fanaticism and exaggerated enthusiasm are defects of the individual personality, while moderation springs from a broader outlook and often leads an opponent to salutary reflections. The 

Lord said: "And when they shall persecute you in this city, flee into another" (Matt. 10:23). Out of such prudence and moderation St. Polycarp, proscribed by the pagans, hides in the

The testimony spreads. Throngs of religious set out for distant lands to announce the message of Christ; missionaries, priests, sisters and laymen, leaving the comforts of their native home, adopt another fatherland to win souls to God, and not a few of them meet death in the field of their apostolate. 

Testimony is given to Christ even in the most commonplace and ordinary circumstances of life. Profession of faith without human respect and compromise, virtue and honest living inspired by Christian maxims and fashioned according to the ideals of Christ, are an evidence of sanctity and a firm testimony to God. There is no land where the doctrine of Christ is not accepted with virtue and heroism.

THE JESUIT MARTYRS OF NORTH AMERICA (SEVENTEENTH CENTURY) 

Missionaries to the Algonquins, Hurons and Iroquois

CHAPTER I THE EIGHT JESUIT MARTYRS OF NORTH AMERICA ISAAC JOGUES, JOHN DE BREBEUF, GABRIEL LALEMANT, NOEL CHABANEL, ANTHONY DANIEL, CHARLES GARNIER, RENE GOUPIL, JOHN LALANDE 

'N THE year 1625, only a few years after the foundation

of Quebec (1608), a band of Jesuit missioners under 

Father Charles Lalemant came to that city from France.

Hardly had they time to become acquainted with the land, the language and customs of the Indians whose conversion was the motive for their coming to the New World, than the French colony found itself in distress. Exploitation and persecution of the colonists and natives alike by those in control of the Merchants' Company led to the revocation of its charter. Before the destitute colony could be relieved it was set upon by the English, who compelled Champlain to surrender and forced the missionaries and colonists to return to France (1629). 

Three years later England restored the territory to France and the colony including the Jesuit mission was re-established. 

Among the original band which had accompanied Father Lalemant on his voyage in 1625 was Father John de Brébeuf. Born on March 25, 1593, at Condé-sur-Vire, of an illustrious Norman family, Brébeuf entered the Society of Jesus in 1617. Though a giant in physique he was in such poor health that he finished his studies only with difficulty.

Returning to the New World in 1633 after his forced abandonment of his mission in 1629, he set about again with his fellow-Jesuits to attempt the immense task of evangelizing 21

the native Algonquins and Hurons of Canada and the Iroquois of what is now New York State. It is estimated that these nations numbered at that time some 200,000 souls. They were real savages. Father de Brébeuf wrote of them: "Leaving a highly civilized community you [the missionary] fall into the hands of this barbarous people who care but little for your philosophy or your theology. All the fine qualities for which you are loved and respected in France are as pearls trampled under the feet of swine, or rather mules, which utterly despise you when they see that you are not as good pack animals as they are. If you could go naked, and carry the load of a horse on your back as they do, then you would be wise according to their doctrine, and would be recognized as a great man; otherwise not.”

John de Brébeuf himself was able to match the best of the Indians in physical prowess. The missionaries set to work with a will, receiving strong support from Cardinal Richelieu. 

In 1625, Father de Brébeuf had made a preliminary journey to some of the tribes, and in wigwams near the present city of Toronto had learned the language and customs of the Hurons. In 1634, he was made first superior of the Huron 

Mission. With his companions, Fathers Chaumonot, Daniel, Davoust, Jogues, Bressani, Garnier, LeMoyne, Pijart, Raymbault, Ragueneau, and others, he pressed forward into the heart of the Huron country, to the north of the Great Lakes. In the course of six years a flourishing mission with five main stations and thirty posts had been established. The town of 

St. Ignace, situated between Lake Superior and Lake Huron, was the principal center of the mission. Father Jerome Lalemont succeeded Father de Brébeuf as superior in 1638 and was, in his turn, succeeded by Father Paul Ragueneau in 1646.

Jesuit Martyrs of North America 23 In the meantime Father Isaac Jogues and Brother René Goupil had been captured by the Mohawks, a tribe of the Iroquois, in August, 1642, and carried off to New York State. 

Isaac Jogues was born in Orleans on January 10, 1607, and entered the Society of Jesus in 1624. René Goupil, born in

Angers in 1607, was received into the Society by Father Jogues after their capture. After frightful tortures Brother Goupil was martyred on September 23, 1642, because he had traced the sign of the holy cross over an Indian child. Father Jogues, who had many opportunities for escape, remained to learn. the language and baptize some seventy infants and old people before their death. Finally, after much prayer, and convinced that it would be for God's greater glory, he escaped with the aid of the Dutch and made his way back to France. In 1644, he returned with Brother John Lalande, a native of Dieppe, to preach the Gospel to his former captors, the Mohawks. He was martyred at the instigation of the medicine men on October 

18, 1646, at Auriesville, New York. His companion suffered a like fate on the following day.

Meanwhile, the Huron Missions had been developing and now counted some thousands of neophytes. But in 1648 and 

1649, the Iroquois, allies of the English and Dutch, made surprise attacks on fifteen villages of Christian Hurons while the warriors were absent. The villages were burned, the men slaughtered, and the women and children imprisoned. It was during these raids that Anthony Daniel, John de Brébeuf, Gabriel Lalemant, Charles Garnier and Noel Chabanel shed their blood for Christ.

The surviving missionaries led the remnants of the Hurons to safety near the French settlements. In 1650, evangelization of the Iroquois and Algonquins was continued. 

The martyrs were men of prayer and surpassing holiness. 

Charles Garnier in a letter to the Father General, Muzio Vitelleschi, states: "Concerning ours who are in this mission of the

CHAPTER III

JOSEPH ROSATI, C. M. (1789-1843) First Bishop of St. Louis

CHAPTER III JOSEPH ROSATI, C. M. 

FIRST BISHOP OF ST. LOUIS 

HILE it is known that Bishop Joseph Rosati was eminent in the Church of America for his learning, his great labors and his noble achievements in the missionary field, we possess scant information about his early life. He was born at Sora, in the kingdom of Naples, January 

12, 1789. During his youth he witnessed religious persecutions, the imprisonment of Pontiffs, suppression of religious houses and revolutions of society and government. It is a matter of record that he entered the novitiate of the Congregation of the Mission at Rome, that he studied the sacred sciences under Father Felix De Andreis, that in 1811 he was ordained priest of the Mission, and that for several years he was occupied giving missions in the suburbs of Rome.

These are the few known facts of the early life of this man to whom American tradition has given the title "The 

Model Bishop." But from the year 1815, when Father Rosati was appointed to accompany Fathers De Andreis and Acquaroni to establish the Congregation of the Mission in America, there is no lack of material. Joseph Rosati was an apostle in the young Church of the United States, and his name is stamped in her illustrious history.

Father Rosati and his confreres landed in America July 26, 1816. After a month's stay in Baltimore, awaiting further in- 37

structions from Bishop Flaget of Bardstown, to whom they had been recommended, they proceeded to Kentucky (October 1816). There they enjoyed the hospitality of Bishop Flaget for ten months, learning English.

In a short time Father Rosati was able to hear confessions and to preach in English. However, instead of having such duties assigned him, he was appointed Professor of Theology in Bishop Flaget's seminary at Bardstown. In October, 1818, Bishop Dubourg transferred the seminary to The Barrens, Perry County, Missouri, and appointed Father Rosati its first president. Here he taught logic and theology. 

In addition to his duties at the seminary, Father Rosati was now made pastor of the village church; he became likewise an indefatigable missionary. On horseback or on foot, through the scorching suns of midsummer and the storms and snows of winter, he made long journeys to visit isolated Catholics in the region. His fare was poor, his bed hard, his chapel a primitive log cabin; yet never did he spare himself the immense labors and fatigues of the missions. So great was his zeal that Bishop Dubourg of Louisiana, at that time in charge of all this territory, exclaimed, "If Father Rosati fails us, all will go to ruin.”

Within a few years, Bishop Dubourg, wishing to make certain that the religious interests of his diocese would be well cared for, petitioned Rome to elevate Father Rosati to the episcopate and to make him his own coadjutor. Knowing Father Rosati's reluctance to accept the episcopal dignity, the bishop asked that, if his request were granted, the papal bull to that effect should be accompanied by a precept of obedience. In 1823, therefore, Father Rosati received an apostolic brief, appointing him Coadjutor Bishop of New Orleans and

Joseph Rosati, C. M. 39 commanding him to accept the appointment. On March 25 of the following year, at Donaldsonville, Louisiana, he was consecrated Titular Bishop of Tenegra by Bishop Dubourg. In 

1827, Pope Leo XII appointed him first Bishop of St. Louis, which then embraced the States of Missouri and Arkansas, twothirds of Illinois and the territories stretching northward beyond the sources of the Mississippi and westward beyond the sources of the Missouri. 

His great worth as bishop can best be gleaned from the results of his administration. At his suggestion Bishop Dubourg had invited the Jesuits to St. Louis (1824) and founded 

St. Louis University. In 1836, Bishop Rosati invited the first 

Sisters of St. Joseph to America. The Religious of the Sacred

Heart, the Visitation Nuns and the Sisters of Charity from Emmitsburg were likewise encouraged to establish their work in his diocese. He was instrumental in securing the union of 

Mother Seton's Sisters with the Daughters of Charity in France. 

In addition, a home for orphans, an institute for deaf-mutes, St. Louis Hospital (capable even in 1840 of receiving 1,200 patients) and St. Louis Cathedral, which was erected at a cost of $60,000, were made possible by his zeal and untiring efforts. 

Bishop Rosati was not only a builder. An active member of the first four Provincial Councils of Baltimore, he wrote many of their most important documents. A pulpit orator accomplished in three languages Italian, English, French — he was so impressive in personality, and so convincing in argument, that his converts during a single year (1839) numbered 299. 

While absorbed in the functions of his episcopal office, Bishop Rosati found time to perform even the most onerous duties of the priesthood. None of his time would he permit himself to call his own. At all hours he was ready to give his fullest at- 

---

tention to anyone who might desire to speak with him. In one year alone, Bishop Rosati had 1,548 Baptisms and 684 Confirmations and prepared thousands for first Holy Communion. 

He wielded a mighty influence for good and was everywhere treated with extraordinary honor.

In 1841, Pope Gregory XVI appointed him Apostolic Delegate to settle ecclesiastical affairs in the Republic of Haiti. 

After a pact had been drawn up, Bishop Rosati journeyed to Rome to obtain the Holy Father's approval. During his sojourn in the Eternal City he was stricken with an acute attack of lung trouble and died September 25, 1843. Thus Rome both saw the commencement of his saintly and useful career, and was the witness of his untimely death. Honored and esteemed in two hemispheres, he was mourned by the Church throughout the world. He was a holy bishop, worthy of the brightest ages of the Church.

While no formal steps have as yet been taken with a view to the beatification of Bishop Rosati, evidences of his zeal, his sanctity and his extraordinary work for the Church, accomplished at the cost of untiring self-sacrifice, give reason to hope that his title to be numbered among the blessed will in due time be the subject of a canonical investigation.

CHAPTER IV

MATHIAS LORAS (1792-1858) First Bishop of Dubuque

CHAPTER IV MATHIAS LORAS FIRST BISHOP OF DUBUQUE 

HE French Revolution was nearing its climax when, on 

August 30, 1792, there was born to Jean Mathias Loras and Etiennete Michelet, at Lyons, France, the tenth of their eleven children, Pierre Jean Mathias Loras. While the child was very young, his father, a wealthy merchant and councilor of the city of Lyons, was seized by the revolutionaries. 

In vain did his mother, with the little boy beside her, plead with the tyrant Couthon for the life of her husband: he was among the first to be guillotined in the Square des Terreaux. 

His last words "Tell a lie to save my life? Never!" give some idea of his character. A few days later the same fate overtook two of his sisters and two of his brothers, one of the latter the mayor of the city of St. Cyr. In all, seventeen members of the Loras family lost their lives for their religious and political convictions. 

Young Mathias Loras received his early education from priests who sought refuge in his mother's home. It was a home in which all the children were edified by the deep and enlightened piety of their mother. As soon as she recognized evidences of Mathias' vocation to the priesthood, she encouraged his inclination and arranged that he should begin his priestly studies in the old Carthusian house at nearby Ecully. It was there that he became a classmate and intimate friend of Jean 45

Marie Vianney, later the sainted Curé of Ars. It is interesting to note in passing that as a boy Mathias had the privilege of visiting the exiled and imprisoned Pope Pius VI at Valence. 

In 1807, he entered the seminary at Largentière, going later to St. Irenaeus, Lyons, where he received the diaconate. 

Continuing his studies at Meximieux, he also served there as an instructor. 

He was ordained at Grenoble, November 12, 1815, in his twenty-third year, and celebrated his first Mass in the Church of St. Paul in Lyons on the sixteenth of the same month. It was a tribute to his scholarship and character that he was immediately appointed rector of the Petit Séminaire of Meximieux and in 1824 was promoted to the important post of superior of the Seminary of Largentière, an office he later resigned in order to become pastoral missioner in the Archdiocese of Lyons. 

It was at this time that he met Bishop Michael Portier of the See of Mobile, Alabama. Yielding to the bishop's entreaties, he decided, in 1829, to accompany him to America and there to devote himself to a more vast and arduous missionary work.

For seven years after his arrival in the New World, the ardent priest served in the Diocese of Mobile, as pastor of the cathedral, as rector of the newly founded Spring Hill College and as vicar general of the diocese. On April 22, 1837, the bishops of the United States, assembled in the Third Plenary 

Council of Baltimore, recommended that the Holy See erect the new Diocese of Dubuque and that Father Mathias Loras be appointed its first bishop, with jurisdiction northward from the State of Missouri to the Canadian boundary, and westward from the Mississippi River to the Pacific Ocean - an area then known as the Wisconsin Territory.

Mathias Loras 47 On July 28, 1837, Pope Gregory XVI erected the new see and named Father Loras its Ordinary. His consecration took place in the Cathedral of Mobile, on December 10 of the same year, the consecrating prelate being, very fittingly, Bishop 

Michael Portier.

Bishop Loras immediately set out for his native France to recruit priests and collect alms for his vast missionary diocese. 

When he finally arrived in Dubuque on April 19, 1839, he found in the great expanse of his far-flung mission three chapels, one priest -the illustrious Father Samuel Charles Mazzuchelli ministering to 3,000 souls, a few widely scattered white settlements and some 30,000 Indians.

Endowed with apostolic zeal and penetrating vision, Bishop Loras saw immediately that the endless stretches of rich soil in the upper Mississippi Valley could be transformed into an agricultural empire. He planned wisely and strove courageously to draw the Catholic immigrants from their crowded huddle in the cities of the Atlantic seaboard. However, his efforts were less successful than they should have been, because the bishops in the Eastern sees were not at that time persuaded of the wisdom of his policy. 

As a true missionary, Bishop Loras went about ministering and organizing among the immigrants of his wide diocese, traveling by horse, canoe, steamboat, stagecoach and on foot. He came to know well the great Father of Waters, going as far as the present site of St. Paul by steamboat and returning in a birch-bark canoe with Indian guides. The stagecoach was his means of reaching the early settlements at Sioux City and Council Bluffs, in the western extremity of Iowa. To the Indian tribes he became known as the "Black Robe," and at length he succeeded in establishing the reign of Christ over the length

and breadth of the vast territory. Undismayed by the lonely and severe stretches of mountain and prairie, he envisioned the possibility of religious foundations everywhere. A consummate leader, he selected church sites in new villages even before the arrival of Catholics and purchased with his own savings ground upon which today some of the best parishes stand. 

Through the influence of relatives Bishop Loras was offered an archbishopric in France, but he refused decisively to leave his American flock. Aroused by the remarks of some 

French bishops, he wrote: "We have no martyrs of blood in the United States, but many of charity. We impose on ourselves personal privations of which our worthy bishops of France have no idea. In Dubuque I have no horse, no carriage; I visit the sick on foot one or two miles from the city; my table is as frugal as was that of St. Augustine and other holy prelates, and sometimes more so. I have just been ill four months with a trembling fever during which I had to travel 200 leagues to plant the faith on the shores of the Missouri River, as otherwise heresy would have taken hold forever in these counties."

An ardent lover of American institutions, Bishop Loras participated actively in many civic movements for the welfare of the communities in his great diocese. In May, 1846, he and 

Bishop John Hughes of New York made an appeal to James

Buchanan, then Secretary of State, the result of which was that President James Polk appointed chaplains to care for the spiritual needs of the Catholic soldiers during the Mexican War. 

Bishop Loras died in the late night of February 19, 1858, with the Divine Office in his hands. He was buried in the crypt of St. Raphael's Cathedral, in Dubuque. His zeal for souls, his poverty, patience in trials, humility, gentleness and honesty

Mathias Loras 49 of purpose fascinated those who surrounded him. His people felt their lives touched by Christ in the presence of their bishop, and they sought to be near to him whenever possible. In his endurance of hardships they recognized the fortitude and courage of a martyr, and the faith, the hope and the charity of a saint.

As during the saintly bishop's life, so in the long years afterward, narratives of his heroic sanctity were told at firesides, and the name of Loras was given to children by many parents, who regarded him as the holiest of men. 

During the twenty years of his episcopate, Bishop Loras' apostolic zeal and tireless labor changed the primitive trails in the great wilderness into pathways among the Indians, villagers, soldiers, traders, miners and immigrants. He left as heritage to his successor 54,000 Catholics, 60 churches, 40 mission chapels, a Catholic school in nearly every parish, Mt. St. 

Bernard's Seminary, New Melleray Monastery and a commu nity of religious women. 

In October, 1936, the Archbishop of Dubuque, Most Reverend Francis J. L. Beckman, urged the priests, religious and laity of the archdiocese to plan, as a fitting commemoration of the centennial (1837-1937) of the establishment of the see and the appointment of the first bishop, a year of solemn religious exercises, which began July 28, 1937 and ended July 28, 1938. This highly successful celebration served as a historic revival of the faith inspired by the memory of the holy life of Bishop Loras.

REDERIC BARAGA, the first Bishop of Marquette, was born in the parish of Dobernice, in the dukedom of

Carniola in what is now Yugoslavia, June 29, 1797. He was the fourth child of devout Slovenian parents, John Nepomucene Baraga and Maria Katharine de Jencic Baraga. Unhappily, he was bereft of both of them at an early age, though the loss was partly supplied by the pious lay professor in whose household he then went to live. Under this man's direction he pursued his classical studies, and at nineteen commenced the study of law in Vienna. It was there that he became acquainted with the saintly Redemptorist who perhaps inspired him to embrace the clerical state, Clement Mary Hofbauer, the "Apostle of 

Vienna," later canonized by Leo XIII.

After completing a course of studies at the seminary of his native diocese of Laibach, or Lubiana, Frederic was elevated to the holy priesthood on September 21, 1823, in his twenty-sixth year. As assistant in a small, neglected parish of his native diocese, he soon became renowned as a preacher, confessor and spiritual writer. 

Through the Leopoldine Society, founded to aid the North American missions, Father Baraga learned of the sad plight of the Indians, and resolved to devote his life to missionary work among them. With the permission of his ordinary he 53

left his homeland, and on January 18, 1831, presented himself to Bishop Fenwick of Cincinnati. After three months' study of Indian dialects, he was assigned to Arbre Croche (now Harbor Springs, Michigan), where he began a thirty-seven-year apostolate among the red-skinned inhabitants of Michigan and northern Wisconsin.

With all the enthusiasm of his soul Father Baraga gave himself to his new field of labor. Though unaccustomed to hardship and privation, he found great happiness in the sacrifices of missionary life. From the very outset he loved the 

Indians, and ever afterward, whether as missionary or bishop, his joy was to spend and to be spent for their conversion. 

We see him following them to their hidden haunts, building churches, equipping little schools, establishing Christian colonies, teaching the things of God and man. From Arbre 

Croche (1831) to Beaver Island, from Indian Lake to Grand

River (1833), to La Pointe (1835), to L'Anse (1843), on and on, through the woods on snowshoes or on dog sleigh, over the choppy waters of the lakes in birch-bark canoes, this dauntless missionary made his way with only one intent to bring the message of the Gospel to the red man. "The snow was very deep and the temperature bitterly cold," he writes again and again; but "oh, the salvation of a single immortal soul is more worth than all the millions of this world!" In each place "chapel, house and school have to be built." At the same time he was preparing a grammar of the Indian language, compiling a dictionary and composing catechisms and prayer-books.

CHAPTER VI JUNIPER SERRA, O. F. M. 

APOSTLE OF CALIFORNIA 

HE Franciscan chosen by God to found and for fifteen years to govern the celebrated California missions, was born on November 24, 1713, in the village of Petra on the Island of Majorca. His parents, Antonio Serra and 

Margarita Ferrer, were humble people of the laboring class. 

Baptized on the day of his birth, the future apostle of California was named Michael Joseph, a name which was bestowed upon him again when, on May 26, 1715, he received the Sacrament of Confirmation from the Bishop of the Diocese. 

In his pious home the boy learned those first lessons of Christian faith and discipline that were to be his guide and stay during his later life in the sacred ministry of the Church and in the educational and missionary enterprises of the Franciscan Order. Taken frequently by his parents to the Franciscan church in Petra, the lad delighted as much in the friars and their way of living as the friars admired his piety, alertness and candor.

It was from the Franciscans at the city of Palma, also in Majorca, and from a priest attached to the cathedral of that city that Michael Joseph received his elementary and part of his secondary education. Before the completion of these studies he asked to be received into the Franciscan Order, and on September 14, 1730, his request was granted. During the impor- 61

tant year which followed a year spent in a friary on the outskirts of Palma the promising young novice learned to know and love more profoundly the grand ideals and virtues so characteristic of Francis of Assisi and his followers. On the day of his profession, September 15, 1731, he took the name of Juniper.

After his profession Juniper continued higher studies in the Franciscan Friary at Palma and at the university in that city. Even before his ordination to the priesthood, the exact year of which is not known, though it is believed to be 1737, he lectured in philosophy to the student clerics of the province and to the secular students who also attended its school at Palma. During the three years in which he was thus assigned to the province, he pursued the regular courses in theology leading to ordination. Meanwhile, in recognition of his profound learning, the Lullian University at Palma conferred upon the young friar the degree of Doctor of Sacred Theology and engaged his services for the university as Professor of Theology, a position which he held until his departure for the missions in the New World. His lectures, in manuscript, are still extant in the Franciscan Friary in Palma.

Fray Juniper's place as one of the scholars of the Order was assured, and the outlook for success and distinction promising. Many perhaps would have disregarded the inner voice which all this while called him to a life of comparative obscurity, inescapable hardships and discouraging adversities in the foreign missions. Serra, however, hearkened to the voice and besought his superiors to approve his desire to follow its promptings. Permission to join the Franciscans in Mexico was finally granted him, and accompanied by Father Francisco Palou, his former pupil and future biographer, he left Spain on Au-

Juniper Serra, O. F. M. 63 gust 28, 1749. He reached Vera Cruz on December 7, and finally arrived at San Fernando College, in Mexico City, on 

January 1, 1750.

During the ensuing nineteen years, Juniper Serra was active in central Mexico. Between 1750 and 1759 he headed the Franciscan group entrusted with the difficult Sierra Gorda missions northeast of Mexico City. In 1758, when the college agreed to supply missionaries for the devastated San Saba missions in Texas, Serra was recalled and put in charge of this undertaking. Nothing came of it, however, and for the next nine years he was active in and around Mexico City. Then came the turn of events that brought him to the land with which his name is generally identified - California.

The Jesuits having been peremptorily ordered from Spanish territory by the government in Spain, their missions in Lower California were quite as peremptorily forced upon the Franciscan College of San Fernando by the government in Mexico. 

Juniper Serra was chosen by his superiors to head this new enterprise. But Lower California was not to be his field of labor. 

In 1769, only a few months after he reached the peninsula, these peninsular missions were ordered to be turned over to the Dominicans, and the Franciscans, with Juniper Serra in charge of the mission, were directed to proceed to the new and uncultivated region of Upper California.

The year 1769 inaugurated those fifteen years of unwearying labor for which Juniper Serra is best known. During this period he founded nine missions: San Diego in 1769; San 

Carlos in 1770; San Antonio in 1771; San Gabriel in 1771; 

San Luis Obispo in 1772; San Francisco in 1776; San Juan

Capistrano in 1776; Santa Clara in 1777; and San Buenaventura in 1782. From his headquarters at San Carlos Mission, he di-

rected the friars in their work. Visiting them regularly, encouraging them in periods of despondency, warning them against impending opposition, defending them against unjust accusations, he was, in truth, a father to them all. He was frank and fearless in his dealings with the civil and military authorities, uncompromising when principles were at stake, fair and conciliatory when compromise was in order. As Administrator, he was the embodiment of St. Augustine's direction: “In necessariis unitas, in dubiis libertas, in omnibus caritas." 

Fray Juniper's love for the Indians and his zeal for their welfare were limitless. In each of them, even the very lowliest, he saw a precious soul, and the salvation of souls was the allabsorbing preoccupation of his life. The fact that at the time of his death over 5,000 persons had received the Sacrament of Confirmation at his hands speaks volumes. 

Few missionaries in America ever lived a more faithful and energetic life than Juniper Serra. The ability of an administrator and the zeal of a missionary mingled in him with the personal virtues that mark a worthy priest and a true son of 

St. Francis: deep piety without ostentation; profound faith without presumption; love of poverty and simplicity without niggardliness or slovenliness; deep-seated affection and sympathy without softness or sentimentality. 

Death crowned this career spent in the service of God and humanity, on August 28, 1784. He died as he had lived a saint. His remains rest at the mission he loved so well - San 

Carlos, near Monterey in California. In Statuary Hall, in the nation's Capitol, an impressive statue honors the memory of the man who is justly styled "The Apostle of California."

CHAPTER VII MAGIN CATALA, O. F. M. 

THE HOLY MAN OF SANTA CLARA 

MONG the many missionaries whose careers in America were inconspicuous in their own day, and who even now do not receive the acclaim they deserve, must be numbered Magin Catalá, "the holy man of Santa Clara,” who, loyally and unobtrusively serving the mission to which he was assigned, spent an unusually fruitful life of prayer and good works.

Magin Catalá and his twin brother were born in 1761 at Montblanch, a village in the province of Catalonia, Spain. Their father, a doctor, was royal and public notary in Montblanch. Their mother, Francesca Guasch y Burgueras, was the daughter of a prominent merchant in the city. The Catalá family, though not wealthy, were comfortably blessed with the things of this world. 

On January 31, 1761, a few days after his birth, the future

Franciscan missionary and his twin were baptized in the parish church, each receiving three names: the former, Magin, José, Matias; the latter Pedro, Antonio, Juan. Of Magin's boyhood days nothing is known. Early schooling and training must have been of the best, however; while the piety he learned from the example of his parents doubtless attracted him to the circle of Franciscans who had a friary in Montblanch. In time he applied for admission into their ranks, and received the habit 69

of St. Francis in the friary in Barcelona on April 4, 1777. After pronouncing religious vows, young Magin pursued the required courses in philosophy and theology, and in 1785 was ordained to the priesthood.

At this time the vast Franciscan mission fields in America clamored for recruits. Magin Catalá, a year after his ordination, volunteered for these missions. In October of the same year he sailed for Mexico and in due time reached San Fernando College in Mexico City, where he labored until 1792, when the Guardian of the college assigned him to the California field. 

In July of the following year Father Catalá arrived at Monterey, accompanied by Father José da la Cruz Espi. Proceeding to 

San Carlos Mission, the two newcomers reported to the Superior of the Missions, who thereupon appointed Father Espi to 

San Antonio Mission and directed Father Catalá to accompany the Saavedra expedition to Nootka Sound as chaplain of the frigate Aranzazu. On July 2, 1794, the vessel was back at Monterey. After resigning as chaplain for the second voyage northward, Father Catalá was sent to Mission Santa Clara, where the baptismal register has an entry by him for the first time on 

September 1, 1794.

At Santa Clara, the zealous friar labored without interruption for thirty-six years. During that entire period, barring some visits for ministerial purposes to the neighboring missions, he never left the place to which holy obedience had assigned him, and of which, after 1797, he was the responsible senior missionary. Twice, in 1800 and again in 1804, he asked leave to return to the college in Mexico (for he suffered constantly from inflammatory rheumatism), and reluctant permission was granted; but each time, the friar's love for the Indians triumphed over personal considerations, and kept him at his post.

Magin Catala, O. F. M. 71 where he cared for the neophytes as well as his health would permit and sanctified himself by prayer and suffering. 

While Father José Viader took charge of the material affairs of the Santa Clara Mission, Father Catalá devoted himself almost exclusively to the spiritual instruction and direction of the Indian converts. The mission records show that during the thirty-six years of his administration not less than five thousand persons (nearly all of them Indians) were baptized at 

Santa Clara Mission. During the same period, 1,905 marriages were solemnized and about five thousand Christian burials performed. Scattered over the valley in which the mission was located were ten Indian settlements. These, too, had to be visited at regular intervals and the Christian Indians living there had to be instructed in the tenets of the Catholic faith. Considering Father Catalá's physical ailment, this phase of his duties as a missionary must have called for a spirit of sacrifice that bordered on the heroic. 

Even more remarkable than his active life as a missionary was Father Catalá's private and hidden life as a priest. There is no earthly record, of course, of the many hours he spent in prayer before the Blessed Sacrament in the mission church or before the crucifix over his favorite altar; of the rigorous fasts he imposed upon himself and the mortifications he inflicted on his ailing body in the secrecy of his little room near the church; of the many ejaculations of resignation to the Holy Will of God that escaped his lips when his limbs were racked with pain, or when he was tempted to despondency and despair. It is to be noted that this sanctified soul did not proclaim itself with outward show. Father Viader, who spent thirty-three years with him at Santa Clara Mission, scarcely ever in the many

letters and reports he wrote makes mention of Father Magin's activities. In this, we are moved to believe, Father Viader merely followed the wishes of his senior companion, who preferred to remain unnoticed and unknown. 

Finally the end came, serene and peaceful, welcomed by the saintly priest whose life had been a ceaseless preparation for its close. At daybreak of November 22, 1830, the soul of "the holy man of Santa Clara" passed to the better life. "The saint has left us!" cried the Indians at sight of their father and friend in death. Placed in a coffin of redwood, his body was laid to rest on the Gospel side in the sanctuary of the mission church.

CHAPTER VIII THE VENERABLE FELIX DE ANDREIS, C. M. 

FIRST SUPERIOR OF THE CONGREGATION OF THE MISSION IN THE UNITED STATES, AND VICAR GENERAL OF UPPER LOUISIANA ELIX DE ANDREIS was born of respected and pious parents on December 13, 1778, at Demonte, a hamlet in the present Diocese (and former province) of Cuneo, Piedmont, Italy. Particulars respecting his early youth are meager, but his intense attraction to the religious life at an early age can be gleaned from manuscripts found after his death.

Having completed his early studies in the place of his birth, he was sent to Cuneo at the age of fifteen, to learn rhetoric and philosophy. He was gifted with superior talents, and won the sincere admiration of all who knew him. 

Admitted at the age of seventeen into the Congregation of the Mission by Father Laugeri, he pronounced his holy vows in the Congregation on the twenty-first of September, 1800, after having made his novitiate in the house of the Mission at Mondovi. He continued his studies for a time at the house of the Holy Martyrs at Turin, and from there was sent to Piacenza, where he was ordained a priest (1801). His student days were marked by solid virtue and profound scholarship. 

His priestly duties, covering a variety of special assignments, were discharged with uninterrupted fidelity. A prodigious capacity for work clearly indicated that Divine Providence had destined the young priest for extraordinary achievements. 77

In March, 1806, Father De Andreis was sent to the house of Monte Citorio in Rome where, notwithstanding his great desire to labor in rural districts, he was commissioned to teach theology to clerics both of his own Congregation and of the College of Propaganda. As a professor he gave evidence of the vast treasures of sacred learning he possessed. "When 

Father De Andreis took the chair to give us lectures in theology," declares the Most Reverend Joseph Rosati, first Bishop of St. Louis, writing of his student days, "his disciples were astonished, I may almost say thunderstruck, by the richness, the ease and solidity and clarity of his arguments. ... But what 

I prized even more than all this was that, while he enlightened our minds, he inflamed our hearts, his words being as so many fiery darts that pierced the inmost depths of the soul." 

In addition to being a celebrated teacher, Father De Andreis was renowned throughout Rome for his retreats for priests, candidates for the priesthood and the laity. Cardinal 

Della Somaglia, who attended one of these retreats, later described Father De Andreis as "a treasure of science and piety.”

His prominence as a retreat master attracted the attention even of Pius VII. 

Although endowed (as the foregoing instances make clear) with unusual talents for the work entrusted to him in Rome, Father De Andreis could not conceal his ardent desire of joining the foreign missions. China beckoned him, but the light of 

Providence showed him unmistakably instead a path to America, where his labors were destined to be so great as to consume his life.

The fulfilment of this vocation came in 1815. In that year Bishop Dubourg, Apostolic Administrator of Louisiana (which at that time extended from the Gulf of Mexico to the

So outstanding was the discharge of his ministry that his accomplishments have immortalized his memory in the history of the Missouri Territory. He had to deal with uncivilized Indians, hostile unbelievers, professed heretics and lapsed Catholics. Moved by his zeal and sanctity, it is little wonder that thousands of heathens were converted, that infidels earnestly begged to receive Baptism, and that wayward Catholics returned to the zealous practice of their religion. 

Before leaving Rome, Father De Andreis had secured permission to establish a novitiate of the Congregation of the Mission in the New World. This he did in St. Louis, with the generous assistance of Bishop Dubourg. In addition to his onerous duties as Master of Novices, he filled, in fact though not in title, the office of Superior of the Congregation in America. At the same time he had been entrusted with the education of the clerical students who were teaching in the Latin School in St. Louis, founded by Bishop Dubourg. So many and such difficult tasks fell to the lot of the young missioner that one is filled with wonder and admiration at the diligence and exactness with which he was able to discharge them.

CHAPTER IX SAMUEL CHARLES MAZZUCHELLI, O. P. 

MISSIONARY APOSTOLIC, "BUILDER OF THE WEST" 

LOMBARD of that same Milan which boasts of St. 

Ambrose, St. Charles Borromeo and Pope Pius XI, Samuel Charles Mazzuchelli was born November 4, 1806, the sixteenth of a family of seventeen children. His mother, Rachel Merlini Mazzuchelli, died less than six years after his birth. His father, Luigi Mazzuchelli, a banker, envisioned a brilliant career for his son. 

Times were difficult. The star of Napoleon was in the ascendant, and it was very probably because of the troubles in Italy that young Mazzuchelli was sent off to Switzerland at an early age to study with the Somaschi Fathers in that country. 

Just how long he remained there is not certain. We know that he was seventeen when he declared his desire of becoming a Dominican, and with the consent of his father entered the Convent of St. Dominic at Faenza (October 6, 1823), receiving the habit of the Order and the religious name of Brother Cajetan. There, fourteen months later, he made his profession of solemn vows. Shortly afterward he proceeded to Rome to pursue his ecclesiastical studies at the historic Convent of 

Santa Sabina.

The apostolic life of the young Milanese Dominican may be said to have assumed definite shape and purpose in 1827, while he was yet a student. It was during that year that the 85

first Bishop of Cincinnati, the Most Reverend Edward Dominic Fenwick, O. P., appealed to the members of his own religious. family for help in the care of his vast diocese in the New World. 

The appeal stirred a willing response in the heart of Brother Cajetan; three years later (July, 1830) he was ordained deacon by Bishop Fenwick in the cathedral in Cincinnati, and on September 5 of the same year, he was ordained priest. 

Father Mazzuchelli entered upon his long ministry at a time of stress, when only the most urgent and crying needs could be allowed to command attention. And so it was that the young priest, bred in a vastly different civilization, and deeply rooted in the best traditions of an illustrious Order, set out to become a pioneer missionary among Indian savages and Canadian traders. The island fort of Mackinac at the juncture of Lakes Huron and Michigan was the scene of his first assignment. There he found himself one among some five hundred souls, the greater number of whom were Catholics of Canadian origin, about two-thirds of them married to Indians. 

In 1835, as the consequence of a call from his Dominican brethren in Ohio, Father Mazzuchelli entered upon what was destined to be the second period of his apostolic endeavors, a period during which he was to earn the title, "Builder of the West." The journey, through Dubuque, Galena, by way of the 

Mississippi to St. Louis and thence to Ohio, taught but a single powerful lesson, that the mission-fields were "ripe for the harvest." In July, having arranged his affairs and secured the necessary permission, the zealous friar made his way to Dubuque and there began his labors among Irish and German immigrants and the ever-increasing number of pioneers from the East. 

Throughout southwestern Wisconsin, northwestern Illinois and eastern Iowa Father Mazzuchelli set about organizing parishes,

Samuel Charles Mazzuchelli, O. P. 87 building churches and performing the other functions of the priestly ministry; then, when a start had been made, he moved on, leaving to others what he had begun, while he himself unselfishly commenced anew. In Dubuque he built a college which later served as episcopal residence and seminary. The 

State Capitol at Iowa City is the fruit of his inspiration, if not of his actual planning. Everywhere and in every possible way he accomplished something for the greater glory of God and the salvation of souls.

As a kind of Indian summer, a respite after this period of intense activity, Father Mazzuchelli was sent to Europe to appeal for volunteers. In Rome he brought to a happy consummation a plan which had long been maturing in his mind. He requested and was granted permission to establish in the northwest a separate province of the Order of Preachers. Besides appealing to his relatives and friends for help, in his effort to finance the project he assumed a new rôle that of author. 

His Memoirs of a Missionary Apostolic affords an insight into the hidden life of the apostolic Dominican. 

During the next five years, Father Mazzuchelli was to work as a founder and an educator. Firm in purpose and armed with the blessing and approval of his religious superiors, he returned to America in 1844 to enter upon what was at the same time the most truly great and the most genuinely tragic period of his apostolic career. Of the five foundations which he established in southwestern Wisconsin during these five years, only two endured. The idea of a Province of St. Charles was abandoned when three of his brethren who had been sent from Rome failed in perseverance. Of necessity, the novitiate for Dominican novices was likewise abandoned. The title and direction of the College of St. Thomas, which proved a success,

he was forced to commit to the Fathers of St. Joseph's Province. 

St. Clara Academy for the education of girls and the Dominican 

Sisters of the Congregation of the Most Holy Rosary, of Sinsinnawa, Wisconsin, alone endured. It is the latter foundation, numbering today some 1,500 members, which may be said to constitute Father Mazzuchelli's chief title to enduring fame in the Church in the United States. 

The last period of his apostolic life, 1849-1864, Father Mazzuchelli spent quietly as a simple pastor of souls. Residing at Benton, Wisconsin, he cared for the spiritual welfare of the flock at Prairie and New Diggings. He divided his time between his people and the Sisters whom he, like his Father St. 

Dominic before him, had brought together as auxiliaries in the cause of Christ. It seems somehow fitting that when death finally entered in, it was by way of Father Mazzuchelli's everloving zeal and thoughtfulness for those of whom he had been appointed shepherd. As the result of a call to the deathbed of a parishioner, February 16, 1864, he was stricken with a fatal chill, and soon yielded up his own soul in what had become for him a second homeland.

That, in brief, is the record of an extraordinarily full and busy life. But what of that hidden, interior life which has only been hinted at, and without which true zeal is impossible and no activity of any avail? The late Archbishop Ireland, in the 

Introduction of the Memoirs, testifies as follows: 

"Mazzuchelli was the saint. He was the saint; immaculate of life; scrupulous of duty; exquisite in tenderness of piety; in every attitude the man of God; his every relation with fellow-men revealing the spiritual lucidity of his inner soul; his every act sending forth the fire of love that burnt so brightly

We recall the unparalleled solitariness of his priesthood, the arduousness of his labors, the uncouthness and the peril incident to his evangelization. He was the youth of twenty-four when bidden into the wilderness. The nearest fellow-priest was hundreds of miles away. Savage-like roamers were his associates.

It is pathetic to read that when saying Mass in Indian hut or under oak-tree branches, Father Mazzuchelli would strive to buoy himself into reverence and exaltation of heart through memories of the stately temples of Milan and Rome, and of the splendors of the ceremonies there symbolical of the sublime grandeur of the Christian faith. After Mackinac and

FRANCIS XAVIER SEELOS, C. SS. R. (1819-1867) Redemptorist Preacher and Missionary

CHAPTER X FRANCIS XAVIER SEELOS, C. SS. R. 

REDEMPTORIST PREACHER AND MISSIONARY 

RANCIS XAVIER SEELOS was born in Fuessen, a village in southern Bavaria, on January 11, 1819. His parents, devout Catholics whose custom it was to assist at Mass daily with their entire family, trained their children in solid piety. The blessing of Divine Providence on this truly Christian household was manifest in the grace of religious vocation bestowed on three of the children. 

From his early years Francis expressed a desire to be a priest, and to this end he was instructed in Latin by the pastor of his native village. From 1834 to 1839, as a pupil in the 

Gymnasium of Augsburg, he ranked among the leaders in his studies. In 1839 he began the seminary course at the University of Munich.

Devotion to his patron, St. Francis Xavier, inspired in the young man a strong inclination for missionary activity. Accordingly, after spending three years in ecclesiastical studies, he applied for admission to the Congregation of the Most Holy 

Redeemer, at the same time volunteering for the American missions. He was accepted toward the close of 1842, and early the following year set out for the United States. The prescribed year of novitiate was spent in the Monastery of St. James in 

Baltimore. There he was professed as a Redemptorist on May 93

16, 1844, and on December 22 of the same year ordained to the priesthood by Archbishop Eccleston of Baltimore. 

The first nine years of Father Seelos' priestly life were spent as a member of the community of St. Philomena in Pittsburgh, where his first superior was the Venerable John Neumann, later Bishop of Philadelphia. Distinguished from the outset by the faithful observance of his religious rules, by his charity and especially by his zeal for souls, Father Seelos attained such a widespread reputation as a confessor and spiritual director that his penitents were very numerous. In recognition of his extraordinary holiness, his superiors appointed him novice master in 1847, a post he held until 1851. In that year he was designated rector of the Pittsburgh community and in 1854 he became superior of the community of St. Alphonsus in Baltimore. During the period from 1857 to 1862 he filled the office of spiritual director of the professed clerical students of his Congregation at the seminary in Cumberland, Maryland. 

In 1860 Bishop O'Connor of Pittsburgh, who was about to resign his see, informed Father Seelos that he planned to suggest him as his successor, but the humble religious persuaded his Superior General in Rome to prevent the appointment. 

From 1862 until his death in 1867 Father Seelos was occupied in giving missions in various parts of the United States. 

His piety and apostolic ardor caused him to be regarded as a saint. Penitents everywhere flocked to his confessional. Although 

Redemptorists are exempt from many of the spiritual exercises of their rule while giving missions, Father Seelos was accustomed whenever possible to perform these devotions even on his busiest days.

In 1866 Father Seelos was assigned to the Redemptorist community in New Orleans where the Fathers had three

Francis Xavier Seelos, C. Ss. R. 95 churches caring for the faithful of three nationalities-English, French and German. Father Seelos, being familiar with all three languages, found abundant opportunity for zealous labor. A year later, however, he was stricken with yellow fever. After lingering three weeks, he died a holy death on October 4, 1867, and was buried in the vault of the Church of the Assumption. 

In a letter sent out after his death to the members of the 

Redemptorist Congregation, Father Helmpraecht, the Provincial 

Superior, wrote of Father Seelos: "He was considered a saint during his life and is even more so now than before his death. ...He did wonderful work during life, and during his last illness bore the sharpest pains with all patience. He was conspicuous for his love of poverty and mortification, for his love of his neighbor and his zeal for souls." 

On February 28, 1903, the documentary records of five prescribed processes were deposited with the Sacred Congregation of Rites in Rome by the Reverend Joseph Wissel, C. Ss. R.

An order for the juridical investigation of the writings of 

Father Seelos was issued by Cardinal Cretoni, Prefect of the

Sacred Congregation of Rites on March 11, 1908. In May of that year Cardinal Gibbons, Archbishop of Baltimore, ordered all writings of Father Seelos in the archdiocese to be submitted, and in the course of that same month two sessions were held in Baltimore to investigate these documents. In 1910 the Summarium of the ordinary processes was printed in Rome, and in 1912 the Postulator presented to the Sacred Congregation of Rites his Informatio, based on this Summarium, with a view to having the cause introduced before the Roman Curia.

LEO HEINRICHS, O. F. M. (1867-1908) 

Martyred While Distributing Holy Communion

CHAPTER XI LEO HEINRICHS, O. F. M. 

FRANCISCAN MARTYR 

ORN in the village of Oestrich, in the Archdiocese of 

Cologne, Germany, on August 15, 1867, and baptized on the same day, Joseph Heinrichs spent his childhood under the watchful care of pious parents. In due time he took up the study of the classics in order to prepare himself for the priesthood, to which from an early age he felt a special calling. 

At the age of nineteen, after finishing preparatory studies, he applied to the Franciscan Province of Fulda, Germany, for admission to the Order. It was a time when young men were needed in the United States to replace the heroic Fathers who had emigrated to America when Bismarck drove the religious out of Germany, and young Joseph was among those sent to the New World. He was stationed in Paterson, New Jersey, where at the parish church of St. Bonaventure the Franciscans had founded a monastery that served as a novitiate and house of higher studies for the American branch of the Province of Fulda. This was the foundation upon which the Province of the Most Holy Name was later erected (1901). 

It was in the fall of 1886 that the young man arrived in 

Paterson. On December 4 he was clothed in the habit of the

Poverello and admitted to the novitiate, receiving the name of Leo. After making simple vows on December 8, 1887, he began the customary courses in philosophy and sacred theology at 99

the monastery. His solemn profession took place on December 

8, 1890, and his ordination to the holy priesthood on July 26, 1891. Father Leo's first assignment was at the Paterson Monastery, where the greater part of the seventeen years of his priestly life was spent. Many positions of responsibility were entrusted to him, including the assistant mastership of novices. 

In April, 1902, after a great fire had destroyed the entire group of church buildings in Croghan, New York, Father Leo was sent there as pastor, charged with the gigantic task of replacing all that had been lost. Almighty God blessed his efforts; within two years a church, a monastery, a school and a convent stood where there had been but ashes, and every cent of the $200,000 which the project cost was paid through the efforts of the pastor. In the fall of 1904 he was recalled to Paterson, to assume the duties of pastor of the church and vicar of the monastery. After three years of zealous work in those capacities, he was appointed in the summer of 1907, superior of the monastery and pastor of the parish of St. Elizabeth at Denver, Colorado.

It was on September 23, 1907, that Father Leo arrived in 

Denver. Exactly five months later, February 23, 1908, while administering Holy Communion to the faithful at the six o'clock Sunday Mass, he was shot through the heart by a man who knelt at the communion rail. The assassin had approached with the other people and knelt down, one arm thrown carelessly over the rail. After Father Leo had come to him and placed the Host upon his tongue, the man threw off his disarming pose of piety, spat the Sacred Species contemptuously upon the floor, pulled a revolver from under his coat and emptied its contents into the heart of the priest. Still conscious, Father Leo swayed for a moment, reached the foot of the altar of Our Lady

Leo Heinrichs, O. F. M. 101 and managed to place the ciborium safely upon it. Then he fell to the floor, helpless. Some of the Particles dropped from the ciborium, and the dying priest made an effort to pick them up, though he had not sufficient strength. In a few moments one of his confrères, Father Eusebius Schlingmann, O. F. M., was at his side. Father Leo pointed to the Hosts on the floor, his concern for the Blessed Sacrament too great to allow him to think of his own extreme condition, or to be distracted by the commotion caused in the church by the capture of the murderer. 

While Father Eusebius gathered up the Sacred Particles, Father Wulstan Workman, O. F. M., gave absolution to the dying priest and administered Extreme Unction. After ten or fifteen minutes of intense agony, during which his lips moved in silent prayer, Father Leo died. It was a singular coincidence that he should have given up his life before the altar of the Blessed Mother, for it had always seemed to him a privilege to have been born and baptized on the day of her Assumption, and to have solemnly vowed to seek perfection on the Feast of her Immaculate Conception. Only a few days before his death he had declared in a sermon to the young ladies of the Blessed 

Virgin's Sodality: "How sweet it is to die at the feet of Mary!"

The Solemn Funeral Mass in St. Elizabeth's, Denver, was attended by an immense throng including the Governor of Colorado, the Mayor of Denver, and many other distinguished members of the clergy and laity. Five thousand men and women, unable to find room within, stood outside in the biting cold. The body was taken to Paterson, New Jersey, and laid to rest in the friars' burial plot. Some four years later, in November, 1911, when the remains of all the friars were transferred from the old plot to a larger one in the same cemetery, the body of Father Leo was found to be in an unusual state of preservation. The head

and face were in perfect condition, showed no sign of decay; the hands were folded, and still held the small crucifix placed in them before the burial. The facts surrounding the transfer of the remains were recorded by Father Stanislaus Woywod, O. F. M., as Ecclesiastical Notary, and the document duly signed by those present was placed in the archives of the province. 

The remains were reinterred in the new friars' plot and have not since been disturbed.

Veneration of Father Leo commenced with his death and has continued to this day, not only in Denver and Paterson, but in many other places in the United States, as attested by the many letters received by the Province of the Most Holy Name. 

So convinced were the faithful of the martyrdom and the heavenly glory of the Servant of God that immediately after his death they besought relics of him. Within a short time the habit, alb and cincture he wore on the day of his death had to be cut into small pieces and distributed among innumerable petitioners. That such confidence on the part of the faithful had not been misplaced would seem to be proved by the many blessings ascribed to the intercession of Father Leo. 

The devotion of the people urged the General of the Friars Minor, in the summer of 1926, to appoint the Very Reverend Benvenutus Ryan, O. F. M., then Provincial of the Province, to request the bishops concerned to initiate the Processus 

Ordinarius. Father Stanislaus Woywod, O. F. M., was appointed 

Vice Procurator, and in the fall of 1926, the Most Reverend 

John J. O'Connor, Bishop of Newark, New Jersey, organized the Beatification Committee. After the first meeting at Newark, New Jersey, under instructions of the Postulator General

Leo Heinrichs, O. F. M. 103 of the Order of Friars Minor the Vice Postulator went to 

Denver in February, 1927, to start there the Processus Rogatorialis concerning the death of Father Leo. The plea on which the Postulator General wished the Case to be based was that of martyrdom. After the Process at Denver, the Process as to the life, character and conduct of Father Leo was instituted at 

Paterson, New Jersey. Meanwhile, through the Bishop of Newark, a request was sent to the Archbishop of Cologne to conduct the Rogatorial Process there regarding the early life of Father Leo. After the Process dealing with the life and death, the section of the Process de Scriptis Servi Dei was initiated. 

The President of the Beatification Committee, the present Bishop of the Diocese of Paterson, at that time Monsignor McLaughlin, and the Secretary of the Board collated the collected letters, sermons and writings of Father Leo. 

The Process de non cultu was last conducted at Paterson, New Jersey, and all documents in the case were bound into volumes. One set was sent to the Sacred Congregation of Rites and another, sealed in the same manner as the first, was placed in the archives of the Diocese of Newark. 

Included in the acts is a complete and duly authenticated copy of the trial of Joseph Alia, the man who killed Father Leo. 

The assassin had pleaded insanity. The acts of the criminal court indicate the painstaking examination and investigations that were made on this issue. The unanimous verdict of the jury held that the man was guilty of murder and deserved the death penalty according to the law of the state. The Franciscan 

Fathers of the Denver Monastery petitioned the Governor to commute the sentence, but he refused, and the man was hanged in July of the same year, 1908.

Father Brucker presented the Vice Postulator with carefully compiled accounts of some forty remarkable cures attested to have been gained through the invocation of Father Leo. The saintly Jesuit Father had gone about the city himself making inquiries and investigating reported cures. The Vice Postulator, having selected a few of these cases, called witnesses to place them on record under the title of Fama Miraculorum.

CHAPTER XII BLESSED FRANCES XAVIER CABRINI FOUNDRESS OF THE MISSIONARY SISTERS OF THE SACRED HEART RANCES XAVIER CABRINI was born at Sant'Angelo di Lodi (Lombardy), Italy, on July 15, 1850, the youngest of a family of thirteen children. Even as a child she learned to love prayer, following the splendid example of her parents, her brothers and sisters. At the age of seven she was confirmed and at ten received her First Holy Communion. 

Frances, who possessed a ready intelligence and an unselfish disposition, was fired with enthusiasm upon reading the Annals of the Society for the Propagation of the Faith. Although she was but a child, the missionary ideal was beginning to fasten itself in her heart only half understood perhaps, but none the less real. In her child's play, she would gather violets and, placing them in tiny paper boats, send them away on the waters of the country brooks, dreaming the while of herself making long voyages to distant lands to carry on the work of converting pagans. When she was thirteen she revealed her desire to her sister Rose, fifteen years older than she. "You who are so small and not yet educated dare to dream of becoming a missionary!" was the comment she received. But 

Frances kept her radiant dream in her heart and waited patiently. In the meantime she continued her studies, and at the age of eighteen obtained her teacher's certificate. A year later her parents died. 107

Frances then definitely considered the question of her vocation. She sought to enter two religious communities, and in each case was rejected on account of her delicate constitution and poor health. Thus apparently unable to follow her heart's desire, the young girl nevertheless gave her leisure to works of charity and religion. At the request of the parish priest she taught catechism to children and visited the poor; when she was twenty-two (1872), she distinguished herself in assisting the victims of an epidemic of smallpox. 

For two years Frances now taught in the public school of the nearby town of Vidardo, and became known even to the civil authorities for her ability and her kindness. In 1874 she accepted the position of directress of a school for orphans at Codogno always, however, keeping the missionary ideal before her. In 1880 the Most Reverend Dominic Gelmini, Bishop of Lodi, who had learned of the extraordinary intellectual and moral qualities of the superior of the orphanage, summoned her and said: "You wish to become a missionary; the time is ripe; I do not know a community of missionary Sisters; you must found one." Frances, as an obedient daughter, answered humbly and confidently: "I shall seek a house." On November 

10, 1880, with a few companions she took up residence in an abandoned monastery, formerly the property of the Franciscans of Codogno. The new home was soon surmounted by a statue of the Sacred Heart, and on the door was placed an inscription that is now known throughout the world: "Institute of the

Missionary Sisters of the Sacred Heart." 

The new Institute grew rapidly, and in a few years there were houses in many parts of Lombardy and Italy. In 1887 

Mother Cabrini went to Rome to request the approval of the Rule by the Holy See, and to found a house in the Eternal City.

Blessed Frances Xavier Cabrini 109 The Cardinal Vicar answered her: "A Community founded only seven years ago wishes to establish itself in Rome and to be approved? This is too much! In Rome there are already many such Communities. And then, the funds? Do you have at least 

150,000 lire ($30,000) to build a house? Return, return to 

Codogno: we will talk about it some other time." This answer was a deep disappointment, but Mother Cabrini, undaunted, comforted her weeping companion: "Be calm; the Lord will soon change his heart." God rewarded her faith, for a few days later she was again received by the Cardinal Vicar. "Are you ready to obey?" he asked. "Well then, found two houses instead of one." And shortly afterward, the decree of approval was given to the Institute.

During her residence in Rome, Mother Frances Xavier had occasion to make the acquaintance of the Most Reverend John B. Scalabrini, Bishop of Piacenza and founder of the Missionaries of St. Charles Borromeo for Italian Emigrants. It was this holy bishop who first informed her of the conditions existing among the Italians in America, and requested her to send some Sisters to coöperate in the work that the Scalabrinians had already begun in New York. Mother Cabrini had always earnestly desired to go among the infidels of China, and she would not even now have changed her plan had it not been for the advice given her by Pope Leo XIII. "Not to the East, but to the West," said the aged Pontiff; and Frances no longer had any doubts or regrets. She was provided with letters of recommendation from the Secretariat of State of His Holiness and from the Sacred Congregation of the Propagation of the Faith; and in just two weeks, she was on her way to the United States with six companions.

- 

She landed in America on March 31, 1889. Immediately there were difficulties, but she was able to overcome them by her spirit of faith and fortitude, in the knowledge that she was fulfilling the mission entrusted to her by the Vicar of Jesus Christ. The condition of the Italian immigrants socially, economically and religiously was then very poor. The work of an apostle was urgently needed among them to help them combat the constant danger of loss of faith. 

Mother Cabrini began and extended her work for them with an energy that at times seemed rash. Taking for herself the motto of the Apostle, "Omnia possum in eo qui me confortat" - "I can do all things in Him Who strengtheneth me" (Phil. 

4:13) she opened schools, colleges, kindergartens, hospitals and free clinics in New York, New Orleans, Chicago, Seattle,

Denver and Philadelphia. She did not confine her work to the 

United States but extended it to Nicaragua, South America, Spain, France and England. Her program was to follow the multitudes of Italians who had gone abroad from their native land, to keep alive and renew the faith of the adults and to care for the new generations. Unsparingly, she undertook long and repeated journeys with the daring of a fearless explorer. 

Her health, never strong, had become worse and often she burned with a fever for months at a time; yet she went without hesitation to whatever place offered a new field for the charity of herself and her Sisters. 

Mother Cabrini studied the advisability of purchasing new properties, drew up contracts and directed construction of buildings with an insight that seemed inspired. "She constructed islands of rest, havens against germs, cold, hunger and death; restful houses of prayer and schools ventilated with fresh air as if by the very breath of Jesus; and by all those buildings she

Blessed Frances Xavier Cabrini 111 put in circulation the wealth of the rich for the benefit of the poor. Mother Cabrini placed the education of the learned at the disposal of the uneducated, turned kindness to the relief of misery, and brought fresh air and sunlight for the destruction of typhoid and tuberculosis. She was a swift and determined agent of the Heart of Christ."¹ Her work did not destroy in her the great tranquillity of one who entrusts all to God Omnipotent; in the midst of her feverish activity "she seemed a portrait of peace."2

If we can say that Mother Cabrini favored one field of endeavor rather than another, it was the United States of America. She lived and worked here about twenty years. She came to know and love this country, and she saw in it the path of opportunity for her fellow-countrymen and their children. Because of her attachment to the United States, as well as to develop her enterprises better and to unite her institutions to the country more securely, she obtained American citizenship in Seattle, Washington, in the year 1909. Mother Cabrini understood the great future reserved to the Church in the United States. She founded here many of her community's most elaborate institutions. She understood the American mentality, and, in turn, Americans, in their admiration for the complete dedication of the "little Sister" to the work of God, assisted her generously. After 1912, Mother Cabrini spent five consecutive years in the United States; that is, until the day of her death, which occurred in Chicago, on December 22, 1917.

CHAPTER XIII CATHERINE TEKAKWITHA "THE LILY OF THE MOHAWKS" 

ATHERINE TEKAKWITHA was a North American 

Indian, born in 1656 at Ossernenon, now Auriesville, New York, the site where Isaac Jogues, René Goupil and John Lalande had been martyred (1642-1646) and where the Society of Jesus now has a shrine, a house for Jesuits in final probation, and for priests making retreats.

Tekakwitha's father was a pagan Indian of the Mohawk tribe, one of the Iroquois nations, her mother a Christian Algonquin whom he had taken captive. As both of her parents died of the plague when Tekakwitha was four years old, her uncle, an

Indian chief, adopted her. Partly because the plague had affected her eyes, but chiefly to shun the village scandals, she kept to her cabin and devoted herself to domestic and other duties. The Mohawks were known as an immoral tribe, given to idling, gambling, torturing prisoners and to sorcery and debauchery.

After the plague the tribe moved to another site. Seven years later (1667) the French, to stop their invasions into Canada, destroyed these new villages, and the Mohawks migrated again, this time settling at Caughnawaga, now Fonda, in the State of New York, where the Franciscan Conventuals now have a Tekakwitha Friary.

As a result of a treaty signed with the French, the Mohawks agreed to receive missionaries. Tekakwitha's uncle, as 117

chieftain, entertained these men of God when they first came, but opposed their mission and later refused a welcome to them in his cabin. Thus, since it was dangerous for a young girl to move about alone, the missioners could not come to know her nor could she seek them.

Her aunts kept urging Tekakwitha to marry; because she would not consent, she was treated as a slave. Her own people charged her with having an improper regard for the uncle, and denounced her to the missionary Father de Lamberville, but he perceived their evil motive, and believed that the girl they were accusing was innocent. Later Father de Lamberville, encountering Tekakwitha by chance, found her gentle and retiring. 

She asked to become a Christian, and after inquiry about her character he baptized her within a year (Easter, April 18, 1676), although it was customary for Indians to spend two years or more in preparation for Baptism. She was given the name Catherine.

Pagans and Christians alike admired her, but it was not long before some of her wicked tribesmen began to threaten and persecute her because of her Christian belief. When one entered her cabin and raised his axe to strike her, she said to him, "You may take my life, but not my faith," and he left without harming her. 

The missionary helped the young girl escape from her vicious surroundings to LaPrairie, Canada, where the Jesuits had a village for Christian Indians. (It is now known as St. 

Francis Xavier's, Caughnawaga, where over 2,000 faithful Catholic Indians are devoted to the memory of the "Lily of the 

Mohawks.") Father de Lamberville recommended her to the missionaries in Canada as "a soul very dear to God." This was in the autumn of 1677. On Christmas Day of that same

Catherine Tekakwitha 

119 year she was privileged to receive Holy Communion (though Indians were usually required to make a long preparation often two or three years for that Sacrament also). 

Tekakwitha at first joined the other Indians when they went on the winter hunt, the men doing the killing, the women making the cabins, collecting the game and doing the household work. Her free time was spent in the woods praying before a cross cut in the bark of a tree. But this life tried her soul. She missed her chapel, visits to the Blessed Sacrament, daily Holy Mass. Moreover, she had been accused by a jealous wife of familiarity with her husband. She easily convinced the missionary of her innocence, and although the accuser repented for calumniating her, she never went on the hunt again. 

Catherine wanted to give her life completely to God. 

Because of her deep piety and exemplary Christian life, the missionary Fathers admitted her into the Confraternity of the Holy Family established by Bishop Laval of Quebec, for those only whose faith was long and well tried. In this association she became a model even for the more fervent. 

Tekakwitha's virtues led great numbers to imitate her. 

Her faith in God and in the teachings of His Church, her confidence in Him, her charity toward her neighbors, marked her as the highest type of Christian. To her the Blessed Virgin was a mother, and she loved to recite the Rosary. She was prudent and just; bore persecution, calumny and privation with exemplary patience, It required obedience to moderate her penances. She was so chaste that she obtained permission the first of her race to do so! - to vow virginity. 

Catherine Tekakwitha's death, like her life, made a deep impression on all who knew her. She foretold not only the hour of her death but the place of her burial, and declared how

she would obtain favors for friends from heaven. She died 

April 17, 1680 — the Wednesday of Holy Week - "made fect in a short space." per- 

Immediately her promises came true: she appeared to several persons; protected her village in storm and warfare; and created such fervor among her people that it actually had to be restrained. Proofs of her favor with God became so convincing that her directors wrote her life as if to seek her beatification. 

Remarkable answers to prayer through her intercession, growing in number and importance, have been received. 

The bishops of the United States, assembled in Third 

Plenary Council of Baltimore, 1884, petitioned the Holy See to institute the process for her beatification along with that of the martyrs Jogues, Brébeuf and their companions. The Hierarchy of Canada made a similar petition.

When the martyrs were beatified in 1925, preparations were made for the Cause of Tekakwitha. For good reasons it was instituted in Albany in which territory she lived for twenty years.

Beginning in June, 1931, the tribunal appointed by the 

Most Reverend Edmund F. Gibbons, Bishop of Albany, devoted a year to taking the testimony of those familiar with the story of Catherine Tekakwitha's life. In June, 1932, their report was transmitted to Rome. The Historical Section of the 

Congregation of Rites, after studying the testimony and documents in the case, reported to the Congregation in June, 1938, that the documents were genuine, and that they offered historical proof of her virtue and renown for sanctity.

At the age of three Philippine suffered an attack of smallpox which disfigured her face for life. Much of her schooling was received privately, but when she was about twelve years old she was sent to the nearby Visitation Convent of Ste. Marie d'en Haut to prepare for her first Holy Communion. Not long after this joyous event took place her parents, suspecting her future religious vocation, withdrew her from the convent. 

Nevertheless the devout girl retained her attachment for the institution, and she entered there as a postulant about 1788. 

The terrible scourge of the French Revolution followed shortly, and her convent was closed by the civil authorities on 

January 14, 1791, being soon afterward turned into a prison.

The young religious, forced once more out into the world, devoted herself to works of mercy for the numerous unfortunate the sick, the poor, the imprisoned. 125

At length peace was restored to her distracted country, and in December, 1801, about eleven years after her expulsion,

Sister Philippine and a few of her former religious companions regained possession of the confiscated Convent of Ste. Marie. 

Many difficulties arose, however, which finally prevented the permanent restoration of the religious life of this little community in their old home. After three years of disappointments, therefore, they began their novitiates there (December 31, 1804) for admission into a new Community the Society of the Sacred Heart, which had been established by Madeleine 

Sophie Barat four years earlier in Paris. At Ste. Marie, on 

November 21, 1805, Mother Barat (who is now a saint herself) renewed her vows in the Society, and Mother Duchesne and the other former Visitandines made their first profession. 

Mother Duchesne, who from childhood had merited the name later given her by the American Indians - "The Woman 

Who Always Prays" - longed to spend her life in the foreign mission fields. Her particular interest centered in the North 

American aborigines because of the stories she heard told by missionaries returning to France. These hopes seemed to be definitely frustrated when she was elected Secretary General of the Society in 1815. The appointment took Mother Duchesne away from her beloved Convent of Ste. Marie (which she never saw again) to the headquarters of the Society, then in Paris. 

In her forty-eighth year, Mother Duchesne entered upon what may be called the second great chapter of her religious life. On January 14, 1817, while acting as portress at the motherhouse, she opened the door to Bishop Louis William Valentine Dubourg of the Diocese of Louisiana in America, who later became Archbishop of the Cardinalatial See of Besançon. He had come to France seeking priests and nuns for

Their American membership grew, and many other convents were established even during Mother Duchesne's life.

In 1840 the distinguished Russian convert, Mother Galitzin, was appointed Visitor of the American houses of the Society. When she came to Florissant, Mother Duchesne earnestly begged of her to be relieved of the office of Superior. The petition was granted, and for a short time thereafter Mother Duchesne remained in St. Louis.

In 1841, her long-deferred desire to work among the Indians was fulfilled. Mother Mathevon was appointed Superior of the foundation at Sugar Creek, and Mother Duchesne was one of her companions. It was an extremely difficult work; the weather was very cold in winter, very hot and dry in summer; the housing conditions and habits of the Indians were crude and their numerous dialects difficult to master. 

After a year in the Far West, this valiant missionary received what proved to be her last obedience. She had hoped to die among her beloved Indians, but in 1842 she was called back to her first home in St. Charles. She returned to Missouri with Archbishop Kenrick, who had been visiting his Kansas missions that summer. The last ten years of her life were spent mostly in prayer. Thus she became a spiritual powerhouse for the far-flung houses she helped to establish in the New World.

CHAPTER XV MOTHER ELIZABETH ANN SETON FOUNDRESS OF THE SISTERS OF CHARITY IN THE UNITED STATES COOL 

LIZABETH ANN BAYLEY SETON, affectionately known as Mother Seton, Foundress of the Sisters of 

Charity in the United States, born August 28, 1774, was the second daughter of Richard Bayley, surgeon, health inspector of the port of New York, and first Professor of 

Anatomy at Columbia University. Her mother, Catherine

Charleton Bayley, was the daughter of Reverend Richard Charleton, Rector of St. Andrew's Episcopal Church, Richmond,

Staten Island. Elizabeth was reared by her parents as a devout 

Episcopalian. 

At the age of nineteen, she married William Magee Seton of New York, a wealthy descendant of an ancient Scottish family. Mr. Seton too was an Episcopalian. The wedding took place in Trinity Church, January 25, 1794.

Because of the war between France and England the Seton fortune was ruined, and the health of William Seton failed rapidly. It was but ten years after her happy bridal day when 

Elizabeth, at the advice of physicians, took her young husband to Italy in the hope of rebuilding his health. Little eight-yearold Anna Marie, the eldest of their five children, accompanied them. 

They sailed on the "Shepherdess" October 2, 1804, and reached their destination of Leghorn on November 19. Be- 133

cause of a rumor that there was yellow fever in New York, the Setons were quarantined for thirty days in the Lazaretto. 

This enforced stay in the damp, gloomy stone building was believed to have been the immediate cause of the husband's death eight days after their release. 

Her three-months' stay in Italy was, Mother Seton afterward stated, the reason for her conversion to the Catholic faith: "My conversion is due to the fact that I visited a Catholic country." She had the opportunity to see for herself and study at first hand the sublime truths of the Catholic Church and the practical demonstration of their application in the lives of the noble Filicchi family, whose guest she was. Possessed of clear judgment and being most desirous of pleasing God, the moment she became convinced that the Catholic Church was founded by Christ Himself, she wished to make her formal profession of faith. She was advised, however, to wait until her return to New York.

When she was back in her native city Mrs. Seton found, instead of the sympathy and support she had a right to expect from relatives and friends, only reproaches, opposition and persecution. She preferred to renounce all offers of worldly prosperity and position rather than sacrifice that pearl of great price that had so recently come to her - the gift of faith. In order to gain a livelihood for her family, the young mother opened a school. Even there, relentless persecution followed her, and she was forced to cast about for other means of support. 

In the meantime, she had placed herself under the direction of the Reverend Matthew O'Brien, pastor of St. Peter's 

Church. In words that glow with a sweet fire of love she described her reception into the Church, March 14; her first confession, March 20; and her first Holy Communion, on the Feast

Mother Elizabeth Ann Seton 135 of the Annunciation, all in the year 1805. "At last God is mine and I am His," she exclaimed in happy abandonment to Divine Providence.

After Elizabeth's reception into the Catholic Church, persecution against her grew more violent and her position in New York became desperate. Providence arranged at this juncture that Father Dubourg, president of St. Mary's Seminary, Baltimore, should during a visit to New York City meet this saintly widow. On learning of her situation he suggested that she come to Baltimore and open a school for girls. Due consideration to this plan was given by the then Bishop Carroll, of Baltimore, and by Dr. Matignon and Father Cheverus of Boston, who were her advisers, and they finally advised her to proceed with it.

In the year 1808 Elizabeth Seton therefore bade farewell to her native city and embarked with her children on the "Grand Sachem" for Baltimore. They arrived June 16, the 

Feast of Corpus Christi. The following September she opened a school for girls in a modest red brick house on Paca Street in the shadow of St. Mary's Seminary. God blessed the work so abundantly that it was evident He intended its continuance. 

It was during this year in Baltimore that her life as a religious began. Since her husband's death her desires had all been in that direction, but the responsibility for five young children seemed to forbid the carrying out of such a plan. 

Father Dubourg, who for fifteen years had prayed earnestly that he might be able to found the Daughters of Charity of St. 

Vincent de Paul in this country, saw no reason why Elizabeth Seton, whose director he was, should not both carry out her desire for the religious life by beginning this foundation, and at the same time maintain the guardianship of her children.

Under the guidance of Archbishop Carroll and Father

Dubourg, the work of founding the Order in America was initiated. In May, 1809, the first tentative steps had been taken, a religious garb suggested, temporary rules adopted and 

Father Dubourg appointed superior. On Corpus Christi, June 2,

Elizabeth Seton and the little band of holy women associated with her wore publicly for the first time their religious garb: "a long black robe with a cape, and a white cap having a crimped border; a black band passed around the head and fastened beneath the chin." That same day, Elizabeth knelt before Archbishop Carroll and, in the presence of many of his priests, formally pronounced her first vows of poverty, chastity and obedience; receiving from the lips of that august prelate the title of "Mother" that she was henceforth to bear.

The Paca Street house soon proved to be too small for the growing school, and on June 21 Mother Seton and her Sisters moved to St. Joseph's Valley, Emmitsburg, Maryland, fifty miles from Baltimore. Because of the unfinished condition of their future home, they lived for six weeks in a small log hut on the mountainside, which had been placed at their disposal by the Reverend John DuBois. On July 31 they took possession of the "Stone House" in the valley - ever since termed the "cradle of the Community." Six months later Mother Seton opened her free school in the newly finished "White House." 

The work of the Community progressed rapidly in spite of sufferings and hardships. Affiliation with the Daughters of 

Charity in France, so much desired, was arranged at this time, but the French Sisters were prevented by the Napoleonic government from coming to America and the union was delayed. 

A copy of the French rules was obtained, however, and adopted for the time being.

Mother Elizabeth Ann Seton 137 For twelve years Mother Seton guided her growing Community, training her daughters in holiness and sending them forth as pioneers to lay foundations for works of mercy and charity. NNE-THERESE GUERIN, known in religion as Mother Theodore, Foundress of the Sisters of Providence of St.-Mary-of-the-Woods, Indiana, was born at Etables (Côtes du Nord), Brittany, France, on October 2, 1798. Her parents were highly respected for their integrity and piety. Although nothing extraordinary characterized her early life, Anne Thérèse was a devout child, given to self-sacrifice and prayer. 

She acquired her education chiefly from her cousin, a devout seminarian who, driven from his studies by the Revolution, awaited for several years in her home the opportunity of ordination to the priesthood. Under his tutelage, she became well versed in Church History, Sacred Liturgy, Christian Doctrine and Holy Scripture.

Anne Thérèse received her first Holy Communion at the age of ten years, and on that occasion resolved to consecrate herself to the service of God. But she was not so readily to attain her great desire, for when she was fourteen, her father's death brought poverty and in its train the burden of caring for her mother, an invalid for many years. Not until she was twenty-five was she able to enter the Community of the Sisters of Providence at Ruille-sur-Loir, in 1823. A year later, she received the religious habit and, by dispensation, made profession of vows, September 8, 1824, being on the same day ap- 143

pointed Superior of the convent at Rennes. After eight years of fruitful service at Rennes, she was transferred to Soulaines. 

In public recognition of her accomplishments as a teacher at Soulaines, the French government bestowed a decoration on her.

Severing all ties of kin and country, she set out for America with five companions, arriving at St.-

With what aid the bishop could give her, himself suffering poverty, she established her little Community in this wild, destitute and isolated region with but faint hope that an educational institution could be made to take root there. Yet within a year, by dint of brave and strenuous efforts, Mother Theodore had opened a boarding school for young girls. Great trials had to be endured and progress was slow; but her Community increased from year to year, and in the course of time she founded many new houses throughout the diocese before her death on May 14, 1856.

Mother Theodore was distinguished by boundless charity, a tender affection for Our Lady, a remarkable devotion to the holy angels, above all, an intense love for the Blessed Sacrament. Amid many tribulations, amid poverty, hardships, losses by fire, defection among the members, defamation of her institute (for seven years on the verge of dissolution), death of many of her most valued subjects, misunderstanding, persecution, she gave a perfect example of the religious life to her Community.

CHAPTER XVII

MOTHER MARY MAGDALEN BENTIVOGLIO (1834-1905) 

Foundress of the Poor Clares in the United States

CHAPTER XVII MOTHER MARY MAGDALEN BENTIVOGLIO FOUNDRESS OF THE POOR CLARES IN THE UNITED STATES NNA M. MAGDALEN BENTIVOGLIO was born on 

July 29, 1834, in Rome, to Count Domenico Bentivo glio and his wife Angela Sandri, a pious, zealous couple, who had the infant baptized the following day. The 

Bentivoglios were an ancient family of Bologna, proud of their name and their colorful history. Their child was destined to end her saintly and heroic life in a convent of Poor Clares in faraway America. 

The twelfth of sixteen children, Anna in her early youth was restless, fearless and highstrung. At the same time, “religious impressions touched her so deeply that the sight of the Coliseum and the stories told her of the martyrs who had suffered there bred in her the desire to do penance, that she too might imitate their sufferings and 'endure hardness for Christ.'" 

When she was not yet five years old, Anna's parents sent her to the Religious of the Sacred Heart, of which Community some of her older sisters were members. Here her education was begun. Great sorrow came to her at the age of seventeen in the death of her mother, and again nine years later when her father died.

Anna's beloved sister Constance was the first of the two to join the Poor Clares. It was not long before Anna followed, 151

and on October 4, 1865, the Feast of St. Francis of Assisi, she was solemnly professed as an Urbanist Poor Clare at San Lorenzo in Panisperna, Rome, taking the name of Magdalen. 

When Mother Ignatius Hayes petitioned for a foundation of the strict observance in connection with her convent (Third 

Order) in Belle Prairie, Minnesota, Pope Pius IX (July 1, 1875) selected the two sisters, Magdalen and Constance, to undertake this mission under the jurisdiction of the Minister General of the Franciscans. A friar was to accompany them as chaplain and counselor. On their journey, the Minister General at Nice canonically transferred them to the strict observance of the First Rule; Magdalen was appointed abbess of the foundation. 

Upon her arrival in New York, October 12, 1875, a way of the cross began for Magdalen. The chaplain, persuaded that the Minnesota foundation was not advisable, counseled her not to go there; he himself returned to Italy. But Magdalen's native tenacity kept her steadfast. She wrote to the Minister General, who suggested that she try to effect a foundation in the Diocese of New York. However, the Ordinary of New York did not welcome a foundation of contemplatives at that time. Since the Minister General had failed to recommend an alternative course, Mother Magdalen was left to her own resources. In the course of time she was even suspected of being an impostor, a situation all the more trying because she was penniless. 

Meanwhile, however, the Ordinary of Philadelphia had received her kindly, and on August 10, 1876, offered her a small house, which was furnished for her by friends. Mother 

Magdalen's joy was great; but when, on October 27, 1876, she applied for formal permission to make the foundation, in compliance with directions from Rome, she was informed that the

Mother Mary Magdalen Bentivoglio 153 diocesan council was opposed "because a convent such as yours was not in conformity with the spirit of the people." 

Together with Constance and a postulant, she found hospitality with the Religious of the Sacred Heart at Eden Hall, Torresdale. Here she made a private retreat, of which she wrote: "I have made all my meditations on the Will of God and on submitting myself to His Holy Will. Nothing could be better than that."

God's will manifested itself to Magdalen. At the invitation of the Archbishop of New Orleans, she went there to found a convent of Poor Clares, arriving on March 11, 1877. Suitable property with a house thereon was bought, and within a short time two more postulants were received. Meanwhile, the Minister General had placed the little Community under the jurisdiction of the provincial of the Sacred Heart Province of the Franciscans. On July 30, 1877, the provincial directed Mother 

Magdalen to leave New Orleans and to proceed to Cleveland, Ohio. She obeyed. There, on December 15, 1877, Colettine 

Poor Clares called from Holland by the provincial arrived at the convent, and Mother Magdalen, with her Community, was ordered to embrace the Colettine reform or to leave. With canonical permission, she left.

Mother Magdalen did not change her course. With the fixed purpose in mind of making a foundation of her Community, she first undertook the trying task of collecting funds. 

Informed that a foundation was possible in Omaha, Nebraska, she went there. Twice a storm wrecked her temporary convent. 

Finally the building was made fit to house the Community, and on March 5, 1882, the Sacred Congregation of the Propagation of the Faith permitted the erection of the monastery, subject

to the immediate jurisdiction of the Minister General. Mother 

Magdalen's perseverance had won. 

On June 16, 1885, the dauntless and holy woman made a second, and this time a successful attempt at a foundation in New Orleans. This became for her a source of great trials, because the Bishop of Omaha was displeased at it, even though she had the permission of the Minister General and of the 

Archbishop of New Orleans. 

Soon thereafter opposition arose within the Community against her and her sister. Accusations were made against their religious observance, their sobriety and even against their purity. 

The bishop, believing the charges, put the convent under interdict and urged that the two be secretly recalled to Rome. Authorities in Rome, having other information, directed that the bishop remove the interdict and conduct a judicial investigation. 

The principal accuser fled, instead of appearing before the tribunal; later it developed that she was insane. The Congregation of the Propagation of the Faith, having examined the proceedings of this tribunal, pronounced Magdalen and her sister innocent.

Many other trials were in store for Magdalen, as well as bodily infirmities, but she bore all, relying on God's grace, with an equanimity that was beyond natural powers. 

In 1897, although advanced in years and weak from illness, Mother Magdalen set out on what was to be her final work the third foundation of her Community, at Evansville, in the Diocese of Indianapolis, Indiana. She took three Sisters with her, leaving Constance in Omaha. Again, adversity went hand in hand with accomplishment. The Community suffered from actual hunger and want, but eventually their friends, understanding their plight, gave them practical assistance.

Mother Mary Magdalen Bentivoglio 155 There in Evansville, Mother Magdalen spent the remaining years of her life, and when the trials attending the establishment of the house were over, she found comparative peace. Her beautiful interior life with God became even more manifest. But there, too, perhaps her greatest sorrow befell her. In 1902, word arrived that Constance had died. It was not unexpected news, but for a little while it crushed the valiant spirit of Mother Magdalen. After a time her own infirmities became worse; she endured great physical pain and was obliged to undergo an operation, which because of her weakened heart had to be suffered without an anaesthetic.

It was on her deathbed that word came to Mother Magdalen that permission for a new foundation in Boston had been granted. 

On August 18, 1905, at the age of seventy-one, her great soul went to God. Like St. Francis she had begged to be lifted from bed so that she might die on the ground. When her 

Sisters knew that the last moments had come, they granted this wish and laid her on the floor. There death found her. Her body lies buried in the crypt of the monastery at Evansville.
