CHAPTER I INTRODUCTION 

This is a report of the activities of the Small Business Administration for the semiannual period ending June 30, 1955. 

This period completes the 2-year life term which was given to the Small Business Administration when it was established by the Small Business Act of 1953, enacted on July 30, 1953. This report not only describes the accomplishments of the Agency during the last 6-months period, but also reviews its accomplishments during the entire period of operation. 

This Agency was established as the first independent Government agency created to serve and represent all small business both in peacetime and in periods of national emergency. 

Its activities are designed to help small business: (1) To gain access to adequate capital and credit; (2) to obtain a fair share of Government procurement; and (3) to obtain competent management, technical and production counseling. 

Many of the programs conducted by the Small Business Administration were inherited from agencies which were formerly in operation. The authority to make business loans to small business concerns as well as loans to assist victims of floods and other disasters were powers formerly exercised by the 

Reconstruction Finance Corporation. This Agency also is charged with the broad powers and responsibilities of its prede cessor agency, the Small Defense Plants Administration, with respect to Government procurement and various other activities designed to assist small business and protect its interests. It is notable, however, that even with respect to these inherited functions of the Small Defense Plants Administration, the Small Business 

Administration has a greatly enlarged range of operations. From an area primarily confined to defense manufacturing and essential civilian industry under a mobilization economy, Congress expanded the range of our responsibilities to include all types of small business enterprises such as mining, construction, transportation, distribution, and service industries. 1

2 Many of the activities carried on by the Small Business Administration were not in existence previously and were initiated by this Agency. One of these programs is our Products Assistance 

Program, which is designed to help many small firms with product development problems. The development and introduction of new and improved products and processes are essential to the continued expansion of our national economy and maintenance of our high standard of living. This program has proven in a short period of time that it is an essentially valuable one to small firms in an effort to remain in a favorable competitive position. Since the start of the program in January 1954, more than 4, 700 requests for assistance have been received. Another program started by the Small 

Business Administration is the program of educational courses for small business owners and managers in schools and universities. 

This program was given a trial run during the spring of 1954 in co-operation with two educational institutions. The courses have been so successful that the program has been extended to include 54 courses in 40 universities during the 1954-55 school year. 

A new emphasis has been given to many other programs of the Agency, which has meant a decided improvement in programs previously conducted in the small business field. Our bank participation program is an example. To the greatest extent possible, the Small Business Administration strives to provide credit in co-operation with private lending institutions. Its success in this is evidenced by the fact that about two-thirds of all loans made by the Small Business Administration are in participation with private banks, with about 75 per cent of the Agency's participation loans on a deferred basis. This proportion of participation loans is far higher than that under the programs of the Reconstruction Finance Corporation.

Important objectives in any loan program are the reduction of time taken to process a loan, reduction of backlogs which make for delay, and elimination of unnecessary expense. Steps have been taken by the Small Business Administration to expedite actions on loans which can be approved. In July 1954, regional offices were given authority to approve loans up to $50, 000 under certain conditions; during this last period, the Small Business 

Administration authorized regional directors to approve direct and participation business loans up to $10,000 without the prior approval of the Washington office. It is estimated the recent delegation, coupled with the previous one, will account for about

one-third of the total volume of business loan applications. When the Small Business Administration began its lending operations on September 29, 1953, it took the Agency 45 to 60 days to complete action on loan applications. This period of time has now been reduced to 2 to 3 weeks.

A highly important factor in obtaining defense contracts for small firms has been the Joint Determination Program of the 

Small Business Administration and Department of Defense. This program has shown marked progress during the 2-year life of the Small Business Administration. In the last 6-months period, there were 2, 441 proposed military purchases, totaling $214, 044, 530, reserved for small business, as compared with 1, 105, totaling $118, 274, 535 in a comparable 6-months period a year ago. This is attributable in part to improved co-operation with the military services. A liaison officer appointed by the Small Business 

Administration participates at the planning level of the Defense 

Department in decisions made concerning small business activities. 

Progress in this program has also resulted from new instructions issued by the Defense Department in 1954 in response to proposals made by the Small Business Administration to strengthen the Joint 

Determination Program. 

Another achievement of this Agency has been the formulation of a definition for small business; unless small business is successfully identified, the benefits which are designed for small firms may actually be obtained by firms which Congress intended to exclude. The Small Business Administration worked out a definition of small business based on the variation in the size structure of manufacturing and other types of business activities and successfully applied this definition to the loan activities of the Agency. This was the first time that such a size definition was used by any agency in operating a loan program. The Small Business 

Administration is now engaged in discussions with other agencies of the Government with a view towards modifying the single 500employee standard now used in procurement, in view of the successful application of variable standards in the loan program. 

These are a few highlights of the achievements of this Agency program-wise during its existence. Details of many other programs carried on by this Agency are explained in this semiannual report. These include, among others, the financial counseling program, procurement counseling, publications in the management

aid field, direct technical and production assistance to small firms, aid to them in securing tax amortization benefits, and assistance to them in securing materials and equipment. All of these programs have shown an increased tempo of activity during this semiannual period, resulting in expanded benefits to small businesses. 

The general outlook for small business has improved substantially during the past 2 years. As explained in the following chapter on the "Economic Positión of Small Business, " small businesses now share in the economic expansion of the Nation; there has been a marked upturn in all the indexes recording their health. In previous years, the indexes showed a deterioration of the financial position of small, independent manufacturers compared with the larger manufacturing firms. However, in the Third Semiannual Report, it was reported that this long decline in net sales and earnings of small firms had been stopped and in the statistics analyzed in this Fourth Report, a sharp upturn in small business health is plainly indicated. Various policies of the Administration designed to ease the burdens on small business, including the complete revision of the tax laws, have been effective in promoting small business recovery.

However, there are new challenges and new frontiers ahead. As conscious as we are of the progress which has been made, we are equally conscious of some tasks ahead. They include: 1. 2. 

More effective co-operation between the 

Small Business Administration and other agencies so that we can act more effectively as an advocate of small business in the 

National Administration. This includes tax recommendations designed to provide further relief for small business and recommendations to the Federal Trade

Commission and the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice in the interest of giving more protection to small business. 

Aid by the Small Business Administration to open the door for small business to more equity capital. This includes not only education of the small business owner as to the opportunities for tapping the securities

markets, but also recommendations to the

Securities and Exchange Commission in order to modify burdensome requirements. 

3. Improving plans and procedures for channeling more loans of very small size to very small businesses, such as retailers and wholesalers. 

4. Improving relationships in making joint loans with development credit corporations, both local and State corporations, which are now enjoying a period of great expansion in the Nation. 

5. Increasing the small business share of procurement above the percentage now going to small firms. 

6. Formulation of a successful program that will enable small businesses to obtain a larger share of subcontracts where the prime contract goes to a large firm. 

7. Gearing our Products Assistance Program to aid small businesses in labor distress areas. 

These are among future goals of the Small Business 

Administration in the 2-year period ahead.

CHAPTER II ECONOMIC POSITION OF SMALL BUSINESS 

During the first half of 1955, the national economy was in a state of remarkable expansion. New records were registered in such indexes as gross national product, which reached a figure of $383 billion in the second quarter, compared with the previous high of $369. 3 billion in the second quarter of 1953. Other indexes, such as personal consumption expenditures, industrial and residential construction, and expenditures for new plant and equipment recorded new highs. Furthermore, there are few signs on the horizon foreshadowing any check during this year to the unparalleled economic growth which the Nation is now experiencing. 

As will be demonstrated later in this chapter, the economic position of small business has shown a marked improvement beginning with the final quarter of last year. It is a fundamental proposition that the position of small business improves during periods of peace and rising economic activity. Small business contributes a larger share of civilian goods and services than it does of defense goods and services. While national security outlays have declined, there has been an upturn in demand for civilian goods led by the record volume of new car purchases and the rising crest of the housing boom.

As a result of the continuing high level of economic activity, an increasing number of small businesses have solved their most urgent problems. Despite this development and the improvement in the economic position of small business, a number of problems which have faced small firms in previous periods continue. They include lack of facilities by small firms to engage in product research, reduced subcontract opportunities for small firms where Government prime contracts have been curtailed, the high cost of carrying inventory, pressure on small firms due to the increase in mergers, influx of imports which jeopardize domestic markets, and the chronic problem of surplus labor in sections which are undergoing economic readjustment.

A fresh analysis and review of small business problems indicates that in the future certain of them will be intensified. The growth of automation will place on small businesses an ever-greater financial burden if they are to acquire the more modern equipment and other facilities to keep up in the competitive race. Automation and atomic energy point up the need for highly specialized technical and professional personnel which small businesses will have difficulty in attracting. The guaranteed annual wage, if applied by unions to small businesses, may mean a backbreaking cost load which many will be unable to bear.

On the plus side of the ledger, small businesses are benefiting from the improved situation in the equity capital area. 

For all business, there has been a growing stream of equity financing in response to the general business improvement and increased optimism among investors. In addition, the tremendous rise in prices for "gilt-edged" stocks and established securities, together with the corresponding decline in yields, has increased investor interest in potential profit growth in small companies. 

The result has been to build a market for issues of small securities which had been lacking in previous years.

The threat of an inflationary movement has been posed by various economic developments such as the higher wage settlements in vital industries, the increased price of steel, the tight supply situation in such metals as copper and aluminum, and the general absence of slack in the economy. The long-run effects of these developments are particularly harmful to small businesses. They experience great difficulty in getting materials. Their material costs rise, but they are unable to raise their prices as readily as large businesses. The result is an attrition of profit margins, which were smaller in the first place. Steps taken by the National 

Administration to check any inflationary movement that might develop would be highly beneficial to the small business sector of the economy.

The Business Population 

There are about 4, 200, 000 businesses in the country. The number has remained fairly static during the past 2 years, with the establishment of new businesses being offset by business

discontinuances. During 1954, contract construction was the only major industry division to gain significantly in number of firms with an increase of 3 per cent. The number of manufacturing firms has declined slightly, while the number of concerns in the aggregate in the remaining industry divisions remains virtually unchanged. 

The total number of businesses in operation is considerably larger than at the end of World War II. Since that time, due largely to veterans establishing their own businesses, the number of business concerns has increased a third, or by more than a million enterprises.

The trend of business failures, which was visibly downward throughout most of 1954, leveled out during the first half of 1955. 

However, the number of failures through June 30 was slightly lower than in the comparable period of 1954. The weekly average through 

June 30 was 223, compared with a weekly average of 226 for the first half of last year. As shown in chart 3, another favorable note was that failures were substantially below the 1900-1954 average of 263.

With the exception of wholesale trade, in which the number of failures increased to 596 from 551, each major division of industry showed a slight decrease in the number of failures during the first half of 1955, as compared with the like period a year ago. 

The number of failures in the metalworking industries, which are included in the major division, manufacturing, increased to 245 from 241. It is hoped that the number of failures in this segment of manufacturing may decrease as small fabricators of metal products either succeed in developing products for civilian use or obtaining subcontracts from military prime contractors.

Financial Position of Small Business In the Third Semiannual Report of the Small Business 

Administration, it was stated that "the long decline in net sales and earnings of small firms appears to have been stopped. There is evidence that the trend of the past few years has finally been halted. " The latest analysis based on the quarterly financial reports of the Federal Trade Commission and Securities and Exchange Commission, which provide the most current data on the financial position of small business, confirms the statement. Moreover, it appears that not only has the downward trend been halted, but the financial position of small business has improved substantially as the trend line has moved upward. Small businesses are now sharing to a greater extent in the national prosperity. 

Beginning with the fourth quarter of 1954, quarterly earnings before and after taxes of United States manufacturing corporations have been higher than during the corresponding quarters of a year earlier. This is true of smaller as well as larger corporations. During the first quarter of 1955, corporations having

total assets of under $1 million reported earnings before taxes of $298 million. This was a very substantial increase over the $195 million they earned during the first quarter of last year. 

Earnings after taxes of the smaller corporations for the first quarter 1955 were $144 million compared to $72 million a year earlier. Earnings before Taxes

As stated above, earnings before taxes of manufacturers with assets under $1 million were substantially higher during the first quarter of 1955 than during the same quarter a year earlier.

What appears to be a new upward trend is shown in chart 6, page 19. 

Although it is too early to say that the earnings of these small corporations will continue to improve until they are relatively as high as during the base period years 1947-49, the outlook is encouraging.

In the last 6 months for which data are available, the earnings-before-taxes index of smaller corporations rose sharply to 69 from 60. This was a relatively greater increase than the index of the larger corporations, which rose to 139 from 131. 

Since many smaller corporations do not prepare quarterly reports, some of their expenses, such as depreciation, are charged off in the fourth quarter. Because of this, each quarterly earnings index number is based on average dollar earnings for four successive quarters. 

Earnings after Taxes

Earnings after taxes of the smaller corporations were twice as great in the first quarter of 1955 as during the same quarter of last year. True, they are still relatively much lower than those of the larger manufacturers during the base period years 1947-49. However, a more significant fact would seem to be that there is now definite evidence of a new trend upward in earnings after taxes of the smaller corporations.

Just as in the case of earnings before taxes, there was a sharp rise in the earnings-after-taxes index. On the basis of the most recent data available, the after-taxes index for the smaller corporations was up to 59 from 46 in a period of 6 months. For the larger corporations, the index was up to 110 from 101. 

Earnings on Stockholders' Equity 

Earnings on total stockholders' equity provide another comparison of earnings of large and small manufacturing corporations. Stockholders' equity is composed of the amounts of capital stock outstanding, earned surplus or undivided profits, surplus reserves and that portion of other reserves which is considered as surplus. 

Average annual earnings before taxes on stockholders' equity decreased for both larger and smaller corporations from the peak reached in 1950 into 1954. However, the decrease for smaller corporations was to 10. 4 from 26.3 per cent, whereas the larger corporations showed a relatively smaller decrease -dropping to 18.6 from 32.6 per cent. A similar comparison is indicated for earnings after taxes. 

As shown in chart 7, page 21, earnings of smaller corporations are now increasing at a rate greater than larger corporations.

Along with the rising demand for loans, the Federal Reserve System has been exercising a restraint on bank reserves so that there is a decreasing supply of loanable funds. Now, however, outstanding loans are higher and free reserves of banks are sharply down. In June, free reserves averaged $168 million compared with $712 million the year earlier.

It is expected that the seasonal upswing in the demand for money may be more intense in the last 6 months of the year. Inventories of durable goods manufacturers are expected to go up, along with spending for plant and equipment. The holiday retail selling will probably set a record. Ten per cent of the current year's estimated corporation income taxes will have to be paid this year instead of next. Crop loans will be particularly heavy.

Equity Capital for Small Business The improved health of the securities market is facili tating public financing on a larger scale than has occurred for more than 20 years. According to the Securities and Exchange

Commission, total public financing for the period January through May 1955 was $3, 018, 477, 000, compared with $2, 087, 785, 000 in the period January through May 1954, an increase of almost 50 per cent.

Common stock financing has been at a relatively low level during the postwar years. Between 1946 and 1953, stock financing provided only about one-tenth of new equity funds, compared with almost half of the new equity funds obtained by corporations in the 1920's. There has been a notable shift to retained earnings as a source of equity capital. In 1954, retained profits amounted to 41 per cent of all earnings in contrast to less than 30 per cent in the 1920's.

This high ratio of retained earnings has been attributable to the reluctance of owners of companies to raise money through security issues, as well as a desire to enhance the capital value of the companies by reinvesting the earnings instead of paying them out in dividends. Small companies have been less able than large companies to retain earnings to plow back for expansion, because their earnings have been smaller and because they have relatively less left after taxes.

The figures for smaller stock issues are in total far from impressive. Between January and May 1955, issues between $100,000 and $300, 000 totaled only $108, 734, 000. This was in

contrast to the $3, 018, 477, 000 in total public financing during the period. An encouraging feature, however, is that the $108, 734, 000 represents a striking increase from the $62, 647, 000 figure for small issues in the period January to May 1954. 

Undoubtedly, some of the increase is attributable to the rash of issues for uranium and other mining companies, although such issues would generally be below $100,000. The increase in issues by smaller companies is due not only to the improved tone of the securities market, but also to the lessened attractiveness of the better established issues to many investors. 

As the yield on Dow-Jones stocks has fallen below 4 per cent, and prices have gone up with a diminished chance for capital gains enhancement, many investors have looked with new interest to the capital gains prospects of smaller companies. Security houses have reported interest in small companies which show either (1) glamor; (2) record of earnings; or (3) growth. Among companies which have been able to secure financing in the public markets in the last few months are manufacturers of such items as prefabricated homes, lighting fixtures, bowling pins and porcelain enamel.

The ability to finance small issues under $300, 000 through a short form under Regulation A has been an important aid to small business financing. It is highly important for the owner of a worthy small business to have a cheap method of raising capital up to $300,000. In fact, there is continued pressure to raise the maximum limit to $500, 000. 

Abuses of Regulation A by certain uranium issues and other promotional excesses have resulted in a tightening of the regulation and even demands for its elimination. This tightening may affect adversely legitimate issues and may impair financing for the type of company which is a true growth company and from which the public may reap substantial benefits from investments. 

Even under Regulation A, the requirements are extremely difficult for small companies. Here is a case which has been brought to our attention: "The company reached agreement with the bankers for the public sale of its securities but was blocked by the new provision under the so-called Regulation A exemption which requires financial statements not less than 90 days old. Having a very complex inventory

of work-in-process, together with raw materials, this company has been closing its books and having full inventory audit only at the year end. Although the local /Securities and Exchange Commission/office advised that unaudited statements would be acceptable, the company's auditors, a firm of national prominence, refused to permit the company to use unaudited statements because of the implication that any figures which the company might issue during the interim would, in effect, carry the endorsement of the accounting firm. "Moreover, the company's legal counsel, a very large law firm with much experience in SEC matters, advised the directors that under no condition could they undertake the liability of using financial statements in any SEC filing which had not been carefully audited. penalty for an accounting error, even though inadvertent and honest, might be too severe. The company's public financing was abandoned. "'

Development Corporations 

.

The 

Statewide institutions known as development credit corporations or business development corporations have been established and are now functioning in the New England States. The fundamental purpose of these corporations is to pool loan funds advanced to them by financial institutions and then grant intermediate or long-term loans and other assistance to small businesses which are considered good credit risks but have been unable to obtain the money from other sources.

Enactment in the last session of the New York State 

Legislature of the New York Business Development Corporation is a stride forward in this area. Credit corporations raise money for operating purposes by capital stock. Although stockholders consist in most cases of members of the business community, such as manufacturers and utilities, New York, like Massachusetts, will permit banks to become stockholders. Financial institutions become members and agree to supply funds as called for by the corporation up to a specified "loan limit. " This is a low percentage; in New 

York, it will be only 2 per cent of each member's capital and surplus. In addition to the individual loan limit, the amount that a corporation can borrow from members is limited to a multiple of the amount of stocks sold. The New York charter sets the limit at 

10 to 1, or $50 million, whichever is greater.

The extent of help to small business by the New England development corporations is underlined by the fact that more than 90 per cent of the firms that have been aided have fewer than 350 employees, and more than 80 per cent fewer than 100. The average size of loans has been moderate. The mean average size has been $20,000, while that in Massachusetts has been around $100, 000. 

Loans are often made on a participation basis with either banks in the community or with the local industrial foundations. 

The great interest which has been evidenced in development corporations and the plans which have been made by various other State legislatures to establish them give promise that they may function all over the country in the years to come. 

The Small Business Administration is anxious to co-operate actively with such corporations, as well as with community industrial foundations. For example, the Agency extended a loan to the 

DeQueen Corporation of DeQueen, Arkansas, for the construction of a poultry processing plant. More recently, a 10-year loan was made to Dadeville Industries, Inc., of Dadeville, Alabama, with a local bank taking a 25 per cent participation in the loan. 

The pur pose of the loan was to aid the community in completing the con- · struction and equipping of a building to be leased to a private firm engaged in manufacturing men's and boys' sport shirts. In time, the plant will provide employment for 300 persons.

There are many reasons for believing that the application of the guaranteed annual wage will be limited in American industry. Insofar as small business is concerned, the wage plan is highly impracticable.

A more pressing problem insofar as a segment of small business is concerned is the raising of the minimum wage to $1 an hour. The greater trend toward uniformity in wage rates is a new, important factor in the competition between large and small businesses. Mergers

The economic position of small business is affected by any pronounced rise and fall in merger activity. The recent study of the Federal Trade Commission is a significant contribution to our knowledge of the effect on small business of recent corporate mergers and acquisitions. In its study of 1, 773 mergers and acquisitions between 1948 and 1954, the Federal Trade Commission found that nearly two-thirds of these acquisitions were made by companies with assets of $10, 000, 000 or more. In contrast, companies with assets of less than $1, 000, 000 accounted for less than 8 per cent.

The largest number of acquisitions during 1948-54 were in the nonelectrical machinery industry, with 249 mergers, and in the food products industry, with 243. Together, they accounted for more than one-fourth of the mergers in manufacturing and mining. 

The next 10 industry groups in number of mergers were: Chemicals, 168; fabricated metals, 161; transportation equipment, 125; textiles and apparel, 117; electrical machinery, 111; nonmanufacturing, 96; mining, 81; primary metals, 78; stone, clay and glass, 70; and paper and allied products, 60.

The Federal Trade Commission found that strictly competitive factors are not the only forces underlying acquisition. The 

Commission reported that "when many acquisitions by larger companies are examined, it is often found that the lesser ability of smaller companies to command financial resources needed for expansion or modernization is one of a number of factors which cause the selling company to take the initiative in offering to sell. " 

Insofar as the efforts of the Small Business Administration in behalf of small business are successful; the tax laws and governmental controls and regulations are changed to help small business; and greater helath of small business is achieved, the trend toward mergers will be arrested.

Imports 

There have been many complaints by small businesses about the threat of competition from imports. A highly dramatic instance has been that of the imports of oil, which is a matter of special interest to small business. The production of foreign oil is dominated by a few large companies (seven companies control 85 to 90 per cent of the free world supply outside of the United States). 

In contrast, approximately 12,000 companies, partnerships and individuals, are engaged in domestic oil production. The maintenance of a growing oil industry in the United States is vital to national defense and to the competitive position of thousands of small business units.

Under the Trade Agreements Act of 1955, the National 

Administration was authorized, whenever the Director of the Office of Defense Mobilization has reason to believe that any article is being imported into the United States in such quantities as to threaten to impair the national security, to adjust the imports of such article to a level that is deemed proper. This legislative

authorization to the President to limit imports does not mention petroleum, however, since Congress had before it several proposals on commodities such as fluorspar, lead and zinc.

CHAPTER III FINANCIAL ASSISTANCE 

During the 6-months period January 1 to June 30, 1955, the

Small Business Administration approved 523 business loans in a gross amount of $24, 313, 080 to assist small firms. In the Disaster Loan 

Program, 273 loans amounting to $3, 139, 053 were approved. For this fiscal year, four times as many loans were approved as during the first fiscal year.

To date, the Small Business Administration has approved 

1,645 business loans for a gross amount of $83, 713, 513, and 1, 243 disaster loans totaling $8,614,587.

In carrying out the Financial Assistance Program, the Small Business Administration has developed close co-operation with the banks of the Nation. To date, banks in nearly every State of the 

Union, as well as in Alaska, the District of Columbia and Hawaii, have made loans to small business concerns with varying percentages of participation by this Agency. The extent of success in this objective is reflected by the fact that over 66 per cent of all business loans approved were in participation with banks. Through these participation loans and through direct loans by the Small Business Administration, more than $83, 000, 000 of credit has been made available to small firms, The $83,000,000 in approved loans does not include financing obtained through private lending channels for small firms resulting from the Agency's Financial Counseling Program. 

The Small Business Administration has placed emphasis on attempting to have as many of its loans as possible in participation with banks. Aside from the requirements contained in the Small 

Business Act, participation-type loans are mutually advantageous to the small businessman, the participating bank and Small Business Administration. A bank participation loan provides the small business borrower the opportunity of building up a satisfactory credit record with the bank and after the loan is repaid the bank has a firmer basis for extending necessary credit without enlisting the aid of the Government, Thus the bank participation-type loan is of more assistance to the small business than a direct Government loan. Of interest to the taxpayer is the fact that a majority of the Agency's share in the 37

participation loans probably never will be withdrawn from the Treasury and the cost of processing such loan applications is offset by interest received. In contrast, direct loans require immediate disbursement of Federal funds. Of benefit to the bank, in the participation-type loan, is its assurance of a fair return on money loaned, a broadening of its lending activity while maintaining liquidity, and ability to give better service to its depositors.

Congress has appropriated $80, 000, 000 for the Small Business Administration revolving fund. As stated above, business loans have been approved for a gross amount of $83, 713, 513. 

Approved loans exceed the $80, 000, 000 in the revolving fund because a portion of the amount approved is the banks' share in participations; because loan repayments can be used to make additional loans; and because approved loans have been canceled at the borrower's request prior to disbursement. In the case of many of these cancellations, the small firm was able to obtain the loan from a bank after the Small Business Administration had approved the firm's loan application. 

Financial Counseling 

Experience has shown that small businesses which encounter financial difficulties generally think first of borrowing funds, failing to recognize other possible courses of action which would eliminate the need for incurring debt. As a rule, these small enterprises are not able to employ specialists in financial management. It is in assisting these firms that the Agency's Financial Counseling Program is most effective.

Whenever possible, financial specialists in the Small Business Administration field offices assist small firms to solve their financial problems by pointing out solutions which do not involve intermediate or long-term borrowing. Often a firm's problem is undercapitalization, or perhaps a need for inventory liquidation, revised credit policies or changes in product pricing principles. In other cases, a contemplated expansion may be premature or too extensive, the firm's product line may be too diversified or too restricted, or its sales policies may need review.

These are only a few of the possible factors considered by the Agency's specialists in counseling small firms which have financial problems.

Where a firm does need intermediate or long-term credit or short-term working capital for general business purposes or normal

growth, the Small Business Administration often can assist it in preparing a more effective request for funds to a prospective investor or financial institution.

By providing advice and counsel on financial problems, facilitating transactions with private lending institutions, and giving other specialized services, the Small Business Administration has been able to assist many small firms. Exactly how many have benefited from the program is not known since the results of any advisory service are difficult to measure. On the other hand, many concrete examples of aid provided, such as the following, indicate that the program has far-reaching benefits: 

1. A young chemical engineer requested assistance in buying an interest in a plastic company for which he worked. The 

Small Business Administration was unable to provide the needed financing, but suggested that the engineer might be able to arrange with the company for the establishment of an escrow account through which he could acquire company stock over a period of time. Both the engineer and the company agreed to this arrangement. 

2. Because of destruction of a fruit crop by a spring freeze, a manufacturer of fruit-packing equipment found himself faced with no payment from growers for cooling devices sold to them on the basis of the 1955 crop. The Small Business Administration suggested that, since the manufacturer's customers were financially responsible persons, their local banks might discount or advance funds against the deferred payment notes. A week later, the manufacturer advised the 

Agency that the problem had been solved in this way. 

3. Operators of a granite quarry were hard pressed for working capital because of expenditures for fixed assets. Their bank declined to supply the needed funds because the amount was greater than it cared to risk on one borrower. A Small Business Administration financial specialist suggested that the local bank try to interest another bank in sharing in the loan, This was done and the two banks supplied the applicant's needs on a share basis without the Agency's help, 4. A small shoe repair and hat cleaning operator required $1,000 to purchase additional inventory. Through the Small Business 

Administration's efforts, a bank which held a first mortgage on the business agreed to increase the mortgage, thus solving the businessman's problem.

5. A banker in a small Illinois town inquired about Agency participation in a loan to one of his customers, an operator of a restaurant and a greenhouse. A Small Business Administration financial specialist discussed the problem and found that the businessman was overextended because he was trying to handle too much business. The specialist suggested that the businessman consolidate his position by disposing of the greenhouse. He did so, and his financial difficulties were solved. 

6. A pre-award survey of a low bidder on an Army contract showed an extended financial condition. Through the efforts of the 

Small Business Administration, the firm's bank advised the Army that it would hold available for processing of the contract a "V" loan credit, which it agreed to renew with the Federal Reserve bank, The firm then was awarded the contract. 

7. A small manufacturer of portable folding seats expanded rapidly, and as a result needed additional financing. The Small 

Business Administration found that a need for equity capital was indicated. However, the owner feared that by issuance of capital stock he would lose control of the business. A regional financial specialist of the Agency met with the applicant's banker, auditor and two principal creditors, with the result that $50,000 in capital stock was subscribed in a few days without the owner losing voting control of the firm. 

8. A manufacturer of model airplane motors in need of working capital sought aid from the Agency. The regional financial specialist conferred with the manufacturer and the vice president of a bank, and, as a result, the bank furnished the necessary working capital without Small Business Administration participation. 

9. A concrete products company which had obtained a $25,000 loan from the Small Business Administration sought additional working capital to purchase lightweight aggregate from a new source at a savings of some $5 per ton over its existing sources. Factoring of accounts receivable and inventories had proved unsuccessful so the Small Business Administration suggested the possibility of using field warehousing, The applicant contacted a field warehousing firm, a storage agreement was signed, and funds are being provided by the local bank. 

10. A wholesale fruit and vegetable distributor in the Northwest, who had developed a large volume of business, was in need of working capital. However, change in the management of his bank of

account had resulted in a substantial reduction in his credit at the bank, With the wholesaler's consent, the Agency's regional financial specialist discussed his problem with the bankers. The result was a better understanding between the bank and the wholesaler and the reestablishment of the former line of credit, Lending Program The lending program of the Small Business Administration is divided into two main parts as set forth in Sections 207(a) and 207(b) 

of the Small Business Act of 1953. 

Section 207(a) of the Act gives the Small Business Adminis tration authority to make loans to small business enterprises when credit is not otherwise available on reasonable terms. The loans are intended to fill a gap in the financing provided small firms by private financial institutions and are designed to stimulate and preserve the initiative, independence and enterprise of small businesses. To the greatest extent possible, the Small Business Administration is striving to provide this credit in co-operation with private lending institutions. Its success in this is evidenced by the fact that about two-thirds of all loans approved are in participation with banks and other financial institutions.

In order to expand financial assistance to small business concerns and to promote a balanced national economy, the bank-Small Business Administration participation loan plan offers the greatest possible benefit to the participating institution consistent with the intent of the Small Business Act. For example, the plan enables a bank to broaden its lending activities, while maintaining desired liquidity of assets. The bank is assured a fair return on money loaned and is able to give better service to its depositors. 

Section 207(b) of the Small Business Act of 1953 gives the 

Agency authority to make loans to assist owners of homes and small businesses which have been damaged or destroyed by storms, floods, and other natural disasters, Loans can be made only to the extent of actual losses not covered by insurance and, as in the case of regular business loans, cannot be approved if the required financing is otherwise available on reasonable terms. 

Since the disaster loans are intended as a rehabilitation measure, much more liberal credit standards are applied to them than would be prudent for the Agency's business loans, Interest rates, too, are lower.

In the Disaster Loan Program, there is close co-operation with the Red Cross, and the Small Business Administration refers to it cases in which there seems no possibility of repayment of a Government loan, if one were made. On the other hand, the Red Cross refers to the Agency cases where repayment of a loan seems possible. 11 In the administration of its lending program under Section 

207(a) of the Small Business Act, the Small Business Administration is governed by the policies established by the Agency's Loan Policy Board and the requirement of the Act that "loans be of such sound value or so secured as reasonably to assure repayment. 

The Agency applies the most liberal interpretation of these policies and require ments possible, consistent with sound credit principles, but at the same time is fully aware of its responsibility to protect Government funds. Therefore, each loan application is thoroughly analyzed and a loan is never approved or declined without full consideration of all factors concerned. The Agency's lending program is under continual study, and the area in which credit can be provided safely is being constantly reviewed.

Basic Loan Policy The Small Business Administration has recently authorized regional directors to approve direct and participation business loans up to $10,000 without prior approval of the Washington office. Prior to this additional delegation of authority, the regional directors could approve only business loans up to $50,000 in which private lending institutions were sharing in at least 25 per cent of the total amount of the loan. It is estimated that the recent delegation, coupled with the previous one, will cover about one-third of the total volume of business loan applications and action on the balance of loan applications handled by the Washington office should be greatly expedited.

One of the Agency's principal concerns in the operation of the Financial Assistance Program has been inability to make loan funds available, to the degree desired, to the small retailer, wholesale distributor and service establishment. These concerns usually have very little in the way of tangible collateral which they can pledge for a loan; on the other hand, they may have a good earnings record, competent management and a reputation with local banks for meeting obligations satisfactorily. They are genuinely small businesses and are deserving of assistance in meeting their working capital needs and requirements for expansion and modernization.

CHAPTER IV GOVERNMENT PROCUREMENT 

During the past 6-months period, the Small Business Administration's program to ensure for small firms a fair share of the Government's purchases continued to increase in effectiveness. 

In the Joint Determination Program conducted by this Agency and the Department of Defense, both the number and dollar amount of military purchases reserved for competitive award to small business increased substantially. During fiscal year 1955, the Agency obtained Defense 

Department agreement to reserve 3, 924 proposed purchases totaling $386,610,589 for award to small firms; this compares with 1, 621 purchases amounting to $228, 504, 359 earmarked for small business in the '11 months from August 1, 1953, when this Agency began operations to June 30, 1954. Other phases of the Small Business Administration's Procure ment Assistance Program similarly have made good progress. The major difference between joint determinations agreed to and the resulting contract awards is due to the time lag between the making of a joint agreement and award of the contract by the procurement agency. The time lag is especially long on larger procurements.

In most instances, the Small Business Administration is receiving good co-operation from military purchasing offices. Such co-operation is essential in obtaining for small firms a fair share of Government business, since, under the authority given the Agency in the Small Business Act of 1953, it can only recommend, not demand, that suitable procurements be restricted to small business. 

Unfortunately, there still are areas of disagreement between the Small Business Administration and the military services concerning what specific procurements can properly be earmarked for small business. The main reason given by some contracting officers in rejecting proposed joint determinations is unwillingness to restrict entire procurements to small firms because they believe that large firms might bid slightly lower than small business suppliers. When this occurs, the Agency's representatives then request that a portion of the procurement, large enough to insure an economical production run, be restricted to small business. Since small business bidders would have to meet the price at which the advertised portion of the procurement was awarded, or in case of multiple awards, the weightedaverage price, the Government could not possibly pay a higher unit price for the restricted portion. However, a drawback to this procedure is that a partial joint determination often is not appropriate, since many procurements are too small to be divided into more than one economical production run.

Another area of disagreement concerns research and development contracts. Although many of these contracts are well suited for performance by small business, the Small Business Administration has had difficulty convincing military contracting officers that procurements of this type should be restricted to small firms. During the past 6 months, however, some Navy contracting officers have agreed to joint determinations on research and development procurements, and it is hoped that Army and Air Force procurement officials will do likewise in the future.

In the opinion of many observers, small firms fare better on formally advertised than on negotiated procurements. Consequently, whenever possible, the Small Business Administration is encouraging and assisting contracting officers to adopt firm specifications on

procurements, so that the formally advertised technique of buying can be used more broadly. 

The Small Business Administration has noted with concern that large firms are bidding to an increasing extent on items and services previously furnished largely by small businesses. To compensate for this, officials of the Agency, the Department of Defense, and the Army, Navy and Air Force are now developing a program which should result in small firms receiving a greater share of the military business which is suitable to their facilities. 

The earmarking of suitable procurements for competitive bidding among small business concerns is only one of the benefits of having Small Business Administration representatives at military procurement centers. In addition, these Agency representatives assist small firms in obtaining the opportunity to bid on specific procurements; advise and assist them in general in selling to the Govern ment; assist them in presenting grievances to procurement officials; and serve to offset the tremendous advantage enjoyed by large firms which have agents who regularly visit the procuring installations. 

Small Business Administration representatives also serve as spokesmen for small business in top-level conferences at the purchasing centers. 

A main objective of the Small Business Administration is to increase the number of items that purchasing officers will restrict to small business competition. One way in which this goal can be attained is by making certain that military bidders lists include a sufficient number of established or potential small business suppliers capable of producing the items to be purchased. The Agency's programs of providing additional small business sources of supply where greater competition is needed, and of making selective referrals of procurement opportunities to small firms, are resulting in greatly increased small business competition. This objective ties in admirably with the program of the Defense Department to maintain a sound mobilization base and to achieve widespread dispersal of its suppliers. 

The Small Business Administration also plans to extend the Joint Determination Program to other military installations and to the more important Federal civilian agency purchasing offices.

Procurement Assistance Counseling Program 

In its counseling program, the Small Business Administration helps small firms determine which items or services they can supply the Government, and assists them in requesting inclusion on the appropriate bidders lists. In addition, by agreement with the Defense 

Department, the Agency may recommend additional small business concerns for inclusion on the list of bidders to be solicited for any specific procurement which is suitable for performance by small business.

Upon request, small firms can receive from the Small Business Administration notification of suitable procurement opportunities in both the prime and subcontract fields. The Agency also informs the firms as to how bid sets, specifications, and drawings relating to the purchases may be obtained or where they are available for inspection. In this connection, each Small Business Administration field office has available for reference and estimating purposes a set of packing and packaging specifications especially selected to fill the needs of small firms.

Here are a few typical comments by the Agency's regional offices regarding the Procurement Assistance Counseling Program: 

"As a result of our counseling service, more and more firms are getting on bidders lists with the view not to support their business with Government work, but to find those items on which they can successfully compete and which will serve as stopgap work to shore-up their production . to provide production work during slack seasons and to enable them to hold their key working force intact."'

"An increasing number of small firms are being referred to our office for procurement counseling by individuals who have received counseling in the past. 

Word-of-mouth advertising by satisfied 'customers' seems to be very effective. A growing number of small firms also are getting on bidders lists as a result of our counseling. Many small firms that are trying for Government business the first time were previously reluctant to do so. They believed that 'red tape' in doing business with the Government was insurmountable. Counseling to dispel this belief is a real service, "This office has become a clearinghouse for information on Government procurement. Numerous telephone calls and personal visits are received requesting advice on pending procurements. In many cases, we read over the bid forms with prospective bidders, pointing out various clauses of importance. An explanation and simple discussion enables them to clearly understand what is expected under the bid terms and, therefore, results in better estimates and bids to the Government. "

There are many facets to this type of assistance. For instance, one small manufacturer was saved from incurring a loss on a military contract when an Agency staff member pointed out that the manufacturer had erred in his interpretation of the procurement requirements. Another small manufacturer, who had made 5,000 too many knitted cuffs on a Government contract, was able to dispose of the surplus when our representatives informed him of a new Government purchase on which he bid and obtained the award. Prime Contract Referral Program 

A Small Business Administration service of great value to small firms is notification to them of procurement opportunities suitable to their facilities. In this program, the Small Business Administration field offices review bidding forms, drawings, and specifications received from purchasing offices to determine whether or not small firms in their areas can supply the items. Firms whose facility records, as filed with the Small Business Administration, indicate that they can furnish the items are notified of the procurement opportunities by use of a standardized "Procurement Notice," which presents detailed information about the proposed purchases. 

The co-operation of other Federal agencies has led to increased effectiveness of this program. Military and civilian agencies provide 

Small Business Administration field offices with current invitations for bids, which the offices then bring to the attention of small firms. For example, the General Services Administration forwards to the Small Business Administration regional offices information on all purchases to be made by General Services Administration in their areas. 

The Foreign Operations Administration (International Cooperation Adminis tration) furnishes Small Business Administration field offices with similar advance information on export opportunities under programs financed by that Agency.

Small Business Administration regional and branch offices also obtain current procurement information from Government purchasing installations in their areas. These local procurements are especially suitable to smaller firms, many of which are unaware of the sales potential in local Federal purchases. 

The over-all objective of the Prime Contract Referral Program is to acquaint small firms with the necessary steps in competing for Government business and to furnish them with opportunities to bid until they have obtained listing on appropriate bidders lists. 

Accordingly, at the time that it notifies a small firm of a procurement opportunity, the Small Business Administration also advises it to request placement on the bidders list for the particular item or

service. The Agency also includes with the notice a leaflet which outlines important points in selling to the Government. Referrals pertaining to a specific item being purchased by a specific purchasing office are discontinued after the small firm has had ample time to be placed on the office's bidders list.

A problem faced by Government procurement offices is the difficulty of determining how many bidding forms and prints to reproduce when making a particular purchase. When a small firm reports to the Small Business Administration that it is unable to obtain a bid set, the Agency invites the firm to inspect the bidding forms at one of its regional or branch offices, or, if the Agency does not itself have the forms, suggests other Government offices where they may be available for inspection. At times, insufficient copies of prints or drawings restrict the opportunity for small firms to bid on a particular purchase. This plan has proved advantageous both to small firms desiring to bid on the purchases and to the purchasing offices themselves. 

A method used by some military offices to overcome the problem of insufficient copies of bid sets is that of (1) providing advance notice of procurements to firms on their bidders list and (2) publishing notices of the proposed purchase in the Department of Commerce publication, "Synopsis of U. S. Government Proposed 

Procurement, Sales and Contract Awards," All requests for bid sets

received within 10 days of the notice are honored. This plan gives firms adequate advance notice for requesting bid forms and enables the purchasing office to prepare the proper number of bid sets to satisfy the demand from bidders. However, the procedure has been used only sparingly by procuring agencies. 

Insufficient Lead Time Between Issuance of Invitations for Bid or Requests for Proposal and the Opening or Closing Date 

Military instructions to contracting officers are that they "endeavor to allow a minimum of 30 days from date of issue of invitations for bid or requests for proposal to opening or closing date on all procurements, "

Despite this, invitations for bid or requests for proposal frequently provide insufficient lead time to enable potential new suppliers, who are not on the bidders lists, to obtain bid sets and prepare and submit their bids or proposals. West Coast firms, because of their greater distance from many of the large purchasing offices, constantly complain of inadequate lead time. These firms are given all possible information concerning the procurements by the Small Business Administration field offices and urged to request placement on the bidders list so as to receive appropriate bidding forms on future procurements direct from the purchasing offices. 

This will eliminate the time now required for the firms to request and obtain bid sets.

In a few cases which have been brought to the attention of the Small Business Administration, invitations for bid were not actually mailed to firms on the bidders list until several days after the date of issuance shown on the bidding form. These cases, and others in which the procuring agency has provided exceptionally short lead time, are brought to the attention of procurement officials by the Agency.

Subcontracting Program 

The reduction in military spending, coupled with expansion of their own facilities, has caused many prime contractors to curtail their subcontracting programs. The present tendency among prime contractors is to keep work in their own plants or to place a larger volume with affiliated plants or subsidiaries. This tendency may be encouraged by the weapons system concept of procurement, whereby the prime contractor is assigned responsibility for delivery of the complete end-product. Under this concept, some items of equipment

formerly purchased by the Government directly from component manufacturers are now being purchased by the prime contractor. 

The Small Business Administration has an active Subcontract ing Program designed to increase the volume of subcontract awards to small firms. The program is carried on through direct contact with prime contractors to determine which items they plan to subcontract and to refer to them qualified small firms with suitable subcontracting facilities. An effective subcontracting program is primarily dependent upon personal contacts with prime contractors in behalf of specific subcontractors on definite contracts. The 

Agency's efforts in this regard have been handicapped somewhat by lack of personnel.

This approval is subject to the concurrence of the Attorney General and the Chairman of the Federal

Production pools consist primarily of groups of small concerns voluntarily associated together to obtain and jointly perform contracts for the production of articles, equipment, supplies, and materials or for the furnishing of services needed for the defense effort. The firms have a mutual agreement as to their organization, relationship, and procedure.

Although interest in the formation of small business production pools has declined considerably, the Agency has under consideration at present two applications for approval of proposed pools. Inventory of Small Plant Facilities The Small Business Administration's facilities inventory is a nationwide plan for registering the productive facilities of small companies. Some 30, 000 firms now have supplied the Agency with data on their productive capacity. In the event of a national emergency, the present inventory would serve as a basis for rapidly increasing the number of registered plants.

Small concerns which express interest in being registered in the inventory are furnished a questionnaire by the Agency. Upon return of the completed questionnaire, Agency staff members carefully examine and classify the information according to type of facilities indicated. The entire method of operation is on a decentralized basis, with the regional and branch offices of the Agency working directly with the companies involved.

The facilities inventory provides a means for obtaining quick, accurate operating data on small plants, with particular emphasis on capacity, machine tools, number of employees, type of products, and other essential manufacturing elements. Such information can then be analyzed to determine a manufacturer's industrial potential, including the type of product which he can manufacture to meet mobilization requirements.

Field offices of the Agency have been delegated authority to collaborate with State and local governments, chambers of commerce, and trade associations in the establishment and expansion of facility registrations. Considerable additional information also has been collected from the Department of Commerce and Federal procurement agencies.

Some principal uses of the facilities inventory include (1) assisting the registered companies to obtain prime and subcontracts, (2) locating new sources of supply for the military services, and (3) locating scarce or specialized machine tools and equipment. 

As of June 30, 1955, there were 30, 129 small companies of all kinds registered with the Agency. This compares with approximate ly 27, 000 at the end of the last semiannual period. At present, the 

Agency is making a special effort to register small plants which are located away from metropolitan areas since these are less likely to be damaged in the event of attack.

Inability of small business concerns to obtain or review copies of Federal and military specifications needed in bidding on Government purchases continues to be a serious problem. Many

small firms have reported to Small Business Administration field offices that bid sets are sent without specifications; that in other instances the basic specification alone is included, while the applicable specifications are omitted; and that in still other cases the supply of specifications is frequently exhausted at procurement centers.

The Small Business Administration has continued to increase the amount and variety of specifications assistance it provides small concerns. The Agency has placed in its Washington and field offices 

4-volume reference sets of packaging and packing specifications considered to be of most value to small concerns in selling to the Government.

The reference sets are helpful to Small Business Administration field offices in assisting small concerns with their packaging and packing problems. At the same time, the sets provide small concerns with a ready means of studying the most commonly used packaging specifications, eliminating the inconvenience of writing to multiple sources for the data. 

As an additional convenience to small concerns, Small Business Administration field offices maintain files of Government specifications compiled from expired bid sets, and make the se limited stocks available to small firms which have need for them. 

"The U. S. Surplus Labor Areas 

The Small Business Administration has continued to receive requests for assistance from areas with surplus productive labor. 

All of the Agency's assistance to these areas is provided through its field offices. The Washington office coordinates the program of plant facilities surveys and assists field office personnel in their work with individual small business concerns in the affected areas. 

When the Agency receives a request for assistance to small plants located in a surplus labor area, a representative of the appropriate regional office visits the area to obtain information on all plants having facilities which qualify them for defense work. 

Companies which are qualified to perform defense contracts are advised by the Agency's representatives of the steps they should take to be placed on appropriate Government bidders lists. The staff member also notifies the Small Business Administration representatives or, where there are no Agency representatives, the Defense Department small business specialists at appropriate military procurement centers, of the qualifications of the companies and the types of bids or proposals they wish to receive.

As another part of the program, Agency staff members interview and encourage prime contractors to award subcontracts to small firms in labor surplus areas. The Small Business Administration also maintains files of information on individuals who are seeking to establish new small businesses, to move going concerns, or to obtain additional facilities. These cases are referred to the regional offices which are serving surplus labor areas, with a request that the offices give all possible help in regard to them. 

In the area of financial assistance, applications for loans from small firms located in surplus labor areas are given priority in processing. Assistance also is provided to small firms which have filed for certificates of necessity under the Government's accelerated tax amortization program.

In reply to requests for assistance to surplus labor areas, the Agency's field production and industrial specialists made 94 surveys in the period January 1 through June 30, 1955. In most instances a study was made of the small business concerns in the area, and the companies were given assistance and information in regard to Government procurements suitable to their facilities. Agency staff members also suggested ways in which the firms could increase the efficiency of their operations, expand their market possibilities, diversify their products, and in other ways expand and absorb surplus productive labor of the area.

The Agency's Cleveland Regional Office has initiated trial small business clinics in surplus labor areas. At these clinics, representatives of the armed services procurement agencies, the chambers of commerce in the area, the U, S. Department of Commerce, and other organizations, meet with small business owners and managers. 

All phases of defense procurement, subcontracting possibilities, and development of new products, as well as operating practices and management, are discussed as a basis for expanding the operations of the small firms. If the clinics prove helpful, they may be expanded by the Agency to other areas.

Certificates of Competency The Certificate of Competency Program of the Small Busi ness Administration benefits small companies by helping them obtain prime contracts which otherwise would be denied them. A certificate of competency is a statement from the Administrator of the Agency addressed to the contracting officer of a Government procurement agency certifying that the small company named therein is qualified to perform a specific contract on which it has submitted a bid. 

If a small company or a group of small companies is the low bidder, or within negotiation range, on a proposed Government contract, but is being rejected because of a question of credit or capacity, or both, the company or group may apply to the Agency for a certificate. A comprehensive field survey of the plant and production facilities, including an evaluation of the management's manufacturing experience, technical ability and financial capabilities, is made. 

If the Agency finds that the bidder actually is qualified to perform the contract, a certificate is issued. The certificate must be accepted as conclusive by the procurement officer, as far as technical and financial requirements of the contract are concerned.

After a certificate has been issued and the contract awarded, the Small Business Administration makes a systematic follow-up in order to assist the firm should it encounter difficulties in maintaining production schedules. Through periodic visits by Agency staff members, it has been possible in a number of instances to anticipate or detect problems before they become serious, The authority of the Agency to certify a small business concern or production pool as financially and technically competent to perform a Government contract has proved to be a valuable aid in increasing the small business share in Government procurement. As of June 30, 1955, the Agency had received 231 applications covering procurements totaling $44, 572, 036 and had granted 107 certificates.

Through 89 of these certifications, small firms had received contracts totaling $9,073, 821. Of the contracts, 29 have been completed, including 14 which were completed ahead of schedule. The following table indicates the source and disposition of certificate applications:

Management Publications 

Every small business manager must constantly seek ways to make his business more efficient, in order for it to remain successful.

The penalty for not doing so is loss of competitive strength and eventual elimination. To improve requires new awareness, new knowledge, new action, all based on new information. However, many owners and managers of small concerns find it difficult to locate and analyze the information they need to keep pace with their larger rivals. This the Small Business Administration does for them through its management publications. 

The January 1-June 30, 1955, period showed a growing use of the Agency's publications, both for management education and individual counseling purposes. 

Five distinct, but related, series of Small Business

Administration publications are currently offered to small business operators. Four are distributed free and one is sold. Subjects are selected from among topics suggested by businessmen themselves. 

Great care is taken to assure that each project begun fills a genuine need, that it has practical value, and that it represents an original contribution written expressly for small business. 

Similarly, close attention is given to the choice of authors, who are drawn from among recognized specialists in the various fields. 

Management Aids for Small Manufacturers 

Since the start of this series, 63 leaflets have been issued. 

Through June 30, 1955, some 1,410, 350 copies had been requested by businessmen and others; 216, 605 copies were distributed during the 6-months period January 1-June 30, 1955. There is no charge for this series.

The three main classes of these Aids are: (1) Business- Government relations of which "How Good Records Aid Income Tax 

Reporting" is an example; (2) internal general management, such as "How To Price a New Product"; and (3) external sources of special ized assistance of which "How Marketing Research Helps Small 

Manufacturers" is an example.

In addition, the Agency has issued a volume titled 

Management Aids for Small Business: Annual No. 1. 

Annual No. 1. This book contains in bound form the first 31 publications in the series, which are believed to be of sufficient usefulness to small business managers to keep them available. Since it is not feasible to stock individual 

Aids indefinitely, the annual was issued and the printing of individual copies was discontinued. The annual is offered for sale by the 

Superintendent of Documents for 65 cents. As of June 30, 1955, total sales were 10, 863 copies.

Small Marketers' Aids 

This series is designed to serve owners and managers of smaller retail, wholesale and service enterprises in the same way that the Management Aids for small manufacturers serve small plant operators. Authors who are specialists in their fields contribute material on a public service basis. Copies of this series also are distributed free on request.

Examples of the types of material offered in Small Marketers' 

Aids are: "Records Management in Smaller Stores"--written by an officer of the National Records Management Council; "Effective

Advertising for Small Retailers" --written by the manager of a management engineering firm; and "Attracting Customers to Your Small

Store"--written by a professional author, lecturer and consultant. 

Technical Aids for Small Manufacturers 

These leaflets present various technological aspects of operating a manufacturing plant. In any production setup there are many opportunities for developing improvements which yield competitive advantages. A typical small plant operator finds it difficult to keep up-to-date on all techniques.

Designed to present highlights in basic production technology and written specifically for small producers, the Technical Aids cover four main areas: Processes, materials, equipment, and

maintenance. In the area of processes, "Gas Welding of Cast Iron" 

is typical; in the area of materials, "Radioisotopes and Small Business" is representative; in the area of equipment, "Reducing Costs with Jigs, Fixtures and Gages" is an illustration; in the area of maintenance, "Preventive Maintenance in the Small Plant" is a good example.

From the start of this series, 40 publications have been issued, and 473, 445 copies had been distributed through June 30, 1955. 

In the 6-months period January 1-June 30, 1955, the distribution amounted to 25, 570 copies.

Individual Managerial Counseling 

Many management problems of a small business can be satisfactorily solved only through study and research. The Agency endeavors to help with such problems through its Managerial Counseling Program, which consists principally of referring small business owners to reliable sources of assistance. These include national or local trade associations, which may have already studied the problem involved and published a solution; trade papers; consulting firms; public accountants; market analysts; or other business analysts who specialize in one or a limited number of management fields. 

An increasing number of business owners are taking advantage of the Agency's counseling service. For this purpose rosters of national associations of business specialists have been placed in the Agency's field offices. When individual businessmen seek assistance on managerial problems, it is sometimes useful to refer them to private, professional sources which will provide information or service, probably on a charge or fee basis. In this event, the Small Business Administration's field staff may recommend names from these lists. Not less than three names must be recommended to each individual businessman, The name of any firm or individual provided a businessman is for information only and is in no way an endorsement by the Small Business Administration, Also, the Agency is preparing a nucleus of a reference library for each regional office. The library will be useful in counseling work and as a reference source for businessmen of the area. 

Individual counseling work is done by the field offices and the Washington office through correspondence, as well as through conferences. 

During the last 6 months, Washington office alone has answered, by letter, more than 1, 100 specific requests for individual managerial counseling. Many of these have necessitated extensive research. 

The type of assistance given is illustrated by these examples: 

1. A manufacturing concern which had been making carpenters' tools for more than 50 years was faced with declining

sales. The company sought counsel from this Agency. A change in the policy of relying solely on distributors' orders was suggested and a plan for employing direct salesmen was recommended. The plan was adopted, and the resultant increase in sales is solving the company's financial difficulties. 2.

Typical of many inquiries about establishing a new business was one concerning the requirements for starting a phonograph records store. The inquirer was given names of publications dealing with the establishment and operation of a music store and of leading record manufacturers who could furnish valuable information. 

Pertinent Government publications of help in such tasks as estimating initial inventories and computing costs of record displays also were furnished. In addition, the inquirer was given the names of trade associations which could furnish specialized data on suppliers, costs of doing business and other significant subjects. 

3. The president of a small plant, desiring to expand the market for his product from his own State to the rest of the country, was interested specifically in distribution through the use of franchises. 

He sought assistance from the Small Business Administration. This was given him and he wrote: "Many thanks for your /assistance/ 

The various bulletins will be invaluable and I have already begun to profit from them. The . other suggestions contained in your letter were most welcome. 

4. A plumbing supply company requested assistance on setting up a pension plan for its work force of 25 employees. The company was referred to appropriate published material and associations known to have information on pension plans. 

Management Courses for Small Business Owners 

· • To assist the owner of a small business to become a wellrounded administrator, the Small Business Administration encourages educational institutions to provide short, practical courses in administrative management. These courses embody two principles: (1) They are designed for owners and managers of small firms; and (2) the subjects taught are limited to business administration, not operation. 

The educational institutions assume full educational and financial responsibility for all courses, while the Small Business Administration aids as a cosponsor, often with the assistance of such

local organizations as the clearinghouse association, chamber of commerce, local or State manufacturers, retailers or wholesalers associations, and other nonprofit business organizations. The businessmen-students pay tuition to the educational institution to cover the cost of the course. 

Since the successful experiment made with two pilot courses in the spring of 1954, this program has expanded rapidly. From 

October through December 1954, nine courses were offered. 

During the period January 1 through June 30, 1955, 44 more courses were offered, bringing the total number of courses offered and completed to date during the 1954-55 school year to 53. One other course is scheduled for July 1955, which will make the total 54. 

These 54 courses have been conducted by 40 educational institutions with 8 of the institutions conducting 2 or more courses. For example, the university of one State, in addition to holding a conference on its campus, conducted courses in three other State communities. The universities, colleges and schools offering these courses are listed on page 96.

The organization of these courses starts with discussions between representatives of the Small Business Administration and the educational institution. Following this, the typical procedure is to form an advisory committee of business leaders to help in determining the needs of the area's small businesses and in making the availability of the course known to members of the business community. 

Most of the subjects discussed in the more than 50 courses offered during the 1954-55 school year could be grouped roughly into 4 categories. These were: (1) Management policies and problems, in general; (2) money problems; (3) markets; and (4) men. 

Typical subjects under the first category were: "Policies -- 

What They Are and How to Use Them," "Decision Making--How to

Analyze Management Problems," "Sources of Information and 

Assistance" and "Planning for Future Growth." Subjects concerned with money included: "Financing the Small Business," "Using Your 

Accounting Records for Profit," and "Cost Control for the Small 

Business." Under markets, such subjects were discussed as: "The Development of New and Old Markets," "Management for 

Increased Sales" and "Meeting Competition. "I Those subjects having to do with men included such topics as: "Human Relations," "Motivating Employees to Contribute to Your Business" and "Public and 

Customer Relations in a Dynamic Economy."

The costs to the educational institution of promoting and conducting these courses were defrayed by tuition fees. By far the most frequently used fee was $50.00 for a total of 20 hours in 8 sessions. For courses offered as part of a distributive education program, fees were generally lower, ranging from $4.00 for 12 hours and $5.00 for 17-1/2 hours to $20.00 for 16 or 20 hours. Also, fees for the 2-day conference sessions varied considerably, since the cost of any one or a combination of the items of tuition, meals and lodging were included in the fees. 

One feature of the courses, encouraged by the Small Business Administration, is the completion of evaluation forms by the students at the end of the courses. The opinions expressed in these forms will help the educational institutions to improve future courses. Those institutions which have already offered two or more courses have used these opinions to advantage. 

In addition to proving of direct value in itself, the Management Courses Program also has indirectly helped the owners and managers of small businesses by leading to their increased use of other services offered by the Agency. The approximately 1, 900 businessmen participating in the courses have become more fully aware of the assistance which may be obtained from many sources, including the Small Business Administration.
