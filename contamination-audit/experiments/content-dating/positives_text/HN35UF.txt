I. THE COLLEGE OF THE GOOD ROAD 

I. EST POINT and Annapolis, Sandhurst and Saint-Cyr were founded to provide officers for the war of arms. The College of the Good 

Road is being founded to provide officers in the war of ideas. It recognizes that this is an ideological age and that the instant need of the age is a rising generation trained both to understand the conflict of ideologies and to equip their nations with a superior ideology. 

The need for such a college is imperative. Alien ideologies have had a long start and have largely succeeded in confusing or capturing the minds and morals of youth-in college and industry-across the world. Today Communist China is but one of the countries being run by those trained twenty years ago in ideological schools in Moscow.

This answering development is in line with the movement of history. 

Every great advance in education has been designed for a special purpose. The universities in the first place were guilds, spontaneous associations of scholars and teachers. They often had a vigorous virtual existence long before they obtained legal recognition.

They met a universal need, and from the Twelfth Century onwards were founded at key points right across Europe. "What the Church and Empire had both aimed at and both failed in, the knitting together of Christian nations into a vast commonwealth, the Universities for a time actually did." (Green's "History of England", page 132.)

The universities of the Middle Ages were specifically designed to train men for the ministry and for the work which the clergy of those days normally performed, such as the tasks of government and law. In early America, Harvard, Yale and the eastern universities were founded to prepare young men for

"public employment both in Church and civil state.' 

During the Nineteenth

Century, with the new needs of the industrial revolution and the growing thirst for scientific knowledge, a second phase in the development of the universities began. The University of London, for instance, first initiated as a private company, arose in response to these demands and marked the entry of a new emphasis in education to equip men technically for the industrial and scientific age.

The founding of the College of the Good Road marks a third phase, this time in response to the needs of the ideological age. Its aim is to turn out officer-statesmen in the war of ideas, equipped with an answering ideology based on absolute moral standards and the guidance of God. 

The College of the Good Road is the West Point, the Annapolis, the 

Sandhurst, the Saint-Cyr in the war of ideas. 2. 

The College of the Good Road is being founded in different countries and linked together by an international committee. Each country will have its own governing body and its representatives on the international advisory committee. This committee will consist not only of eminent educators but also of leaders from all branches of national life. 

Lectures and tutorial classes form a definite part of the education of the 

College and part of each year is spent in residential training centers. Much important knowledge and training, however, is gained from practical experience, by traveling in different countries and working with those already trained in the war of ideas. 

For the past four years the training the College of the Good Road offers has been given to the youth of twenty-five nations. 

Several hundreds of youth from these nations have been trained continuously by traveling widely in more than one country with the forces of Moral Re-Armament. They have attended lectures, written essays and taken part in tutorial groups. They have met and talked with ordinary men and leaders in every section of a nation's life; lived in the homes of workers, industrialists, labor leaders and politicians. They have visited parliaments, local governments, factories and newspaper offices. They have spoken at chambers of ΙΟ

commerce, service clubs, union meetings, in factories, in schools and colleges, and over the air. They have prepared and given ideological training courses to thousands of youth, as well as to adults; they have gained rich experience in writing, composing and producing the musical revue, "The Good Road", and other weapons in the war of ideas. 3.

Among the subjects taught in the College of the Good Road are the 

HISTORY. A moral and spiritual interpretation of history is given with particular reference to the history of the student's own country and that of the country in which he is studying. Lectures are also given on the materialist interpretation of history which provides the basis for the communist ideology, and to which this course provides the answer. Students are given opportunities to apply what they have learned from the past in molding the future. 

PHILOSOPHY. Teaching is given in the basic concepts of the nature of man and of his relation to the universe. Philosophy is considered as a dynamic which shapes the affairs of men, as well as explaining them. 

PSYCHOLOGY. The understanding of people and of relationships in home, industry and nation is taught by men and women who know: (1) that human nature can be changed, because that change has taken place in themselves, and (2) that God can guide, because they daily experience His direction. Psychology so taught becomes a study of the answer to problems and of the art of liberating the best creative resources in the human personality for the task of remaking the world.

ECONOMICS. Students are taught to live and to articulate the new economy where Give is the primary motive instead of Get; where everyone cares enough and everyone shares enough so that everyone may have enough; where the wealth of the world is made available for all and for the exploitation of none; where ownership involves stewardship. Students are trained to live and give the answer alike to the casual materialism of the ordinary man in every class and country and to the calculated economic determinism of the Marxist. 

SOCIOLOGY. This subject is approached as a study, not only of man in 

II

relation to society, but of how men can find a creative relationship with each other so as to become a force to remake society. 

GOVERNMENT. Courses are given that deal especially with the history and foundations of democracy. Students also have opportunities of meeting leaders of local and national administrations and learning the implications of their responsibility for their own governments. 

INDUSTRIAL LEADERSHIP. Lectures are given by experts representing management and labor in such subjects as the true function of management, the ideological struggle within labor and within management, industrial relations, industrial incentives, industrial democracy, and the history of trade unionism in different countries. Students are enabled to study particular industries and factories at firsthand, and to take part in industrial round-table conferences.

LANGUAGES. Students are trained to become proficient in at least one language other than their own. 

Students are en WRITING. Essays are set periodically by the tutors. couraged and taught how to be correspondents for their home newspapers and are given training in the interpretation of news and the preparation of articles. 

Selected students are given training in creative writing and in the editing of periodicals such as "New World News", and in the preparation of books and pamphlets for publication. 

PUBLIC SPEAKING. Students are trained in the planning of meetings, studying how to present the truth each has to offer in terms the modern man can understand. From a first hard-won utterance of a few sentences many a student has progressed until he is addressing large assemblies. One young man, a labor leader's son aged nineteen, spoke effectively before 70,000 people in the course of a single year. Others have prepared and presented radio programs. 

DRAMA. Students are trained to understand the function of drama as a primary ideological weapon and given an opportunity to take part, front or backstage, in productions such as "The Good Road" and "The Forgotten Factor", and in the preparation of films. 

MUSIC. Teaching is given in the history of music, with special reference to its ideological interpretation; in musical appreciation; in the study of music as an ideological weapon today; and in the creation and use of positive music 12

in the war of ideas. Selected individuals are trained in vocal, instrumental and choral technique, composition, conducting, publishing, radio and film music.

HOME ECONOMICS. Students live during their course in the homes of workers and industrialists and in especially selected homes where Moral Re- Armament is the basis of family life. This gives them an intimate understanding of the life and culture of the nations in which they are studying. Women are given special training in all the domestic arts, cooking, housekeeping, home management and the care of children. 4.

The College of the Good Road is a non-profit, educational corporation, incorporated in the state of California. All those who teach, lecture or work with the students give their services as a contribution toward building a new world.

The College has thus far been financed by the sacrificial giving of students and others who are convinced of the necessity for ideological preparedness. 

Corporations, unions, service clubs, foundations, as well as private individuals who are concerned that the youth of their nations receive such training as is given in the College of the Good Road, are invited to contribute to the Foundation Fund, particulars of which can be obtained from the Treasurer. 

Certain donors may desire to provide or equip the necessary campus buildings as a fitting memorial for some loved one. 

Scholarships of $500 to $2000 are needed to enable students to attend and travel with the College of the Good Road for a complete year. These scholarships provide for students from the United States as well as from those nations whose national economy does not permit their financing themselves. 

Scholarships of $150 are needed to enable candidates to attend the annual summer training courses given at the Moral Re-Armament Training Centers at Mackinac Island, Michigan, or Los Angeles, California.

II. TRAINING CENTERS 

Participation in the training centers of Moral Re-Armament plays an important part in the life of the College of the Good Road. Permanent centers have been established in America at Los Angeles, California and on Mackinac Island, Michigan; in England at Tarporley, Cheshire; and in Switzerland at 

Caux-sur-Montreux. 

Students are thus afforded the advantage of meeting and talking with prominent professors and others from universities the world over. They attend lectures, tutorial classes and discussion groups conducted by those trained for years in ideological warfare. 

Students take a full part in the running of these centers. They are shown how to make administration, catering, the manual arts and household tasks part of the basic training in democratic living. 14

III. ON THE ROAD 

PON COMPLETION of their course in a training center, students spend a period of time in a country other than their own, applying in practice what they have learned.

During the first three months of 1949, groups of students of the College visited various countries. The following pages give extracts from their own reports. One student summarized their findings in this way: "1949 is a fateful year. The word 'ideology' has become as common in newsprint as whiskey ads and divorces. The cold war has become a hot reality in the minds of ordinary people. And the need of an answer has forced itself upon nation after nation. "A year in the College of the Good Road has taken us through many countries. Our classrooms have been in mines, factories, drawing rooms, lecture halls and parliaments. Our professors and teachers have been labor leaders, businessmen and statesmen. Our fellow students have come from twenty-five different nations, from homes rich and poor, and every possible background. "In traveling, learning and working together, we have found the answer which we are being trained to give the world, to be simple and always valid." 26

ONCE I WAS BLIND MORGAN FIRESTONE 

New York, New York

Attended Hotchkiss School and Princeton 

University. Grandson of the founder of 

Firestone Tire and Rubber Company.

This essay answers three questions: (1) What is Moral Re-Armament trying to do? (2) How do I fit in? (3) What have I discovered with it? 

God? Rot. Prove it! Sex? What's wrong with sex? What do you think we are made that way for? Money? Money is power. What I do with it after I get it is my business. Who the hell cares about the communists? 

I don't. Let Europe settle its own problems. These ideas represent actions and attitudes which can tear down and undermine a country the way the old Roman Empire was torn down. 

Ideas and what they represent are calibers, good barometers of men and nations. Ideas control men. When a force of men is regimented behind one idea, history is made. 

Everyone knows there is need of peace and plenty in the world. One cannot pass a day or read a newspaper without realizing the misery and agony 55

men face. Everyone knows this unkindly world must be changed. The question is how?

Some men have set about to change the world according to their own personal ideas and ambitions. They rally men about them and force others into line. This is called totalitarianism. One great force today claims to know how the system under which men own things and have possessions must be changed; the capitalistic classes must be destroyed, and then there will be peace, abundance and happiness for all. But at the very word "destroy," we are reminded not of peace but of more war and human unhappiness. 

Then one man, Frank Buchman, came along and told us, or reminded us, of the answer to that question-how to change the world. He saw that to change systems and laws and to evolve new treaties and pacts would only temporarily alleviate world problems. He saw that the crux of the matter lay with the individuals within those systems and laws. The answer to war, hate, greed and fear is personal change. Real peace and security will only come when you and I, and the millions like us, change personally. Frank 

Buchman saw that the real issue behind world problems, and personal problems, was not economic, political, or social, but moral. Political, economic and social problems find their source in moral breakdown. Nations find their strength in the moral backbone of the people. 

That is where I come in. I am one of the many thousands enlisted behind this idea. I am behind it because it benefits me, my family, my home town in the States, my nation, the world. My thinking and acting were sinking to the level of those ideas at the beginning of this essay. My personal life and moral background were far from exemplary. I put girls and a good time before my family, and consequently had nothing to contribute to our family life together. My most ardent desire was to be a success in business. How could I hope to make a go of it in business when I was not willing to make a go of it at home?

Last spring I finished one year of education at Princeton. This summer 

I worked in the Firestone Retread Factory down on Miller Avenue—a wonderful experience. The men in that plant are the heart and backbone of our nation. After work I went home to play a few holes of golf and enjoy the company of my friends. I was starting out on the same path any boy in my shoes might take to equip himself for a job in the industry of the United Stateshigh school, college, a job during summer vacation, the life of work and fun typical of an American youth. 56

When I met Moral Re-Armament and enrolled in the College of the Good Road, I saw at a glance the reason for family breakdown. The purpose of 

Moral Re-Armament and the World Conference at Caux is to spread a democratic ideology based on moral standards and make it work in everyday life; to incorporate in people's living a system of ideas, beliefs and actions which will answer the two most important questions in the world—what to do about communism, and how can men learn to live together in peace and harmony. 

I was compelled to examine my motives in life which were in the end selfish. I saw the lack of moral backbone in my personal life, and how others paid for it in public life. You see, the College of the Good Road is not merely another college set up for more learning and more absorption of knowledge. 

It deals with human nature. It deals with the basic facts of one's character which determine in the end what we do with our knowledge. 

Like too many others I was completely ignorant of the fact that we have moved from an Industrial Age to an Ideological Age. During the Industrial 

Age leadership went to the nation with the best machines and skilled technicians. In an Ideological Age leadership goes to the nation with the superior ideology. And only a superior ideology will answer the ideology of communism. You cannot buy it off, fight or organize it out of existence. 

If the western democracies find no stronger ideology to match the communists, soon our hard-built industries will see themselves captured by alien ideas-ideas strong enough to win loyalties-and our trade unions captured and turned against us.

Formerly I was only interested in my job in business, my success and a close-knit group of friends. Anything else-Europe, the world-was three thousand miles away, geographically and in thought. It had never occurred to me that my job, my success could be harnessed to benefit the whole world, not just myself; that they could become an important factor in creating an age of abundance.

When I met Moral Re-Armament and enrolled in the College I was forced of necessity to broaden my world. My new friends were the son of a labor leader from Detroit, an ex-Nazi, a Norwegian underground leader, a Finnish boy my age who had already fought in three wars-people it would take me. a lifetime to meet otherwise. This close to the Iron Curtain, I saw the effects of the Politburo right before my eyes. I saw that my selfishness and that of a horde of others like me was the very thing which helped to create the Politburo and its counterparts over the world. 57

When I enrolled in the College of the Good Road I grew up. I met 300 new people from 25 new countries. I learned a world of facts about a world I had never been interested in before. I broke through a sphere of interest ten feet wide. I found the answer to family life. I found the strength and happiness of a moral backbone. I found that selfish interest in one's self is the least satisfying of all interests. Like the cured blind man in the Bible, "All I know is that once I was blind, and now I see." 58

NORMAL LIVING IN AN IDEOLOGICAL AGE 

It's a very interesting feeling to come back to your old home town after having been on the road for a year. Many things have changed because you have changed and a lot of things are seen in a completely new perspective. 

All your old friends want to know what you have been doing. 

Zurich is the main industrial and banking center of Switzerland. My last week there is an example of "normal living" in an ideological age. On Sunday night sixty workers and trade union leaders piled into the living room of a worker's home in Oerlikon, where all our main factories are situated. They all came to hear the former general secretary of the Socialist women of France and several British miners who had come to Zurich to attend an industrial conference. Then a young Italian trade unionist appeared and wanted to meet the head of the Wood and Building Workers of Switzerland on Monday. On 

Tuesday-my birthday the same man had to be accompanied to Berne where we were received by the head of the Swiss T. U. C. and the secretaries of the 

Metal and Clothing Workers. The teachers in Zurich wanted to hear about 

England and Japan. So we gave them an ideological training session on 

Wednesday night. On Thursday night workers from all the major factories of 

Oerlikon met with us to plan the revolution in their industry. Zurich is also a university city. Every Friday noon students and professors meet. 

The same evening we had a large youth meeting. The week-ends are a very important time if you plan a revolution, because you have greater chances to catch the people in a receptive mood than on week days. So we took off for the French part of Switzerland to attend there an industrial meeting. To use every opportunity to proclaim your ideology without becoming an activist, that's living in the revolution! I learned these days that it isn't enough to know absolute moral standards, but you need to express yourself; to learn what it takes to run a revolution; to know how to use your team; to analyze situations quickly and to change quickly to be adequate. This was all excellent training for Germany. 

WAR AGAINST SCEPTICISM 

On a Thursday noon my train arrived in Beethoven's and Marx's city, Bonn. 

It was in the middle of "Karneval" and the team that prepared the arrival of the cast of "The Forgotten Factor" seemed to enjoy life. I didn't realize at that moment that I entered a new phase on that day. On the same evening we met with the leading students of the university who had already helped in the preparations of the week. Many meetings were held and the Rector, the head 60

of the University, was very much for us already. On that evening I first met the scepticism and the frustration of German youth. One student, a former officer and national socialist, was atheistic and very bitter in a brilliant way. 

Another one was the son of a cabinet minister in the Russian Zone who had been several years in prison camp in Russia. A third one, a former officer, now married, had lost all his real incentives after the war. There was something broken in him. Here we had all the problems in one room. Many questions had to be answered. What was really the answer for the East? What can you say to a former national socialist who refuses to take any responsibility? 

What to say when you see all the mistakes all the occupation troops have done?

We had three weeks in Bonn, meeting every day hundreds of students. 

Never before had we met so many people who, deep down in their hearts, didn't know how to live and were coming to us to find an answer. Out of 

Bonn came the invitation to Wahn to attend a conference of delegates of all major universities of Western Germany, where in one night we got invitations from sixteen universities, either to send "The Forgotten Factor" or to come with a representative student group. Everybody in Germany says that the German youth is the biggest problem of the country. We had the answer and students and trade union leaders came to us. Only the best effort was good enough. 

In Bochum in the heart of the Ruhr mining industry we started to tackle systematically the trade union youth. One of us lived with the youth secretary of the miners union of that district. We met there the cream of the younger generation of working youth and learned to personalize people and win them in individual talks without making big public speeches and appearing in the headlines. Sometimes you would have liked to say something in the discussion and it was very hard to be quiet, but that was a good thing to learn too. 61

Imagine the large room of an inn, a long table with a dozen glasses of beer, behind the glasses sceptical but attentive faces of young men who seemed to have gone through a hard time of war and hopelessness. That was the meeting we had one morning in Opladen, a small town near Cologne. But we had now got and developed an instrument which we didn't have before. The trade union secretary of the town, a bitter communist, tried very hard to beat us with arguments, but as he left he had lost his fight because his youth secretaries had found a superior ideology. 

War against scepticism and bitterness. You saw very clearly here the power of an ideology in the life of youth. You saw the effects of the prison. camps in Russia. Humanly speaking, this was a lost generation. It took a certain time till you could live into these people, live through their experiences and see their deepest needs. All the aspects of our ideology which till now had seemed a little simplified or confused, were worked out and tried with people every day. It was like learning to walk again. 

We had to answer two basic questions, first in ourselves and then in public.

"LAND THAT I LOVE" 

I was asked what I had learned with the College of the Good Road that I would like to give to America. There are so many things-imaginative things and practical things, beautiful things and things that make your stomach heave and the back of your legs weak.

All that I bring to the task of telling America what I learned here are a sense of thankfulness to God, of honest conviction that I have learned something I didn't know before, and a great sense of urgency about giving that something to the youth of America.

I know that my family is thankful, as I am, that I have this great opportunity to learn. To learn a clean, good way of life, to learn to love and respect people, to learn something I can carry with me and use all the rest of my life. 

It was through the farsightedness, cooperation and sacrifice of my family that this great experience was made possible for me. 

We study with the College of the Good Road. We study as I have never studied before. There is a spirit in the teachers, the students and the very air that makes a sloppy, slip-shod, "just-get-by" job something to be ashamed. of. I am studying three languages, French, Italian and English. I hated 65

languages at home. But here I desperately want to be able to talk with the people and understand what is going on around me. I am farther now, after ten weeks, with the study of French than I was after four years of studying 

Spanish at home.

We're studying geography-economic, political and historical. And we're learning something of the map of Europe and the world because we meet and talk with people who make the names we read into pictures of places, people who make the dry texts live. We're studying history-political, economic and cultural-with youth who are as familiar with Napoleon and Gladstone as we are with Washington and Lincoln. We're studying nations through an aperture into their real lives-their youth. We live, eat, play and study together. We grow to know each other. We're studying life by seeing people hungry and cold in bombed and gutted countries; by seeing people when the suave smile of diplomacy is replaced with the grim determination of a relentless pressure to live from day to day; we're studying life by seeing men live by absolute moral standards and by striving to live them ourselves. 

We have lectures that make the people of the past walk out of the pages of time to repent and rejoice, sorrow and sing in front of our eyes. We are having a vivid personal contact with history while we are studying it. 

The greatest thing I have learned here is an understanding and love of people which comes from a better knowledge of them. The humility, gratitude and new awareness of the world, with all the people in it, that comes from soaking up the atmosphere which pervades the hundreds of time-honored and hallowed spots throughout the continent, is an education in itself. The feeling that comes with seeing the grey walls of the hospice of the Holy Order of St. Bernard, perched atop the Alps between Switzerland and Italy—and their dogs, the greathearted, kindly beasts that have been plodding out in the dead of winter from this same hospice for over 11 centuries. The tracks on the old stone road through the pass that your imagination tells you are those of Napoleon's cannon; and the heaps of stone that mark the winter graves of the armies of the "Little Corporal." The wonderful black-clad fathers who serve humanity so humbly and with such Christian charity, the hours spent enthralled by their tales of Tibet and the weird winter happenings in the mountain passes. The chill that runs up your spine when you look at the deep track worn by Father Bonivard in the dungeon stones of the grim Chateau de 

Chillon; when you find yourself talking in whispers as you walk the stones where Byron trod writing his famous poem. The mingled feeling of hope and 1 66

despair that flows through you when you walk with the thin, frock-coated ghost of a great American who strides the marble passages of the Palais des Nations in Geneva; the horror and wonder that mixes when you stand under the shadow of the Caesars and the Borgias. Rome, Paris, Berlin, Londonplaces where every American finds the tug at the roots of his heart, the tug of his ancestors, the tug of the spirit that made his country great. 

At Caux, a black-skinned son of Ceylon sitting in the evening sun-his blood-red jacket glistening with gold embroidery-enchanted 400 people from forty nations with the folk music of the East. The haunting, dreamy beat of the songs, his ancient instruments, the true love of the man for his country and his music; these did more for unity and understanding between East and 

West than many conferences.

I remember a big, smiling Swede and a quick, intense Welshman working together as the left side of the forward line in a soccer game, with a young Detroit auto worker and the leader of 3,000 British students forming the defense; the dress from the typical Levi's and letterman's sweater of the 

California high school student to the flowing robes and turban of a leader of the Burmese youth resistance; the manners-from the familiar, informal 

American to the contained, formal Englishman, to the proud, emphatic Finn; the customs a smile and "Hi ya, Joe," from the American, a straight look and quick bow with the hands folded on the breast from the Asiatic; the wonderful Scandinavian birthdays and the gay Scotch "paaarrties"; the music and dances of the different nations and races—all these things make up the College of the Good Road. These are the things that were never put in books. 

These are the things that weld the spirit of the youth here into a force that will create a world where the tragic blunders of 1914 and 1939 can never happen again. Here is the training in statesmanship and international relations that will do what has never been done before-supply the western world with trained, inspired leadership in the fight for the minds of men. 

The path I have seen here for the United States is far different from the one I saw sitting in my comfortable home in California. I have discovered that we are not alone in the world and that it does matter what goes on outside the city limits of Los Angeles. I found there are more important things for the youth of America to think about than what kind of a car we own and who we are going to take to the dance Saturday night. I found, to my great 67

amazement, that there are youth thinking of other things. Youth who are fighting incessantly with their whole minds and hearts to sell their ideas. to the world.

The feeling I have found here for America has deeply touched my heart. 

The people I have talked to have been very grateful for the aid given them. and their countries through the Marshall Plan, and by groups and individuals. 

We have done a wonderful job. But we have not done enough. 

One of the lecturers of the College of the Good Road is Mr. Leonard Allen, former INS and NBC correspondent in the Far East. He has just returned from several years in China. Mr. Allen gave us a clear illustration of what America needs today. "The United States has sent over two billion dollars to China since the end of the war," he said. "We have given the Chinese everything to fight with, but little to fight for. The Russians have given the Chinese communists little to fight with-but everything to fight for." Here in the 

College we find what it is that we must give to our country, and then to other countries, to keep the tide of communism from sweeping over our nation. 

We have studied the principles of Marx and had them explained to us by lifelong Marxist leaders. This has made us more efficient fighters in the battle to give the communists and socialists something better. A superior idea is the only way to change these revolutionaries. We have seen rockhard communist coal miners from Germany and Wales grasp a superior ideology, throw over the Party and start the battle for a God-inspired society. 

From the Ruhr mines to the Rhondda coal faces, from the auto plants of France to the shipyards of Scotland, the students of the College of the Good Road have traveled among the working classes and lived in the homes of the people of each country. Managers and miners, workers and employers-we have talked with them all. We have learned at firsthand both sides of the problems that are tearing at the vitals of France and sinking Britain deeper and deeper into economic instability. We have come to understand the problems of these people through understanding the people. When I sat down over the dinner table with a British miner who had been a Marxist for twenty years, I told him I didn't understand what made a man a communist or a socialist. When I got up from dinner, I did. I understood what had made this man a socialist and therefore what was making other men socialists. 

This understanding will help me in the fight to give these men that greater ideology-in a framework of a democratic form of government which can be 

America's true gift to the world. 68

EDUCATION IN AN IDEOLOGICAL AGE 

I'm one of the many thousands of Canadian ex-servicemen who returned home to study after the war, eager to have a solid base of education on which to build the future. Mine turned out to be a revolutionary new kind of education. 

I served as a radar engineer in the navy, and I poured every ounce of energy into my work in order to gain promotion and to find what I thought was satisfaction from a job well done. I didn't bother too much about the fellow I worked next to, and I was, generally speaking, all out for myself. Somehow this never satisfied me underneath, and I've since seen that I never will be satisfied on that basis. Really, I was lonely, proud, ambitious and insecure. I think of the way I lived then, and still live sometimes, and then I think of the masses of people that make up my country. Many are so like me. We live and think for ourselves, rather than for each other, our nation and the world. 

In the meantime, powerful materialistic forces take over nations without armed conflict. Could my country be next? Am I equipped with a superior ideology adequate in this age of atomic bombs and ideological warfare? Can I give to a communist the superior idea of teamwork as opposed to class warfare? 

Yes, I've been at college, the College of the Good Road to be exact, and I haven't been limited to the study of theories, statistics, ballistics and problems 69

ad infinitum. Today education must go much farther than that alone if we are to retain civilization. A brand-new world of study has been offered me. I've been studying and, as a result, seeing and helping to create democracy lived on an inspired level; a new dimension of statesmanship in governments; industrial relations built on a basis of trust; sound homes and absolute moral standards as the backbone of the nation; an answer for fear, greed and hate in the world. 

In 1948, while studying with the College of the Good Road, I was able to use and further my training in electricity, as a lighting effects technician with the musical revue, "The Good Road" on Broadway, in Hollywood and in the West End of London. After Christmas twenty of us were invited to Holland. 

It was a surprise to find Holland a reality and not the bygone fantasy of childhood days in school, or the tourist's dream. Sure, there were the dykes, the windmills, the tulips and the wooden shoes, but for me Holland came to spell much, much more than that. Had not my Canadian comrades in arms died on her rich soil to stem the tide of Nazism? Would yet another "ism" rise and take control of Holland? The Dutch are people like you and me, with the same problems and living in the same ideological age. 

With the signing of the Atlantic pact, I felt Holland begin to take security behind the power of the United States. There has been a struggle by the people to return to the prosperity of prewar days by hard work, and they have done wonders. Unfortunately, this has blinded them to the materialistic forces which play for their country. They have no answer for the national advance of communism which creeps on. I've seen that unless Holland finds again the ideology of great living that she once had, she too will lose her freedom. 

I saw that as students trained in a superior ideology, we could be used to bring renaissance to the living and thinking of Holland. All of us were under twenty-five years of age. We discovered that leadership went to the fellow who got his direction from God. Dr. Frank Buchman once said, "When man listens,

God speaks. When man obeys, God acts." This became the force behind our daily living. There was no older person to look to for plans and decisions. I learned that without a common mind and heart, and absolute honesty between us every day, we might as well forget about the training we had received. 

In teamwork we could be used, but as individualists we were useless. 

We visited the simple homes of laborers and the mansions of big industrialists. We accepted formal invitations to visit many industries, and thus had a chance to use our valuable training in industrial relations. What happened at an aircraft company was typical of these visits. We were taken on a 70

conducted tour of the plant in the morning, and then given a luncheon at which the directors, the chiefs of every department and the leaders of labor were present. After lunch we sang and spoke to them along the lines of the training we had received in our industrial relations course. We gave them fresh stories. from the Ruhr where some of our friends had been putting their training into action. In those few hours they caught the idea of a new spirit in industry. 

They were eager to know more. A director cancelled an engagement next day so that he could lunch and talk further with us. He told us that 60% of his employees were communists, and that he didn't know what to do to stop the spread of their ideology. He pleaded for ideological training for his employees, and for us to help him arrange to send a management-labor delegation to our summer training center in Caux, Switzerland. The personnel manager, a Marxist, insisted on going to Caux with the proposed delegation. 

He told me, "It's a great idea you have. I want you to be my personal guest next time you come to Holland." Later he gathered 

Later he gathered together the past and present chief shop stewards, all communists, to plan who should go to Caux. 

I learned that day that age and position are no barrier in ideological warfare; that youth trained in an answering ideology hold the keys to the future. 

Our visit to a blast furnace caused national repercussions. The Marxist chairman of the Works Council listened to our ideology and decided to fight for what he heard. At a public meeting attended by five hundred people he said, "Without Moral Re-Armament there is no hope of management and labor ever being able to cooperate." Representatives of management heard of this and invited the Works Council chairman to dinner. Today there is a team in that plant working together on a level away above the class struggle. 

I saw things happen in the universities as well, which gave me great hope for the future of Holland. For just as the fathers and grandfathers of today's students lead the nation, the students will be the leaders of tomorrow. I found them wanting a big idea to live for. The year by year routine of studying all day and parties all night wasn't satisfying them. I felt in them colossal fear of the day when they would finally graduate to find no job awaiting them in their small and thickly populated country. Probably they would be forced to emigrate in search of greater opportunities. They had taken on a hard, cynical outlook on life, and were full of theories and "isms". Not one had a philosophy adequate for this ideological age. Students everywhere responded to the idea that we in the democracies needed to live and give an inspired ideology to the world, through first starting with change in ourselves. 71

We spent many wonderful days in the Universities of Utrecht, Leyden, Delft, Rotterdam and Amsterdam, giving students a big idea to live for which made life full of fun and fight. The response we met at Delft was typical. 

There we were invited to luncheon by the president and board of the Men's Club to which came the presidents of all the Athletic Clubs. We met the students at small parties and over teas in their rooms. We talked late into the night. The students took responsibility for all of our expenses. One student stopped his life insurance policy and gave the money to us. 

I saw that students all over Holland are longing to find the way to great living. With the training I've been receiving with the College of the Good 

Road I could be used along with my friends to cause a chain reaction in the thinking and living of students. Our training gave us authority which made the most cynical and self-important students want to find a new and creative life. 

One evening we gave some young army officers from a military depot a little ideological training. Their answer to communism in the army had been to forbid communist newspapers in the barracks and to use force to stamp it out. 

We told them what General Ho, Premier of China, had once said at a conference for Moral Re-Armament. He said, "In my fight against the communists, the method I used was force against force, and organization against organization. 

I have now come to the very firm conclusion that I must fight an idea with an idea." The next day we received an invitation to give ideological training on a voluntary basis to the officers at the depot. Our friends of the night before had caught a glimpse not only of an answer to communism, but how the communist himself might be captured. 

Those three months were perhaps the best I've ever spent. I grew to love a foreign country as much as my own. I began to see and appreciate the great qualities of the Dutch, especially their home-making; and we Canadians can learn much from them on that score. Think of how many marriages today end in divorces! I saw the fine oil paintings of Rembrandt, Vermeer, van Gogh; the ancient cathedrals, the historic places. I left with an appreciation and love for fine art.

I began to see it as the great creative part of man set free. Once 

Holland sent ambassadors across the sea to pioneer. Today she must give and is beginning to give ambassadors of inspired democracy to the world. I think of those who left university to go into Germany and sow the seed of an inspired democracy there. They had seen that the future of Holland and Europe depends upon a sound Germany. They had begun to live for their nation. 

They are the leaders of tomorrow. 72

EUROPE BECAME OUR CLASSROOM 

(The following are excerpts from letters written by 

Jane and Blanton to their parents. Jane begins.)

The Swiss mountains are covered with fresh white snow this morning. 

It's the first real sign of winter. It's hard to believe that I have been in Europe a year. So much has happened to the world: conferences and crises, recovery, revolt; new Paris fashions and new world frustrations. 

A lot has happened to me this year. When I crossed the Atlantic Ocean, everything inside me grew and stretched like the endless waves on the horizon. 

I felt as if I had lived in a cell all by myself, and forgotten the world and the people in it. Everything revolved around me. That was my world. Everything outside was someone else's headache or responsibility. 73

When we went to England with "The Good Road" revue as part of our college training, something happened to me. I was ashamed of my small world of self-centered living.

It started to happen when we lived in the English homes. I came to know England through her families. That was part of our training—whether in East Ham or at Eaton Square, eating kippers or cake. The Exton family were so like us, or better still, we were so much like them. I remember the day I shopped for meat with twenty ration books under my arm. I stood in a "queue" for an hour; then walked home in the rain through Hyde Park and by the Palace. I began to feel a little part of England then. Or that 

Thanksgiving Day I baked pumpkin pie "Mom's way," using powdered eggs.

It was good, anyway. We worked and studied and played with the English. 

They showed us the country-the great universities, the rubble in East London, the gardens at Kew, Westminster Abbey.

After Christmas with Lord and Lady Hardinge at their home in Wimborne, England was no longer a stranger. To be taken into the heart of a family opened my heart and changed me a lot. It was like breaking out of my little cell to realize that we are the same underneath; that Europe is divided from us by water, but that people on both sides have the same feelings, fears and hopes. I began to see that to understand the world is to understand people.

There was the fog and the rain, the damp trains, the cold rooms; I saw 

England as she was, and I began to love and understand her. I loved America more than ever, not only because I knew what she was, but because I knew what she could be. Yes, I was homesick—the smell of oak logs on the fire, the pies, the puddings, all made me think of home. But one morning I thought, "The world's your family, if you'll let it be."' 'll let it be." It seemed foolish, when the morning papers had headlines of the growing disaster in China; but I knew in my heart that the real crisis was whether we who have learned to split the atom and conquer space could discover in time the power that unites man with man and nation with nation.

After Christmas we divided into small groups. Whole countries in 

Europe became our classroom. Holland was my home base for four months. 

I shall never forget the rough Channel crossing we made to the Hook. My childhood dream to walk on the dykes and have wooden shoes was fulfilled, but Holland was far more than that. The weeks we spent in her universities -Leyden, Delft, Amsterdam and Utrecht-in her student clubs, her great 74

industries, her farms, her homes and families, were a course in history, language and economics all rolled into one. I was delighted when the customs officer at the Belgian border asked me if I was Dutch. Certainly something in me was Dutch.

The shock of war's remains brought a reality into my life and made me think. The old battlefields, the rubble, the look on people's faces along the road were part of the ruin. But I was a part of it too. 

We all were.

The College of the Good Road stretched all over Europe through the winter, but in the spring we all came to our center at Caux. 

I began to grow up this summer when I lived with a German girl from 

Munich. I saw that peace begins in my own heart, and forgiveness begins there too. We have for so long repeated the cycle of hate, revenge and disaster, that it's time we answered hate and bitterness by starting with ourselves. 

God gave me the courage to forgive; and there were some things I had to ask forgiveness for. Honesty and change are hard, but no price is too great at this time in history. Sentimental goodness or hard indifference is a cheap substitute for change but that's the real answer.

Next door lived a Finnish girl who had twice been driven from her home during the war and still can't go back. I began to help her with English and found myself learning a little Finnish. But more than that, I was learning to know another country. Kirsti and I cooked together all summer as part of our training. We had girls from Switzerland, Germany, Sweden, France, Holland and Burma on our team. Sometimes there were as many ways to cook beef as there were countries. But through laughter, tears, burnt soup and change, we found the secret for cooking a perfect meal. We learned things around the kitchen table from which a conference table could profit. 

We discovered that absolute honesty was the only basis for teamwork. thought I knew how to make mayonnaise. I ruined one bowlful before I had the courage to share with my friends that I was just plain proud and needed advice. In the end about six of us made the most delicious mayonnaise you've ever tasted. We discovered, too, that God could be the real authority in the kitchen as well as everywhere else. All through the summer we had lecture classes at 7:30 each morning. 

Our lecturers had often just come from the places they lectured about-a radio correspondent just flown in from China, a businessman from the Ruhr, an Oxford Don, or a leading woman educator from Burma. We met them at class, on jobs and informally over meals. 75

By the end of the summer I knew that what I had written that morning in England, almost a year before, was true. The world was becoming my family.

Blanton continues: 

A year has passed since last I waved good-bye to the native hills of America. 

I have missed my country and I have missed home, but now Europe has also become home to me. It hasn't happened overnight, but it has happened. 

At first I was so American that nothing interested me here unless it reminded me of America or Americans. For months I was blind to the continent of 

Europe and its people. I am no less American because I have opened my heart and eyes to Europe. On the contrary, I think that I have found a new love for my nation. 

I remember those days last October when 250 of us took "The Good Road" revue through Germany. Our first glimpse of Munich will forever live in my memory. Our bus slowly rounded a curve on the Autobahn, one of Hitler's great highways, and there, stretched out in the moonlight before us, lay Munich. 

No one talked in my bus. For some of the boys it was a second glimpse of that famous city, but for the vast majority of us it was our first sight of a blitzed city. There was no light. The moonlight cut weird designs in the hollow buildings and on tons of rubble that still lay piled in the streets. Except for a few American GI's lounging on a street corner, there didn't seem to be a sign of life. It was a ghostly sensation to come suddenly upon the ravages of war.

I had seen nothing like it in my tour of the Pacific. I felt strangely uncomfortable and cold inside. I secretly thanked God that it wasn't my city or my nation.

The Ruhr is the heart of Germany. We spent a week there. We lived in the homes of the miners, talked with their wives and played with their children. One evening after the showing of "The Good Road" to coal miners in Essen-Werden I sat at the back of the theater talking to a young German boy of twenty. His home had been blown up, his father was missing, and he lived in a basement with his mother and brother. We sat silently together. 

All around us knots of people were talking together. Some of them were 

Communists; most of them were Marxists. These were the men who made the 

Ruhr strong.

What they were saying about America and our capitalistic system was burning my insides. Who were they to talk? They had started this war, hadn't they? It was the first time I had heard my country spoken 76

of like this, and it made me boil. But the reality of what some in Europe thought of America made me stop and question myself. Where had we missed the boat? Wasn't Marshall aid supplying them with food and supplies to rebuild with? What more could they expect? out.

There was a sound on my left and I turned to find the young German lad whom I had completely forgotten. He was crying; something was cracking in his heart. And then, as if a dam had broken, the tears came flooding 

The boy poured out all that was in his heart-things he had never told anyone. "This show broke something in me," he said. It's funny, but he seemed to be a boy again, and that hard look of hopelessness began to leave his face. As he talked I began to see for the first time where we had failed as a nation. In 1945 he and all his family had welcomed the end of the war and the beginning of the occupation by the American forces. "Now," he had thought, "I shall find out what democracy is like and how to live it.' 

He hadn't been a Nazi, but like millions of German people he had been caught up and swept forward in the great war machine that was Nazism. Hitler had had a compulsive idea; others had none. 

Hitler had been able to capture the minds of the millions of Germany. 

And now the war was over and Nazism had been destroyed, but what was going to fill the vacuum left in people's hearts and minds? The Marshall aid had saved this boy's life and the life of his family, but not until he had seen "The Good Road" that night did he see what ideas could make democracy work, or see the way he and his nation should go. Lincoln and Jefferson spoke to that German boy that night in the words they had given to America. 

We have grown in numbers, wealth, and power as no other nation has ever grown. 

But we have forgotten God.'

As I rode along the Rhine I thought a lot. I had felt for the first time some of the suffering that had gone on in people's hearts in Europe. I had seen the real wreckage. It wasn't in those miles of blitzed buildings. in the hearts of the ordinary people of Germany. Men like Lincoln began to live again for me. What they had given my country was what the world. was dying for lack of today-a burning faith, a dependence on God, a life based on absolute moral standards. That's what America was built on. 

That was her richest gift for others. If only my country would live that way today!

I saw that night where we had failed in the occupation of a nation—a nation that had looked to America to find out how democracy should be lived 77

and had seen an ordinary, selfish fellow like me, with little faith in God and with moral standards which were far from absolute. No wonder there was so much scepticism in Germany about democracy as a way of life. No wonder that for many, communism seemed the only way left. It was because of people like myself that democracy was to them a farce and not a force. I wanted to be different that night. I had seen a glimpse of America's destiny and I was determined that she should not miss that destiny.
