History of The Royal Gorge 

W" 

HEN La Salle paddled his own canoe down the Mississippi in 

1635 and made friends with the 

Arkansas Indians, he discovered the mouth of the Arkansas River, but it was almost two hundred years before the Royal Gorge was seen in 1806 by Lieutenant Pike, the first white man to give a word of mouth record of his discovery. From the best information available he walked the entire distance with fourteen United States soldiers and one civilian.

After building a block house at the east portal of the Royal Gorge, he went north through Garden Park and back to the Arkansas near Salida and then passed down through the gorge with the current of the river. Later he was captured by the Spaniards, who confiscated his maps and papers. These papers were recently found among old documents in the vaults in Mexico City, and were, as a neighborly act, handed over by the Mexican Government to authorities in Washington. Here they are being restored to use for their rare historical value.

Captain Bell and his party, a detachment of Major Long's expedition in 1820, couldn't take it either. When they approached the gorge from the east, they turned back.

In 1845 Captain Fremont found the Royal Gorge inaccessible and was about to go home when Kit Carson, a trapper from Missouri, showed him the way around through the Wet Mountains and back to the upper Arkansas, where he later rescued the Captain and his party from disaster in a severe snowstorm. 

Captain Gunnison in 1853 left the Arkansas and went up the Huerfano Valley and around to the upper Arkansas, but lost his life in a battle with hostile Indians before he completed his survey. 

These were the reports of the Royal Gorge that reached the states. in pre-territorial days.

However, it has international fame as well. From 1800 to 

1803 it was the boundary line between France and Spain, from 1803 to 1821 it was the boundary line between the United States and Spain, from 1821 to 1836 it was the boundary line between. 

Page Three

the United States and Mexico, from 1836 to 1845 it was the boundary line between the United States and Texas. From 1846 to 1848 it was again the boundary line between the United States and Mexico. In the treaty at the close of the war with Mexico it was ceded to the United States and has remained so ever since. 

The Royal Gorge was not conquered until the Denver and Rio Grande Western Railway Company built the road through it in 1874. It did that only after a railroad war with the Santa Fe 

Company, both of which were laying track right up to the east portal. At one time the Santa Fe Company had 1200 men armed, who would shoot, on the job. In the words of an old timer who was interviewed, "Yes, they did some shootin' around here then, but both sides were, 'tut, tut,' careful not to hit anybody." The case went to the Supreme Court, and finally the Rio Grande bought out the Santa Fe for $1,400,000.

Since that time the gorge has become by far the most scenic feature on the transcontinental line. During the past season 6,000 souvenir telegrams were sent by travelers to friends and relatives from the bottom of the gorge over the Western Union Telegraph lines. The passenger trains stop to give the travelers a chance to enjoy the view from below, while the tourists at the top look down and thank the railroad company for giving them a better chance to see a train from above This is a case where one man's gain is another man's gain. 

In 1929 an engineer came along with the idea in his mind of a bridge, and immediately placed that bridge at the top of the gorge by means of wire cables a foot thick built up of number nine wire. When asked by a youthful admirer how they got the first wire across, he said, and I quote, "Oh, that was easy; we lowered a wire down from the top on either side, welded them together at the bottom and pulled them back up a continuous wire." 

With the high bridge overhead, the hanging bridge at the bottom and the incline railway penetrating the sheer granite walls, the Royal Gorge has no equal anywhere for those who enjoy the spectacular in either the field of engineering or in the realm of natural wonders.

Geology of The Royal Gorge 

MIGHTY task has been accomplished here. The Arkansas River has cut its channel more than a thousand feet deep, through granite and associated rock at the estimated average rate of one foot in two thousand five hundred years. At some places the walls are vertical, at others they overhang. The width at the bottom is not more than forty or fifty feet, completely covered by the bed of the stream, while at the top it measures only a few hundred feet. The river channel has been cut through the center of Fremont's Peak, which rises from one thousand to two thousand feet above the immediate countryside.

These features place the Royal Gorge in a class by itself when compared with other famous gorges. The Grand Canyon of the 

Colorado River is deeper, but only a small part of it is cut through granite. It is wider. In some places it is twenty miles or more across at the top. It loses its chasm-like appeal and seems to be a rugged valley instead. The Black Canyon of the Gunnison River is a close second to the Royal Gorge. It is one thousand feet deep in places, but since it is cut through a high plateau, known in western terminology as a mesa, it does not present the striking appearance from above. From the top of Fremont's Peak one can look down into the Royal Gorge, as well as down into the valley on either side of the gorge, and immediately the question as to how this mighty task could have been accomplished presents itself. 

At the top of the gorge one often hears the tourist remark that a terrible earthquake must have torn the earth assunder and later the river converted the fissure into its present channel or that a volcano erupted and split the mountain open. These views were held by geologists not many decades ago, but now it is conceded that the entire task was accomplished by erosion. This conclusion is substantiated in several ways. In the first place, the canon follows a zigzag course like the bed of a meandering stream, which perhaps it is. If the canon were the result of an earthquake, it would be more nearly straight. The fissure would then have fol- 

Page Five

lowed a fault in the earth's crust. If volcanic eruption had taken place, the seams between the laminations of the various rock formations would have opened up rather than have been cut in crosssection, as is the case. The laminations in the schists and gneisses cross the canon at right angles and can be traced back and forth for long distances. They run north and south across the canon. 

Therefore, if the gorge were the result of an eruption, it would run north and south through the mountain rather than east and west. There is probably no instance on record of a stream bed of such vast proportions having been formed by any other process than erosion.

The Arkansas River is one of the long rivers of America. It flows more than fourteen hundred miles through mountains and plains without any large tributaries. It drains an unusually large area for a single stream. It may be older than the Mississippi if one thinks in terms of the geologist. The great inland sea that once covered the entire west may have extended beyond the Mississippi on the east as well as beyond the Rocky Mountains on the west. The upper part of the valley from Leadville to Howard is several miles wide. This is a good farming section where by means of irrigation considerable grain is produced and cattle ranches are numerous. The general contour suggests an old valley, one that has yielded to extensive erosion. From Howard to the west portal of the Royal Gorge the valley is U-shaped. Here there are neither farms nor ranches. There is scarcely room for the river and the roadways that lead down the canon. It seems that the valley here is young and that the mountains have been uplifted more recently. Erosion has not had time to take its toll from the hillsides and lay it down on the level for farm land. The Royal 

Gorge from its west portal to Canon City is still more narrow than the valley above. It is V-shaped in the extreme with no room at the bottom for more than the river bed and the railroad. At one place the gorge is so narrow that there is no room for the river and the railroad side by side. Here the railroad passes over a hanging bridge that runs lengthwise with the river. The bridge is suspended from trusses attached to the canon walls. Here the valley of the Arkansas is very young. Lateral erosion has made no inroads into the mountain sides at all. Only the downward, sawlike action of the sand and water in the bottom of the stream has had time to do its work. A second upheaval must have elevated the mountain through which the Royal Gorge is cut after the U-shaped section of the valley had outgrown its V-shaped form. 

In like manner the U-shaped section of the valley must have been elevated after the upper reaches of the valley were so extensively eroded.

When the great inland sea began to subside toward the east, the Arkansas River developed its course on the sandstone, shale and limestone of the exposed sea bed. Some of this formation can yet be seen between Canon City and Pueblo. This laid the river down from above upon the Pre-Cambrain or granite formation. The elevation caused by the folding process of the mountains was no faster than the erosion of the stream in its channel. 

Therefore, the stream was not blocked, and no lakes were formed. 

Since that time the sedimentary formation has eroded out, and the 

Royal Gorge is left to tell the story.

Minerals and Fossils of the Canon City Embayment*

HE Canon City Embayment of the great inland sea that stretched at one time from the Gulf of Mexico to the Arctic Ocean is about forty miles long, extending from Pueblo, Colorado, west to the foot of Fremont's Peak, just west of the city of Canon City. The embayment is about twenty miles wide near Pueblo and tapers to about four miles at the western extremity. It is bounded on the north by the Front Range and on the west and south by the Greenhorn Mountains, and is drained from west to east by the 

Arkansas River.

PRE-CAMBRIAN 

The Front Range, Fremont's Peak and the Greenhorns are of Pre-Cambrain formation, whose country rock such as schists, gneisses and quartzites occur on the higher levels and contain various non-metallic minerals and some semi-precious stones. 

Muscovite mica and feldspar are shipped from a quarry on the northwest side of Fremont's Peak. In that quarry is also found rose quartz that approaches gem quality. 

Black tourmaline is found near the north edge of the Royal 

Gorge, but the diggings are not worked now.

A poor quality of beryl is common in the pegmatite dikes north of the summit of the peak. These dikes are famous in mining history.

Columbite is common near the top and is found in well formed crystals but not in paying quantities.

Vermiculite is sometimes mined in the Greenhorns along Grape Creek.

Gold has been found on the Greenhorns on Cow Creek, but the assay does not warrant investments necessary for mining. 

A large pocket of garnet is located on Green Mountain in the 

Greenhorns. The garnet is of high abrasive quality. *From Rocks and Minerals Magazine, April, 1937.

North of Marble Cave are small eroded garnets of about watch jewel size that may have gem value.

Rose quartz is found in abundance on top of the Greenhorns, on Fremont's Peak and at Zingheim's quarry near Texas Creek.

ORDOVICIAN 

The Ordovician series lies unconformably upon the Pre-Cambrian at the west end of the embayment along the base of Fremont's Peak, Priest Canon, and Twin Mountains. The outcrop occurs in an uneven faultscarp that rises in some places to a height of two hundred feet or more. All three formations of the series are present and contain their characteristic fossils. 

The Manitou or lower Ordovician outcrops at Priest Canon, bearing echinoderms, gastropods, brachiopods and graptolites. 

The Harding Sandstone occurs at the east end of the Royal Gorge and is rich in rare fossils such as phillopods, orthoceras, Ordovician fish scales, and gastropods. 

The Fremont or upper Ordovician is eroded out at Twin Mountains and contains coral formations in various types of single, double, and mass developments.

The limestone of the Fremont formation is highly dolomitic and in some places has been metamorphosed into a marble of high quality, estimated by government geologists to be equal to Siena marble for building purposes.

At the south end of Twin Mountains is an outcrop of travertine, some of which has been shipped to Denver for building purposes. Another outcrop occurs at Cotopaxi, Fremont County, from which the finishing veneer of the Telephone Building of 

Denver was obtained.

A vein or pocket of ochre is exposed in the north wall of the 

Frankenberry quarry. The ochre is of a dark red color and tests. high in iron, containing no sand or grit. 

North of the embayment the Ordovician dolomitic limestone forms the greater part of the country rock for about fifteen miles.

CRETACEOUS The Cretaceous series follows the Ordovician and rests doubt fully upon it. The Red Beds or Triassic, as it is called by some geologists, consists of a coarse red or pink sandstone that yields readily to erosion. It occurs in the form of a wide trough on the west and north side of the embayment. The trough will average about 3000 feet wide and continues northward through the state.

The eroded Red Beds make a beautiful road material for lawns and parks since it is of a deep pink color, but in some places it contains clay enough to hang on to your shoes and naturally spoils the porch floors.

West of Sand Creek near the prison farm is Palace Cave which really is a natural bridge about 75 feet long, 10 feet high and 30 feet wide. The abutments on either side are of coarse red sandstone. while the span or floor is of a hard conglomerate. 

A few hundred yards south of Palace Cave is a mountain distinguished for its diagonal lines of cross-bedding, capped with a hard conglomerate. 

Red Canons, a municipal park, noted for its grotesque figures caused by differential erosion, is about 15 miles north of Canon City.

In Garden Park, dinosaur remains are excavated from time to time by various scientific groups. They are found in the Morrison 

-probably Jurassic formation. The Canon City High School has fragments of two individuals partially excavated, to which science classes are taken for study.

The Dakota formation in south central Colorado consists of two layers of buff sandstone with several large layers of shale between. The lower formation of sandstone is called the Purgatoire, which was laid down in brackish water. The shales consist of probably the Benton, then the Niobrara and the Pierre, that run in colors, black, light grey and dark grey, respectively. Above the shale is the upper Dakota sandstone known as Fox Hills. 

At the contact of the Morrison and Dakota is a deposit of gypsum that measures in some places as much as thirty feet in thickness. 

The floor of the embayment through which the Arkansas River flows consists of about four feet of very fine sand which suggests wind deposits. Under the fine sand is about two feet of

river drift composed of coarse gravel and small cobblestones, rounded and worn by water erosion. Several mesas of Fox Hills sandstone are left on the plain and several small buttes of Niobrara shale appear. Coal, fireclay and oil are shipped extensively from 

Canon City and Florence.

The Purgatoire formation or lower Dakota sandstone occurs in a high "hogback" running north from Canon City for about four miles, then northeast along the foothills toward Denver. 

This is a hard buff sandstone that lends itself readily to building purposes. The "Skyline Drive" has been built over this "hogback." by prison labor. 

The Niobrara outcrops along the base of the main "hog back" in a row of little "hogbacks." This formation produces the fireclay west and south of Canon City and is very fossiliferous. producing clam shells, cephalopods and baculites in almost every outcrop. 

The Pierre produces the oil of Florence.

A ridge or bar of Benton shale runs northeast along the "hogback" about four miles north of Canon City.

The Wolf Park coal mine near Alkali Gap probably pierces the Fox Hills sandstone and reaches the coal in the upper shales.

The Tunnel Drive enters the Royal Gorge from the east and follows the contour of Fremont's Peak above the railroad and the river and below the bridal trail for several miles through tunnels and across bridges to within walking distance of the hanging bridge.

The Diggers 

I T is all very simple to find a dinosaur, but sometimes it is not so easy. A dinosaur digger has much in common with a gold digger-both go to the sands of a stream to begin their work, both examine the sands, but the dinosaur digger does not use a gold pan. He examines the dry sand for fragments of petrified bone. Sometimes the fragments are as small as the grains of sand themselves. Sometimes they are larger-even as large as a peanut. 

They may be imbedded in the rocks. The size of the fragments depends upon the distance they have been carried by erosion from the main find.

If the digger finds fragments, he goes up stream and examines the sand again, and if he is successful, he continues farther until he finds no more fragments in the stream bed. Then he leaves the streams and follows a zigzag course up the hillside in search of the trail of fragments that may lead him to the find. If he fails on one side of the stream, he goes to the other side and searches in the same manner. If, in his zigzag course, he finds the trail of fragments, he follows it up the hillside to what he hopes will be a complete individual. Sometimes he is disappointed and finds only a minor part of an individual. If he finds a major part, he is hopeful because he may find the missing parts in the immediate vicinity with a little more effort. Five of the six most recent finds in this vicinity have been made by this method of procedure. 

In recent years the work in the fossile beds in Garden Park has been done with the aid of the National Youth Administration. 

These young men spend their spare time prospecting for new finds and digging in the field. When a major find is announced, interest picks up immediately and the excavation is done rapidly. However, some finds are not worked as easily as others, so the hammer and chisel must be used to separate the fossil from the rock matrix. 

This entails a lot of time and tedious, painstaking work. These young men do not think of themselves as scientists in the field of paleontology, even though they have made major finds and have presented to the Colorado Museum of Natural History, the individual whose picture accompanies this writing.

The paleontology of western America was entirely unknown up to 1877 when some plant fossils from Colorado reached Washington. These were of insufficient number to warrant conclusions, but about that time Mr. Lucas, then superintendent of schools at 

Canon City, reported a find in the field of vertebrate paleontology to Professor Cope of the Philadelphia Academy of Science. Immediately Professor Cope and Professor Marsh of Yale University came to Canon City to study the find that had been reported by Mr. Lucas. These gentlemen were visited by Dr. Hayden, then chief of the United States Bureau of Geological Survey. The work done by this trio of eminent scientists in Garden Park about ten miles north of the Royal Gorge soon became famous in the world of science.

Twenty-eight of the eighty-four species and genera in the field of vertebrate paleontology have been referred to Garden Park alone. This work attracted world-wide interest, while today source literature in these sciences refer to Garden Park as the typelocality of many species of pre-historic life. In the early reports of these activities the word dinosaur does not appear at all. Evi dently scientists coined a general name for them after study in the laboratories.

The dinosaur lived upon the earth during the Jurassic period of geological time and seems to have inhabited the earth for a very short time only, since the remains are found in a stratum of the earth's crust not more than a foot thick. The dry climate and lack of vegetation here make this area an ideal place for field research. in the earth sciences.

The Deer Herder 

A 

LONG with its other superlativesworld's highest bridge, world's steepest railway, world's finest climate, world's most unique skyline drive-Canon City can add that of the world's only deer herder.

He is Ed. W. Coryell, by name, an employee of the state game and fish department, who spends from nine to fourteen hours each night driving deer out of the garden tracts and yards, virtually all of which are in the city limits of Canon City. 

Where else is it necessary to hire a man to spend his whole time chasing deer out of town?

Because they could not think of any other title, the state of Colorado gave him that deer herder title. It so happens that there are three other men with this same title employed on the western slope, but their duty is far different from that of the Canon City man. They do protect isolated patches of vegetables, but their more important work is to put out salt and food in areas where heavy snow makes it virtually impossible for deer and elk to get natural food. They do not have to worry about chasing deer out of town.

Ed Coryell has been employed on his unique job for eight years, four years steadily without a break. How many thousands. of deer he has seen and driven out of western Canon City gardens in that time even he does not know.

His route is confined largely to the fine garden area on Canal Street around Soda Point, up past the state prison gardens, and through the gardens in Hot Springs addition, all of which are in the city limits of Canon City at the east end of the Royal Gorge. 

He once saw more than 100 deer in one hour, once counted 

67 deer in one herd before he started shooting his shotgun loaded with buckshot to chase them away. 

He says the prettiest sight he ever saw appeared one night as he was driving along Canal Street. His powerful spot light swung along the top of the old state ditch west of the waterworks. 

There, lined up on the ditch looking down upon him was a row of deer. He moved his spotlight along, counted 28, all standing almost shoulder-to-shoulder in a perfectly straight line. 

Page Twenty-three

The deer that inhabit Colorado vary in color from a light grey to a dark tan. They are known as mule deer because they have slightly larger ears than either the black tail deer of the Pacific coast or the white tail deer of the Atlantic coast. Mule deer usually have a white tail with a black tip, but sometimes the whole tail is white and sometimes the whole tail is black. The large cushion of white hair on the rump of the mule deer is also characteristic of the species. During moments of excitement this cushion of white hair bristles up and becomes very noticeable. The mule deer also has a pronounced, bouncing sort of gallop that is peculiar to its kind. When surprised or startled it always reverts to this bouncing gait for a quick get away.

Just recently Coryell started out to work, and found five deer down at Soda Point. He rounded them up, drove them ahead of his car for a couple of blocks, after which they disappeared over the crest of the Skyline.

He has a pet-a doe-that it is not necessary to chase away. 

About every night the doe comes down to a point near the gardens on Canal Street, gets a drink of water, and obligingly trots back into the hills without bothering the garden crops. Coryell can drive almost up to the doe, and he thinks it will not be long until he will be able to reach out and pet her from the car window. 

He makes his complete circuit past all gardens at least once an hour, covers from 80 to 170 miles every night, with an average somewhere around 100 miles. His 1931 coupe has gone over 

130,000 miles and still runs like a top, thanks to the perfect care 

Deer Herder Coryell gives it.

Deer are not the only animals he sees. He has seen mountain lions, once took a couple of shots at one, but it got away. He frequently sees porcupines, gently chases them back into the hills along with his deer. He helped a state trapper catch eleven beaver in a ditch on Canal Street, after which they were transported to a new home in nearby mountain streams. 

His method of chasing deer is use of guns, the noise frightening them mostly. He carries a .22 revolver, never points it at the deer, although he occasionally stirs up the dust around their feet with it, if they will not move otherwise. He also uses a smallcaliber shot gun with bird shot. With this he dusts off the deer if the noise of his .22 revolver is not enough to frighten them out of the gardens.

Trail & Saddle Recreation The Royal Gorge Trail 

HE Trail and Saddle Club of 

Canon City that lists among its members the leading horsemen of the vicinity has built a bridle trail from Soda Point on Highway 50 over the top of Fremont's

Peak, along the more precipitous parts of the canon walls to the high bridge. The trail is about ten miles long and for two-thirds of the distance it is only wide enough for saddle horses to pass in single file. This does not mean that horses and riders are crowded into a narrow canon with rocks to right and rocks to left and only the blue sky overhead, but quite the contrary. On this trip the rider follows the knife edge, as it were, of a long dike of mountainous uplife and looks down on the canon side for distances measured in miles. If he were thrown by a good bucking broncho into these distances measured in miles he would go tumbling down into the river below. Looking south and west across the canon one can see the long Sangre de Cristo range with its fine array, of snow covered peaks fifty miles or more away. To the north about the same distance is Pike's Peak, Mount Pisgah and the historic gold fields of Cripple Creek and all the unnamed peaks and fields that lie between. To the east lie miles and miles of level land that fades into the Great Western Plain near the boundary line of the 

State of Kansas.

Fremont County is larger than the state of Rhode Island. It is almost as large as the state of Delaware, even so, the whole county can be seen from the highest point on the trail. From this point of view, Fremont County is just a big canon. In panorama the horseman sees a major part of the state of Colorado, its highest mountains and its deepest canon at one glance.

A visit to the Royal Gorge is not complete unless the Bridle Trail is included in the itinerary.

The Tanner Trail 

The Tanner Trail leaves the Oak Creek road a few miles south of Canon City near the Basin Ranch. Oak Creek Grade on 

Oak Creek road is a feature in itself. As the traveler ascends the grade from Canon City, he sees Pike's Peak on the left, a few moments later he sees the same peak on the right as he rides along. 

In the next few minutes he sees it on the left again and, believe it or not, just as he makes the grade and starts down on the other side he sees Pike's Peak again on the right. At this point the road enters a canon that is forest covered wherever there is soil enough to support vegetation. The trail is well marked with a white and green sign, erected by the Forestry Service. It follows a small water course south to the top of the Greenhorn Mountains where it turns west and follows the crest of the range to Grape Creek Canon winding its way down to the Royal Gorge road and back to Canon City.

On this trip the tourist enters the San Isabel National Forest, the best preserved and the most beautiful of Colorado's fifteen national forests. It has suffered less from fires and logging camps than any of the others. The timber in this part of the forest is principally fir with some aspen and scrub oak. The vegetation 

When one goes is entirely different from that at the Royal Gorge. from the Royal Gorge over Oak Creek grade and up the Greenhorn Mountains he changes life zones. He climbs from the pine belt of the foothills to the fir belt and attains an altitude of 8,000 to 10,000 feet. Here the columbines grow. The aspen parks are numerous. Here the white fir trees with their fernlike branches take their characteristic cone shape. The Douglas fir with its pendant paper-like cones grows profusely. This is a timbered area, a dense forest in places, where one can seldom find an outlook down into the Arkansas valley to the north or the Wet Mountain valley to the south. The Tanner Trail thru the San Isabel Forest will tempt any horseman to a camping trip with a pack train at Tanner cabin where the water runs clear and the grass grows high for the horses.

The Cow Trail 

Those who long for the long ago and wish to ride a far-famed cow trail up into the real cow country of Colorado towards Hartsel and Fairplay in South Park should take this trail north from Canon City thru Shaw Park and into the Bare Hills. Samuel 

Hartsel, after whom the town of Hartsel is named, is attributed by some writers to have been the first man in this area to go into the cattle business for profit. He rounded up the old, lame and worn out work oxen that were abandoned by their prairieschooner owners on the plains, drove them up into the South Park hay fields where they fattened up and were sold as beef to the camp cooks at the gold mines. In 1864 Hartsel trailed the first breeding stock, 148 high grade Shorthorn cattle, this way from Missouri. It took him two years to do the job. Kipling was right "There is no road thru the woods.

These men are enthusiastic but not professional horsemen. 

They do not "play the races." They would laugh if one should mention their "stable" of mounts, or even refer to their "string" of saddle horses. It is a hobby with them. They do not commercialize their sport. They go beyond "stables" and "strings" and include all the saddle horses of the country side. It is not unusual to see two hundred horsemen on parade in Canon City during a celebration that is of general interest.

The annual Old Timers Day in May is colored with bright shirts, ten gallon hats and jingling spurs. The major part of the program is staged by the horsemen. 

The annual rodeo in June is an "all out" call to local talent in horsemanship. Every cowhand and his lady friend are there with "spurs on." The streets clatter with galloping hoofs, while pedestrians and motorists pull over to the curb either for courtesy or safety, it is doubtful which-however, it is advisable. 

The annual ride over the Royal George Trail is fixed in the minds of the people as a day on the open range. Last season one hundred fifty-seven horsemen formed the cavalcade. The Club serves a chuck wagon lunch or a barbecue at the top of the gorge, and those who do not ride up over the trail take the highway by motor and meet the horsemen at the top. Here they perform for the tourists by means of race, rope and stunt until the day is done, when they all file down the trail again to green pastures.

Trees and 

Wild Flowers

The fruit of the pinon pine is an edible nut, that grows in each scale of the cone. Even though the Navajo Indians make famous blankets, the source of their major income is the sale of pinon nuts.

The vegetation of the Royal Gorge area is charactertistic of the foothills of the Rocky mountains throughout. The altitude ranges from 5,000 to 7,000 feet above sea level and therefore limits the variety of trees to only four species of evergreens and not so many broad-leafed varieties. Grasses and wild flowers are even more scarce than timber since they are more directly effected by weather conditions and surface erosion than is timber. The variety of wild flowers that one sees when visiting the park is somewhat misleading when compared with the number of varieties that appear during an entire season. Since the growing season is fairly long at this altitude, some flowers appear in the early spring and die as mid-summer approaches. The yellow pea is one of these. Some flowers flourish only during mid-summer. The pentstemon is a good example. Late autumn flowers are the bee weed, gum weed and several varieties of stonecrop.

Three distinct varieties of cactus grow in abundance in the area. Even though they are disagreeable to handle and inconvenient to picnic parties they are interesting specimens of plant life. 

The cane or joint cactus grows to a height of about three feet, usually with several stems, bush-like in form. The branches are jointed.

The prickly pear is a flat disc-like growth that is always found in patches. It spreads out in circular beds several feet wide. During early summer time cactus blossoms are bright red, purple, and yellow. They are large and can be seen at a distance.

Trees... 

The most common tree is the pinon pine (Pinus edulus). It seldom, if ever, grows more than thirty feet high, has many branches and a bushy top. The needles grow two or three in a bundle and are from an inch to an inch and a half long. The cones are flat, mushroom-like and contain an edible seed or nut. 

The pinion nut in the southwest is of considerable commercial importance. It is claimed that the Navajo Indians in northern 

Arizona receive a better income from their nut harvest than they do from the sale of blankets. 

The Western Yellow Pine (Pinus Ponderosa) is the monarch of the forest at this altitude. It grows to be forty or fifty feet high with large stiff branches that usually grow straight out from the trunk of the tree. The needles are from three to five inches long and grow in large bunches at the end of the twigs with two to five in a bundle. The cones are woody, about two to four inches long, and are covered with short spines that are disagreeable to the touch.

The Rocky Mountain Red Cedar grows to about thirty feet in height, is cone-shaped with drooping branches of a pale green color.

The Juniper is a bushy shrub-like tree that grows profusely on dry, rocky ridges. It is used for posts and stove-wood. 

Cottonwoods grow along the water courses. They are a broad-leafed tree about fifty or sixty feet high and often three to four feet in cross-section. The cottonwood belongs to the poplar family and has leaves and bark very much like the silver poplar, but the upper branches are not so white. In late autumn the leaves turn a bright yellow.

Scrub-oak grows on the hills in thickets that provide excellent shelter for cattle in the winter and good fence posts for the farmers. 

After frost the leaves of the scrub-oak turn different hues that vary from pale yellow to a bright scarlet and thus provide the best color display of the year.

In fact, the sparse vegetation of this region is an asset rather than a hindrance to the beauty of the park, because the bare rocks, highly mineralized in some localities and fossiliferous in others, are the feature attraction. Here erosion has taken an enormous toll, but not without just compensation.

This graph illustrates the upturned edges of a number of the geological formations west of Canon City on Highway 50 as the traveler goes to the Royal Gorge.

A study of the graph shows that the entire series of the Cretaceous formations occur east of the Skyline Drive. West of the Skyline Drive from near the crest of the hogback to the highway at the base, the Jurassic shales and sandstones outcrop in green, gray, and maroon colors. The red sands and sandstone along the bed of Sand Creek are Triassic, locally called Red Beds. 

The Ordovician occurs in the faults and folds of the light gray to pink limestone or dolomite higher up the mountain side to the west, while the Pre-Cambrian can be seen in the dikes running up from the sedimentary formations towards the top of Fremont Peak. All of the mountain tops west of the Skyline Drive are schists, genisses, quartz, and feldspar, rich in their associated nonmetallic minerals, beryl, tourmaline, garnet, and mica.
