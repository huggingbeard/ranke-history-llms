THE AUTHOR The author of 'How to Get Ahead in America' 

is editor and owner of an international news feature service. She has had many years experience writing and editing for various magazines, newspapers, publishing houses in this country and Europe. During her work as a reporter and editor in the fields of finance, economics, politics, the arts and social sciences, she has been more and more impressed by the wide gulf between success and failure. 

She has studied hundreds of successes as well as failures, and has come to the conclusions reached in this book - that given the key to successful creative endeavor, anybody can get ahead. Believing this to be so, she has tested, time and again, the method in this book, using her friends and acquaintances as well as strangers for her experiments. She has given the rules for success to certain individuals (just as she does in this book), instructing them step by step (as here), giving them a lift when they needed it, painstaking attention where the individuals were working incorrectly, all with such tremendous, gratifying results that she believes everyone should have the key. 

She has studied closely those people who seem thwarted by chance, luck, the 'breaks.' She has given them the key to success, and worked with them till their lives became fruitful and a blessing to themselves and others. She has seen the wide-spread need for such a book that would give definite, particularized instructions for solving the problems all of us meet in our journey through life, and to fill that need, she has written this book.

SYNOPSIS 

For many years the humble guinea pig has been used by scientists to test and prove their experiments.

In this book on getting ahead in America, I shall use this same method using as my guinea pigs of experiment and proof, five individuals the sort of people you and I know and talk to every day...people from various walks of life, of various ages, experiences, backgrounds. I shall show how the method for getting ahead in America works with these five individuals, and you, the reader, can see for yourself, as if you had a scientific experiment under a microscope, just how a person achieves success, and how you can apply the method with complete success in your own life. 

To work personally with each and every reader of this book would be something I would very much like to do. The narrow confines of a book, however, limit the writer to a few specific cases. Nevertheless, the plan I am using here, of taking five individuals and their problems and showing how these problems are solved, then showing you how you can apply the same method to your own lives, enables me to establish an intimate contact with each of you from the first page of the book to the last. 

So you must know that when I am speaking of

John Hunter who is planning on going to aviation school, of Harry Moore who is on his way to becoming a cartoonist, of Rosemary Jones who will soon be married, and of the others, I am really speaking of you, and to you. Because I say in Chapter 1 that you are young and just beginning life, does not mean that the instructions in that chapter are not for all ages, sexes, races. 

You must, therefore, put yourself in the place of the individual in each chapter, each situation, each circumstance that I write about, then set about applying the directions and instructions to yourself. 

This is absolutely necessary in order for you to get ahead quickly and to build a strong foundation for your future activities.

I want you to feel that this book is an entirely personal matter between you, the reader, and me, the writer. You need not feel that you are being conceited when I ask this. You are young. 

You are just beginning life. You may even be new in this country. You are ambitious. 

You want to get ahead. You want - within the next few years - to step out of the rank and file to plunge ahead - make a place for yourself a name for yourself...secure fame, wealth, health, happiness for yourself and those you love. 

But you look all around you at the thousands who are thinking the same things, wanting the same things. The competition, you say, is terrific. You ask, "What chance have I got?" 

You've got every chance in the world. Within the next few years in America, new industries will be born, new careers mapped, new fortunes made. Greater advances in science, agriculture, radio and television, aviation, economics, politics, finance, the arts, academic endeavors, will be made within the next few decades than ever before in the memory of man. Our guinea pig for this chapter turns out to be a girl, twenty-seven years old. Her name Rosemary Jones. She is passable looking, doesn't carry herself too badly, isn't too bad-tempered.

The latter are mostly items that concern her daily activities...the cows' new pasture, the hens not laying, the borers in the corn, a new radio, good health, Henry's disposition, the neighbor's children, woodchucks in the truck garden, their leaky old house on the knoll. 

It is taking Matilda several days working over her list, but we won't hurry her. Her problem is no bigger and no less than John Hunter's and Harry Moore's, but she is at a disadvantage because her failures and disappointments have 'conditioned' her to the point where she has almost come to believe that life has nothing further in store for her except a continuation of her pain and lack and disappointments. She almost believes that her life is practically over; that she's just got to wait till the eventual end.

But, surprisingly enough, she has taken on a new interest in things these last few days that she has been working over her list. She even is beginning to feel that she is getting a little bit of fun out of this crossing off things she once had hoped for and that had meant so much to her. She gets her list down to ten items. Then down to five, then three, then one. And that remaining item is - and she is as much surprised as any of her neighbors would be were they to see Matilda Brewster working carefully over her list to locate her BURNING DESIRE - to have good health and a comfortable home. She can't for the life of her see how she at her age of sixty years bent up with arthritis and other aches and pains, and living in the ugly frame house can possibly get her desire - but she is willing to do as the others have done before her, and see what she can do about achiev ing her BURNING DESIRE.

First - her tonight's list. Then tomorrow's. Then her plan to go over in detail, before she falls asleep each night, what she would like to do the next day, were her goal already in sight. In fact, her mind has already responded to the invitation. 

She has caught the idea immediately, and has jumped away into the future to the place where she is already seeing herself in that comfortable home, and even what seems more surprising and wholly amazing to this sixty-year-old farm woman, sees herself walking around in that home, erect, strong, without her glasses, without her cane. Her Yankee 'gumption' has risen from the depths of past reverses and she is going to see if she can't do what the other young ones are doing for themselves.

Within a week after beginning her list and starting to work toward her goal, Matilda Brewster has found that her appetite is better. She finds she is sleeping better, that Henry speaks less irritably to her, and that the neighbor's children do not torment her and steal her apples as they did. She notices that her sister-in-law does not pick on her as formerly, and does not find fault with the meal Matilda lays before her.

As the next week swings into the present, Matilda finds what were laborious, tiring chores are now done in very little time and with much less effort. As she visions herself in the future - in her new home and free from her pain - she catches herself regarding herself, for the first time in many years, in the bedroom mirror. She is a wise woman, although her wisdom from long years of disuse, had not shown itself, and now she knows that she has disreputably let herself go in a way that now she is ashamed of. In the mirror in her room she sees her scraggly wisps of hair, her dull, weathered skin, her gnarled hands, and resolves that all these shall be put down on her lists as things she will tackle, in her campaign to better her life. 

At night, before she falls asleep she sees herself, in her imagination, as free from her disabilities. Her fingers are straight, her shoulders erect, her face smiling. With great effort, she sees herself throwing away her cane, and walking to the wood shed, then the tool house, then the barn, without her cane. 

She shuts her eyes and hears the tap of a hammer on the roof of a new colonial house under the elms across the little valley. She hears the voices of men through the trees as they shout to each other at their work. She smells the odor of tar as they work on the cellar. And she sees the plan of each room where she wants each piece of furniture, the color of the curtains, the handsome carpets, the modern lights, the electric refrigerator, the short-wave radio. She smells the roses in her solarium, hears the canary in his cage in the East window, envisages the home coming of her children with their families for a big Christmas dinner.

She is dressed in a fine dress then, she has brought out her rings from their box in the back of the clothespress, and she is wearing her hair differently (it is carefully brushed and parted becomingly.) 

Matilda Brewster sees the reaction of her children to her as they see her for the first time in several years. She sees her eldest daughter gaze with mixed surprise and wonderment. 

She sees her youngest son take her in his arms, then whirl her 'round and 'round, even dancing a waltz to the music in the air. 

She sees her husband come to her side, put his arm around her waist, and press a kiss on her smiling lips. She sees herself completely happy, completely healthy, looking ten years younger, and feeling like forty-five. 

Then she jumps quickly back into the present. She plans in detail her increased activities for tomorrow. She is going to can again this year the first year since '36. She'll have her neighbor stop in town tomorrow and leave her order. 

Then the question of the chickens. They've got to be more productive. She's heard there's a training camp coming to the valley, and she's going to see that they take her eggs and milk. She lists the first steps in her plan to get a second-hand car and has fun with herself imagining Henry learning to drive it. But drive it he will; she has made up her mind. 

After a month, Matilda Brewster has ceased wondering at the new energy developing within her. She has ceased to take the

time to gloat over the way she is gaining the new respect of her neighbors and her sister-in-law. They ask her now for advice. Unconsciously, they wonder what has come over her, but she gives them little time to think, so busy is she with new ideas and new plans. 

Matilda works ceaselessly and tirelessly. Her back is straighter now, her fingers are less knobby; her days are so filled she has little time to think that two months ago she never walked beyond the barn. She spends more time out of the house now than indoors her household chores she finishes in the morning and after dinner is about with her chickens, and her cows, and the orchard.

There is no truer saying than that faith and your mental attitude have everything to do with your health. You have only to try this out for yourself to see. You need not take my word for it.
