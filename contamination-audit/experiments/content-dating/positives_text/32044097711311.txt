COMMENTARY Introduction 

The Thirty-seventh session of the South Dakota Legislature adopted Senate Concurrent Resolution No. 9, which directed the 

Legislative Research Council to undertake a comprehensive study of the duties, powers, and organization of a state department of labor in South Dakota (see Appendix IV for the text of SCR 9). The state has no centralized department at the present time which acts in all or most of the capacities in which such an agency would serve. 

The problem which prompted the 1961 Legislature to adopt the resolution was expressed by Governor Archie Gubbrud in his inaugural address, in which he requested the study. Appropriate excerpts from the Governor's address have been reproduced in Appendix V. Background During the early years of South Dakota statehood, its economy was almost purely agricultural. In recent years, although the state is still predominantly agricultural, the growth of industry and commerce within the state has given impetus to increased governmental attention to labor-management relations. Also contributing to the state's acceptance of labor as a valid field of governmental action have been the tendency toward concentration of workers in larger plants and the creation of labor organizations and unions. 

South Dakota has no constitutional handicap regarding authority in the field of labor. Not only does the state have plenary power in labor granted by the "police power" provisions of the State Constitution, but the Constitution itself, through amendment, deals with a specific phase of the matter through the adoption of the "right to work" amendment.

With the growth of the national economy and the expansion in number of workers connected in some way with interstate and foreign commerce, the federal government has become more and more active in the field of labor. As a matter of fact, labor legislation and the enforcement of labor regulations have developed, especially since the early 1930's, into one of the principal functional sectors of the national government. Nevertheless, a great portion of the state's workers produce only for intrastate commerce, and accordingly are subject only--or at least primarily--to state authority. 

There are six major functions in which South Dakota has had labor obligations to meet on the state level. In practice, of course, there is a good deal of overlap between what is purely a state function, and what is a federal function. The six broad functional sectors in which South Dakota has a responsibility in labor as a field for public action are: promotion of employment, unemployment insurance, fair employment practices, health and safety,

-6- workmen's compensation, and labor-management relations.

Major Functional Sectors for State Action in Labor 

The six major functional sectors of public action in the field of labor, as now administered by South Dakota state government, have been analyzed below. In each case, a definition of the function, the present South Dakota laws regarding it, and the manner in which it is administered, have been presented. 

(1) Promotion of Employment 

It is obvious that the first necessity of workers is employment, and that their greatest hazard is the lack of it. For this reason, every state has concerned itself with finding jobs for workers in need of them. 

There are, of course, private employment agencies of many kinds in existence. Some of these operate for profit, while others are philantropic. With growing recognition of high employment as a matter of public interest, several states began, however, to provide state services, and with the adoption of the Wagner-Peyser Act of 1933, which provided a program of federal aid for state employment agencies, all states created an agency for this purpose. As with virtually all federally-assisted programs the states accepted not only the federal funds offered, but also the federal supervision entailed.

South Dakota entered the field of promoting employment early. 

In 1920 (Chapter 54, Special Session Laws), the state began a program of maintaining, in cooperation with the several counties of the state, a service which provided free employment agents for labor. 

This act was amended in 1936 in conformance with the provisions of the Wagner-Peyser Act, and remains today without further amendment as SDC Chapter 17.07. 

The state employment service is administered by the State Employment Security Department, which operates the agencies. These agencies have the primary duties of receiving applications and making placements of workers, and in addition provide service in these special fields: labor clearance, service to agriculture, counseling service, testing service, service to Indians, service to the older worker, service to the handicapped, service to youth, service to employers, service to veterans, and teacher placement.

Under the terms of the Wagner-Peyser Act, states agreed to maintain a public employment service which would be affiliated with the national system and to match federal funds on a dollar for dollar basis. One very significant modification of these provisions has been that employment services are now paid wholly from federal funds (since 1948). (2) 

Unemployment Insurance

Even in periods of high general employment, workers are some-

times "laid off" or lose their jobs. Especially as a result of technological changes, shifts in demand, and plant relocations, not every individual is able to maintain continuous employment and to provide for his needs when unemployed. To carry such workers along, at least for a temporary period, precludes the necessity of their applying for public assistance. It is therefore generally agreed that it is in the public interest to provide for an unemployment compensation system. These systems have been devised so that payroll taxes yield funds for benefit payments for a time, not as charity, but as a matter of right.

Unemployment insurance programs came into being in the 1930's; the first state to introduce a plan based on payroll taxes was 

Wisconsin, in 1932. Under the unemployment compensation provisions of the federal Social Security Act of 1935, the system was adopted by all states. South Dakota's plan was adopted in 1936. The federal law sets up general standards, but provides sufficient leeway to allow variance between the states in coverage, rate of payroll tax, scale of benefits, and other features. The present South 

Dakota program provides for a maximum tax rate of 3.6 percent, on a tax base limited to wages of $3,000 or less, and with a scale of benefits with a top weekly payment of $33, for twenty-four weeks. 

The tax may be reduced for an individual employer if his plant or shop has contributed a less than proportional share to the number of persons receiving benefits--this reduction is determined through an "experience ratio." Up to 90 percent of the payroll taxes are used for finance benefits only; the remaining .03 percent of taxable income is paid to the federal government which provides there from the funds for administration of the plan. 

The South Dakota program is administered by the State 

Employment Security Department.

Fair Employment Practices 

The adoption of laws prohibiting discrimination in employment is primarily a post-World War II phenomenon, although efforts in this direction originated in 1941 with an executive order of the President forbidding discriminatory employment practices in government service and war industries. Unlike other major programs dealing with labor--which frequently came about through federal urging or forcing of state action--there is no national fair employment practices law. In fact, Congress has not seen fit to embody any fair employment practice program in general legislation. In practice, of course, the federal government exerts a good deal of pressure on federal contractors with regard to their employment practices. 

On the other hand, a number of state legislatures have declared discrimination against Negroes and other minority groups in matters of employment to be contrary to public policy. In some cases the laws adopted have been mere expressions of legislative sentiment, but in a few cases, penal provisions have been adopted to make it a punishable offense for an employer of more than a stated number of employees (usually six) to refuse to employ, or to discharge, any

Dersons because of race, color, creed or national origin. Some states have also forbidden labor unions to exclude, expel or discriminate against laborers for these reasons.

South Dakota has no fair employment practices law, although the Legislature has considered bills on the subject in each of the last three sessions. Only the State Commission of Indian Affairs has been delegated any power in this field--the Commission is charged with promoting employment of Indians as a means of absorbing them into the economy of the state. 

(4) Occupational Health and Safety 

The scattered South Dakota laws on the subject of occupational health and safety have resulted in part from pressures exerted by organized labor, and in part from a general legislative recognition of the obligation of the state to promote general well-being. 

Occupational health and safety laws are in effect in all states, and many of them date back sixty or seventy years. Usually included in state statutes for the preservation of the health of labor are those regulating the employment of children in industries not covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act; restricting the hours which may be worked by women; requiring proper heating, ventilation, and lighting; and setting standards for sanitation. Aimed especially at safety are statutes which are designed to require safeguards for machinery, fire escapes, safety appliances, and so on. 

South Dakota has no single comprehensive law on the subject of health and safety for labor, and the responsibility for existing laws is diverse. For example, women and children are protected when employed in industries, stores, and hotel by laws administered by the 

Commissioner of Labor; miners are protected under health and safety laws administered by the Inspector of Mines; petroleum use and storage laws for the protection of workers are administered by the Commissioner of Revenue; the safety and health of laborers working with liquefied petroleum gas are regulated by laws administered by the State Fire Marshal; and workers are protected from radiation exposure by laws which are the responsibility of the State Department of Health. 

Most of this state's laws on the subject of occupational health and safety are necessarily broad and general, with the listed administrative authorities left to prescribe more detailed rules and regulations. 

(5) Workmen's Compensation 

Workmen's compensation is a form of social insurance designed to protect workers in cases of work-connected injury or contraction of disease. All states have workmen's compensation laws, as a result of the now generally accepted belief that occupational accidents, caused by willful negligence or misconduct of the injured worker, are a proper charge of industry and business as a whole. The laws vary widely between the states--some are optional and some compulsory, some apply to all accidents and some only to those in the more hazardous occupations.

South Dakota entered the program in 1917. The first South Dakota workmen's compensation law, adopted in the Fifteenth session of the Legislature, has been revised, amended, investigated, or re-exam-

ined in some manner during virtually every session since that time (the single exception: the 1937 session). The law is now codified as Title 64 of the South Dakota Code and its 1960 Supplement, as amended in 1961. 

South Dakota's workmen's compensation laws are administered by the Attorney General, in his capacity as Industrial Commissioner of the state. Actually, of course, the principal functions are carried on by the Deputy Industrial Commissioner, who is one of the Assistant Attorneys General. To determine controverted medical questions in cases of claim for compensation for an occupational disease, the law provides for a Medical Panel. The Panel is comprised of three physicians appointed by the Governor. 

To guarantee that an employer subject to the South Dakota program will make benefit payments, the law requires that he take out workmen's compensation insurance or provide satisfactory proof to the Commissioner of Insurance and the Industrial Commissioner of his ability and solvency to pay the compensation required by the act. The state itself is not bound to provide insurance or security under the law, but the Legislature does appropriate money to a workmen's compensation fund to cover state employees. 161 

Labor-Management Relations 

Most state laws in the field of labor-management relations are designed to prevent economic harm to labor, management, or both, through unfair practices employed by either, and to prevent economic disturbances through some regulation of strikes, walkouts, and boycotts. Since federal laws have been passed which have usurped the authority for labor-management relations in interstate commerce, most state laws have limited applicability, usually applying only to smaller, purely intrastate business and industry. The state laws vary, generally tending to be more far-reaching and comprehensive in the larger, highly industrial states, and more limited in scope in smaller states, especially those with primarily agricultural economies.

Under the doctrine of preemption, the courts have held that states are generally precluded from asserting jurisdiction under state law over those employers and employees subject to federal labor management statutes [National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act, the 

Railway Labor Act, and the Taft-Hartley Act]. Until 1959, therefore, the maximum area left by the federal laws for state regulation was relations in purely in intrastate commerce. 

The administrative agency for the principal federal acts--the National Labor Relations Board--found, as a practical matter, that it could not exercise jurisdiction over all cases subject to federal law.

It has, therefore, adopted minimum jurisdictional yardsticks to limit the cases brought before it largely to those having "substantial" impact upon interstate commerce. However, a 1957 U. S.

Supreme Court ruling prohibited states for exercising jurisdiction

over cases subject to federal law, even those declined by the NLRB because of its jurisdictional minimum--creating, thereby, a "nomans land." An amendment to the Wagner Act, incorporated into the 

Labor-Management Reporting and Disclosure (Landrum-Griffin) Act of 

1959, reserved to the states jurisdiction in cases involving interstate commerce which were denied by the Nlrb. As a result, states now have power to regulate labor-management relations in both intrastate commerce and in certain types of interstate commerce. 

The basic South Dakota law on the subject of labor-management relatings is constitutional: the right-to-work amendment. In statutory law, there are regulations affecting labor unions, collective bargaining, boycotts, picketting, labor disputes, union solicitations and contracts restraining employment. There is, however, no comprehensive South Dakota labor relations act, and the authority for executing the laws which exist is diverse. The 

Commissioner of Labor (who holds that office ex officio, by virtue of serving as Deputy Industrial Commissioner) has general authority to enforce the laws, but the responsibility is for the most part discretionary. States attorneys are required to investigate any suspected or reported violations of labor relations laws, and to take the necessary legal action when violations exist but this function appears not to have been considered mandatory by most holders of the offices. And several sections of the South Dakota law fail to specify the responsible officer for their execution. 

(7) Other South Dakota Laws Affecting Labor 

The six preceding sections have outlined in very brief form the six facets of governmental responsibility for action in the field of labor. Many other South Dakota statutes impinge on labor in one or another manner. An example is the provision of several boards for licensing of persons in specialized trades, such as cosmetology, barbering, and embalming. 

Existing South Dakota Departments 

The Legislature has assigned to a diverse group of state offices the responsibility for carrying out the laws which relate to labor, and in several instances no officer at all has been assigned to execute the law--which suggests that some state labor laws are virtually negated through lack of administration. Generally, however, where responsibility has been assigned, the laws are administered by either the Industrial Commissioner of the Employment Security Department, as a major function, or by one of several other offices as a minor function. These administrative agencies are discussed briefly below. 

(1) Industrial Commissioner 

The distinction between the offices of Attorney General, Assistant Attorney General, Industrial Commissioner, and 

Commissioner of Labor is rather tortuous. The pattern, as

prescribed by law is: the Attorney General, an elective official, who is ex officio the Industrial Commissioner, selects one of his appointive Assistant Attorneys General to serve as Deputy Industrial Commissioner, by virtue of which selection the Deputy becomes, ex officio, the Commissioner of Labor. No additional compensation is awarded the Deputy, however, for either position. 

For all practical purposes, the Deputy functions as the Industrial Commissioner, and is charged with administration of all South Dakota laws pertaining to workmen's compensation. At the same time, as Commissioner of Labor, he acts as mediator and conciliator in employee-employer disputes subject to the state's labor relations statutes. Finally, he is directed by law to investigate violations and enforce all state laws relative to the employment of women and children.

The Deputy operates under biennial legislative appropriations.

(2) Employment Security Department The State Employment Security Department administers two of the major functional areas of state action affecting labor mentioned earlier in this paper: unemployment insurance and promotion of employment. Since the Department's administrative costs are borne by the federal government, federal civil service regulations are applicable--only the Commissioner is appointive. All other employees are hired through the Merit System Council. The Employment Security 

Commissioner is appointed for a four year term, and is removable only for cause. As a result of a 1961 statute, the Commissioner must be an attorney.

The main office of the Department is located in Aberdeen in addition to which fourteen employment offices are operated in locations throughout the state. The Aberdeen office occupies 

13,300 square feet, which has been called inadequate by the previous Commissioner. All space is rented, the rental being considered part of the administrative expense of the Department. The 

Department employs an average of 169 full time personnel, and an average of 13 part-time help, of which 71 and 8, respectively, are located in the Aberdeen office.

No state appropriation is made for the operation of the Department. In fiscal year 1959-60, the administrative costs of the Department were $837,146.97. 

(3) Other Offices and Agencies 

With the exception of the Medical Panel, which convenes only as often as necessary to pass medical opinion on certain workmen's compensation claims, no other office is assigned execution of state labor laws as a primary or even major function. State offices or agencies with minor responsibilities for labor are: Inspector of 

Mines, Conmissioner of Revenue, Fire Marshal, Department of Health, and Commission of Indian Affairs. At the county level, administration of state labor laws is a duty of the state's attorneys. 

Labor Departments in Other States 

Except in general terms, the organization of labor departments in the several states in the Union are not comparable. The states vary widely in the scope of enactments, and the administrative organization of departments. Usually, the larger the state in terms of population, industry, and employees, the broader is the scope of labor laws. ileavily industrialized states generally have comprehensive laws, and consolidated departments of labor. An outling of functions conducted by the states is shown as Appendix VI. 

A recommendation as to the basic principles of a strong state labor department, prepared by the U. S. Bureau of Labor Standards, is included in this paper as Appendix VII. 

Budgetary Needs 

The budgetary needs of a state department of labor in South Dakota, exclusive of those involved in moving of equipment, providing office space, and other incidental one-time requirements which would be involved in consolidation, have been reviewed by the Committee extensively. Except to the extent that the argument for increased efficiency in centralization is valid, the cost of a centralized department would certainly not be less than that involved in operation of the several individual agencies now operating in the labor field, and, for reasons listed in the statement on expenses (Appendix VIII). the cost would probably be greater than the approximately $160,000 now appropriated by the state biennially.

(The inclusion of the Employment Security Department in a tralized state department of labor would not effect the federal government's contribution for administration and maintenance of employment offices and unemployment compensation insurance.)

RECOMMENDATION 

The Committee recommends against the creation of a state department of labor for South Dakota at this time. 

During its considerations, the Committee developed a listing of arguments in favor of and in opposition to the creation of a state department of labor in South Dakota. These are submitted, for the information of members of the 1963 Legislature, in Appendices I and II. Although there is some validity in the reasoning of the proponents of a department, the Committee believes that the weight of the issue is against the establishment of a centralized department at the present time.

Despite the submission of an adverse recommendation, the Committee has presented a model bill for the creation of a state department of labor in South Dakota (Appendix III). This bill, which is not recommended for adoption, was prepared in keeping with the mandate of SCR 9, 1961.

Appendix I ARGUMENTS IN OPPOSITION TO THE CREATION OF A CENTRALIZED DEPARTMENT OF LABOR IN SOUTH DAKOTA 

In Opposition to the Creation of a Centralized Department of 

Labor, in General

A state labor department is unnecessary because of the lack of industrial businesses in the State. A labor department, like all government agencies, would have a tendency to grow, to assume new powers and authority requiring increased personnel and appropriations. 

Naturally, the Legislature would, if additional sources of revenue were added, prefer to tax those individuals or companies receiving benefit, resulting in an additional tax burden on business. The additional control over the labor situation would also discourage business development in South Dakota. A very clear example of this occurrence was the New England and Northeastern experience when industry moved to the Southeastern part of the nation to decrease labor costs. South Dakota is currently in a favorable position to encourage business development, This situation could only be worsened by the creation of a labor department, which would not provide any compensating benefits. As a corollary to this problem, it is widely recognized that labor departments usually encourage the development of labor unions. Despite South Dakota's "right ot work" provision, labor unions would gain a major ally with the creation of a state department of labor--the only organized proponent for this legislation was AFL-CIO. 

The matter of costs is an important problem. State and local officials currently handle our few labor law violations on a parttime basis. A labor department would certainly increase the costs of administration. Increased costs of labor law administration is a normal and natural occurrence as increased specialization develops. Such specialization in the labor field would be wasteful because South Dakota is a non-industrial state. 

It is feared that since there is limited business development in South Dakota, a labor department is likely to cause restrictions in agriculture. Our major industry has enough problems and is regulated in enough ways already. Farming is already marginal, financially speaking; most farmers are merely "breaking even" or going into debt. A government agency requiring the adoption of special safety devices by every farmer before he can employ personnel would represent a death blow to the family farm. 

Those who claim that South Dakota needs a labor department have not mentioned the fact that most of the larger employers are already regulated by the federal government because these employers operate in interstate commerce. The Federal Government is, continually through NLRB decisions and regulations, as well as Court cases together with activities of other federal agencies, further preempting this field. The need for regulating small business employer's

simply does not exist.

In small businesses, the owner is usually the operator and works directly with his few employees. He is ordinarily subject to the same conditions as his employees and ordinarily would not intentionally allow a hazardous condition to exist. ¡lazardous conditions are easy to see; there is no need for a high-priced field staff to tell a garage owner that he should have a good ventilating system when everyone knows about the dangers of carbon monoxide. Likewise, there is no need for such a staff to tell a farmer about the dangers of handling stock or machinery; he knows the dangers.

The suggestion made by the proponents of a department of labor is that a need exists. But if needs exist, there would be some evidence of lack or failure of the present system, such as union strikes, lockouts, boycotts, etc. It is well known that

South Dakota has had little trouble along these lines, and certainly much less of it than the vast majority of states which do have such departments. "Labor," in the usual sense of the meaning of the term, constitutes less than ten percent of the population of the state. 

If a specialized department were to be established for every group of this size, the breadth and cost of state government would increase greatly.

It is usually the case that state departments of labor become arms of the federal department. South Dakota has already become too much dominated by federal policies, and creating such a department would merely subject the state to further infringements on states' rights. 2) In Opposition to Inclusion of the Employment Security Department, in Particular

The related immediate problems of finding adequate space under one roof to house the combined individual agencies would create an almost insurmountable problem should a central department be located in Pierre, where logic would dictate its situation. Moreover, the cost of moving the Employment Security Department from Aberdeen would cost more than money--many employees with valuable experience and training would undoubtedly be lost. It also has been suggested that the removal of the Employment Security Department to Pierre would involve subjecting this part of a consolidated department to greater political pressure than it has had in Aberdeen. 

Other disadvantages of moving the offices from Aberdeen would include the loss of close contact with the offices of the Internal Revenue Service in that city, with which the Employment Security Department works closely, and the hardships which would be worked on retirement-aged personnel.

Generally speaking, establishment of a centralized department of labor would result in a space provision problem even with the inclusions of all previously discussed functions except Employment 

Security.

Appendix II ARGUMENTS IN FAVOR OF THE CREATION OF A CENTRALIZED DEPARTMENT OF LABOR IN SOUTH DAKOTA 

The reason for establishing a state labor department are many and compelling. For example, the present lack of coordination between agencies having some responsibility for labor problems would be eliminated. Specifically, the Commissioner of Revenue has been made responsible for the petroleum storage workers. At the same time the Fire Marshal also has responsibilities in this area. 

It goes without saying that the staff of the Commissioner of Revenue cannot spend a great deal of time inspecting or otherwise working in the field of petroleum storage safety, and few regulations have been promulgated for their safety. The policy of South Dakota officials responsible for the various aspects of safety also differ in major respects, for example, the occupational health personnel in the Health Department have difficulty using the records of the Industrial Commissioner and usually must make an independent investigation. Both agencies are concerned with injuries to workmen, but no uniform policy has developed for investigation. 11 State's attorneys are required by law to investigate all violations or suspected violations of labor laws, but the conflict of time and interest with other matters for which the state's attorneys are also responsible has resulted in spending little time on labor matters, except in workmen's compensation cases where an accident has already occurred. One state's attorney remarked that although this responsibility for workmen's compensation matters existed, ...both the state's attorneys and the workmen. would be better off if the state's attorneys were not obligated to take these cases on." It is simply a matter of the state's attorneys having too much to do, particularly since these officials are allowed to practice privately while in office. It is unlikely that state's attorneys would expend a great deal of effort in the broad area of investigating violations or suspected violations if they have insufficient motivation and time to deal with concrete instances of problems arising out of workmen's injuries. A properly coordinated and functioning Department of Labor with sufficient staff in the field would give this work its proper attention, status, and authority.

The State Health Department, responsible for radiation hazards and general occupational health, probably has done more than any other agency in this area of industrial safety, but this agency staff feels that it had to move into the vacuum. It is believed that eventually, the occupational safety unit will have sufficient statistical data to show what models of farm machinery cause the most injuries and will be able to offer constructive suggestions for the design of safer farm machinery.

It is known that most businesses hiring less than 500 employcan not make use of a safety program as an economic and money--

savings management tool.

For these smaller business, it is cheaper to pay the increasing insurance costs than it is to work at accident prevention. A state labor department would provide advisory services to these smaller businesses to assist them in eliminating accidents and lowering their insurance costs. The occupational health staff indicated that no large corporation could tolerate the use of poorly designed machinery causing as many injuries as farm equipment does. Accidents also result in losses in production of machinery and personnel; this is the primary reason any large corporation adopts safety programs.

There are only two other states besides South Dakota without a separate and independent agency whose major responsibility is the administration of all or most of the state's labor laws. fact that need for such a centralized agency is recognized in 47 states--some of which are less industrialized than South Dakota-certainly points to an acceptable rationale for the creation of such a department. Coordination in administration of labor laws, both State and Federal, between South Dakota and other states, as well as the Federal Government would be of great benefit to the labor force of South Dakota.

South Dakota spends many thousands of dollars annually to attract industry. One of the inquiries that each potential industry makes in considering whether to come here is what sort of labor situation we have. The situation, of course, is one of which we can be proud, but there is no one to tell that to the management of industry, since no agency in the state is in a central enough position to have the data requested. This is discouraging to industry.

A department of size--not size for the sake of size--but resulting from the consolidation of all the scattered bits and pieces which now exist, would allow the hiring of a director of great competence, as well as the consolidation of a workable staff, fully coordinated in all phases of a Department of Labor. This situation would be beneficial to both labor and industry. 

Despite any argument to the effect that South Dakota is basically an agricultural state, and therefore does not need such a department, the fact is that agricultural employment in South 

Dakota has declined from 79,935 to 72,611 during the ten years period ending in 1960, while non-agricultural employment has increased from 144,214 to 165,562 during the same period. This reflects South Dakota's gradual change from a rural to an industrial state. Effective solutions must be found to problems arising from an expanding industrial economy--this is the job of a state department of labor, and this is a job which is not now being done.

Appendix VII BASIC PRINCIPLES FOR THE ESTABLISHMENT OF A STRONG STATE LABOR DEPARTMENT 

A State labor department has responsibility for the determination and carrying out of programs for the conservation of the State's human resources and their most effective utilization in economic development. 

The labor department's primary function is to promote and develop the welfare of the wage earners of the State. Measures to advance a stabilized economy, good labor standards, safe work-places, and to minimize industrial disputes are essential parts of this function. Farseeing planning and leadership are vital to achieving these objectives in the light of changing social and industrial developments. 

A labor department should administer the labor laws of the State but it should do much more. It is the agency of the 

State government to which workers and employers should be able to turn for advice and help in meeting the day-by-day problems of employment and in planning programs that will promote the best interests of both labor and management. 

First and foremost, a department of labor should have responsibility for the administration of all the labor laws in the State including workmen's compensation and employment security. This centralization or responsibility in a single department is important for several reasons. It makes for a consistent policy in the administration of related legislation. 

It makes possible coordinated record-keeping and permits the dovetailing of enforcement methods and practices in the different fields covered. It provides one place where workers and employers may get service on a wide range of problems. It saves the employers from visits, advice and orders from a multiplicity of State agencies. 

It also saves State funds through the economy of operation made possible by a coordinated approach. to A department of labor should have an adequate inspection service to see that there is compliance with the labor laws, advise with employers and help them find ways to meet employment problems. Also, it should have authority to study and investigate employment conditions and to make reports of conditions affecting workers over which it has authority or for which remedial action is needed.

The department should be under a full-time administrator, qualified by experience and understanding for sound, constructive handling of labor problems. He must be alert to changes and developing situations and active in keeping the public aware of these changes. He should be given the necessary powers, authority, and funds for effective administration of the laws and programs designed to protect workers in their places of employment. 

The department should have a trained and competent staff, selected on a merit basis. Adequate appropriations are essential to permit employment of qualified and experienced staff needed to carry out the administrative functions, to perform necessary fact-finding and to develop standards and programs for improved labor legislation and to assist employers and workers with their day-to-day problems. 

Public relations are an important factor in obtaining the objectives of labor legislation; the public, as well as workers and employers, must understand the true functions and responsibilities of the department. To this end the department should have facilities for preparation of simple, brief pamphlets giving the provisions and the purpose of specific labor laws. 

It should report annually to the Governor and the State legislature on its activities and on various problems for which remedies are needed. It should likewise report to workers, employers, and citizen groups. It must work out and recommend remedial action. It must have funds to make this information available through reports and other public media such as radio and television. Public relations of the department should be strengthened by the appointment and use of advisory boards and committees in connection with certain of its programs which for successful administration require the advice and assistance of management, labor, and the general public.

Appendix VIII BASIS OF ESTIMATES CONCERNING BUDGETARY NEEDS FOR A CENTRALIZED DEPARTMENT OF LABOR IN SOUTH DAKOTA 

The responsibility for the enforcement of state labor laws in South Dakota is widely diffused. Computation, therefore, of collar amounts spent on labor problems, at best, is difficult and inexact.

The Aeronautics Commission, according to the law, appears to have some responsibility for the safety of ground crews and airport employees. Most of this function, however, because it is involved in interstate commerce, has been assumed by the federal government. Wherever there is a possibility of state jurisdiction, without any federal interest, federal standards have been applied. No amount of time spent or monetary amount could have been ascribed to this relatively narrow safety function. 

The State Mining Inspector estimates that he spends about $5,270 annually in mine safety. This, of course, is one of those functions which clearly ought to be part of any labor department.

The State Commission on Indian Affairs is the only state agency for which any responsibility might exist in the field of discriminatory employment practices, a field in which most state labor departments have some responsibility. 

There has been no expense over the past few years, however, attributable to fair employment practices.

The State Department of Public Health has labor functions in two distinct areas. This department is responsible for radiation hazards and general occupational health and safety.

This approach would appear to imply that costs of a state department of labor in South Dakota would begin at about $160,000 biennially if the listed functions were all to be encompassed, and, from that figure, go up. However, to assume this estimate would invite criticism, since the approach used does not take into account at least two major factors which would influence costs. One of these is that a man cannot be divided in half. By this, it is meant that if it takes, for example, half the time of a man in an existing department to carry out certain labor functions, it cannot be assumed that half his salary is all that would be needed to carry out the same job in another department. 

The other factor ignored in this approach is that there are many areas of labor law into which the state has not yet advanced, and perhaps should. With no central agency, these items have been pretty much ignored, but if they were not immediately assigned to a new department, chances are that they would be in time. Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board. The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
