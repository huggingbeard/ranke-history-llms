The Need for Counseling Services 

These are not isolated problems, nor are they unimportant. They are rapidly becoming a part of our everyday situation, and it is the rare citizen who does not know a serviceman who is facing real problems of adjustment, problems that cause him concern. The type of services offered to these men will help to determine whether these problems grow and accumulate and fester in social sores, or whether adequate help is offered in assisting these men to become self-directing competent citizens, capable of managing their lives and their affairs in a manner satisfactory to themselves and to the community. 

It is because the nation is aware of this situation that one hears everywhere a growing talk of counseling services for servicemen. The army is training counselors. The Veterans 

Administration supplies counseling aid. Chaplains and ministers and USO workers are seriously applying themselves to the learning of counseling skills. Selective Service 3

boards are expected to be of assistance. State agencies for veterans are setting up counseling centers. A fresh interest in counseling has grown up in schools and colleges because teachers are increasingly being called upon to assist in the matter of offering help to veterans, particularly in the smaller communities. In short, national, state, and local organizations, government agencies and private agencies, professional workers in education, social work, psychology, and other fields, are all interested in the field of counseling. 

How great will the need for counselors be? No one can with assurance predict the answer, though one committee charged with making an estimate came out with a figure of 10,000 to 15,000 counselors as a minimum. The plain fact is that these thousands of counselors do not exist and that full professional training of any such number could not possibly be completed in time to be of help. 

Because of this situation, there is an urgent need for brief, intensive training of counselors who will be dealing with the vocational, personal, and educational problems of our millions of men and women in the service. To be realistic, this training will need to be planned to turn out counselors with less than a full professional training, but adequately equipped to perform a sound though limited counseling function.

This brings us to the purpose of this little volume. Based on counseling experience with both servicemen and civilians, it endeavors to present a viewpoint and a method that can be acquired by a person with reasonable qualifications for a counseling position. It proposes to develop certain concepts basic to effective counseling and to open the doorway to further reading and study in this field.

Counseling: Wartime and Postwar Two Viewpoints in Counseling 

It may be said at the very beginning that the counseling viewpoint presented in these chapters is a new one. It is likely that it will be sharply different from any counseling which the reader may have done or with which he may be familiar. For that reason it may be well to state in a few words the difference between counseling as it is traditionally understood and counseling as presented here. 

Traditionally, counseling has meant primarily the carrying out of three major functions in endeavoring to help the individual with some problem of adjustment. The first is the gathering of information about the client, collecting all pertinent data that might throw light on his situation. 

The second is the formulation of a diagnosis or explanation of the client's problems, based on the complete picture that has been assembled. The third step is the solution of the difficulty-pointing out to the client the course he should pursue, the steps he should take, to meet his problem. 

The viewpoint presented in this book is decidedly at variance with this concept. Counseling is presented as a way of helping the individual to help himself. The function of the counselor is to make it possible for the client to gain emotional release in relation to his problems and, as a consequence, to think more clearly and more deeply about himself and his situation. It is the counselor's function to provide an atmosphere in which the client, through this exploration of his situation, comes to see himself and his reactions more clearly and to accept his attitudes more fully. On the basis of this insight he is able to meet his life problems more adequately, more independently, more responsibly than before. He has experienced psychological growth in the counseling situation, as well as discovering

for himself a way of adjusting to the realities and demands of life.

The Risks Involved 

Whatever one's offhand reaction to this very brief description of the two viewpoints, it should be evident that the traditional method of counseling should not be undertaken unless the counselor has satisfactory professional preparation in the field of psychology, psychiatry, or social casework. Extensive knowledge is required to judge what information is relevant and significant, and a thorough background in the psychology of personality is necessary to make a sound diagnosis of an individual and his adjustments. Wisdom and experience to a staggering degree are necessary to prescribe possible solutions to the varied problems that arise. Hence, the newcomer to the counseling field should avoid this traditional procedure, since neither his knowledge nor skill are adequate to undertake it and he may cause very serious damage to a maladjusted individual by attempting these procedures.

The client-centered counseling approach advocated in these chapters, however, is a much safer avenue to working with human problems. As the reader will see, it does not demand omniscience on the part of the counselor, since the stress is upon the full use of the strength and capacity for growth within the client. It is the counselor's function to provide an atmosphere of a type that permits the client to work out his own solutions, and while subtle skills and attitudes are called for, the risk of damage to the client is very slight indeed in utilizing this second approach. Hence, it seems especially sound for the wartime counselor.

Individuals are basically alike, whether they are civilian or military.

However, the counselor's experience will gain meaning and usefulness if he begins with a knowledge of certain basic principles underlying behavior. Motivation

Everything a person does is in an effort to fulfill certain needs or motives he has. Each bit of his behavior is directed toward some goal. This is obvious with regard to our physiological needs. Because we are hungry, we eat; because we are thirsty, we drink; because we are tired, we sleep. However, the average person often overlooks the importance of psychological needs. We need to receive and to give affection; we need to feel that we "belong" to some social group; we need to feel that our achievements are worth while; we need a feeling of self-esteem. Whereas the physiological motives of a person are few in number, there is an almost unlimited number of psychological needs that may develop.

Understanding the Individual 

Psychological motives are no less important or urgent than the physiological. Consider the man who endures cold and hunger in order to wait for an opportune time to storm a pillbox, risking his life in the process. He is going directly contrary to his physical needs for warmth, food, and selfprotection. Why? Because his need for his own self-respect, his need for the approval of his platoon, his officers, and his country, his need to revenge his buddies who have been killed are much more important in his behavior than his physiological needs. Especially in adults social, emotional, and personal satisfactions may be far more important than physiological satisfactions. 

Thus far you may be saying, "This is obvious. I understand that everything a person does is aimed at gratifying some need." But now another consideration enters to complicate the picture, and this is that any given motive does not always lead to exactly the same behavior. Any motive may give rise to different kinds of behavior at different times.

Thus, why a person acts as he does, what his motives are, can be understood only by taking into account the kind of person he is and what his life situation is. The counselor must never lose sight of the specific person who confronts him. It is never safe to go by some stereotyped pattern or explanation. It may apply to many people, but may not apply to the client with whom he is speaking. The counselor must be ready to understand the motives that this client has for this behavior, without trying to fit him into some preconceived pattern.

We May Be Unaware of Our Motives 

If we always knew exactly why we behave as we do, life would be much simpler. Sometimes, of course, we do know what our motives are. More often we think we know our motives without being aware of the real motives. That is, quite frequently we are attempting to gratify motives of which we are not even aware. For example, one ex-soldier said that he wished to live in a certain town other than his home town. In a later counseling session, however, he suddenly became aware of his real reason for not wishing to return to his home town. It was that in his absence his fiancée had married somebody else, and without being aware of it he had felt that the situation at home would be too unpleasant for him to face. The reason he gave for staying away was not the real reason behind his decision. In another case an aviation cadet who was strongly motivated to become a flier found himself at a loss to explain why he 10

In the course of counseling, however, he gradually came to see that his instructor showed many of the characteristics that he had objected to in his father. Just as he had earlier rebelled against his father, he now realized that he had been unable to cooperate with the instructor. Thus his attitude toward his instructor was a result of motives of which he was unaware. In another instance a veteran who had been very bitter toward civilians and resentful of his treatment following his return from overseas gradually came to recognize that his bitterness arose in large measure out of his own feelings of inadequacy. 

It was because he was annoyed and disgusted at himself that he had been showing resentment and anger toward others.

An important function of counseling, as we shall see, is to permit the individual to become aware of some of his deeper motivating attitudes of which he has previously not been conscious. Usually the motives of which a person is unaware are those which it would be difficult or painful to admit. In our society these attitudes are often concerned with sexual matters, with desires for aggressive action toward others, or with those ways in which the individual has protected his sense of esteem, the measures by which he has kept himself from "losing face." 

Satisfactory Adjustment

Good adjustment may be said to exist when the physiological and psychological needs of the individual are continually being satisfied to a reasonable degree, and in a reasonably direct fashion. When physical needs are not too difficult to satisfy, when worthwhile work gives a sense of achievement, and friends and family meet the needs for

11

L affectional give-and-take and social response, then the individual is basically well adjusted. 

Other characteristics of good adjustment are a minimum of conflict between motives and a maximum of integrated purposes, ability to face the facts of an unpleasant reality without excessive disruption, the ability to withstand frustration and to work for goals that cannot be immediately achieved. Maladjustment

Maladjustment may likewise be simply defined. The person is maladjusted when the simple satisfaction of basic needs is blocked and more elaborate or devious behavior is initiated in the attempt to satisfy the need. The behavior produces partially satisfying results, or it would not be retained. For example, a soldier is given an unexpected 36hour pass. He phones his wife to come and join him, and she replies that she has other plans. The soldier gets drunk. 

Put in psychological terms, his need for affection and social response is sharply blocked, and he turns to a substitute satisfaction. Another example can be given. Military life is full of situations that block the satisfaction of normal needs. 

As a substitute satisfaction the serviceman pours out much complaint and abuse. It is due to frustrations that "griping" is common in all regimented organizations. 

It is a fact of great importance to the counselor to realize that when the individual is aware of his motives, and aware of the way in which they are being blocked, he can make a much more constructive choice of substitute satisfactions.. 

Growth Toward Maturity 

Not only is it necessary to understand good and poor adjustment, but the counselor should also be acquainted 12

Understanding the Individual with the psychological growth process of human beings. As with any living creature, the development of a person almost inevitably shows certain trends. The few easily gratified motives of the infant expand into the many and complex motives of the adult. The complete dependence of the infant upon other people and upon his environment gives way to the greater independence of the adult. The infant's exclusive preoccupation with himself and his own needs gives way to the adult's interest in other people and their needs, becoming social behavior. The self-love of the child becomes the heterosexual love of the adult. The socially irresponsible behavior of the infant changes to the socially responsible behavior of the adult. All these developments are expressions of the growth impulse common to human beings as members of society. Under appropriate conditions the person will grow and develop into an individual with a more mature and socialized sense of values which enables him to maintain the satisfactory balance between his own demands and the demands of society. 

However, the course of growth is not a simple uncomplicated process, for conflicting tendencies develop as a result of it. Thus, as the person grows he becomes capable of more independent and self-determined action. The increase in independence, however, is accompanied by an increasing sense of isolation, of aloneness. If the individual attempts to recapture the feeling of security and belongingness that he may have known as a child, he must surrender some of his independence. And even this will not be successful, since each stage of development requires that security be gained through new means. Thus, the person increasingly faces the conflict between his striving for independence and his need for security. This conflict can be 13

V easily observed in adolescents. At this stage of development the desire for conformity-for popularity and approval, for belonging to a group is extremely strong. At the same time the trend toward independence rebellion and emancipation from restrictions on the adolescent's freedom-is also strong. One of the problems of living, then, is to work out a satisfactory balance between dependence and independence, between freedom from restraint and security. This can occur only when the individual is free to develop his own standards, to build his own sense of self.

This striving toward maturity in spite of difficulty, this tendency toward growth that exists in each individual, is the motivation that underlies the client's ability to solve his own problems during good counseling. This is the force, the dynamics, upon which counseling depends. Counseling then is a growth experience in which the counselor provides a situation that enables the client's striving toward maturity to proceed, free from the obstructions that have been hampering it.

It may be clear from the above that the person who is likely to profit from counseling help is the person who has an inadequate or only partially satisfying adjustment. The servicemen described in Chapter I are unsatisfied in a number of respects. It is this that motivates them to seek and profit from counseling help.

Perhaps for the first time in his life the client experiences a relationship with another person that is truly permissive and acceptant. The counseling hour comes to represent a period of stability and objectivity in his life, a period in which the stresses and pressures that have prevented him from developing a more mature adjustment cease to be felt. Gradually at first, but to a greater degree later, the client comes to accept his own responsibility for himself as a person. The client ceases to be afraid to examine himself, to explore his personality. And with increasing self-understanding and selfacceptance comes improvement in adjustment. Growing up at its easiest is a somewhat painful process. The pressures of society, which too often fail to take into account the differences between one person and another, make growth even more difficult. It is the task of the counselor to provide a situation in which growth can take place at a more rapid 16

The Attitude of the Nondirective Counselor pace and with less pain than in everyday life. The situation must not contain factors that restrict or inhibit the client's development toward a mature adjustment. 

The counselor's basic responsibility, then, is the establishment of an atmosphere or climate that frees the client from the forces hindering his growth, and that makes possible self-initiated development. Everything the counselor says and does, every facial expression and gesture, contributes to the atmosphere the client feels. Whether the client feels free to make maximum use of the counseling periods depends on the type of atmosphere that he discovers.

With the returned serviceman the counseling climate will be more important than ever. Having lived in an atmosphere of regimentation, restriction, danger, and anxiety, he may be unused to accepting decisions as his responsibility. The major responsibility for his activities has been shouldered by his superiors. The authoritarian atmosphere will inevitably have held in check the positive development toward the acceptance of complete responsibility for his own decisions. From his time in the service the ex-serviceman will have formed habits of expecting decisions to be made for him. Despite possible dangers surrounding his daily existence, the over-all structure of life will have assumed a somewhat predictable pattern. He has not needed to concern himself over what clothes to wear, where and when he will eat, what occupation he will follow, where he will live, who will be his companions, etc. These decisions have been handed to him in terms of army necessities. Now, however, upon separation from the service he must resume responsibility for his behavior. The future is not predetermined. Responsibilities will weigh more heavily upon 17

him. He must face problems that did not immediately confront him in the service. He will again become aware of social expectations, moral codes, family standards, which he laid aside upon entering the service. Perhaps he has a child he has never seen, and the problem of his responsibilities as a father awaits him. Perhaps his wife has been engaged in war work during his absence and wishes to continue working; he may feel loss of self-respect, bitterness, envy at resuming his former job which pays less than his wife has been earning. He may have to decide whether to take more schooling or go directly into a job. There will be problems of adjusting once again to the old surroundings, to family and friends after a life of activity, excitement, and constant change. There will be an infinite variety of problems he must face without "orders from headquarters."

All these problems will become especially complicated for the handicapped or disabled veteran, who may bring with him distorted values and attitudes concerning his role in society, his usefulness, his abilities.

With the ex-serviceman, then, the counseling atmosphere becomes extremely important, not only because it will determine the success of the counseling, but also because it will shape the orientation which the veteran carries over into his everyday life. The behavior of the nondirective counselor constantly expresses and reinforces his faith in the client's ability to cope with his own difficulties. The atmosphere thus established is such that the client comes to recognize his own responsibility for himself and to carry this recognition into everyday life. 

Regardless of what specific techniques the counselor may master, the success of counseling is largely determined by the atmosphere he is able to establish. This, in turn, stems 18

The Attitude of the Nondirective Counselor directly from the attitudes of the counselor. The techniques of nondirective counseling are relatively simple. For most beginning counselors, however, some time is needed before they fully appreciate the importance of their own attitudes toward the client and his problem. In other words, the use of certain counseling techniques merely as techniques leads to a lack of sincerity until the counselor himself develops a genuine appreciation of the process of therapeutic counseling. This emotional sincerity can be gained only through experience, but it will be well to attempt to describe the basic orientation that lies behind successful counseling. 

RESPECT FOR THE INTEGRITY OF THE PERSON 

The counselor's attitude of respect for the integrity of the client contributes much to the counseling climate. The individuality that the military services have been forced to strip from the serviceman is restored. No longer is he just another G. I. Joe. Instead he again becomes Bill Hanks or 

Harry Williams. In contrast to marching troops who are “men without faces," the client begins to resume selfhood as a specific, unique individual. As he begins to feel the respect of the counselor for his integrity, as he begins to think of himself more as a person and less as just another serviceman, it becomes easier for him to face his problems. 

The counselor's basic orientation of respect for the integrity of the person manifests itself through more specific attitudes. 

1. Respect for the Personal Autonomy of the Client.-The counselor feels that each person has the right to make his own decisions. He has the right to seek and make use of assistance, but he also has the right to refuse help. He is responsible for his own life, and every precaution should be 19

taken to build up this sense of self-responsibility rather than to tear it down. 

2. Belief in the Person's Capacity for Adjustment.—The counselor realizes that most individuals have an enormous capacity for adaptation and for readjustment. It is the strength within the client, not the strength within the counselor, upon which therapeutic counseling relies. 

3. Respect for the Whole Person.—The counselor's atten tion is centered upon the whole man, the entire person, not upon particular fragments. That is, rather than being solely concerned with this man's problem of vocational placement, or his adjustment to his wife, or his problem of financial security, the client-centered counselor is oriented toward both the positive and negative strivings—the feelings, hopes, and worries-of the entire person. Nothing the client says is disregarded. Instead of attempting to force the client to reveal some sexual problem, or some problem of adjustment with his parents, the nondirective counselor assumes that the client alone can lead the way to an understanding of his difficulty. People cannot be nicely pigeonholed into discrete categories according to some type of emotional problem. The nondirective counselor is concerned with the adjustment of the person and not with the solution to some specific type of problem that happens to be of most interest to the counselor. 

4. Tolerance and Acceptance of the Client's Difference.- 

Far from believing that all people will or should feel, think, and act as he does, the counselor expects to find the client different from himself. He realizes that he cannot expect to diagnose the client and prescribe a course of action for him. The differences between them preclude this. Any advice or suggestions that he might give would be based 20

The Attitude of the Nondirective Counselor upon his own values and not upon those of the client. No person can solve another's problems for him. Since he expects differences, the counselor is tolerant of them and can expect them unemotionally. Being interested solely in the welfare of the client, the counselor discards moralistic and judgmental attitudes. The counselor endeavors to be totally acceptant of the client as a person different from himself. As he endeavors to accept the client's attitudes as a natural part of this other person, he avoids being shocked at anything the client may say or do. He restrains himself from showing surprise, disapproval, or even strong approval. The counselor is not concerned with judging the client in the light of his (the counselor's) moral standards but with helping him to understand and accept himself realistically. 

5. Desire to Help the Client Understand and Accept Himself. The counselor realizes that people learn (in such a way that it becomes a living part of them) only what they want to learn. The attitudes described above all implement this final attitude. As the client becomes aware of the respect, dignity, seriousness, and tolerance with which he is treated, he is enabled to express deeper and more intimate attitudes and feelings. These, then, can be accepted and integrated into his own personality, thus providing the basis for later more successful adjustment. The counselor's behavior is all aimed at helping the client himself gain these understandings-never at arriving at a diagnosis of his own which, although it may be correct, does not help the client. 

It is the client's problem to understand himself and assimilate this knowledge, not the problem of the counselor. Perhaps this last attitude seems somewhat obvious, but it is surprising how easily some counselors unknowingly assume 21

an entirely different attitude. In some cases it is the satisfaction of certain motives of the counselor which is uppermost. It feeds his ego to play the role of an all-knowing 

Jehovah, of a stern and moralistic father figure, of a tender and solicitous mother substitute. The counselor must never let his own personal needs and motives enter into the situation to obscure the goal of therapeutic counseling. 

The outgrowth, then, of the attitudes of the counselor that have been sketched above is a truly permissive relationship. A climate is established in which every expression of attitude and feeling is permitted, but none is demanded. 

The client, after testing the counselor's intentions, comes to realize that he is free to talk of anything he wishes without criticism, disapproval, praise, or suggestions. The social pressures of everyday life that force him to hide many of his innermost thoughts and motives are removed. He is helped to feel free to say anything, to discuss any attitude, to bring to the surface his repressed antagonisms and hostilities-even toward the counselor-and to reveal also the very tentative positive impulses that he discovers within himself, but he is not compelled to express any of these things. The decision as to whether to discuss a topic must rest with the client. He is as free to withhold as he is to express. The permissiveness of the counseling relationship thus provides a predictable, stable situation in which the client can come to accept the responsibility for facing his problems fully.

The counseling atmosphere is built upon respect for the person, tolerance, and acceptance of differences, faith in the person's ability to accept responsibility for his own conduct, and freedom for growth toward maturity. It is perhaps no accident that this emphasis in counseling has reached its fruition in America.

Defining the Counseling Relationship 

When the client comes for help, he has only the vaguest of ideas as to how he will be treated. If he does have some definite preconception about what the counselor will do, it is probable that he expects the counselor to give him some simple formula to apply. "Here's what you are to do—A, B, and C. This will solve your problem." Unfortunately, perhaps, solving personal problems is not so simple. The client's participation is by far the most important element in the process. It is precisely to help the client give his whole-hearted participation that the counselor may need to define the relationship. The client must understand the method of procedure the freedoms and limits in it—if he is to decide whether he wishes to make full use of it. 

1. Defining in Terms of Behavior.—The best way to help the client to an understanding of the nature of the counsel- 25

ing relationship is to let it grow directly from the behavior that he and the counselor engage in. The client does not always need to be told in words that the responsibility for solving his problem rests with him. He can gather it from the counselor's conduct. The client finds that he is the one who decides when the appointments will be. The counselor informs him as to what hours are available, but the decision as to when he shall come, and how often, is left to the client. The client finds that he may come on time for his appointments or may come late if he chooses, that he can close a counseling contact early if he so desires, that he can avoid talking about his problems for an hour by speaking about neutral topics, etc. In short, the client discovers that he is the one who sets the pace of treatment, who decides what topics to discuss, who decides what action to take outside the counseling hour. Never should he get the feeling that the counselor is shouldering the responsibility for his problems. This feeling arises when the counselor presumes to know what should be discussed by asking questions and probing for information, when the counselor gives suggestions and advice as to what is to be done, when the counselor makes the decisions as to frequency and dates of appointments, etc. In short, from every minute aspect of the counselor's conduct the client will build a conception of the nature of the relationship. The best way to define the relationship, then, is by behavior, which constitutes direct proof of the democratic intentions of the counselor. 

2. Defining Verbally. Some clients, however, will attempt so strongly to shift the responsibility for solving their problem onto the counselor that it becomes necessary to state in words the nature of the relationship. Clients of this type will either sit back and wait for the counselor to 26

The Counselor's Methods ask questions until he reaches a diagnosis, or else they will ask outright for advice and suggestions. In these cases, it is often helpful during the first contact for the counselor to describe the method of procedure. In effect, he might say something like this, or might intersperse ideas of this sort in the contact: "Perhaps I ought to give you some idea of how we go about things here. I can't solve your problems for you. No person can solve another's problems for him. 

After all, anything I would suggest would be based upon what is good for me. You can be the best judge of what you want to do. Now, what I can offer is this. We find that most people can handle their own problems if they get to understand themselves a little better. Sometimes we don't know what it is that we really want. Sometimes we want something but don't understand why. Well, after exploring yourself you may find that before long you understand yourself a whole lot better. And then it is much easier for you to decide what is the best thing to do much better than any other person ever could. Now the time you have with me here is yours to use as you please. You may talk about anything that you feel is important. You'll find that in the long run we really go faster when you point the way. After all, by asking questions and probing around I would merely bring up a lot of unrelated material that would confuse you. 

This is your hour. Are there some things you would like to talk about?" At first, perhaps, the beginning counselor will find himself depending quite a bit upon verbal definition of the relationship. However, after he has had some experience and has gained a sense of security so that he is more at ease in the counseling situation, he will find it less and less necessary to depend upon verbal descriptions. 

3. Limits.—The permissiveness of the situation has been 27

stressed, but we should not overlook the fact that there are certain limits which occur also. Just as in everyday life it is difficult to conceive of a situation that grants complete and absolute freedom, so in the counseling situation there must be limits against which the client can test himself. There are two main limits that occur in working with adolescent or adult clients. First, the client should not be allowed to prolong a contact beyond the accustomed time. If the regular length of the contact is an hour, and the client talks "all around the bush" for 55 minutes and then begins to come to some important material, the counselor reminds him when the hour is at a close. Although the counselor may wish to continue for half an hour more, it is not helpful to the client to allow him to do so. He gains the most help by learning that the counseling period is a stable, predictable situation, with definite limits-a clear-cut situation that he can use in his own way. Learning to accept and make use of limits is an important part of the client's growth process. 

The second limit is that the relation between the client and counselor remains purely professional. Although the client might like to take the counselor to dinner, or might invite him over to meet his wife and children, the counselor will wisely refuse by explaining that his relation to the client is most useful and valuable as a counselor, a neutral person who does not enter into his home life or his social life. The client will be able to see that it will be easier for him to talk to a counselor who maintains this type of counseling relationship.

Helping the Client Get Started 1. At the Beginning of a Contact.-Beginning counselors often ask, "But how do we get started? What do I say after 28

The Counselor's Methods the client comes in and is seated?" The counselor's opening should be cordial and nondirective. The simpler it is the better. Openings like the following are effective: “Do you want to tell me what you had in mind in coming to see me?" "Well, what's on your mind today?," "What would you like to discuss today?," "Well, what have you thought about since our last meeting?," "This is your time; what would you like to talk about?" Each of these leaves the client free to discuss anything he desires. Sometimes the client finds it difficult to get started and says so. Nothing seems to come up to talk about. After recognizing his feelings, if he still cannot go on, the counselor might comment to this effect, "Perhaps there is nothing you care to talk about now. If so, that is quite all right. You don't have to talk about anything. We can sit and enjoy a smoke." A willingness on the counselor's part for the client to be silent is, interestingly enough, one of the best aids in helping the client to express himself. 

2. During the Contact.-Sometimes after the contact is well under way, the client may remain silent for a period of time. How the counselor handles this will largely be a question of how many previous contacts there have been. During a first contact, when the establishment of a congenial relationship is occurring, it may be desirable to prevent a pause from becoming too awkward. At this stage of counseling, overlong pauses may embarrass the client, who feels that the counselor is waiting for him to go on. The counselor can usually avoid such awkwardness by saying, after it becomes clear that the client is not going on, "Would you care to explore that topic further?" This gives the client the opportunity to go more thoroughly into the same topic if he wishes, and yet it leaves the way open for him to refuse and 29

go on to a new topic. Whatever the lead that is used, the choice of topic should be left up to the client. However, as counseling continues the relationship between client and counselor should be firmly enough established so that the counselor can merely wait for the client spontaneously to break the pause. Although this seems simple, it is actually very important. Often the client is, so to speak, taking a big breath before plunging into some topic that is of great importance to him. The pause signifies that it is not easy for him to bring what he is thinking out into the open. If the counselor waits in relaxed fashion, and avoids breaking into the pause, he will enable the client to face this decision and handle it by himself. However, if the counselor interrupts the pause, he may direct the client's thinking in another direction and prevent him from coming to grips with an important problem at that time.

This section may be summarized by saying that during earlier contacts the counselor will probably use some sort of nondirective lead when the client pauses for an awkward period of time. During later contacts, however, the counselor will make use of friendly waiting and silence as a technique of allowing the client to determine the direction of the discussion.

Responding to the Client's Statements 

The techniques we have discussed under defining the counseling relationship and helping the client get started comprise only a small part of the counselor's behavior. By far the major portion of his activity will be concerned with responding to statements made by the client. How smoothly and successfully the client progresses is in large measure the result of the skill of the counselor in responding. Under- 30

The Counselor's Methods lying the counselor's responses are several basic considerations. The response should be such that (1) it enables the client to continue to express himself freely and fully, without fear or defensiveness; (2) the client is helped to see his attitudes and himself more clearly, without interposing any element of counselor evaluation; and (3) it helps the client to accept his own strivings-positive or negative, socially approved or disapproved-as really his. There are two main types of counselor responses that fulfill these functions. 

1. Simple Acceptance.—In expressing simple acceptance of what the client has been saying, the counselor merely responds with "Yes," "I see," "I understand," "Mm-hm," or some other response of this sort. This seems absurdly simple, but it has been shown that this response is most used when the client is delving most deeply into himself and painfully bringing forth significant material. As is apparent, the function of simple acceptance is to indicate that the counselor is following what the client is saying, understands his feelings, and accepts them. The client is enabled to express himself freely when he sees that material which arouses feelings of guilt or shame in him or which makes him feel foolish and silly is easily accepted by the counselor. By itself, simple acceptance can effectively reduce the emotional tension which the client may be feeling. It facilitates the talking out of his worries, fears, and desires and thus helps remove the emotional blinders that have prevented the client from coming face to face with the problem. 

2. Reflection of Feeling.—The most important of all counselor techniques, however, is that of recognizing and reflecting the emotionalized attitudes of the client-serving as a mirror by which the client may see himself. When the serviceman, in discussing his difficulty in adjusting to his 31

wife, tells how ungrateful and inconsiderate she is, the counselor may reply, "You feel that she is pretty selfish."

Or to the serviceman who is expressing his regret over engaging in extramarital sexual activity while in the Army, the counselor may say, "You feel guilty and deeply ashamed about this."

This technique, which is simplicity itself in principle, is extremely difficult for counselors to acquire. It runs counter to their previous experience. They are required to withhold their own judgment, their own evaluation, their own diagnosis, and (what they conceive to be) the solution to the situation. The counselor's attention is completely concentrated upon the problem of "What feeling is he expressing?" At no time does the counselor become so involved in the factual details of the client's statement that he fails to ask himself what it is that the client is expressing in the way of feelings and attitudes. No matter how deeply negative, or how absurd, or how unfair to other people the client's statements may seem to be, the counselor endeavors to reflect the client's feelings as accurately and fairly as possible.

Perhaps an example of two different types of response will help to clarify what is meant by reflecting feeling. 

SERVICEMAN: "You see, I came from a home in which we never expressed affection. We were all pretty unemotional, I guess. Well, ever since we've been married, my wife hasn't quite understood that. She thinks I ought to be saying sweet things all the time, but somehow I just don't know how." 

COUNSELOR: "How long have you been married?”

SERVICEMAN: "Three years.'

It is comparatively easy to respond to the client's feelings about other people, situations, and events. However, the beginning counselor frequently overlooks feelings that the client may express about himself or about the counselor. 

To give some idea of the various kinds and trends of feelings that the counselor must be constantly ready to recognize and reflect, it would be well to outline them. First of all, feelings may be positive, negative, or ambivalent. By ambivalent is meant conflicting; the client feels opposite ways at the same time. Thus ambivalent attitudes may be expressed which will call for counselor responses of this type: "You like your father a great deal, yet some things he does disgust you." "You want to go to college, yet you feel that you ought to be earning your own living.' "You want to go back to your home town, and yet you don't want to." Secondly, feelings may be directed toward the client himself, toward the counselor, or toward other 33

people. An example of a response to ambivalent feelings toward the counselor might be the following: "You feel that we're not getting anywhere in these conferences, and yet you have the feeling that somehow the problem is becoming clearer.” An example of a response to ambivalent feelings toward the self: "You dislike your tendency to show off, and yet you like having other people pay attention to you." Ambivalent feelings toward the counselor or toward the self are perhaps the kind most frequently overlooked by inexperienced counselors. Research has indicated that negative and ambivalent feelings toward the self usually occur most frequently in the early contacts, while positive feelings about the self increase toward the end in successful counseling. 

One caution should be mentioned here. The counselor should not go beyond the feeling which is being expressed. 

Although he may think that he sees some hidden implication, he must not anticipate the client. It is the serviceman who must make the progress, not the counselor. If an attitude has not been openly expressed, the counselor should not risk forcing the client to become defensive by going beyond what has been said. Notice this example. 

SERVICEMAN: "You see, my father died when I was only two, and so I was raised by my mother. I think that has had a big effect on me." 

COUNSELOR: "You feel that your mother spoiled you." 

SERVICEMAN: "No, that's not what I mean."

The counselor has gone far beyond what has been expressed. The feeling behind the statement is somewhat vague, to be sure. However, if he could not recognize it, the counselor would have done better merely to reply, "I see,' 99 34

However, a simple but accurate reflection of the feeling such as, "You feel that things would have been different if your father had lived," would probably have been quite effective.

The counselor must be careful to wait for an appropriate opening before he makes a response. It is rarely wise to interrupt the client and usually better to wait for a pause. 

Some Specific Problems 

1. Reassurance. When a serviceman is revealing how much he dislikes his present self, how difficult his situation is, how hopeless the future looks, etc., it is very easy for the counselor to make a polite response of reassurance. "I'm sure that things will get better." "Surely you're not as bad as that; you're just being modest." "Cheer up, don't worry about it." "I'm sure your wife will love you in spite of your scar." However, for maximum growth such reassurance is not only unnecessary, but may be actually harmful. In the first place, the client may develop the feeling that the counselor does not really understand his situation. After having poured out his feelings, to be told that "things will work out all right" is to be told that the problem is unimportant, does not exist. Such a response certainly does not make it easy for the client to go more deeply into his feelings. 

Secondly, such reassurance does not really reassure. A pep talk may have momentary effects, but in terms of gaining long-run results it produces little change. Self-confidence develops from within, not from without. Merely because the counselor can feel confident and optimistic does not mean that the serviceman will feel likewise. The client develops 35

his own assurance from seeing that his most painful revelations about his socially unacceptable feelings are accepted without shock by the counselor. 

Closely related to reassurance is praise. When the counselor praises the client for his behavior or his attitudes, he is taking over the responsibility for the problem. By imposing his own values upon the situation, as he does when he expresses approval or praise, he prevents the spontaneous, self-initiated growth of the client. Instead he may be forcing change in the direction of his own values, and before long he may find that the client is playing up to his (the counselor's) opinion rather than working out the problem. 

2. Questions by the Client. It is difficult for inexperienced counselors to see that a direct question or request for advice by the client also may express feeling. Note how the counselor recognizes and reflects the feeling in this exchange. 

SERVICEMAN: "Well, that's the whole story, I guess. What do you think I should do about it?" 

COUNSELOR: "You feel I may have the answer."

Here the serviceman has come face to face with an attitude that he probably was unaware of. As he realizes this and accepts it, he will be able to go on to take the responsibility for the decision. In most cases, the type of response illustrated will suffice to clarify the serviceman's feelings. 

However, some may press the question again. In this case, the counselor may need to define the relationship again (see above, pages 26–27). The ability to handle such direct questions skillfully in the fashion that will prove of most help to the client comes from experience. However, it will help to realize that in many cases the client does not really want an answer to his question. He may be asking the question 36

3. The Man Who Comes Unwillingly.-In most instances, if counseling services are properly organized, the man comes voluntarily for assistance. There may, however, be exceptions, as when a foreman sends a man to an industrial counselor, or a college administrative official sends a serviceman to a student counselor. This situation may be handled by the same basic approach as any other, but the problem needs special consideration.

The counselor will do well to remember that no one is effectively counseled against his will. To see some counselors in action, one would gather that they believe in pouring counseling over the hapless head of the unwilling counselee. This is futile. The only hope of being effective is to make it possible for the man to bring out matters of concern to himself, on which he wishes help.

Thus when a man is sent to the counselor, it is well to 38

The Counselor's Methods reflect all the attitudes he expresses, particularly those of doubt or suspicion or antagonism in regard to coming. The counselor will also need to make it plain that although the client was forced to come, and hence his coming is not a matter under his control, this is not true of his use of the counseling time. He is completely free to use it or not, and to use it for any purpose that would have meaning to him, not necessarily for the purpose for which he was sent. He was not responsible for coming; but responsibility for the counseling situation is freely given him.

It is particularly important that the counselor should be alert to items of concern that this unwilling client mentions. 

Too often the counselor is waiting for "the" problem to be mentioned the dispute with the foreman or the failure in course work for which the man was sent. These may not be the framework in which the individual defines his problem. 

He may express resentment at being sent in, or concern about some issue remote from "the" problem as conceived by the person sending him in. These immediate concerns, no matter what they may be, are the material around which counseling begins.

4. Dealing with the Man with a Disability.—Counselors of considerable experience often throw overboard their own self-discipline when they deal with hospitalized or disabled servicemen. Instead of practicing the self-restraints that have been described in this and the preceding chapter, they let themselves indulge in attitudes of pity. Such attitudes may provide an outlet for the emotional needs of the counselor, but they are most certainly of no help to the man.

In counseling with the disabled or handicapped man, if he has any chance of being restored to normal civilian life, we shall do best to adopt those attitudes which build his independence. We should, in brief, follow the same procedures that are helpful in any problem of adjustment. The viewpoint and approach described in this pamphlet is as 41

satisfactory for the disabled man as for the man who has not been injured. It is as sound for the mildly neurotic man as for the individual who has never shown any tendency to break down.

The reason for this is easy to understand. Any individual who has a problem has certain difficult adjustments to make to reality and also the problem of finding some contentment within himself. The man who is only five feet four in height, or the man who has insufficient mentality to succeed in college, has a problem precisely parallel to that of the man who has lost an arm. The husband who wants to do something constructive about his marriage, although both he and his wife have many faults, is facing basically the same kind of a problem as the man who is meeting life with an artificial limb or an impaired heart. In other words the aim of all counseling is to assist the individual to accept those limitations, within and without, that cannot be changed and to take positive and constructive action to create a satisfying life out of those elements that he can control. 

This concept applies to the disabled man as much as to the healthy man.

I take notes?" The answer, of course, depends upon the purpose and convenience of the counselor. If he is interested in improving his counseling skill, then complete notes are invaluable. The best form to use is some system that records the counselor's responses as well as the client's statements. 

Then it will be possible to analyze previous contacts with a view to determining blunders that may be avoided in the future. "But won't it disturb the serviceman if I write down everything that takes place?" Not at all. If the counselor is perfectly open and sincere about his desire to make a record 42

The Counselor's Methods of the contacts for later study, then asking permission to take notes and explaining that it will be helpful is usually sufficient. As a matter of fact, note taking often has a desirable effect on the conferences. It indicates a respect for what the serviceman is saying and thus brings an added seriousness of purpose to the contacts. It makes the serviceman feel that every effort is being made to understand him. Further, just as a matter of technique, the inexperienced counselor will be less apt to blunder too quickly into a response or to interrupt a statement of the client if he is accustomed to transcribing the counseling session. Long pauses, instead of becoming embarrassing, provide him with an opportunity to fill in his notes and thus help him make use of silences as an aid to promoting growth. 

6. Length of Contacts.—This is a matter to be decided by the convenience of the counselor and the client. An hour is usually quite satisfactory. It might be emphasized again, however, that the most important feature of the length of the contact is that the time limit should be clearly understood by the client and adhered to by both parties. That is, if for some reason, half-hour contacts are more desirable, very little difference will probably result if the client comes to expect just a half hour-no more and no less. There is reason to believe that the client adjusts himself to work within whatever limit may be decided upon. The important point, then, is that whatever the length of the contact it should be consistent, clearly understood, and carefully followed. 

7. Length of Interval between Contacts.-This is a matter for the client to decide. Some highly motivated persons who are under a great deal of stress may request several contacts a week. More frequently, the counseling sessions are 43

about a week apart. The counselor should not feel that he is wasting time if he does not see the client every day. As he will soon discover, assimilation, organization, and interpretation of the material brought out in the interview take place during the interval between contacts. The client himself can best determine what interval will help him progress to the optimum. 

8. Number of Contacts Needed.-This also will be decided by the client. When he no longer needs the counselor, he will say so. Naturally, the number of contacts will depend upon the seriousness of the problem, the degree of stress on the client, etc. The counselor should never attempt to prolong contacts. If he asks at the end of the session each time, "Would you like to come in again?," the time will come when the client responds, "No-I think I can handle it from here on by myself," or perhaps says that he will come just once more. That is, the client himself will terminate the contacts. 

9. What Possible Harm Can Nondirective Counseling Pro duce? As long as the counselor adheres carefully to the techniques and attitudes outlined in this manual, there is little likelihood that he can harm any client. Not every case will be a success. Naturally, in the course of gaining experience some cases will be bungled. In some instances, the counselor may be so inaccurate or clumsy in his responses that he impedes progress until the client gives up the treatment. However, the counselor will not aggravate any existing problems or produce new ones. The techniques are so thoroughly rooted in the responsibility of the client for his own progress that the counselor cannot do damage. Except for certain minor aspects, the techniques used by the counselor in the last interview are the same ones used at the beginning of the contacts. That is, since the client leads the way through the treatment series, there is no need for any change of behavior on the part of the counselor. He merely continues to accept each new revelation and to recognize and reflect the feeling behind the statements of the client. It cannot be stressed too strongly that it is the client, not the counselor, who grows and makes progress. 46

The Development and Growth of the Client 

The characteristic progression on the part of the client can be summarized in five stages: (1) The client comes for help; (2) he expresses emotionalized attitudes freely; (3) this leads to the development of insights; (4) these result in positive planning and actions; (5) the client terminates the contacts. If counseling runs its complete course, these five stages can almost inevitably be seen to occur. However, it should not be thought that these are separate and distinct stages with clearly defined boundaries. On the contrary, they are merely general zones of reference-merging into one another—which guide the counselor in following the general onward progress of the client. 

The Client Comes for Help 

Most counselors who are working with servicemen will probably find that their circumstances are such that it is voluntary whether their assistance is sought. That is, the initiative is left up to the serviceman himself. If such is not the case, the counselor will be wise to organize his function in such a way that his services as a counselor are a voluntary or optional part of the program with which he is connected. 

This is wise for a simple reason. Successful counseling is not very probable if the serviceman is not under enough stress to recognize that he has a problem with which he needs help. The best test as to whether he is feeling sufficient stress to benefit from counseling is simply that he has come on his own, voluntarily, to utilize the services of the counselor. If a serviceman knows that a trained counselor is available to help him work through his problems and yet does not take advantage of this opportunity, it is a reasonably good indication that he is not yet ready to use the counseling experience. To force such a person to come in 47

for counseling by making it a prerequisite for other parts of a guidance or rehabilitation program is to place an obstacle in the way of the most favorable counseling relationship. However, if a counselor does find himself forced to work under such restrictions, he can do an adequate job if he develops skill in handling the initial interview in such a way that the serviceman can become acquainted with a permissive relationship that leaves the decision up to him as to whether or not he wishes to continue (see Chapter IV, pages 38-41).

In most cases, however, the counselor will probably be confronted with a serviceman whose request for help is selfinitiated. This is the first positive step toward his own development. He has made the first progress in the direction toward a mature adjustment when he recognizes that his situation is unsatisfactory as it is and consequently takes action intended to improve it-even though it may hurt his pride to admit that he needs help. 

Expression of Feelings 

After the preliminaries are over and the client begins to understand the relation that is to exist between himself and the counselor, he probably will begin by stating some problem. Perhaps it will be a description of his lack of confidence in himself, or his inability to sleep at night, of his quarrels with his wife, of his uncertainty about what kind of job to look for, etc. Any of an almost infinite number of problems may serve to get him started. As he amplifies this, the client is well launched into an exploration of his feelings. Finding that the counselor shows understanding and acceptance of his disclosures, the client goes more and more deeply into his feelings about the various situations or 48

The Development and Growth of the Client people concerned with his problem. Whether the feeling is negative, positive, or ambivalent, the counselor shows the same calm acceptance.

This stage of the counseling contacts might be thought of as a period of release. The feelings and emotions that have been bottled up inside the client are allowed to come spilling into the open. In most cases the client expresses primarily negative and ambivalent feelings in the early conferences. 

That is, he tells about things he dislikes, which irritate him, which disgust him, which bother him, which worry him; or he discusses things about which he is not sure how he feels, that he at once likes and dislikes. As he gets rid of these negative feelings, he experiences some relief from the tension and pressure that he felt before. Finding that these attitudes can be accepted by the counselor, he becomes able to accept them himself. No longer is it necessary for him to hide these feelings or to deny that he has them. He now faces them squarely and finds that they have lost much of their flavor of guilt, shame, or inferiority. In other words, the client finds himself relieved of the confusing tensions that prevented him from coming squarely to grips with the real problem.

Perhaps it seems peculiar that the problem with which the client comes in is not usually the true or really basic difficulty. It may seem odd that the client does not know what is the cause of his troubles. And yet it is much the same with a patient visiting a physician. The patient complains of a stomach-ache or of a pressure in the chest. It is only after examination that the basic cause of the difficulty is discovered. Adequate medical treatment aims to improve the physical condition of the whole organism, not just to remove the symptoms. Similarly, in the counseling relation- 49

ship the serviceman will usually begin by discussing symptoms the signs that indicate that something is wrongand not the true source of the difficulty itself. After the stress is somewhat relieved by free expression of feelings, the serviceman can go on more easily to explore the situation in which he finds himself. It should not be thought that releasing feelings is a mere preparation for treating the basic difficulty. When the counselor skillfully recognizes and accepts the client's feelings, he is making it possible for the serviceman to come to grips with the problem in the only way possible in terms of feeling. This process might be likened to removing colored glasses from the client's eyes. 

At first his perception of his situation is distorted by the emotional attitudes that keep him under tension and stress. 

As a result, his behavior may often be inappropriate may get him into situations that further increase the stress he feels. Then gradually, as his feelings are talked out, they become less and less important to him. As this happens, he is enabled to see himself and his environment in a truer perspective.

Development of Insight 

The ability to understand oneself and one's relation to the environment is referred to as insight. After the stage of release or free expression of feelings comes the gradual development of insight. The most important part of this growth is the increased recognition and acceptance of the real self. That is, it often seems as if the client has been wearing a mask, a false self, that he has come to adopt through the pressures of society in the person of his family, the army, his friends, etc. In response to the demands of society, the individual has submerged much of his own urge 50

The Development and Growth of the Client for self-expression. The circumstances in which he has lived may have prevented him from reaching a satisfactory integration between his personal demands and society's demands (see Chapter II). Unaware of the forces that lie behind his behavior, the individual has built up an inaccurate picture of himself. Because he has had to repress and to deny many of his feelings about himself as well as about those surrounding him, the client has often developed a distorted conception of the kind of person he really wants to be.

This was true in the case of Private Westwood. At the beginning of counseling he spent his time discussing the kind of person he was.* He did not like dancing, jazz music, popular reading, etc. Before the war he had gone away to college, but after a year there he had dropped out. "The fellows were always after me to go out on a party with them. 

I couldn't get any time to do what I wanted unless I just got rude and shut myself in my room." He then decided deliberately to set about developing certain social skills—such as dancing-in order to be able to fit in with other people. This stage of developing awareness and acceptance of our real self-of what we really want, of why we have been acting as we have is what we refer to as insight. No longer is the client afraid, ashamed, disgusted to admit the kind of person he really is. The permissive, accepting atmosphere makes it possible for him to see his inner weaknesses and strengths in a new and different perspective. When this happens when the client develops insight into his personality-there is a reorganization of old facts that occurs. "I see now why I resented my younger brother so muchwhy we have never been able to get along together. It was because I was envious of the attention my mother showered on him. It wasn't because of anything he did. And all this time I thought it was because he was so unfair and mean.’ 

The person begins to reorient his attitude toward many of his past experiences. He realizes why he acted as he didlong after the action took place. That is to say, as insight develops the client comes to perceive old facts in a new 99 53

relationship—or begins to perceive new and important relations, which were formerly overlooked, among past experiences.

As the person becomes able to recognize and accept his real self, then, he finds his past and present experiences falling into a new pattern; he finds himself approaching his life situation with a new perspective. Following directly from this progress is another characteristic of true insight: the possible choices in the situation become clarified. From the vague, confused frame of mind in which he began counseling, the client moves on to the state in which he is able to see that there are definite choices before him. "Either I go on hiding from myself and remain dissatisfied and restless, or I take myself in hand and develop some social skills." "I must either spend my life pleasing my parents' demands at my own expense, or else I must make a break with them and take the chance that they will become reconciled to it." It is difficult to say what the choices may be, but gradually the client comes to discover that instead of being in a situation about which he can do nothing he is facing a choice about which he must make a decision. And often he finds that behind all the symptoms and minor problems is the basic factor—that for one reason or another he has not wanted to take the responsibility for making the decision. So the alternatives between which he must choose have been disguised, covered up, and hidden.

Some of the elements of insight, then, are (1) recognition and emotional acceptance of the real attitudes and desires of the self, (2) a clearer understanding of the causes behind one's behavior, (3) a fresh perception of the life situationold facts are interpreted in a new frame of reference—and (4) clarification of the decisions that must be made and the possible courses of action. 54

The Development and Growth of the Client 

Unfortunately, insight is not something that the counselor can give the client. The client must fashion it for himself. Self-understanding and self-acceptance cannot be transmitted in the form of words; they must be developed from the interplay of feelings between the client and the counselor. Although it is common knowledge that each man is most partial to his own ideas, believes most thoroughly in his own opinions, and only with reluctance will change his views in response to the insistence of somebody else, yet most beginning counselors persist in believing that all they have to do is to explain the client to himself and the information will be completely assimilated and carried over into behavior. And yet such attempts by the counselor usually have the opposite effect. Nobody enjoys the suspicion that somebody else understands him better than he understands himself. Most people like to believe that they understand themselves better than anybody else possibly can. This feeling of the client must be respected. He must be allowed to develop his insight at his own pace so that he never feels threatened by the process. Only insofar as the client's new perception of himself sinks in emotionally, becomes part of him, is assimilated in terms of feelings and attitudes, will it carry over into his overt behavior. The client may never verbalize the full and detailed relationships that the counselor perceives, but this is not necessary. It is not what the client can put into words that is important—the feelings and attitudes he expresses and the behavior he shows are the crucial test of insight.

Positive Steps 

Concomitantly with, or following from, the development of insight, positive steps toward the solution of the problem situation begin to occur. As the possible courses of action La 55

become clarified, the client begins tentatively to consider what steps he must take. Later he reports on successful actions. Here again, the self-discipline of the counselor is called into play. The temptation is very great to encourage certain decisions and to discourage other choices. However, the counselor must continue to accept and reflect feelings behind the client's statement of choices. Even though the client's decision may appear to be incorrect to the counselor, it is the client who has the right to decide. Perhaps after further consideration of it the client discards the choice. 

Or perhaps he carries it into action. In either case it results from his own coming to grips with the problem situation in his own terms. As he develops case experience, the counselor will probably be surprised by the number of decisions which at first appear incorrect or inadequate to him but which are carried out very successfully and satisfactorily by the client. 

This suggests that the intention with which a choice is followed out may be more important than the choice itself. 

That is, having made his own decision, the client has confidence and faith in it, which in turn will affect his execution of it. He has much more of this confidence in his own choice than he would have in a ready-made course of action forced upon him by the counselor.

The first positive steps are not usually strikingly dramatic, but they are extremely important for what they symbolize. Thus, for Private Westwood to decide that he would learn to dance represented the beginning of a new self. Henceforth he would be the person that his new understanding of himself had disclosed. One of the ways to be this new self was to develop new social skills. And so the importance of one small decision reached far beyond its immediate appearance. When Harry Williams first appeared, he was 56

In the course of the conference he explained that although he possessed an adequate wardrobe of civilian clothes he could not bring himself to wear them. "It's not because I haven't got any civilian clothes. I've got a whole closet full, but I just can't wear them—not even around the house. I was never self-conscious in uniform. But this civilian stuff, I don't understand it. I can't seem to find out what it's all about." Later it became apparent that he was worried by what men in uniform would think of him if they could see that he was only a civilian. Five years in the Army had made too great a change in his way of living. After talking out these and other serious attitudes he appeared at his next conference in a neat double-breasted blue suit with an army discharge pin and silver wings on the lapel. He mentioned that it was the first time he had been able to get into civilian clothes. Here again is a small step, but a very important one in that it symbolized a change in outlook, an acceptance of his status as a discharged serviceman, as a civilian with the Army behind him, whether he wanted it so or not. The first positive steps will frequently be of such apparently trivial character. When placed in their proper context, however, in the total life situation of the clientit is usually fairly easy to see their significance for him. It is the symbolic value of the act that is important, not the act itself.

The situation is quite analagous, again, with recuperation from a disease. When the patient's temperature finally begins to drop after a prolonged crisis, for example, that change is one symptom of a changing condition of the total organism. The significance of that simple change is much greater than a mere indication that the temperature is 57

decreasing. Tentative positive actions are symptoms of successful progress, just as the problems the client first considers are symptoms of maladjustment. 

Of course, the client may report more striking steps such as reconciliation with his wife, a dramatic break with his parents, securing a job through his own efforts, taking his son for a camping trip, etc. The important feature is thatthe client has done something—has actually, on his own initiative and motivation, taken a positive step forward in an attempt to reach a more satisfactory adjustment to his situation. 

Experience suggests indeed that not only are these initial steps significant, but that they help to build in the client a belief in his own strength to deal with life. Consequently it is not surprising that while during the counseling contacts the positive steps, such as learning to dance, are few and symbolic, a check-up a few months later may reveal that these initial steps have expanded into a much more complete reorganization of the client's whole pattern of behavior. 

Ending the Contacts 

After the client has begun to take steps to improve his life situation, he will-before long-reach the point of terminating the counseling contacts. The decision to terminate comes from the client. Perhaps he may set a limit in which to deal with remaining issues. "I think I'll only need another two conferences." Or he may say at the end of a conference something to this effect: “I guess I won't need to see you any more. I think I can handle things from here on by myself." In either case, the counselor will accept the client's decision. Any feelings about terminating will be accepted and reflected.

The counselor should not expect that the serviceman will 58

The Development and Growth of the Client leave the counseling experience with boundless enthusiasm and unlimited self-confidence. Instead there is usually the feeling that although he will not need to return any more he recognizes that there are still problems confronting him. 

He realizes that the road ahead is not all roses and sunshine, but he feels that he can work things through alone. Rather than an over-confidence, the client manifests a satisfaction with the progress he has made and, at the same time, a sober realization of the difficulties that still lie ahead. The goal of counseling, then, is not to turn out an individual with no problems. No counselor can expect once and for all to solve any problems that a client may encounter. Even if it were possible, such an outcome would not be desirable. Living consists in the facing of problems and adjusting to them. 

Realizing this, the counselor will be prepared to understand the limitations on what he can do for a client. If the client has gained some degree of insight and is able to face the issues that underlie his difficulties with the realization that there are decisions to be made, if he has gained some additional control over his behavior through an understanding of motives of which he was formerly unaware, that is as much as the counselor has a right to expect. 

Sometimes in the last interview the client will bring up many new problems one after another. Usually, if these are calmly accepted in the spirit of the preceding interviews, the client will recognize that he is a bit reluctant to sever his relationship with the counselor and has been attempting to prolong the counseling experience. In any case, if the counselor continues to make use of the techniques described in Chapter IV, he will find that the client can go on to accept the termination. It is important that the client be given the freedom to make this decision, for it is the repre- 59

sentation of the final choice to accept responsibility for his own behavior. By terminating the contacts, he is breaking his tie with the counselor and resuming a full measure of responsibility for the life situation in which he finds himself. 

Often the client will wish to continue seeing the counselor in a friendly relationship. Perhaps he will invite him to dinner or to spend an evening with the family. However, the counselor will probably be best serving his function if he points out that this does not fall within the scope of their professional relationship. Such friendly contacts would jeopardize any future assistance that the counselor might be able to offer should the serviceman decide that he needed it. If this limit is clearly stated, the client will face it, accept it, and terminate the contacts in a clean-cut, positive fashion. 60

A SERVICEMAN ACHIEVES INSIGHT 

Many of the examples cited throughout the preceding chapters come from counseling interviews where the issues were rather simple and clear-cut. With the client whose situation is more complex, or whose defensiveness is greater, the development of the counseling process may be more complicated even though the basic pattern of change is the same. If the counselor studies carefully the case of Irving 67

Savrensky, which follows, it will help to prepare him for the handling and the understanding of these more intricate patterns of change.

Irving Savrensky was a private in the Army Specialized Training Program in the premedical curriculum. The strain of an intensive academic program in conjunction with military activities was further intensified by his personal situation. His wife lived and worked in a city close to the university where he was stationed, but they were not able to live together. With his worries about his school work, his wife's job, the future and his inability to plan for it, Savrensky was carrying a heavy burden. Then when it became certain that his wife was pregnant, the strain on him became too great and he came to the counseling service for help in adjusting to the situation. The strength of his desire for help is illustrated by his expectation that he would be required to pay for the counseling. He was fully willing to do this despite the fact that after various deductions from his pay only about $15 per month remained. 

The general facts of the background as they were revealed in the course of treatment can be summarized as follows: 

Irving's parents were Polish-Jewish immigrants who spoke only broken English. The father had built up a business that he expected to turn over to Irving and his brother. However, when Irving tried to work in the store, he just could not seem to learn to sell. Eventually, his father became disgusted with him and Irving left home. He went to a large city where he was able to find work. Before long he met a girl whom he began dating. However, when he realized that she was beginning to become serious about him, he withdrew and ceased seeing her. Later he realized that he loved her and resumed the relationship. After he was drafted into 68

the army, they decided to marry, and, although her parents put up strong opposition, they carried out their plan. 

Eventually, Irving was assigned to the A.S.T.P. and sent to the university. One important formative influence should be noted. From the age of five years until eleven years he was crippled and prevented from playing the sports and games that other boys his age did. When he finally was able to get around again, he found himself so far behind the other fellows that he felt left out of everything. Further, he was quite sensitive about being fat, and this increased his sense of isolation.

With this brief background, let us examine the content of Irving's conferences and notice how he gradually developed an increased understanding of himself.

The essential problem of counseling is not how the counselor can communicate self-understanding to the client, but rather how to create an atmosphere in which the client can work out his own understanding. Whether the client begins by stating that he lacks selfconfidence, is having trouble with his wife, is fearful of going insane, is inadequate sexually, cannot decide whether to go to college, or does not know what sort of occupation he is best fitted for, the goal of counseling should be the same. The counselor, in any case, should be interested in the person and not just in the initial problem. 

Rather than merely devoting his efforts to eliminating the manifest problems that the client has stated, the counselor attempts to provide an environment in which the client can grow to increased self-understanding. This being the case, there is no essential difference in procedure between counseling personal problems and counseling so-called educational and vocational difficulties. For the nondirective counselor, educational and vocational difficulties are personal problems. However, since educational and vocational guidance is generally considered to be a field separate 90

Educational and Vocational Counseling from other areas of counseling, it may be well to consider in what way the nondirective viewpoint applies here. 

Traditionally, educational or vocational guidance begins with as thorough a diagnosis as possible. Extensive test information is collected; the educational and vocational history of the client is secured. The counselor then interrelates the various findings and communicates them to the client along with information concerning the requirements for various jobs or schools. He may perhaps recommend or suggest a certain course of action for the client to follow.

In some instances he will make direct contact with prospective employers or colleges in order to help the client get into the vocational or educational situation that seems the most favorable (usually to the counselor). Many counselors will say, "Yes, but I always leave the final decision up to the client himself. I give him information about himself and information about vocations or educational possibilities, but he makes the decisions." This is probably quite true. 

Such counselors may give advice or suggestions while stressing that the client has to make the final decision.

However, the crucial point is that the counselor's resources and activities have been mobilized around the initial problem suggested by the client. Taking tests and answering questions about his past educational and vocational history does not encourage the client to feel that he may discuss other types of problems with the counselor. When the counselor's entire attention is focused upon vocational and educational factors, it is not probable that the client will feel free to discuss anything he wishes to go into any deeper factors that may underlie the manifest problem. In this lack of freedom lies the difficulty with the traditional method of educational or vocational guidance. For if the 91

client has not been able to examine and explore his feelings and attitudes about the various relevant factors, whatever decisions he makes will be based upon an inadequate understanding of himself. Detailed information about his test performances and about vocational conditions will be insufficient in helping the client to make the most satisfactory adjustment unless he understands the relation of such information to his own hopes, fears, strivings, and ambitions.

From many different sources, evidence is accumulating that the common denominator behind a person's various problems is his personality-his values, attitudes, and feelings. The Use of Information 

There is a tendency, as has been stressed earlier, for the inexperienced counselor to assume that the basic difficulty the client faces stems from a lack of information. However, if a serviceman is unsure of himself, he will not develop self-confidence merely from being told that he has a high score on some test. If he is worried about the future, information that there are many jobs open in his speciality will not eliminate his fears. Information will undoubtedly help in each of these situations, but not if it is used in an attempt to deny the reality of the serviceman's feelings. In other words, there are valuable functions that information may fulfill. However, the counselor must be aware that information cannot demonstrate to the client that his fears are baseless, that his lack of confidence is unjustified, that 95

his hope is futile, etc. Feelings are not true or false in the same way that a statement is. Denying their reality or proving them to be unfounded will not change the fact that the client feels that way. The client's feelings must be accepted, must be recognized. However, there are at least three main uses for information. 

1. Information may be used to help clarify a choice. Frequently, after a client has worked through his problem he will reach a point where he is ready to make some decision, but the actual choices facing him are confused and vague owing to lack of information. In such an instance, the counselor can provide a useful service by presenting appropriate information. The veteran who wishes to change to a new job will have his choices clarified by knowing how much training the various jobs require, what the usual rate of pay is, where the best locations for such jobs are, etc. However, when a counselor gives such information to a veteran, he should be careful not to slant it in such a way that the veteran is practically forced to reach a certain conclusion. 

2. Information may be used to implement a decision. Let us say that a veteran approaches a counselor in doubt as to whether he should return to college or attempt to find a job immediately. After he has explored the various factors involved in making his decision (some of which may require certain information as under point 1 above), including various of his attitudes that are brought into play by the problem and the meaning of the various possibilities in terms of his own values, he may decide in favor of going back to college. Now the question arises as to which college will best meet his needs. At this point he can profitably make use of information concerning various colleges. In other words, after the decision has been made, the client 96

Educational and Vocational Counseling may need information that will help him in carrying through the course of activity he has decided upon. 

It should be noted that both of these uses of information occur during the final stage of counseling. After the client has had full opportunity to demonstrate what use he desires to make of the counseling relationship, has freely expressed himself on any issues that are important to him, has developed a working insight, then-when decisions are in the process of being made and implemented-the counselor makes information available if the client feels that he needs it. 

3. Information may be used to help the client discover the real problem. In many situations, much more than information is needed. However, the client does not always immediately recognize this need. In such instances, the counselor may give the information the client is demanding (or, better, refer to sources of information) to help him discover that information is not enough. A typical example is the following: Private Hamsun came in for counseling with the request that tests be given him to determine what vocation he would be best fitted for. He did not accept the counselor's invitation to talk things through a bit, but demanded that he be given tests that would tell him what to do. An appropriate battery of tests of interests, aptitudes, and abilities was given. In addition, Private Hamsun was referred to sources in the library which gave him detailed information about various vocations. After he had done some reading about vocations, the test results were interpreted to him. Private Hamsun was quite interested in the information, but when this stage was finished he stated that this was all well and good, that he had discovered some 10 or 12 occupations that interested him, but how did he 97

decide which one would be best? How did he make up his mind? The counselor then structured the situation in terms of the usual counseling relationship-pointing out that perhaps if he understood himself better he would find why he was unable to make a decision now. It was suggested that if Private Hamsun wished to avail himself of the oppor- tunity the counselor would be glad to make his time available for Private Hamsun to use. The private quite willingly agreed that perhaps that was what he wanted. The remaining conferences then were utilized by Private Hamsun for talking over his situation in general. He expressed quite a bit of deep feeling concerning his family, their attitudes toward his getting married, their pressure for him to settle in his home town, etc. As these feelings were worked through, he came to a clearer understanding of himself and of what he wanted to do. He also discovered that he had been expecting to have decisions made for him because of his basic lack of confidence in his own decisions resulting from family pressure. When this stage was reached, he decided to carry through an intention he had stated earlier -to go away from home to college. He planned a college course that would make it possible for him to enter a variety of occupations that all clustered around one central pattern of interests. It is clear that for Private Hamsun the information served an indirect function-namely, to demonstrate that his problem was not caused by a lack of information. When the counselor complied with Private

Hamsun's demands and yet the problem seemed no nearer a solution, it was much easier for him to make use of the counseling hours in a profitable fashion. Of course, one danger that arises in this use of information is that the conferences will strengthen the client's dependence upon 98

Educational and Vocational Counseling the counselor and increase his feeling that the counselor can give him the answers. Skillful handling of the early conferences, however, together with a clear structuring of the relationship, will largely avoid this problem. 

Test Information 

Test results are subject to the same considerations that have been mentioned for other types of information. However, because of their personal character certain additional points need mentioning. Information such as the cost of tuition in a given school or the length of preparation required to be classed as a skilled tool-and-die maker does not usually arouse much emotion. However, there are often intense feelings connected with test scores. The client probably feels that this test score will reveal how "good" or how bright he is, how much ability he has, etc. He feels that there are standards against which he is being judged. 

In other words, he has something personal at stake. This emotional factor will influence his reaction to such information. Consequently, it must be recognized that test scores constitute a special type of information. In dealing with them there are certain cautions that should be considered. 

1. Make sure the client knows what the test score means.

Before reporting the client's own performance to him, the counselor should ascertain that the client knows what the purpose of the test is. For example, in reporting the results of an interest inventory the client should realize that this inventory is giving him information about his interests and not about his abilities. In other cases, it will be necessary to find out whether the client understands the difference between an achievement and an aptitude test, etc. 99

Perhaps the best way to determine how the client understands the test results is to see what they mean to him, to let him tell how he understands them. 

2. Information derived from testing can best be given when it fits in with something the client has just said. Following such a procedure not only satisfies the client's desire to know what was discovered, but it also strengthens the relation between the counselor and client by providing a common basis for understanding. Further, it keeps the responsibility for the content of the interview in the hands of the client. Thus, a veteran may be saying that he has never been greatly interested in mechanical activities. The counselor might respond that the interest inventory indicated that his interest in that area was low, but that it was high in activities involving verbal behavior and also in face-to-face contacts. 

3. After information has been given, allow the client time to react to it. Test results represent an objective description of the self. Consequently, they may or may not agree with the client's own description of himself. When test information has been given, then, the counselor has the opportunity of playing a very helpful role in clarifying the serviceman's understanding of himself. If the counselor accepts and reflects the serviceman's feelings about the information, the serviceman can be helped to discover what the information really means to him, to assimilate it into his own way of thinking, and to put it to use. Test results sometimes evoke very significant reactions in the clientif the counselor will refrain from imposing his own evaluation of their meaning. For example, a serviceman may react to unpleasant information by objecting that he does not believe the test is accurate. (Naturally, if there is any real 100

Educational and Vocational Counseling reason to suspect that the test was incorrectly administered, it should be readministered or another similar test given.) 

If the test was given satisfactorily, the counselor should not attempt to persuade him that the test is really quite reliable, widely used, etc. Counseling conferences are not for the purpose of convincing clients of the value of psychological tests-nor to demonstrate how much the counselor knows about methods of test construction and statistics. 

Instead, the counselor might respond something like this, "You feel, then, that this test score doesn't really describe your abilities (or achievements, etc.)." Accepting and reflecting the serviceman's objections may lead him to further self-examination as to why he cannot yet believe that the test has given him an accurate description of himself. No matter how perfectly standardized a test may be, the test results will be of no more use than the client can allow them to be. Assimilating and making use of test information is a problem of feelings and attitudes, and the counselor should not attempt to high-pressure the serviceman into saying that he believes the results. Letting him proceed at his own pace, stating his objections or approval, examining why he objects or approves, expressing his feelings freely, will prove more valuable in the long run.

When More Than Information Is Needed 

It is true that some clients need only certain very specific information. Often they will be satisfied with reference to appropriate books or magazine sources that will tell them what they want to know. In most cases, however, the counselor can never be sure that this is all that is required unless he is convinced that the client has felt free to talk about anything he wished-whether related directly to his vocational or educational problems or not. Any person may go to a book for information, but when he consults another person it is not always just pure information seeking. The meaning of the personal relationship must be assessed.

Perhaps he wants reassurance on a course of action he has chosen; perhaps he is afraid to make a decision by himself; perhaps he wants someone to side in with him against his parents' wishes. The point is that consulting another person for information is a much different action from consulting a book. The counselor should be alert for signs that the person really wants something more than information from the counseling relation. 

There are many indicators that the basic problem is not the vocational or educational one presented. Three such signs will be mentioned here. In the course of training, the counselor will develop skill in responding to these and other indicators. 

1. The Person Who Wants Decisions Made for Him. 

A client of this sort has no idea what he would like to do. 

He wants the counselor to give him some tests and lay out a course of action. He states his problems and asks what to do about them. Continually he requests of the counselor, "What do you think of that?", "Was I wrong in doing that?", "What would you suggest?". In such situations the basic problem is more probably the fact that the client cannot make up his own mind, the fact that he wants someone else to take the responsibility for the decision. Are there pressures on him that make it impossible for him to reach a course of action because any decisions would involve him in an unpleasant choice? Is the difficulty a basic lack of security and self-confidence? Such clients are usually after more than information. Another tip-off that the basic problem is deeper than the vocational or educational difficulty stated is given when the client is never satisfied with any information he receives. He always wants more. This suggests that for some reason he is attempting to postpone making a definite decision. To the client, his request for additional information will seem perfectly justified and logical. Yet, it is a truism that life involves a certain amount of gamble or risk on any choice. There will never be enough information about a future course of action to make a 100 per cent perfect prediction possible. The person who requires complete certainty on every issue is probably a person who is afraid of life and himself. And so the issue in such cases becomes, why does he continually want more information? 

What is the meaning of making a definite decision? What does the client have at stake? More and more information will have little effect upon a problem of this type as long as the client cannot utilize the information that he has. 

3. The Person Who Manifests Anxiety about the Future. 

Clients of this type are more clearly in need of attitudinal treatment than they are of information. They must be given a free situation in which to express their anxiety, fears, and worries. When the client has worked through his anxiety about the future and has developed insight, he will be able to make use of information. But to give factual information as the major form of treatment is to leave the basic difficulty untouched.

Obviously, there are many other situations in which problems appearing as vocational or educational turn upon personal factors. If the counselor succeeds in helping the client to experience the full meaning of a permissive atmos- 105

When the Problem Is the Partner's Fault

Not infrequently the spouse who turns to a counselor for help seems to be the better adjusted of the pair. A wife whose conduct has been blameless comes for help because of her unfaithful husband. A calm and self-possessed soldier wishes counsel in regard to his nagging and neurotic wife. 

The counselor tends to feel uncertain as to what to do because he seems to be dealing with "the wrong person." 

Considerable experience indicates that the place to begin counseling is with the person who feels a concern about the problem. In such a marital situation, it is not necessarily the "problem" partner with whom we should deal, but the person who is dissatisfied and willing to work on the 108

Marital and Family Counseling problem. Any person who finds himself in an unsatisfactory marital relationship will be able, with counseling assistance, to do something to improve the relationship. As in all other counseling, we begin with the person who has a discoverable concern about the situation. 

Not infrequently a wife will come to the counselor in order to ask if she may send her "problem" husband to be counseled. The worker will be wise to indicate that if her husband really wishes help he will be welcome, but that otherwise she might get further by exploring the situation herself.

It is easy to identify oneself with the "innocent" party in a marital situation, and the counselor should carefully avoid any such pitfall. Antagonism and blame that are heaped on the spouse should be fully accepted and reflected (“You feel he is very definitely the one at fault," "She seems to you to be wrecking your marriage," etc.), but the counselor should completely refrain from evaluating the other partner. 

Such responses as, "He surely has mistreated you," or "I should think you would be unhappy with her," are almost certain to do harm to the counseling, as are the attitudes that lie behind these remarks.

The reason is not far to seek. Often the "blameless" client is not entirely blameless. If the counselor has identified himself with this client as an innocent party, it makes it doubly difficult to bring out attitudes and information that contradict this picture. Thus the charming young wife of a coast guardsman described her husband as an inhibited, reserved, thoughtless individual, so lacking in spontaneity and warmth that any reasonable marital relationship would seem impossible. But as these criticisms were accepted, she finally struggled to bring out the other side of the pic- 109

ture, which she had deeply repressed. Although in most groups she was happy and spontaneous, with her husband she was ill at ease, artificially gay or unduly silent, and all because she labored under the continual fear that something she might say or do would reveal some of her own earlier indiscretions, of which he was unaware. If the counselor had sided with her at the outset, she could never have gained the courage to discuss her own part in the situation and her own feelings of guilt. As she saw these aspects, she was able to alter her own behavior very helpfully. 

Even where the innocent party has had little to do with causing the problem, the counselor will do well to remember that he is quite unable to lift the burden of the difficulty from the client's shoulders. He is most helpful if he concentrates on assisting the client to clarify his own relationship to a situation that no third party can solve for him. 

Dealing with Husband and Wife

For the beginning counselor, the technical problems are undoubtedly simpler, and success is more likely, if the counseling relationship is with one partner only. The husband (or wife, as the case may be) can then make use of counseling to explore, understand, and rebuild his own attitudes and behavior in marriage and thus handle those aspects for which he is responsible.

When both partners come in together to consult the counselor, it is usually best to listen to the preliminary statement of the problem and then to make arrangements to see each individual separately. If the matter is handled in this way the counselor should keep the two counseling 110

Marital and Family Counseling relationships psychologically separate. The husband should not be informed of the wife's statements, or vice versa. Even more important, the counselor should avoid, just as far as possible, developing a mind-set or a diagnosis or conclusion about the situation. To the extent that he does formulate a diagnosis, he will find it very difficult to accept and recognize attitudes that are not in line with his preconceived conclusion. This counseling of both husband and wife demands an even greater degree of self-restraint on the part of the counselor than is demanded in other counseling, if he is to remain open-minded and genuinely accepting toward very diverse attitudes held by the two partners. 

To listen to the wife explain how she has striven to be economical and thrifty, and on another occasion to hear the husband relate that his wife is a thoughtless spender of his hard-earned wages, and to reflect both these attitudes with understanding is a difficult task for the counselor. As the contacts continue, it will be found that there is less contradiction as husband and wife become free enough to give more of the real picture, rather than a defensive one.

There may be some situations where circumstances demand that the two partners be interviewed together. It appears possible, from some of the research being done in group therapy, that by being a nonjudgmental individual, and by reflecting the attitudes expressed, no matter how contradictory, counseling of two people at the same time may make progress. By helping each to clarify his own attitudes, those for which he is responsible, a means of adjustment may be discovered. There is not as yet enough experience to justify a discussion of this point, and it 111

demands a degree of skill which the beginning counselor may not possess. Consequently, whenever possible, the husband and wife should be seen separately. 

The Giving of Information 

In dealing with marital situations, it may at times be found that the client is in need of information in regard to divorce laws, management of family finance, sexual adjustment, child rearing, or some other topic. The counselor may wish to give this information verbally or to refer the client to other sources, whether individuals or books. One major point needs to be borne in mind-the information is to be given neutrally, in such a way that the client may use it or disregard it as he sees fit. It is very easy to assume that we are only giving information when we are, in effect, telling the client, "This is what you should do."

A young couple, married several months, came for assistance because they had never been able to consummate their marriage. The young woman had been strongly conditioned against everything sexual by a neurotic and abnormal mother and now found that although she would like to please and satisfy her husband she could not do so. Two medical examinations gave conflicting reports as to whether there was any organic reason for the vaginal contraction that prevented intercourse. The counselor in this situation selected a book that stressed the normal place of sex adjustment in marriage and offered it to the wife. This information did not of course solve her problem, since she still had to work out her own attitude toward sexual matters. It is probable that it was an aid to her in learning some of the facts that her early life had kept from her. Essential progress came however when she made the decision that 112

Marital and Family Counseling she could live a different life from that of her mother and could set her own goals and choose her own values. She was then able to achieve a reasonably satisfactory sexual adjustment.

This case illustrates the place of information in the counseling process. It should be plainly recognized that facts or information rarely provide any answer to a marital problem. Even when the needed facts are given, there still remains for the client the task of relating himself, in some responsible fashion, to these facts. This is the field of counseling. 

Some General Considerations

Because marital problems are so varied, because they deal so obviously with human interrelationships and not merely with conflict in one individual, because they are so closely tied to conventional and moral and religious issues, there is some tendency for counselors to feel that they demand a different type of handling from other problems. 

In order to counteract this tendency it may be well to reemphasize two basic points. 

1. Counseling deals with attitudes, not with situations.

Counseling does not directly affect the external facts of the case the unfaithful spouse, the unreasonable motherin-law, the financial stress in the home. 

2. Counseling can help the individual to delve more deeply and more realistically into all the attitudes he holds toward his situation. Through this exploration the client may come to understand his own relationship to the situation more clearly, to view it without the defensiveness that has previously clouded his perception. Out of this truer perception he can develop new and more mature and more 113

positive ways or relating himself to the reality with which he is faced.

These basic points apply to marital counseling as to any other. They help to explain why the counselor is able to do his best job if he focuses his attention and his skill solely upon the emotionalized attitudes of the marital partner who comes to him, and regards this as his area of effort, rather than “missing the boat" by focusing on the problem as it exists apart from the client's attitudes. 

In closing, a brief account of one case might be given. A young wife, aged twenty, was urged by a friend to come to a counselor. She was defensive and suspicious at first but gradually related her situation. She married a sailor a year previously, after having known him a few weeks. He was "shipped out❞ one week after the marriage. Now he had returned for a 60-day leave, and the two had fought and argued almost continuously and parted on a very antagonistic basis. She was unhappy about this and was thinking of getting a divorce and giving up her job. She placed all the blame on the man. "He's just unreasonable about everything." "He expects me to give up things for marriage. 

I won't sacrifice anything I've gained." "It was just a foolish whim, getting married. I never thought about it being permanent. We should break it up." "I think-of course it sounds funny to say this—I think I'm really better than he is.” Although her attitude might be evaluated as being very self-centered and immature, the counselor refrained from any such evaluation and accepted and mirrored the sentiments expressed.

In the second interview she expressed more of the same viewpoint, but aso went deeper. She decided, "Maybe I'm kinda selfish." She talked about her earlier insecure experi- 114

She also discussed her husband in very different terms, saying that while he had faults she had always felt he was very genuine and that she had more in common with him than with any other man she had known. She felt perhaps she was just bragging to say she was better than he. As the interview drew to a close she said, "Maybe it would help if I just grew up," to which the counselor replied, "You're beginning to feel that perhaps you could do something about your marriage."

It will be our purpose in this chapter to consider this question. 

A Limited Goal

The casual contact is used most satisfactorily if the counselor is willing to adopt a limited goal. A man's life is not going to be reoriented nor a long standing problem solved in a 15-minute interview in a USO lounge. Yet the serviceman may find himself better able to meet his situation if the 15 minutes is well used.

Essentially, the casual interview can supply three highly important elements of helpfulness. It may provide for emotional release the unburdening of tensions, the talking out of pent-up feelings, the satisfaction of getting emo- 117

tionally charged attitudes out in the open, the experience of "getting this off my chest," as the men say. In the second place it provides the individual with the opportunity to look more clearly at his situation, and a problem more clearly faced is on its way to solution. Because of the experience of emotional release, the serviceman is less tense, his thinking is less distorted, more objective. He sees his problem in more accurate perspective. He may not leave with the solution to his situation, but he leaves with a firmer grasp of the real essentials of his difficulty. In the third place such an interview, though casual in its origin, may lead to further contacts, definitely planned, where the individual goes more deeply into his problems, either with this counselor or with some other counselor to whom he has been referred. 66 These are the positive goals of what someone has called 'counseling on the hoof." How may they be achieved?

What procedures will be helpful to the counselor, and what should he avoid?

The rules are relatively simple. The counselor should first of all make himself psychologically accessible through warm personal interest, friendly social conversation, and a readiness to listen. In this way he makes it easy for the man with whom he is in contact to discuss problems if he so desires. In a great many instances no problems will be brought out, and the contact begins and ends as a friendly social chat. The counselor should avoid questioning and probing. It is not the counselor's function to "dig up" problems, but simply to be available if problems are present. 

He reduces as far as possible any psychological barriers between himself and his client, so he may be regarded by the serviceman as "a guy to whom I can talk." 118

The Use of the Casual Contact 

In the second place the counselor is alert for any evidence of deeply emotionalized attitudes, and if such do crop up in the conversation, he immediately drops the give and take of social conversation and utilizes counseling procedures instead. When a serviceman who has been talking about his home town begins to rail at the civilians, the counselor reflects his attitude. When the merchant seaman who has been describing his plans for his leave says "but 

I won't go home if it's the last place on earth," the counselor responds, "You're really on the outs with your home.

Such responses show an understanding that permits the man to continue if he desires and, at the same time, avoid any probing or digging which the man would resent.

TECHNIQUES-GOOD AND BAD 

Thus far the aim has been to practice those types of responses which would be most helpful. It has been found however in training counselors that interpretive responses, reassurance, advice, etc., are often used quite unwittingly by the counselor, simply because he has never learned to recognize these less desirable forms of response. Consequently the exercise that follows is designed to give opportunity for the intelligent practice of errors, in order that they may be avoided in the interview itself. 141

To refresh the reader's mind, simple definitions of appropriate and inappropriate types of responses are given, in order to make clear what is desired in the exercise.

Definitions 

Client-centered Techniques (appropriate and desirable techniques) 

Reflection of feeling: To express, in fresh words, the essential attitudes (not the content) expressed by the client, to mirror his attitudes for his own better understanding, and to show that he is understood by the counselor. 

Simple acceptance: An "M-hm" type of response that does no more than indicate acceptance and understanding. 

Structuring: A simple explanation of the type of relationship that exists between counselor and client. 

Nondirective lead: A very general question designed to open the conversation but not direct it. 

Counselor-centered Techniques (usually inappropriate and undesirable)

Questioning: Questions designed to get deeper into the problem, to discover the extent and the cause of the difficulty. 

Reassurance or praise: A positive evaluation of the client by the counselor.

Criticism or negative evaluation: A negative evaluation of the client by the counselor. 

Interpretation: Any attempt, partial or complete, to explain the individual to himself; pointing out to the client the way in which different aspects of his behavior are related; attempting to show cause-and-effect relationships which the client has not expressed.

Advice and suggestion: Indicating, in any fashion, the steps or actions that the client should take or the standards or attitudes that he should adopt. 

Persuasion: Marshaling the reasons why the client should adopt the actions or goals advised by the counselor. 142

Identification: A response that says, in effect, "I have had the same experience that you are describing."

Neutral Technique (useful in appropriate situation) 

Information: Giving facts in a neutral way that permits the client to make his own choice.

THE NEXT STEP 

The practice that has been obtained through the exercises in this chapter is a sound intermediate step. This manual stressed first the study of basic principles and case material exemplifying these principles. Second, exercises of the sort given in this chapter offer the opportunity to apply these principles in a situation where the counselor feels no urgency and no tension, because it is a paper situation. This in turn should be followed by the next step, supervised counseling interviews. It is to be hoped that the reader of this chapter can arrange for such supervised training. For the greatest learning the interviews should be phonographically recorded, since the opportunity to listen to the whole interview, with the words, the inflections, and the silences all accurately reproduced, makes for the greatest progress. If this is not possible, the counselor is urged to keep very complete notes during the interview, writing them up imme- 148

diately afterward. A single interview adequately reported and intelligently criticized will make for more counselor learning than ten interviews summarized afterward from memory. Where skilled supervisory criticism is unobtainable, definite profit may be gained by a group of counselors studying together and analyzing and criticizing each others interviews. The counselor who is serious in his purpose will endeavor to find some way of carrying on this next step of supervised practice. 149
