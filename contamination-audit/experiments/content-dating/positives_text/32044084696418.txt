The Natural Amphitheater in Rebild National Park 

REBILD NATIONAL PARK 

"I shall never forget the deep impression which the Rebild Park Fourth of July celebration in 1931 made upon me. The beauty of the heather covered hills in whose natural amphitheater the great gathering assembled, the deep emotion evoked by the celebration in the heart of Jutland of our American Independence Day, a profound sense of the value of such international gatherings, made this a memorable event in my life."

RUTH BRYAN OWEN, 

United States Minister to Denmark. "The Rebild National Park in the beautiful moorlands of northern Jutland in Denmark was donated by Danish-born Americans as a loving tribute to their homeland and the people from whom they came. It stands today, unique in the world, as a token of the ties of deep human affection that bind two peoples together." OTTO WADSTED, Denmark's Minister to the United States.

I have learned with much interest and pleasure of the celebration of the anniversary of the Independence of the United States which is to be held in Denmark at Rebild National Park, with participation by American citizens of Danish birth or descent, some of whom may possibly have been instrumental in founding this remarkable international monument. 

It seems singularly fitting, and of good augury for the future of international relationships, that the commemoration of this milestone in the progress of the American people toward a fuller expression of their national and individual life should find a sympathetic response in the land of origin of many of my fellow-citizens. 

It is my belief that the Americans assembled at Rebild Park may, by serving as interpreters between their lands of origin and of adoption, do much to encourage the growth of helpful international understanding, and advance the cause of good will throughout the world.

Crown Prince Christian of Denmark Addresses First Fourth of July Gathering at Aarhus, 

Denmark, 1909

HE fulfillment of a Wisconsin poet's dream, Rebild National

Park, "stands today, unique in the world, as a token of the ties of deep human affection that bind two peoples together." 

With its soft heather-carpeted hills tossing their subdued amethystine splendor over a hushed recess of ancient Denmark, the park is, indeed, unique among international monuments of peace-unique in its physical aspect and in the sentiments it symbolizes. 

The uninitiated pilgrim chancing upon it, spellbound by the poetry of these undulating reaches of blooming heather and carefree skylarks warbling the praises of the purple moor to the blue sky above, might well regard this tranquil plot as the least congruous scene in the whole wide world of anything so effervescent as an American Fourth of July celebration.

Only Special Fourth of July Park in Existence 

Strangely, these ageless hills are in very fact the only spot beyond the confines of the United States where the celebration of America's 5

Independence Day is a regular occurrence, year in and year out—and that with a fervor now rare at home.

Stranger still, Rebild is the only natural park anywhere established specifically as a site for Fourth of July observances. 

Tens of thousands gather in the park each year for the celebration, which has become an outstanding event for Americans in their European summer festival schedule. 

Beyond Poet's Conception 

This extraordinary development far surpasses the dream of the modest Wisconsin bard. That dream concerned itself with men and ideals and took no cognizance of ways and means. Nevertheless, it was the emotion inspired by the poet's vision that eventually led to the establishment of this remarkable American rendezvous on foreign soil and the far-famed Fourth of July festivals in and around it. 

The poet was the late Ivar Kirkegaard, editor of a Danish-language magazine published in Racine, Wisconsin. His ideals, gloriously magnified, still pervade the great festivals. 

The Idea Spreads 

It was at the turn of the century that Kirkegaard envisioned an alliance of Danes on both sides of the Atlantic for the promotion of closer cultural relations between Danish immigrants in America and their kinsmen in the mother country, an end to be achieved in part through an annual reunion in Denmark. 

By 1905 the dream had taken definite form, and Mr. Kirkegaard invited a number of Danish-born American citizens to Racine for a preview of his picture. Men from so widely separated points as New 

York, San Francisco and Chicago responded to the invitation.

Danish-American Association Born As a result of the Racine gathering the DANISH-AMERICAN 

ASSOCIATION was launched in 1906, under the presidency of the late Henry L. Hertz of Chicago, for the purpose of "furthering mutual understanding between the American and Danish peoples, strengthening

First Celebration in Denmark 1909 

Among the Danish-American Association's members was Dr. Max 

Henius, a man active in the civic life of Chicago. In 1908 he visited 

Denmark and found the city of Aarhus, metropolis of central Jutland, bustling with preparations for a national exposition, scheduled for the following year. An alert native suggested an organized Danish-American gathering at the forthcoming fair. Dr. Henius passed the suggestion on to the officers of the Danish-American Association who readily acquiesced. In turn the exposition management agreed to set aside a special day of celebration for the visitors from America. 

Thus it came to pass that on July 4, 1909, over 1,100 Danish-born Americans and thousands of domestic Danes gathered at the Aarhus fair and jointly staged the first American Independence Day celebration on Danish soil.

It was a signal success. Crown Prince Christian of Denmark, accompanied by the Crown Princess Alexandrine, conveyed the good wishes of King Frederik VIII. The late Dr. Maurice Francis Egan, then 

American Minister to Denmark, delivered a special message from President Taft. Members of the Danish cabinet and other high dignitaries were among the guests. Beyond a doubt, it was the most unusual commemoration of the birth of the American Republic held anywhere up to that time.

Americans Select Rebild Hills 

So impressed were Dr. Henius and his friends that, then and there, they decided to make the Fourth of July an annual event in Denmark. 

A search was promptly instituted for a suitable site. The northern tip of the Jutland moor, known as the Rebild Hills and situated 15 miles south of the ancient seaport of Aalborg, was selected. 

An option was obtained on 200 acres of the purple-hued moorland while in the United States steps were taken to finance the purchase. 

American citizens of Danish descent from all parts of the country contributed to the fund, and in 1911 the tract was purchased and subsequently named the Danish-American Park. 

Royalty at Dedication 1912

A group of American citizens was constituted as the AMERICAN 

REBILD COMMITTEE with President Taft as honorary chairman, to prepare a suitable program for the dedication, which occurred in 1912. 

Ten thousand Americans and Danes assembled in the great natural bowl in the park for the solemn ceremonies that featured the official opening. Old Glory was flung to the breeze side by side with venerable Dannebrog, Denmark's national emblem and the oldest flag in the world. The two banners have remained inseparable companions at all subsequent Rebild gatherings.

Again the erstwhile Crown Prince, who had in the meantime succeeded to the throne as King Christian X, was present and addressed the gathering with deep emotion.

American Control 

Previous to the dedication a committee of American citizens had waited on the King at the Marselisborg summer palace near Aarhus, and presented him with the deed to the park-the gift of Danish-born Americans to their mother country. A special arrangement, however, places the park under American control with the Danish Ministry of Agriculture in charge of supervision and maintenance. 

The deed provided specifically that Americans be permitted to celebrate the Fourth of July "and other American memorial days" in the park; also, that the park be at all times open to the public (more than

100,persons now visit it annually), and that it be preserved in its natural state.

For years Danish foresters have been converting the barren moor, which at Rebild ends abruptly before a pleasing panorama of waving grainfields and peaceful meadows, into economically profitable pine groves; and the time is drawing nigh when the Rebild Hills will stand as the sole remainder of this somber primeval landscape.

Danish Committee Formed 

America's Independence Day rapidly assumed the proportions of an institution in northern Jutland, and a cooperating Danish group, styling itself the DANISH REBILD COMMITTEE, was formed by the people of the villages of Rebild and Skörping, both in the immediate vicinity of the park. This Danish body established a loose working connection with the American Rebild Committee. 

Park Grows

During the years additions have been made to the park, partly through purchase by Americans, partly through donations by the Danish government, of contiguous territory. The park covers now about 400 acres. 

Concurrently, the Fourth of July celebrations have grown in scope and importance, and the time came when the project grew too unwieldy for the somewhat casual administration exercised by the two Rebild committees.

Rebild National Park Board Formed In 1930 both were abolished and superseded by a more business-like governing body: THE REBILD NATIONAL PARK BOARD, an 

Illinois non-profit corporation with headquarters in Chicago. The board has 40 members, of whom 25 must be Americans and 15 Danes. Of its five directors, three must reside in the United States, two in Denmark. 

This set-up definitely places the park under American control.

World War Sole Interruption 

With only a brief interruption caused by the World War, the Fourth of July has been celebrated regularly at Rebild since 1912, even at times when official observances were impracticable. On such occasions the folks in Denmark stepped in and carried on the tradition. 

Danish neutrality precluded any sort of observance of the day when the United States entered the fray; but in 1920 the festivals were 11

resumed and have continued without further interruption down to the present.

The Danish Touch 

The wholehearted enthusiasm manifested by the domestic Danes since the very beginning has given the Fourth of July in Denmark that delightful quaintness which makes it distinctive—a blending of charming Danish characteristics with the stirring American patriotism to which the day owes its origin.

Glorious Fourth Becomes a Season 

In 1922 the good Aalborgers organized a FOURTH OF JULY COM- MITTEE, headed by Mayor M. Jörgensen, for the purpose of providing supplementary entertainment in their city during the days immediately preceding and following the solemn exercises in Rebild Park, the latter being, of course, confined to the Fourth itself.

This Aalborg citizens' committee, though it has no official affiliation with the park board, has succeeded in developing our Glorious Fourth into a season of festivals. In these undertakings the Aalborg committee is actively and ably assisted by the local Danish-American Club, another development of the annual influx of Americans. 

July Fourth Now Danish Holiday 

The Danish countryside has not escaped the repercussions of the great American day of commemoration. Within an ample radius of Rebild the Fourth of July has long been recognized as a general holiday, and from afar happy throngs pour into the park for the exercises. 

Attendance Climbs to 40,000

In 1930, when the newly organized Rebild National Park Board for the first time managed the celebration, the crowd aggregated 30,000, and many thought the attendance peak had been reached. 

In 1934, when the 25th anniversary of the custom of celebrating the Fourth of July in Denmark was observed, the attendance climbed to 40,000. 12

On this latter occasion the proportionate increase of native Americans was notable. The American people as a whole had begun to become "Rebild conscious."

Equally remarkable was the nationwide participation of domestic Danes. Individuals and organized parties flocked to Rebild and Aalborg from all parts of the Kingdom. Danish newspapers devoted pages to the event. Within the span of 25 years the Fourth of July had developed from an unnoticed date on the Danish calendar to a national institution.

Spectacular Program Broadcast to U. S. A. 

At the same time millions of Americans, who had never before heard of Rebild Park, sat in their homes and "listened in" on the highlights of the 25th anniversary exercises, brought to them directly from the American corner of the Jutland moor by the National Broadcasting Company. 13

The cabin, which commands a sweeping view of the Rebild landscape, serves as an emigrant museum- -a repository of objects associated with the history of Danish-born American pioneers. 

Avenue of State Flags 

In 1928 a California delegation appeared in the park and planted the Bear Flag beside the Stars and Stripes. The following year a group of New Yorkers added the colors of the Empire State. In 1930 eleven other state flags were unfurled.

By 1934 the number of state emblems assembled at Rebild had grown to 30, and the flags, all fluttering from uniform poles placed in a double row from the park entrance to the speaker's stand, were then formally presented to the Rebild National Park Board. In due time, of course, every state in the Union will have its flag at Rebild. 15

The Cimbric Boulder 

A most impressive landmark in the Rebild area is the huge Cimbric Boulder, commemorating the earliest known inhabitants of the region that encompasses Rebild; a race that wrote early European history with clubs and axes: the Cimbri.

It was from these very hills the courageous Cimbri, driven from their homes by a disastrous flood in the second century B. C., set forth on their memorable march of conquest through Europe, only to be ultimately annihilated by the Roman legions. 

Great Ice Age Remnant 

Some 12,000 years ago, when frigid glaciers covered most of the northern hemisphere, a great wave of ice deposited a nine-ton rock at a point that is now the heart of the Rold forest, near Rebild. There it lay unheeded until a forester stumbled upon it in 1923. Geologists traced its origin to the mountains near Oslo. 

A visiting Chicagoan acquired possession of the great boulder and gave it to Dr. Henius, who in turn presented the park with it as a belated but lasting monument to the extinct aborigines. With its massive bulk looming wide atop Cimbric Hill, flanking the main park entrance, the Cimbric Boulder makes a truly impressive memorial. 

Rebild's Legendary Origin Recalled 

The famous Danish sculptor Bundgaard's chisel has enhanced the boulder's monumental appropriateness by adorning its top with the outlines of a bull, an animal sacred to the Cimbri, according to whose legend a powerful wild bull created their native hills by vehement goring of the earth.

At the base appears the following simple inscription in ancient runic characters: "The Cimbri went forth from these parts in 120 B. C."

Dan-America Archives 

In the stately building Sohngaardsholm that houses the Dan-America Archives and American Library, dedicated in 1932, the citizens of 16

The Dan-America Archives and American Library, Aalborg, Denmark 

Aalborg have generously symbolized their sincere friendship for America and Americans, engendered by the Rebild festivals. 

Once a manor house, on the outskirts of the city, Sohngaardsholm is now the home of a budding research and reference library that promises to become an institution of considerable importance. It is proposed to develop here, through books and documents, an authentic and comprehensive source of information for students of American history. 

An important division is reserved for data elucidative of the part Danish-born citizens have played in the building of the American nation, upon the basis of which a great saga of the Danish emigrant will in time be written. 

Everybody Helps

Dr. Henius advanced the idea of the Dan-America Archives; upon suggestion of Mayor Jörgensen of Aalborg the city council donated the building, with a park; a number of Danish philanthropists paid for 17

One of the Library Rooms at the Archives the necessary alterations and furnishings; Mr. William S. Knudsen, the Detroit motor magnate, advanced a substantial sum to cover all operating expenses for the first four years, and hundreds of sympathizers on both sides of the ocean have contributed valuable library material as well as modest cash donations. 

The institution is operated by the Dan-America Archives Society, an Illinois corporation of international texture and patterned in all respects after the Rebild National Park Board. Thus the consolidation of good will between two friendly nations, separated by thousands of miles, goes on apace. Each year some new link is forged into this uniquely fashioned bond of peace that promises to endure as long as the two nations themselves. 

Perhaps the world needs more Rebild Parks, more poets like Ivar Kirkegaard to dream them into existence and more men like Max Henius to enlarge the material fulfillment of the dreams. 18
