This report relates to the limited subject of inquiry covered by the foregoing resolution of the subcommittee, and legislation later introduced with respect thereto. 

At the outset of this report, it seems pertinent to discuss the meaning of the terms and phrases generally used in describing the problem with which this report is concerned. An understanding of the question requires a knowledge of the meaning of "single-basing-point system," "Pittsburgh Plus," "multiple-basing-point system," "phantom freight," "freight absorption," "freight allowance," "freight averaging, ""uniform-delivered prices," and "uniform-zone prices.' 

A single-basing-point system is a practice wherein all products of a given nature are priced to all buyers in all markets as though originating at a single shipping point. Actually, however, shipments may be made from that point or from any of a multiple number of producing points. The most widely known of these systems was the practice employed in the steel industry for about 50 years prior to 1924, known as Pittsburgh Plus. Under that practice, steel was sold to buyers in all markets at f. o. b. mill prices based upon the fiction that the point of origin for all shipments was Pittsburgh (an exception, not pertinent to this discussion, prevailed on shipments from Birmingham). The buyer paid, irrespective of the location of his seller's mill, the f. o. b. mill price plus the rail freight cost from 

Pittsburgh to the buyer's destination. Thus an Iowa buyer of steel from a Gary, Ind., mill paid the f. o. b. Pittsburgh price plus the cost of rail transportation from Pittsburgh to Iowa.

This practice has long since been held illegal by the courts (Corn 

Products Company v. Federal Trade Commission, 324 U. S. 726, 1945). 

The impropriety of its use has not been questioned before the subcom-

FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION PRICING POLICIES 

mittee and the legality of that practice is not under consideration. 

The nature of the system is described solely for a better understanding of the problem.

The multiple-basing-point system is distinguished from the singlebasing-point system in that it includes two or more points of origin from which transportation costs are computed, which may or may not be the actual point of shipment. Those mills whose locations are used as basing points in determining transportation costs are known as base mills, other mills are known as nonbase mills. There are customarily substantially fewer price basing points than points of production. Under that system the buyer pays the f. o. b. mill price plus the cost of rail transportation from the basing point closest to the buyer's destination, if the sale is made by a base mill. If made by a nonbase mill the buyer would pay exactly the same price. The charge to the buyer is the f. o. b. mill price at a base mill plus the cost of rail transportation from the basing point closest to the buyer's destination.

There is some disagreement as to whether the phrase "multiplebasing-point system" embraces a selling practice in which every point of production is a basing point. It is contended by many that the basing-point system does not embrace a practice where every mill is a base mill because in such case only freight absorption is involved and there is no phantom freight. This distinction is not, however, universally recognized and serves only as one illustration of how disagreements on this subject have resulted from different meanings being given the same words by different people. 

Under the multiple-basing-point system sellers frequently make sales to buyers located closer to the base mill of a competitor than to the base mill of the seller. In those cases the buyer pays the seller the f. o. b. mill price plus a sum which is less than the actual cost of transporting the commodity to the buyer. This results from the fact that the cost of transportation is based on the freight charge from the base mill located closer to the buyer than is the seller's mill. In such case the seller is said to absorb a part of the cost of transportation. 

This practice is generally known as freight absorption. 

Under the multiple-basing-point system a seller, particularly those whose plants are nonbase mills, frequently bases the ultimate charge to the buyer upon the cost of transportation from a base mill located at a greater distance from the buyer's destination than the mill from which the commodity is shipped. In such cases the buyer pays to the seller the f. o. b. mill price at the nearest base mill plus a sum representing more than the actual cost of transportation involved in the transaction. The seller thus receives on account of freight costs a sum actually in excess of what he has expended. This excess charge is known as phantom freight.

It has sometimes been the practice to charge customers a delivered cost based upon all rail freight rates, even though the commodity was actually shipped by water or truck at a lower rate. In those cases the seller also receives from the buyer, on account of transportation costs, a sum in excess of what he has actually expended. The excess thus collected by the seller, and in fact any form of added transportation costs not actually incurred as a part of delivered price, is commonly known as phantom freight.

The foregoing practices apply largely in heavy-goods industries and particularly in the sale of homogeneous commodities. 

In many industries, however, commodities are sold at a delivered price uniform to all buyers, or to all buyers in a given area or zone, without regard to the variation of actual cost of transportation to any individual buyer. When the delivered price is uniform to all buyers throughout the country, the practice is generally known as a national uniform delivered price. This pricing practice is familiar to everyone as the sales practice in candy bars, cough drops, and chewing gum. It is also the theory on which the Postal Department carries the mail. It is also used in the sale of such commodities as breakfast foods, many canned goods, specialty foods, cigarettes, many advertised drug products, and literally thousands of other products for which the seller charges the same price to buyers in all sections of the country without regard to the point of delivery. In such case the seller is said to "average" the transportation costs among his customers. It may be assumed that in computing his costs the seller includes an item for the transportation of his products to the consuming market representing an average of his transportation costs to all his customers. When the actual cost of transportation to a buyer exceeds this imputed average transportation cost the seller is absorbing freight; when it is less than the imputed average he is said to be charging phantom freight.

Zone pricing practices differ from uniform national delivered prices only in that the uniform delivered price applies to an area or areas covering less than the entire country. A seller may have two zone prices, such as charging one uniform price to all buyers located east of the Rocky Mountains and a different uniform price to all buyers located west of the Rocky Mountains. Or a seller may divide the country into several zones, as is done in the parcel-post system, and charge a uniform price to all customers within each zone. 

In this case the seller also averages the transportation costs to his various customers. The averaging is, however, limited to the average cost of transportation to all the customers within each zone. 

The respondents in the Cement Institute case (333 U. S. 683) were charged with conspiring, and were found by the Federal Trade Commission to have conspired, to fix prices by means of the use of a multiple-basing-point system. They were also found to have conspired to discriminate in price in violation of the Clayton Act, by receiving varying mill net returns. These findings were upheld by the Supreme 

Court which thus found their practices illegal. That action is wholeheartedly and unequivocally approved. There is universal agreement that all such practices are and must be illegal. The decision of the 

Supreme Court in the Cement Institute case, as distinguished from the dicta (language of the Court not necessary to the decision), holds only that the conspired use of any pricing practice is illegal. 

In the Corn Products case the Supreme Court held that, when the requisite injury to competition was shown, charging phantom freight constituted an illegal price discrimination under the Clayton Act. 

The propriety of the decision in the Corn Products case is not here under consideration and that subject is therefore not further discussed. 

While the practice of freight absorption was present in the Cement Institute case, it is elementary that any practice may be adopted by conspiracy, and when this is done it is illegal. The language of the

I. THE EFFECT ON THE NATIONAL ECONOMY OF REQUIRED F. O. B. 

MILL SELLING A. ECONOMIC ASPECTS OF REQUIRED F. O. B. MILL SELLING AND THEIR RELATIONSHIP TO THE GENERAL PURPOSES OF THE ANTITRUST LAWS 

The American people have long adhered to the belief that a competitive free-enterprise system provides the best means of achieving economic democracy. The Congress has sought to safeguard this principle in several acts. Such acts have been designed to insure that competitive economic principles are permitted to function free from internal and external abuses. This position is so well recognized that the proponents of every pricing practice loudly acclaim that their theory of pricing best promotes competition. That pricing practice which best promotes and sponsors competition cannot be questioned. 

This report must consider, therefore, whether required f. o. b. mill pricing affords greater stimulus to competition than a pricing practice permitting freight absorption where necessary to meet competitive conditions, or whether required f. o. b. mill pricing stifles competition. 

Innumerable arguments have been, can be, and undoubtedly will be offered in favor of and against required f. o. b. mill pricing. For purposes of convenience these arguments are presented under six 

(1) Does required f. o. b. mill pricing tend to increase or decrease the intensity of competition among both buyers and sellers? (2) Will prices paid by the ultimate consumer tend to be increased or decreased under required f. o. b. mill prices? (3) Is the theory of some economists that competition at the boundries of the markets affords ample consumer protection under required f. o. b. selling realistic and workable? (4) Does required f. o. b. mill selling serve as a deterrent to "price fixing agreements"? (5) Will plant locations tend to be selected on the basis of the greatest social and economic benefits under required f. o. b. mill pricing? (6) Will required f. o. b. selling promote regional self-sufficiency and if so, is this desirable?

Having reviewed the economic consequences of required f. o. b. mill pricing under these six categories, consideration will then turn to the impact upon various sections of the populations representing important segments of our national economy. 

1. Does required f. o. b. mill pricing tend to increase or decrease the intensity of competition among both buyers and sellers?

Some spokesmen for the Federal Trade Commission favor required f. o. b. mill pricing. As will be shown, however, there is considerable confusion on this question within the Commission and its staff and apparently as a result of this congressional inquiry there has been an effort at the Commission to disown advocacy of required f. o. b. mill

selling. There seems to be no dissension concerning the Commission's advocacy of what it calls "price competition." Price competition is claimed to require giving buyers a choice of several prices. Such competition is said to be absent when the buyer has little choice as to the price he must pay for a given commodity. Hence the existence of price competition requires the various sellers of a given commodity in a given market to have different prices.

There can be no argument with this position unless the commodity is publicly recognized as homogeneous in nature. A cement dealer cannot pay more for the product of one producer than for the like product of other producers. A baker cannot economically pay more for the flour of one miller than for like flour of another miller. The candy manufacturer cannot pay more for the sugar or glucose of one refiner than for the like products of another. This same competitive condition applies to steel, chemicals, lumber, glass containers, and innumerable other commodities. For such items of commerce real market competition among both buyers and sellers brings about price uniformity. Significantly the Robinson-Patman Act was expressly designed to permit such dealers in competition with each other to purchase their requirements at the same price. The absence of price uniformity may even be an indication of inadequacy of competition among the sellers in homogeneous commodities. The evidence before the subcommittee is overwhelming that in such homogeneous commodities the business goes to the seller with the lowest price. 

A buyer is interested primarily in the price he pays for a commodity when and at the point where he consumes or resells the comcdity. His interest in f. o. b. mill prices is largely theoretical. But an f. o. b. mill price results in varying prices the buyer has to pay for a given commodity in any given market whether or not the commodity is characterized by a high degree of homogenity. The required adoption of f. o. b. mill-pricing policy fails to provide a freedom of choice by the consumer in the selection of the goods to be purchased. 

The relative quantity of goods purchased from any seller tends to fluctuate with a variety of variables including service, salesmanship, quality, ability to deliver, credit, and many other determinants of action including the relationship of the seller's price to other prices. 

Under the f. o. b. mill pricing philosophy, the consumer is not economically free to recognize any of these factors except price, unless he pays the penalty incident to higher freight costs for exercising such choice. This circumscribing of consumer choice by f. o. b. mill pricing fails to meet the first test of a competitive market. It deprives the buyer of a choice of sellers and compels him to purchase from the geographically closest supplier. 

A truly competitive market, and competitive prices, assumes that there is keen rivalry among sellers as well as among buyers. The adoption of f. o. b. mill prices on standardized commodities tends to create local markets, controlled by the seller most advantageously located freight-wise with respect to that market. There is a likelihood that the ultimate effect will be a division of the national market into small local monopolies, near monopolies, or areas in which particular distributors may enjoy a degree of monopolistic influence because of their natural freight advantage which automatically makes for higher prices for his competitors. Hence it appears that required f. o. b. mill pricing may tend to reduce competition among sellers, as

compared with the greater competition permissible when sellers are allowed to absorb freight whenever necessary to reach beyond their natural markets.

The competition among sellers in a given market flowing from permitting freight absorption results in their ability to maintain competitive prices in the market. This important aspect of competition is the basis of "blanket" freight rates established under the Interstate Commerce Commission Act. Blanket rates permit a commodity to be shipped from any point within a large producing area to a given consuming center at the same freight rate, or permit a commodity to be shipped from a limited producing area to a large consuming area at the same rate.

A well-known blanket rate is that applied to California oranges, under which the freight rate is the same on oranges shipped to Chicago as it is on oranges shipped to Denver. This, the Interstate Commerce 

Commission finds, permits California oranges to compete in the eastern markets with Texas and Florida oranges.

The Interstate Commerce Commission also permits varying freight rates to different ports within the United States to proportion fairly export business. The Commission allows the same freight rate to a 

Gulf port from a given point of origin as to an eastern port which may be a much shorter distance. The Interstate Commerce Commission finds that without this practice export business would be concentrated at well-located eastern ports. It has long been the practice of the 

Commission to permit variances in long- and short-haul rates to promote competition. Thus frequently the rates of two carriers between two points is the same, although the route of one carrier is in a direct line between those two points while the other proceeds by a circuitous route. Such competitive rates are permitted even though on the latter route the long-haul rate is out of proportion to short-haul rates on that same route.

The position of the Interstate Commerce Commission in thus promoting competition was unanimously sustained by the Supreme Court on January 3, 1949, in a case involving a blanket rate for coal mined in Indiana and Illinois. In that case, Ayrshire Collieries Corp. et. al. v. 

United States of America, the Court said:

Group rates have been established by the carriers so that all mines within each producing area are accorded the same rates to the same consuming destinations. * * * The Commission followed that method in this case because in its opinion such a rate structure was necessary to afford consumers, coal operators, and carriers a fair opportunity to compete in the purchase, sale, and transportation of coal from the mines in the various groups or districts to the destinations in the question. * * * It found that there was severe competition in marketing coal in this territory and that a differentially related and finely balanced rate structure on the coal was necessary in order to meet the needs of the consuming public, the mine operators, and the carriers. For, in general, all of the mines in these groups produce coal of the same quality and grade. A difference of a few cents per ton in the transportation charge is normally sufficient to divert a coal contract from one mine to another. * * * We cannot deny the Commission authority to use averages as a measure of the relationship between the rates of the Indiana groups on the one hand and the Illinois groups on the other. The Commission followed the common practice in giving diminishing weight to distance and increasing weight to competition as the length of the haul increased. 

The position of the Interstate Commerce Commission, as confirmed by the Supreme Court, is directly contrary to the Federal Trade Commission's arguments that competition is fostered by f. o. b. mill prices.

The practice of treating transportation costs to permit competitive market prices has grown up with the transportation system of America. 

The testimony before the subcommittee of the Chairman of the Interstate Commerce Commission is that the practice prevailed before the creation of the Commission. It was his opinion that the abolition of these varying freight rates, which he believed permitted and promoted competition, would be disastrous.

It appears that compulsory adoption of f. o. b. mill pricing by industry generally would be equally disastrous to the future of competition. 

2. Will prices paid by the ultimate consumer tend to be increased or decreased under required f. o. b. mill prices?

Competition heretofore characteristic of business cannot continue under adoption of required f. o. b. mill pricing policies. Inability to absorb freight to enter a broader market insures to the local producer a localized monopoly free from outside competition. Each unit will enjoy greater monopolistic aspects. No incentive to reduce costs and increase efficiency is more impelling than keen competition. The reduction of this competitive urge will doubtlessly result in higher costs which the local supplier will pass on in higher prices to the consumer in his freight advantage market.

A strongly competitive economy tends to encourage improvement and technological advances in production and distribution. Each competitive seller tries to increase his production by providing a better and better product at reduced costs. If this keen competitive urge be denied or reduced by f. o. b. mill pricing the necessity of technological progress is reduced and the impelling urge for reduced cost is minimized. Higher costs in the long run must be met by higher prices to the consumer.

The testimony before the subcommittee does not support the argument that elimination of freight absorption will result in lower prices to nearby purchasers. The increased volume resulting from sales in distant markets generally permits mass production economies which more than offset the cost of freight absorption and in fact usually result in lower prices to local customers. This appears to be true in heavy manufactured products no less than in the 5-cent candy bar where tremendous volume is required for a successful operation. 

The elimination of so-called wasteful cross hauling would probably not reduce consumer prices. In most products either the raw material or the finished product must be transported from the location of the natural resource to the consumer. Even if cross hauling were reduced, if we are to maintain our transportation system for national defense, less rail traffic can only result in higher rates for the remaining traffic or demands for Government subsidies.

It is difficult to escape the conclusion that there will be a tendency toward higher prices for reduced quality merchandise resulting from required f. o. b. mill prices. 

3. Will competition at the boundaries afford ample consumer protection? 

The proponents of required uniform f. o. b. mill pricing argue that consumers will not be subject to exorbitant prices under required f. o. b. mill pricing because competition at the boundaries of each local market will determine prices for the entire area served. The price at the boundary will be competitively determined; hence, they

е argue, the prices in the entire market will be competitively determined because of the requirement that the seller's price be uniform to all buyers. Customarily, however, the meeting place of the boundary of the natural freight advantage territory of two competitive sellers would be in areas where consumption is thin. Is it conceivable that a seller would let competition for sales at the boundary of his market, normally representing a small proportion of his volume, be the determining factor in price of his product in his entire market? We think not.

The boundary of the natural freight advantage territory for given suppliers may be the midpoint between two large cities, it may be a natural barrier such as mountains or arid spaces, or it may be determined by peculiarities of freight-rate structures. The concept of competition at the boundary of those areas in exercising a wholesome influence upon price policies, under required f. o. b. selling, is illustrated by the following drawing.

This philosophy may well be sound and beneficial to a competitive economy if we assume that the "middle area" is a large consuming market, that Seller One and Seller Two are each located in urban communities, and each eagerly desires to secure the business in the large consuming market designated as "middle area." At the other extreme, it may be assumed that "middle area" is a mountainous region, a desert, or any sparsely settled area. 

Seller One to sell his products to the right of the middle area requires the addition to his f. o. b. mill price of the substantial cost of transporting his goods across the barrier. This would require such a substantial reduction in his f. o. b. mill price that crossing the barrier with his products becomes economically impossible. The mountainous desert, or sparsely settled "middle area" becomes a barrier between the two sellers which neither will cross. Each acquires a local monopoly in the area served by him with all of the evil and iniquitous effects attending the elimination of competition. 

The theory that f. o. b. mill pricing produces competition is predicated upon the unrealistic thesis that the competitive consuming market is located in the "middle area" midway between two sellers. When the large consuming market is not midway between them, as in most instances is the case, the thesis is inapplicable. The glucose manufacturer in Iowa cannot compete with the Chicago manufacturer for eastern markets without absorbing freight. The lumber mill in the far Pacific Northwest cannot compete in the Midwest and eastern markets with his competitor in Oregon without absorbing freight to meet his cost of transportation. The oil refiners in Texas cannot compete with the refiners in the Middle West without absorbing freight. 

The steel mills in Pittsburgh cannot compete with the mills in Chicago and Cleveland for business south and west of those points without absorbing freight. The writing-paper mills of Maine and the kraftpaper mills in Mississippi cannot compete with their Middle West competitors in the large consuming areas without absorbing freight. 

It seems that our competitive economy is best advanced by permitting as many sellers as is economically possible to compete for the business of every buyer. This, it appears, can only be done when every seller, absent conspiracy or combination, is permitted to absorb any part or all of the cost of transportation in order to make his price at the customer's door competitive with that of other sellers located closer to the customer. 

4. Does required f. o. b. mill pricing serve as a deterrent to "price-fixing agreements"?

It is contended that required f. o. b. mill selling deters the accomplishment of price-fixing conspiracies. Buyers are interested only in the delivered price paid for goods purchased, hence price-fixing agreements have been found most frequently at the delivered price level. 

The Commission's principal attorney on this subject has said that freight absorption is a "handy instrument" to maintain identical conspired delivered prices.

The "handy instrument" concept can also be associated with f. o. b.. mill prices. The live-and-let-live policy of pricing might induce a geographic division of the market among sellers and foster agreements

7. ta among sellers to give each a monopoly within an agreed and predetermined area. The price-conspiracy problem might well be transposed from one national in scope to one of a regional nature, but it could still prevail and be equally contrary to the purposes of our antitrust laws.

Even if delivered prices are a handy instrument of fixing prices, we have not yet reached the point where practices equally capable of lawful as well as unlawful use are to be barred solely because capable of illegal use. The butcher knife is a handy instrument for murder; matches are a handy instrument for arson; and writing paper a handy instrument for extortion. Certainly their use is not to be prohibited in all circumstances solely because they are capable of illegal use. 

5. Will plant locations tend to be selected on the basis of the greatest social and economic benefits, under required f. o. b. mill pricing?

One of the principal contentions of the advocates of required f. o. b. mill pricing is that delivered prices are discriminatory and hence monopolistic in nature. It is contended that delivered prices nullify mistaken judgment in plant location at the expense of the consumer. If this contention has a foundation in fact, the composite of all factors in plant location is ignored under the delivered pricing system and this would constitute a serious charge against that practice. 

Proper plant location is determined only after considering a large number of important cost determinants such as the location of raw materials, the availability of labor and labor costs, adequacy of power and power rates, the size of the market adjacent to the plant and the cost of reaching that market, the adequacy of competitive facilities now located at that marketing point, and many others. Under required f. o. b. mill selling, the factor of freight on out-bound finished merchandise assumes a preponderance out of all proportion to its realistic importance in determining the over-all economies of plant location. Outbound freight is only one of the factors which must be given consideration. However, under required f. o. b. mill selling the plant closest to the consuming market can have the lowest delivered price and the greatest number of customers and thus achieve an advantage over its competitors which is unsound in terms of total social costs.

It must be recalled that the owner of a factory located adjacent to the source of raw materials has a minimum in-bound freight cost. 

However, his out-bound freight costs are substantial in getting his product to the market. The proximity of his plant to raw materials may automatically create an out-bound freight disadvantage, especially if the natural deposits are remote from a large consuming market. 

Conversely, a competitor whose plant is located at a large consuming market may have a maximum in-bound freight cost if the natural deposits of raw material are at some remote point. The latter seller is permitted to absorb all of the in-bound freight costs and has an out-bound freight advantage over the former under required f. o. b. mill pricing. There does not seem to be any justifiable reason why the producer located close to the source of raw materials should not be permitted to offset his out-bound freight disadvantage against his in-bound freight advantage by being permitted to absorb freight on the finished product to reach the market.

An equally serious problem arises with respect to plant location under a system of required f. o. b. mill pricing. Plant construction in such an economy would be designed to serve a market of a size represented by the area as to which the proposed location has the most advantageous cost of transportation. However, the size of that market is subject to the unpredictable changes in freight rates which may be approved by the Interstate Commerce Commission. Freight rate structures numbering into the hundreds of thousands are subject to constant changes and corrections. Changes are not introduced on an exactly uniform basis in all sections of the country but are adjusted as may be necessary to meet or stimulate local competitive conditions. 

Thus the size of the market tributary to any given plant is subject to shrinkage or expansion as may result from freight rate modifications. 

Construction of plant facilities cannot be soundly predicated upon such a tenuous foundation.

The size of the market that a producer may build to serve is also subject to the competitive capital flow which may undertake new plant construction at some point within the area now considered to be a part of the natural freight advantage area of the proposed location. The original supplier, however, is barred from meeting the competition in that segment of the market by freight absorption unless he be willing to reduce his f. o. b. mill price to his entire market by the amount necessary to reach the segment of the market that could be served advantageously from the new plant construction. 

Such change of his f. o. b. mill price may well be adequate to eliminate the profitability of the operation of a plant heretofore constituting a sound business operation in the economy.

This concept of location also ignores the possibility of natural shifts in population, technological improvements requiring new and different raw materials for the manufacturer's product, or even social changes requiring the seller to substantially change the character of the products he sells.

The best plant location is one which is designed for greatest efficiency in terms of all costs entering into the finished product. The total economy is best served when the composite of all such considerations result in a minimum effect for a given quantity of goods. 

It is believed that such desired objectives cannot be achieved when any one segment of cost, such as out-bound freight on finished products, is given the preeminence that it is when that item of expense is made an important determinant of the price the buyer must pay, above all other items of cost that enter into the seller's total expense of serving the market. Out-bound freight cost is one item which merits and must receive consideration in plant location. But it is only one of several. 

6. Will required f. o. b. mill selling promote regional self-sufficiency and, if so, is this desirable?

The proponents of f.o.b. mill pricing argue that such a pricing system will promote regional self-sufficiency and reduce significantly the transportation load upon the economy. Without rejecting or accepting such a hypothesis, would this claimed benefit be a beneficial change in the economy? Within each region there would appear local monopolies and a concentration of industry around the consuming markets or at the source of raw materials. Prices that would prevail in such

at et local areas may well reflect the most undesirable aspects of a noncompetitive industrial economy.

Such a trend is a reversal of the economics that have been worked out in our competitive economy. The production of shoes, textiles, and machinery is now concentrated in given areas. The production of many agricultural commodities is concentrated in specific regions, Canners, we are told, have concentrated near the source of their raw products. It is not conceivable to contemplate an economy which would necessitate moving our forests, minerals, or agricultural products to regions for further processing so as to establish what might be classified as regional self-sufficiency of processed goods. 

Specialization and free intercourse of trade between different specialized sections of the Nation have traditionally been regarded by economists as desirable for the greatest over-all social efficiency. It permits centralization of particular industries in that section of the economy which affords the greatest efficiency for that particular product. It permits a concentration of the technical personnel required by the particular industry. It makes possible adequately experienced capital investment for development of the industry. 

Such centralization of particular industries in given regions is socially desirable whenever the increased efficiency resulting therefrom is greater than the cost of transporting the finished product from that producing center to other markets for exchange with commodities which may be produced more efficiently in that region. The prohibition of trade barriers among the States of this Union was an early recognition of this principle. The present program for the rehabilitation of Europe, as approved by the Congress, is predicated on the basic desirability of encouraging free trade among the various nations of Europe and permitting each to capitalize to the maximum extent upon its natural endowments and aptitude. Our foreign policy is inconsistent with the notion that each area in Europe should operate primarily to supply the needs of the people within that area. is no sound reason for following that policy at home. There

It appears to be to our greatest national interest to advocate a continuance of a policy that each section of our Nation should make its contribution to our over-all supply of goods and services for which it is best equipped and that there should be a completely unrestricted flow of commerce among such various districts. It eppears definitely unwise to erect economic barriers predicated upon a required f. o. b.-mill pricing system within our country. It appears unwise to establish such economic barriers in any country. Although the foregoing review of the economic consequences of required f. o. b. mill pricing has been presented under six categories, admittedly no one of the categories is mutually exclusive. The thoughts thereunder tend to shade gradually into or supplement considerations given in the other categories. The aggregate of these considerations would appear clearly to indicate a lack of desirability of required f. o. b. mill pricing as a national institution. The disabilities of such a pricing system, however, can best be visualized by considering individual segments of our population. This phase will be reviewed in the following paragraphs.

B. THE EXTENT TO WHICH THE PUBLIC INTEREST IS INVOLVED IN REQUIRED F. O. B. MILL SELLING 

1. The national military security

The national military security is of considerable importance in the consideration of any question presented to the Congress. In this atomic age, the geographic concentration of industry presents too vulnerable a bombing target to be encouraged. In September 1948 the National Security Resources Board published a document entitled "National Security Factors in Industrial Location," bearing precisely upon this subject. That Board concluded: 

The whole problem of industrial dispersion boils down to a common-sense application of the old adage about not putting all of one's eggs in one basket. 

If the industrial facilities of the United States were effectively dispersed, that fact alone would make an incalculable contribution toward the maintenance of peace because of the prohibitive expense of any enemy attempt to destroy this country's ability to defend itself. Dispersion could contribute significantly toward outlawing war.

The Board noted a trend beginning several years ago toward locating manufacturing plants in the smaller cities and towns, particularly those with populations of 10,000 to 100,000. That report makes it clear that we cannot afford any requirement that would retard that trend toward locating manufacturing plants in the smaller cities. 

The proponents of f. o. b. mill pricing claim that it will result in industrial decentralization. It appears that required f. o. b. mill selling will result in numerical decentralization for some industriesthat is, there will be fewer large plants and more smaller plants. It does appear, however, that those smaller plants will be geographically concentrated at sites which are most advantageous freightwise. This concentration at freightwise advantageous locations is equally applicable to those industries in which f. o. b. mill selling will not result in more smaller plants. Thus manufacturing activities in an economy where transportation costs are controlling factors in a seller's ability to compete will be located as close as possible to either a source of raw materials or a large consuming market. 

In response to an inquiry from this subcommittee, the National Military Establishment wrote that themilitary aspects of the effect of the basing point decision on the concentration of industry are not clearly discernible at the present timebut thateventually, there may be some tendency for such consuming plants to move closer to their steel suppliers, with increased concentration around steel-producing centers. * * The resulting industrial alinement may bring greater dispersion of steel production but may also result in further concentration of industry in large consuming centers. It is also possible that in spite of shifts which may occur within specific industries, the general pattern of industrial location will remain largely unchanged. 

The Military Establishment does not appear to see any military benefits from the abolition of the basing-point system, and suggests some possible military disadvantages.

Required f. o. b. mill selling may result in the eventual decentralization of steel-producing facilities, to the extent that new mills erected in the future will probably be constructed at sites where there is a demand for steel which cannot be satisfied out of present local production. Nevertheless approximately 4 tons of inbound raw materials

are required to produce 1 ton of steel, and it therefore seems logical to assume that future steel mills will continue to be located close to sources of the raw materials they require. 

The evidence before the subcommittee is that the present cost of constructing an integrated steel mill-one not capable of producing sheet steel is $300,000,000. A mill capable of producing a complete line of steel products, including sheet steel, would, of course, cost substantially more. Current construction costs of steel mills are approximately $300 an annual ingot ton of capacity, as compared with half that cost in the thirties and a quarter of that cost before the first World War. Thus, in order to achieve a fair return on invested capital, the selling price which new steel mills would require might have to be as much as $9 a ton above the prices of present mills in order to compensate for the increased capital required for construction. That differential might well prevent even a more strategically located new mill from acquiring adequate business, during a buyer's market, to operate profitably.

To the extent that there will be a relocation in the steel industry it appears that it will be a movement by the fabricators toward the mills, rather than by the mills toward the fabricators. The public press has already noted the expressed intention of many industries to move to Pittsburgh because of the availability in that city of large supplies of steel. The Commonwealth of Pennsylvania has purchased full-page magazine advertisements bearing the caption, “Will the F. O. B. Mill Pricing System Make It Necessary for You To Have a Plant in Pennsylvania?' Those advertisements state that 

A Supreme Court decision outlawing the historical basing-point system of prices for cement is forcing steel companies to abandon the basing-point system as well. 

It has been predicted that this will mean a great competitive advantage to steel fabricators and steel consumers located close to steel-producing centers and comparable disadvantages to companies far removed from steel centers. Already a number of companies are looking over the many possible plant locations in Pennsylvania where they may enjoy these advantages. 

Those advertisements contain specific references of large industrial concerns constructing "new fabricating plants in steel-producing centers" of Pennsylvania.

In the long run required f. o. b. mill selling will probably result in a decentralization of industry which will tend to make most regions of the country industrially self-sufficient. It seems equally probable, however, that within those regions there will be a geographic concentration of industry at either the sources of raw materials or in the large consuming areas. If advantage of location is to be determined only by advantage with respect to the cost of transportation, then only by locating a plant close to the source of its raw materials, or within the market where it must sell its products, can that advantage be put to use. Admiral M. L. Ring of the Navy's Bureau of Supply testified: 

Abandonment of basing-point pricing will probably result in. concentration of industry around the basic producers of steel. A large segment of Navy contracting is with fabricators of end items made of steel. Dispersion of industry is desirable from the strategic angle.

The probability, or even the possibility, that required f. o. b. mill selling will result in concentration of industry at strategic locations is in itself an answer to the inquiry as to whether such a pricing practice is in the best interest of our national military security.

An additional problem with respect to the military security of the country is presented by required f. o. b. mill selling. The proponents of that pricing philosophy claim that it will eliminate needless cross hauling of commodities which they say constitutes an economic waste. The general effect upon consumers and the Government of a reduction in rail traffic is discussed elsewhere in this report. Its effect on the military security of the country, however, was given by Gen. 

P. F. Yount, Deputy Chief of the Army Transportation Corps, who testified:

The national transportation systems are of vital concern to this country in peace and war from a national defense point of view. Because of the inherent advantages of each mode of transportation, it is necessary that the entire system be maintained in a healthy condition. Therefore, as a representative of the 

Department of the Army and the Army Transportation Corps, I wish to state that any action that would weaken the railroads of the United States would be a threat to our national defense.

H. W. Fraser, chairman of the Railway Labor Executives Associa tion composed of 20 railway labor organizations, testified: 

The railroad industry is a combination of physical properties and personnel. 

Both must be adequate if the industry is to serve the peacetime interests of the Nation, and the emergency needs in time of war if war should come. 

We are by no means free of a threat of war. If we weaken our national rail structure, we shall weaken our defense, if war comes. 

2. The national economic security

The national economic security requires that the financial integrity of small-business interests throughout the United States be maintained whenever possible. The small-business enterprises of America —particularly those in the smaller towns which are supported largely by a single small industry with which the town has grown up-are the backbone of our American economy. The effect of required f. o. b. mill selling on a concentration or decentralization of the ownership of American industrial facilities is an important factor to be considered. 

Walter B. Wooden, the Commission's principal avowed proponent of required f. o. b. mill selling, testified that there was no way to deprive a seller of the economic advantages of constructing and operating a chain of warehouses throughout the country. He testified that such a seller could ship his manufactured products to those local warehouses and sell out of each of those warehouses at f. o. b. warehouse prices. 

This would put a premium on a seller's financial ability to construct a chain of warehouses throughout the country, and be a corresponding impediment to the small-business man who does not have that financial power. It was also the testimony of Mr. Wooden that a seller owning a fleet of motor trucks could consider the cost of operating those trucks as an expense of doing business and average that cost over all of his customers. This gives a substantial competitive advantage to the financially entrenched seller who is able to equip and operate a fleet of motor trucks, and a corresponding disadvantage to his smaller competitor who is financially unable to do so. The resulting effect upon the motor-carrier industry is, of course, obvious. 

Freight absorption has little application to the sale of articles such as clothing where sales are governed largely by the individual taste or fancy of the buyer. That type of business may also be started with relatively little capital. Freight absorption, however, vitally affects the sale of most basic commodities, such as cement, steel, sugar, glucose, lumber, salt, fertilizer, clay pipe, chemicals, and the countless

other commodities where industrial users consider the product of one seller as equally satisfactory to the product of any other seller. 

In the sale of those commodities, buyers will not, and competitively they cannot, pay more for the product of one seller than for the like product of another seller. Thus freight absorption is a matter of great importance to sellers of those commodities whose plants are strategically located with respect to their consuming markets. 

The financially entrenched producers of basic commodities can, if required to do so, relocate their plants at strategic locations permitting them to cope with any pricing practice. But their less wealthy competitors may not be able so to do. The testimony before the subcommittee is that the cost of constructing an integrated steel mill is 300 million dollars, 6 to 10 million dollars for a cement plant with minimum economic capacity, 20 million dollars for a plant to produce corn derivatives, 10 to 25 million dollars for a chemicals-producing plant, and 3 million dollars for a glass-container plant with minimum economic capacity. There are certainly few smaller business concerns in those industries that can afford extensive plant relocation at those levels for current construction costs. 

The economic advantage to the financially entrenched company of f. o. b. mill selling was portrayed to the subcommittee in the testimony of an executive of a corn-derivatives producer. His company, located at Cedar Rapids, Iowa, does 8 to 9 percent of the business in that industry. Its largest competitor operates four plants (at Kansas City, Corpus Christi, and two in the Chicago area) and does between 45 and 50 percent of the business in the industry. He testified that approximately 80 percent of the total business in the industry is in the triangle bounded by the cities of Chicago; Norfolk, Va.; and

Portland, Maine. The Chicago area plants of the largest producer, of course, enjoy an advantageous location as to that entire area under required f. o. b. mill selling. The largest consuming market for corn derivatives is Chicago itself. This Iowa company could have an f. o. b. mill price which would make its price competitive in Chicago, but its price would then not be competitive in the markets to the South, the Southeast, and the Southwest because a plant of its largest competitor is located closer to each of those areas. This witness testified that his company's plant could sell only a small portion of its production in the area in which it had a natural freight advantage over its competitors. He predicted that required f. o. b. mill selling would be an advantage to the larger competitor whose plants were located strategically throughout the country and which already did virtually half of the business in the industry. For his own company he predicted, "either outright economic death, or, at best, a difficult, and perhaps hopeless struggle for survival."

An investment consultant testified that the plant of the larger producer at Corpus Christi, Tex., had just been completed at a cost of $20,000,000, but that in his opinion the smaller Iowa company could not raise $20,000,000 with which to build a new plant at a more strategic location if it were forced to move from its present location. 

The subcommittee heard illuminating testimony from an official of one of the large grain-milling firms. He testified that required f. o. b. mill selling would eliminate "milling in transit" privileges, and that this in turn would diminish the utility of grain elevators and milling facilities in the small towns and rural communities. His company

operates a large number of such elevators and he feared that required f. o. b. mill selling would destroy their value. But that company also owns several elevators in terminal markets. The witness testified that if they were forced to close their rural elevators they would expand the facilities at their larger terminal elevators. He viewed this as an unwelcome prospect to his company, but well within its financial ability. He conceded, however, that the owners of many small rural elevators who compete with his company would in all likelihood not be financially able to thus relocate their plants. 

Of the more than 100 witnesses heard by the subcommittee, the large majority were small-business men. The subcommittee was. particularly concerned with receiving the views of small-business men on this controversial subject. They were unanimous in saying that required f. o. b. mill selling would be to the disadvantage of the smallbusiness man operating a single unit and to the economic advantage of their larger and wealthier competitors operating multiple units and financially able to strategically locate their plants. 

The subcommittee heard from the representatives of three of the largest small-business associations in the country. A view frequently given was expressed by the Washington director of the National 

Federation of Small Businessmen, when he said:

Here let me make one important point crystal clear. We of the federation have not the slightest objection to the employment by any individual manufacturer in bona fide competition with others in his line of business of any pricing system, basing point, delivered price, zone price, or f. o. b.

Let me illustrate in the case of one industry with which I am very familiar: In saying what I have said above, I mean there is no objection to a tire manufacturer located, say, in Washington, D. C., selling a 6.00 x 16 tire at a delivered price of $9 to a dealer in this city, and selling a similar tire to a dealer at San Francisco, Calif., for $9. I want it understood that in saying this I fully understand that freight absorption is involved that it costs more to ship to California than it does to lay an article down at the point of manufacture. 

On the other hand, we of the federation have serious objection to any agreement between manufacturers or suppliers to use any pricing system in collusion to achieve fixed prices and do away with competition. Then, too, we have serious objection to individual manufacturers' use of any pricing system where the effect of that pricing system is to discriminate unfairly between various of his customers. 

When it was suggested to that witness that a small tire dealer who could not absorb freight in the sale of his tires would be out of business, he replied:

That is right. He could not do business and I would oppose any such ruling. 

The president of the National Small Businessmen's Association testified:

I submit that an impartial examination of the record made by the Federal Trade Commission will show that intentionally or otherwise it has moved steadily toward the elimination of freedom of enterprise from the American scene. * * *

If the basing-point method of pricing has been abused by one industry or another, has been used as a means of setting up combinations in restraint of trade, those things should be corrected, but it is not necessary to kill or otherwise destroy the whole pricing system in order to correct these abuses. Doing so, in my judgment, is in exactly the same category as burning the house down in order to kill a few cockroaches in the basement.

The president of the Conference of American Small Business Organizations testified: 

When rules and regulations are promulgated on which competent legal counsel cannot advise their clients with a degree of certainty that they are complying with the law, it destroys confidence and progress.

From all the facts I have been able to get, Congress did not intend in the FTC to grant the Commission the authority to impose f. o. b. prices, the Supreme Court to the contrary notwithstanding. 

May I hope that your investigation will convince you that it is essential to clarify the intent of Congress as to the powers granted to FTC, instead of having the Commission, as in this case, interpret the law to mean what the Commission wants it to mean for its own purposes?

Certainly, it appears that the FTC in this case has succeeded in making its interpretation the law, and it proceeds accordingly. 

As it is, instead of the FTC being a help and protection to small business, which Congress doubtless intended, if the present ruling prevails, it will disrupt longestablished business practices, thus causing great confusion and loss to small business.

The economist for the United Steel Workers' Union disagreed with most of the conclusions of the other labor leaders who testified before the subcommittee. However, he expressed an ultimate conclusion on this problem which succinctly expresses the views of the overwhelming majority of the witnesses from whom the subcommittee has heard. 

He said: * * * we have taken great exception to the basing point pricing system which the industry formerly practiced. We have taken equally as strong exception to the f. o. b. mill pricing system which the industry now practices, in major part at least. We have asked that there be a middle ground. 

I think that is one of the things which I cannot emphasize too strongly here. 

We are convinced that either system, a straight f. o. b. system or a basing-point system, is bound to serve the interests of big business; and that if we are going to attempt to get away from price fixing and to attempt to work out a system under which small business can survive, we have got to find a middle ground between those two.

As pointed out in that testimony, the basing-point system as previously used in the steel and cement industries involved the elements of a conspired use of specified basing points, the charging of phantom freight (both in the form of charging for longer hauls than actually took place and in charging rail rates for water or truck transportation), and uniform charges for extras.

The subcommittee concurs in the conclusion that the basing-point system, as formerly used in the steel and cement industries, and the compulsory f. o. b. mill pricing, which at least some people at the 

Federal Trade Commission favor, are each iniquitous, that each operates to the benefit of big business and is harmful to the small business interests of the Nation. Significantly no witness testified in favor of the former system and only two witnesses (both recommended by the 

Federal Trade Commission) testified in favor of the latter system.

The available evidence is all to the effect that the cost of industrial relocation and readjustment required by compulsory f. o. b. mill selling would be charged against, and payable by, both big and small business. But it appears that however unpleasant, big business will be able to withstand the payment of that bill. Unfortunately it seems that a large segment of small business will be unable to meet that financial obligation. The burdens of required f. o. b. mill pricing appear to fall most heavily on small business. 

3. The effect on the American people 

An economist in Boston has written that required f. o. b. mill selling will benefit New England because it will force the erection of a steel mill in that area. A university professor has testified that required f. o. b. mill selling will accelerate the industrial growth of the Pacific

Assum Northwest. Others have written that required f. o. b. mill selling will force new industries to the Rocky Mountain areas, hasten the industrial growth of the South, and even vastly increase industrial production in the Midwest. Significantly, these people refer only to the new industries which may be attracted to those areas. ing the correctness, which is unlikely, of all those prognostications, the Congress must also consider the effect of such a change in pricing practices on the industries which are now located in each of those regions. It is not likely that any pricing policy will substantially affect the over-all production or consumption of industrial goods. 

The increase in industrial facilities at one location must of necessity be at the expense of a similar decrease in industrial facilities at some other location. If it be assumed that any particular region would gain as many new industries as it would lose in this relocation-an unlikely assumption for any com.nunity but a not unlikely assumption for any region-there would still be two unpleasant problems confronting the American people: (a) Serious and undesirable dislocation of American workmen; and (b) a substantial loss to American investors, which would appear to be a price beyond that which the Nation can afford to pay. 

The problem of industrial relocation through required f. o. b. mill selling must be determined by Congress against the background of our industrial existence in the year 1949. It must be viewed in the light of the industrial growth of the Nation during the past 150 years. 

As one member of this subcommittee has said, even if the change is desirable, we cannot afford to undertake an operation from which the patient might not survive. (a) The effect on American labor.-There are countless rural communities in America in which the livelihood of the inhabitants is dependent upon a single industry. In many cases the town grew up around the industry. The forced relocation of that business would affect not only its workers, most of whom may own their own homes in the community, but the businessmen who rely upon them for trade. It is not unlikely that in those circumstances many workers would not be able to sell their homes at fair value. Some towns might well become "ghost towns."

This prospective picture was painted for the subcommittee by the former mayor of a town in up-State New York with a population of 30,000. He testified that the community relies principally upon a single industry for employment, which has announced its intention to move to Pittsburgh as a result of the "basing point" decisions.

That industry has been in the town for more than half a century. 

The witness feared that the workers, over 90 percent of whom own their own homes, would not be able to sell their homes if forced to follow their employer to Pittsburgh. He testified that the effect of the proposed move had already been felt in the plans for a new high school. Although a bond issue had been voted for the new school, it is now being held in abeyance because of the effect on the town's tax revenue of the loss of that industry.

II. BRIEF SUMMARY OF EVIDENCE BEFORE SUBCOMMITTEE AS TO PRICING PRACTICE AND EFFECTS THEREON OF F. O. B. MILL SELLING 

The subcommittee heard testimony (under S. Res. 241) as to the effect of pricing practices of the Federal Trade Commission on industry on November 9, 10, 11, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19, 29, and 30, and December 6, 7, and 8, 1948. During that period of time 109 witnesses appeared before the subcommittee. Three attorneys for the Federal 

Trade Commission and the Assistant Attorney General in charge of the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice testified as to their interpretation of the law on this subject. The other 105 witnesses all gave testimony as to the effect of those pricing policies on industry. Required f. o. b. mill selling was supported by only two witnesses as being in the best interests of the country. One was an attorney for the Federal Trade Commission and the other an economist recommended to the subcommittee by the Commission. All other witnesses opposed required f. o. b. pricing.

The most significant summary of the voluminous testimony can be made in two sentences. 

1. The spokesmen of no one industry felt that the industry he represented would be as competitive or as economically sound if operating under a required f. o. b. mill pricing system as it would be if permitted to absorb freight where necessary to meet competitive conditions or to establish and maintain delivered prices. 

2. The businessmen from no one section of the country felt that that geographical region would benefit from required f. o. b. mill pricing in contrast to industry being permitted to absorb freight where necessary to meet competitive conditions or to maintain delivered prices.

Inasmuch as this subcommittee could find neither an industry nor a region of the country requiring the adoption of f. o. b. mill pricing, the condensation and characterization of the testimony can most vividly be portrayed and problems understood if presented on an industry basis. This practice is adopted because it permits a discussion of particular problems in the light of the customary practices within each industry. cost.

CEMENT 

Cement is a bulky homogeneous commodity. The expense of transporting it to the market is an important factor in the laid-down A difference in the cost of transportation from two competitive producers, therefore, places one at a distinct competitive disadvantage as compared with the other. The product of each producer is substantially identical and the delivered price is a controlling factor, usually the controlling factor, in determining the particular brand purchased.

Under the pricing system prevailing before the decision in the Cement Institute case, the more distant mills absorbed freight to meet the competitive price of the nearby producer. There is no question concerning the propriety of condemning the price conspiracy which the Commission and the Court found to exist. But testimony offered on the economic consequences of compulsory f. o. b. pricing. believed by many to result from the decision, points to the creation of serious economic problems.

Cement-producing mills located in deficit producing areas will receive substantial windfall benefits under compulsory f. o. b. mill pricing. They will be able to sell their entire production in the local

market at maximum prices, even during a period characterized as a buyers' market. This undesirable result will follow if competitive mills located at less advantageous locations are not permitted to absorb freight to enter the deficit market with their products. Conversely, cement mills located in surplus production areas will be at a serious competitive disadvantage which may well cause some of them to go out of business. Denying these mills the right to compete in distant markets through the absorption of freight costs may prevent them from disposing of the cement they produce in excess of local requirements. This in turn compels a reduction of volume with increased production costs and a resulting unsound economic condition. 

This is particularly true in areas such as the Lehigh Valley of Pennsylvania. Cement was first introduced in the United States in that area and for a long time a substantial portion of the cement used in the Nation was produced there. The valley now produces many times the quantity of cement which is locally consumed. It can sell its present volume of production only through the absorption of freight to distant markets.

Plants receiving a windfall benefit would in all likelihood include cement mills such as the one owned by the United States Steel Corp. within the Chicago switching district. This mill is one of the largest producers of cement in the country. Its location gives it a freight advantage in the entire Chicago metropolitan market of as much as 30 cents a barrel below the freight cost from the three cement mills in La Salle and Oglesby, Ill. Required f. o. b. mill pricing will in all likelihood deny access to the Chicago market to the smaller producers at La Salle and Oglebsy, except to the extent that the steel corporation's mill is unable to supply the total needs in the Chicago switching district.

Dealers in cement who buy from the producers and resell to consumers unanimously complain of the economic effects of the decision. 

Although the decision was to be primarily for their benefit, they appear to be the innocent victims of f. o. b. mill pricing. Many cement dealers are located in small towns and handle not only cement but also a full line of building materials. The inability to maintain a competitive price for cement frequently results in the loss of an order for the materials to be used in constructing the entire building. Under f. o. b. mill selling the dealer who purchases his cement from the closest mill has the lowest laid-down cost. Dealers buying from more distant mills have a higher cost. It is frequently impossible for dealers to make new connections with the closest mill especially during the current shortage of cement. Cement dealers testified that in being compelled to purchase at f. o. b. mill prices from a more distant mill, they are denied the right to compete for business with dealers purchasing from closer sources. Further objection is raised by the dealers that even though they might be able to purchase their needs from the closest mill, required f. o. b. selling would compel the dealer to purchase from that source regardless of the service policy, credit terms, or corporate integrity of the seller. The dealers felt that being forced to purchase from a given mill, because of that producer's local monopoly resulting from his freight-advantage, appeared to them to be disruptive of competition rather than fostering and developing competition.

STEEL 

The problems of steel producers are substantially similar to those of the cement producers. Steel mills located in deficit production areas will receive substantial economic gain by required f. o. b. selling. 

Whereas producers located in surplus-production areas will find themselves at serious economic disadvantage if denied access to distant markets for their surplus production. This can well be illustrated by considering the Pittsburgh area which is the principal surplus-production point in the country. Pittsburgh produces far more steel than can be consumed in its present freight-advantage area. For the producers in this area to maintain their relative position in the industry it will be necessary- (a) to attract a tremendous number of new steel fabricators to the area, or (b) to establish an f. o. b. mill price sufficiently below prices at other supply points as to permit Pittsburgh penetration of markets beyond its freight advantage zone, or (c) to secure a reduction of freight rates for the Pittsburgh group of producers, to permit competition for steel requirements in distant large consuming centers, or (d) to establish warehouses and f. o. b. warehouse prices throughout more distant markets and double-handle a significant portion of their output at considerable increased costs. 

If some one of the above possibilities or the partial accomplishment of some of these alternatives do not result in a steel market equal to the present productive level, there can be no alternative under an f. o. b. pricing system but to reduce the level of operations. The economic consequences of such an act on the Pittsburgh area is readily apparent. 

The economic consequences of many of the possible alternatives are equally apparent. The consumer of steel will obviously pay the bill for such dislocations. The disadvantage to the Nation of a movement of fabricators to Pittsburgh is hereafter discussed. 

One of the steel producers who would secure immediate advantages from required f. o. b. pricing is a large company which operates a single steel mill in the Chicago area. The sales manager of that company testified that his company would be benefited by required f. o. b. pricing, but that in his judgment the over-all effects of such pricing practices would be to so impede competition as to be injurious to all business and thus ultimately reflect adversely on his own company. 

The representatives of steel producers did not defend the charging of phantom freight, which had previously been the custom in that industry. In the main its impropriety was conceded and the witnesses contended only for the right to absorb freight in order to sell in more distant markets. The freight absorption necessary to reach distant markets is frequently not substantial. A steel producer at Kokomo, Ind., testified that his company could compete for the sale of galvanized sheets in Kansas City, Mo., through the absorption of 60 cents per ton freight; that it could compete in Oklahoma City, Okla., through the absorption of 20 cents a ton freight; and that it could compete in Omaha, Nebr.; Denver, Colo.; and Salt Lake City, Utah, through absorption of freight of 80 cents a ton. This witness pointed out a very substantial area in which his company could not compete without absorbing freight. He testified that that large market could

es be reached with a maximum freight absorption of $2 a ton, and with average freight absorption of 40 to 50 cents a ton. 

The testimony of steel users, including fabricators and jobbers, unanimously opposed compulsory f. o. b. mill pricing.

The steel dealers who are unable to purchase their supplies from the nearest steel mill are at a competitive disadvantage as compared with other dealers whose suppliers are more favorably located. This is particularly true of the steel warehousing business. The warehouse with the closest source of supply has a real advantage over all other such businesses. This advantage may be substantial enough to reduce the number of warehouses in many communities to the single concern representing the closest steel mill.

The steel fabricators located at a considerable distance from their steel mill supplier have a marked disadvantage in the cost of raw materials as compared with competitors closer to the plant. However, the problem confronting the fabricator is compounded by the prohibition against absorption of out-bound freight. A steel fabricator whose plant is not located close to a steel mill or to a large consuming area finds itself at a disadvantage both as to the cost of its raw materials and as to its competitive ability to sell in distant markets. 

The subcommittee has heard the testimony of many steel fabricators that a substantial portion of their current business would be lost if they were kept from absorbing freight to distant markets. These witnesses denied that giving up business in distant markets involving freight absorption would reduce the price of their commodities to nearby purchasers. They denied that the funds expended in freight absorption to distant customers compelled a higher price to nearby customers. On the contrary, their testimony was that the increased volume of production through sales in distant markets lowered the unit cost, through mass production economies, to all buyers, and that such savings in the cost of production exceeded the amounts involved for freight absorption, and in fact resulted in lower prices to the nearby customers.

The subcommittee has heard from a very substantial number of business enterprises located largely in the smaller communities, throughout the country, who sincerely urge that required f. o. b. selling would cause them to lose a very substantial portion of the business which they now enjoy. They believe this curtailment in production would substantially reduce employment in the towns in which their industries are located and might well jeopardize the continued financial solvency of the companies. 

CONSTRUCTION INDUSTRY 

The influence upon the construction industry of f. o. b. mill selling is particularly acute since the principal ingredients in most construction are cement, steel, and other heavy basic commodities in which the products of all sellers are virtually identical and in which freight represents a substantial part of the delivered cost. Required f. o. b. mill selling would virtually necessitate each contractor acquiring his supplies from the steel mill, cement plant or producer of other commodities located closest to the site of his construction job or be unable to compete. It seems unlikely that each contractor will be able to dacquire his needs in all cases from the closest mill. This difficulty is 

---

far more common during periods of shortages in steel and cement, as prevail at the present time. It is frequently impossible at this time, for the mill closest to the construction site to take on new customers. 

However, even in normal times it appears that there will be limitations upon the ability of each contractor in all events to purchase his materials from the nearest sources of supply when the construction jobs may frequently be in different communities. This will tend to result in increased costs of building construction. 

Required f. o. b. mill selling would also deprive contractors of the privilege of maintaining continued relations with any supplier. This is frequently desirable in the interests of maintaining credit relations and acquiring the service which permits effective competition. Frequently a contractor's ability to compete for a job may depend on the willingness of a local supplier to sell to him. This may be denied to a contractor for the benefit of a favored customer of the supplier. 

The increased costs of construction materials due to f. o. b. selling has been particularly felt in the road-construction industry where cement is such a substantial item in the cost of building concrete roads. 

CHEMICALS 

Industrial chemicals are frequently heavy, bulky commodities. 

The chemicals of one manufacturer are for all practical purposes the same as those of any other industrial manufacturer. They are sold and shipped in large quantities, and sales are generally made on the basis of the delivered cost to the buyer. 

Sellers of chemicals testified that the effect of required f. o. b. selling in that industry would be to give a competitive advantage to the nearest producer of such proportion that it might eliminate competition. The effect would be particularly felt by distributors or jobbers of chemicals. These dealers generally handle a full line of chemicals representing several producers. Through the absorption of freight where necessary by both the producer and the dealer, various products may be sold in active competition with the production of other manufacturers sold by other dealers.

The effect of required f. o. b. mill selling would be to give each supplier and his dealer a competitive advantage which in many instances might amount to a local monopoly in that area where he has a freight advantage. In areas where two or more competing dealers have the same outgoing freight advantage, the competitive advantage would accrue to the dealer whose product was purchased from the closest source of supply.

The chemical industry is one of tremendous and growing importance to the country. For example, soda ash, caustic soda, and chlorine are relatively unknown to the general public but they are vital in the manufacture of glass, paper, soap, and rayon, and in the manufacture of other chemicals. A most potent example of the necessity of freight absorption is shown by a newcomer to the industry located in Wyoming. The soda ash manufacturers in the United States are now all located east of the Mississippi River, in Texas, and in California. They are all located within close proximity to consuming markets, as compared with the proposed plant in Wyoming. This newcomer to the industry proposes to erect a plant at the site of a large trona deposit. Trona can be converted into soda ash at a relatively

low cost. There are, however, few soap factories, glass plants, or other consumers of soda ash in Wyoming or the surrounding area. This company proposes to enter a market in competition with well-ent trenched companies located more advantageously freightwise to the consuming centers and capitalize on low-cost production of the prime product. It can only hope to sell its output in distant markets at prices which are competitive with those now in the industry. It cannot establish a single f. o. b. mill price that might make its delivered prices competitive in Tulsa but would not make its delivered prices competitive in New Orleans or Chicago. The engineer for this company testified that it can successfully operate the Wyoming plant only through being permitted to absorb varying amounts of freight with each market having a factory net back different from any other market.

FOOD AND ALLIED COMMODITIES 

The food industry includes many allied commodities that are also affected by required f. o. b. mill selling. Specialty food products, such as breakfast foods, brand-named jellies, condiments, and countless other similar items, are generally sold on a uniform delivered-price basis. The high volume required for mass production is essential to low unit cost of production and the cost of transportation is relatively small.

Candy bars, chewing gum, and cough drops are also sold on a uniform delivered-price basis. Testimony was given before the subcommittee that required f. o. b. mill selling would reduce the volume of many plants, increasing unit costs, cause additional bookkeeping by eliminating the practice of a single-invoice price to all customers, and limit competition to the extent that the cost to the consumer of those commodities would, in fact, be increased. 

Heavy food commodities, such as flour and sugar, are generally sold upon a freight-absorption basis. The testimony before the subcommittee is that required f. o. b. selling would seriously restrict production in many flour mills and sugar refineries in the West and far West so as to make their continued successful operation uncertain. 

It is feared that required f. o. b. selling would eliminate "milling in transit" freight rates, which would make the operation of flour mills in rural communities unprofitable and result in a further concentration of flour mills at the terminal markets and consuming centers. 

The canned-goods industry would be particularly affected by required f. o. b. mill selling. Many canneries, particularly those located in the Middle Atlantic States, are close to the large consuming markets and would thus have a substantial economic advantage in required f. o. b. mill selling. The great majority of the canning factories, however, are located in the rural or farming communities close to the crops of catches from the sea, which they package. Fish caught and packed in the Pacific Northwest are sold throughout the country. Agricultural products grown in the West or on the coast of 

California are distributed nationally. These canned goods are frequently sold on a uniform delivered basis, but not infrequently on an f. o. b. cannery basis with freight absorbed where required to be competitive.

The canner, like the beet-sugar refiner and other processors of agricultural products, cannot restrict his sales to the area within which

he has a freight advantage. In fact, it is not possible, as has been suggested by some economists, for food processors to plan their sales activities on the basis of supplying the market within a given region. 

These producers usually contract to purchase a farmer's entire crop of a given commodity. If the crop is bountiful, the canner has a large quantity of canned products which he must sell. If the crops are scarce, he has a much smaller volume to sell. The area within which these producers must sell those quantities of the products they have for sale is thus dependent, to a considerable extent, on the uncertainty of the weather and all the other factors governing agricultural yield.

Farming interests would be adversely affected by required f. o. b. selling, not only through inability of processors to thus purchase their entire acreage, but through the competitive inability of small flour mills to continue to operate in rural communities through the prospective loss of milling in transit privileges.

The impact on the food industry of required f. o. b. mill selling cannot be accurately portrayed within the reasonable limits of this report. The problems generated, however, reach to most commodities. Even the dairy industry is affected. Most large dairy communities are the sites of small condensed milk canneries. Frequently these are cooperative ventures of the dairy farmers. Wisconsin produces approximately 27 percent of the canned milk of the country, yet it utilizes less than 2 percent of the canned milk consumed in the country. Only through absorbing freight can the Wisconsin milk canneries compete with the large integrated canned milk producers who have multiple plants scattered throughout the country. The failure of these small milk canneries to continue operations would adversely affect the dairy farmers who rely upon them, as well as the rural communities in which they are located. PAPER The manufacture of paper requires timber, large quantities of water, and abundant cheap power. These natural resources are found only where Nature deposited them. A very substantial portion of fine paper (paper used for magazines, stationery, etc., as distinguished from kraft paper used as wrapping paper, etc.) is manufactured in northern New England, largely Maine, and the Pacific Northwest. A substantial amount of paper converting is carried on in Wisconsin. 

Much of the kraft paper used in the country is produced in Arkansas, Louisiana, and Mississippi. These producing areas are all relatively sparcely populated. Required f. o. b. mill selling would result in a substantial advantage to the few paper mills located close to the large consuming areas, and result in a substantial disadvantage to the paper mills which have long been established close to the natural resources. 

A large proportion of the producers in this basic industry cannot continue to compete for available business if they are denied the right to absorb freight on sales in large consuming areas. 

The Committee has received a substantial number of telegrams and letters from labor unions in the paper industry vigorously protesting that the denial of their employers' right to absorb freight would inevitably result in either (1) decreased production that would result in a reduction in employment at the respective mills, or (2) pressure

from the employers for wage reductions to have plant prices permitting f. o. b. mill selling with competitive delivered prices in the principal consuming areas. GLASS Glass containers are a relatively cheap and bulky commodity. 

Freight is thus a substantial item in the delivered cost. Glass plants are located primarily at the sources of raw materials (largely sand and silica) and abundant cheap fuel. The market for these products, however, is largely in the metropolitan centers. This requires the glass producer located in a rural area to absorb freight to the consuming markets in order to be competitive with container manufacturers located closer to the consumers. 

There are 31 companies manufacturing glass containers. Twentyone of these companies operate but one plant and do about 20 percent of the business. The remaining 10 companies are multiple-plant operators and do 80 percent of the business. The testimony before the subcommittee is that required f. o. b. mill selling would be advantageous to the multiple-plant operators who could ship to customers from their closest plant, and would be most disadvantageous to the single-plant operators, most of whom are located in rural areas. OIL The oil industry appears to be vitally affected by any prohibition against freight absorption. The largest oil reserves are found in the areas in and surrounding the States of Oklahoma, Texas, and California. No pricing practice can result in the relocation of those oil wells. The legality of freight absorption practices, however, may substantially affect the location of oil refineries. Any processor may absorb in-bound freight costs, but required f. o. b. mill selling prevents a processor from absorbing out-bound freight costs. Thus under required f. o. b. mill selling, refineries located in Texas would be prohibited from absorbing the cost of transportation in the sale of their products in the large consuming markets to the north and east. 

Thus a Texas refiner who could not absorb part of the cost of trans-. portation would be at a competitive disadvantage with a Chicago refiner in the sale of their products in the large consuming markets to the north and east. An oil refiner with plants in Texas and Oklahoma has testified that in his opinion Texas can consume only 25 percent of the oil refined there and that required f. o. b. mill selling would result in forcing a substantial proportion of the total refinery capacity in Texas to move closer to the consuming markets. There are innumerable other industries and commodities concerned with the problem of freight absorption. It is felt, however, that the foregoing is a rough cross section of the effect of required f. o. b. mill pricing on industry and a consideration of other industries would only tend to unduly extend this report.

Although many business witnesses appeared before the subcommittee and testified as to the harmful effects of required f. o. b. mill selling, it does appear that sufficient time has not yet elapsed to determine the actual effect on the cement, steel, and other industries in which that pricing practice has been adopted. The experience

during the last 9 months of f. o. b. mill selling in steel and cement has been distorted, due to the seller's market prevailing in those commodities. Of necessity, an accurate indication of the true effect of f. o. b. mill pricing in industry cannot be determined in that short period of time, especially when the period consists largely of a seller's market. The testimony heard by the subcommittee is thus based largely upon the conclusions of witnesses as to what would happen to their respective businesses if f. o. b. mill pricing was required of all industry.

III. THE HISTORY OF F.O.B. MILL PRICING AND THE ACTIVITIES OF THE FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION IN THAT DIRECTION 

In 1921 the Federal Trade Commission filed a complaint against the United States Steel Corp. group charging their unlawful use of the 

Pittsburgh-Plus pricing system. The Commission entered an order in 

1924 directing those steel companies to cease and desist from using that pricing system. It has been generally accepted since that date that the Pittsburgh-Plus system was an unfair and improper means of pricing steel. The Commission's order, however, prohibited those steel companies from selling their steel products on any other basing point than that where the product was manufactured or from which it was shipped. IV. THE CURRENT CONFUSION AS TO THE LEGALITY OF PRICING PRACTICES 

While varying views are held as to the causes, there is virtual unanimity of opinion that undesirable confusion now exists as to the legality of pricing practices involving freight absorption and delivered prices. It is the purpose of this section to document the extent and at least some of the causes of that confusion. 

On September 21, 1948, Hon. Charles Sawyer, Secretary of Commerce, reflected national business uncertainty when he issued a public statement saying:

The Supreme Court decision in the Cement Institute case, outlawing the multiple-basing-point pricing system as practiced in that industry, has aroused widespread concern in the world of business. From discussions I have held with the Department's business advisory council and from communications which I have received, I know that many firms, large and small, are worried about the impact which the decision may have upon their own pricing practices. This is a serious matter and one with which the Department of Commerce is vitally concerned.

Whether or not the Supreme Court decision actually requires the drastic pricing changes which some members of industry feel that it does require, the fact remains that many changes are already being made and that the effect both on the producer and the purchaser has been unsettling. 

On January 24, 1949, the Secretary of Commerce testified before this subcommittee on S. 236, saying that: * * * The uncertainty in the minds of many businessmen as to what pricing practices are illegal, and the reasons for that uncertainty, have been abundantly documented in the testimony already given to this committee. Obviously some clarification is desirable.

We recognize that the antitrust and unfair competition laws by their very nature must be general in wording and that it is difficult to anticipate by precise language all arrangements which must be avoided by the businessman. I do believe, however, that uncertainty should be kept to a minimum. For this reason, I endorse the general purpose of this bill to remove, so far as possible, the existing confusion as to what businessmen may do legitimately in pricing their products.

The confusion confronting businessmen also appears to prevail within the Commission itself.

At the November hearings of the subcommittee, 16 questions were asked of each of 3 attorneys for the Federal Trade Commission, Attorneys Wooden, Paulson, and Phelps, and Assistant Attorney General

Herbert A. Bergson. The divergence in their answers to the same questions appears to justify a conclusion that considerable confusion by the public as to the law on this subject is inevitable. The questions asked those Government attorneys and their respective answers are as follows:

ANSWERS BY MESSES. PAULSON, PHELPS, Wooden, and Bergson to IDENTICAL QUESTIONS ASKED EACH OF THEM 

Question No. 1: Is it now the law that the individual and separate practice or system of a company in absorbing all or part of the freight costs to distant customers to meet lower prices of nearby competitors results in varying mill net prices and is illegal whenever the Commission finds there may be an injury to competition?

Mr. PAULSON. Under the Conduit order, if one other qualification is added, that those prices are met so that they are matched, so they are identical, the practice would be violative of the Conduit order. 

Mr. PHELPS. My answer to that is "Yes." Even in the absence of conspiracy it is my view.

Mr. WOODEN. There is no doubt about the answer down through the words "mill net prices." The answer is obviously that it results in varying mill net prices.

The question of whether it is illegal whenever the Commission finds that there may be injury to competition, my answer to that, as it stands, is "No." But my answer would be "Yes," if you were to insert the words similar to these I suggest after the word "whenever," these words: "upon evidence that the court will recognize as substantial and convincing and is such that reasonable minds would reasonably accept it."

Mr. BERGSON. Absorption of freight costs on sales made to distant purchasers results in varying mill net prices, depending on the freight rates between the mill of the seller and the places of delivery. 

This is not illegal, however, unless it produces an effect on competition of a kind specified in section 2 of the Clayton Act.

A finding by the Commission as to such effect is not conclusive. 

It is subject to review by the courts and will be set aside if it lacks evidentiary support or is otherwise legally erroneous.

Another consideration applicable to your question is that sales at different prices to different purchasers are not illegal if the seller shows that his lower price was "made in good faith to meet an equally low price of a competitor. 

Question No. 2: Does the law, as interpreted by the Court, permit the Commission to hold such a practice illegal if it finds a reasonable possibility of injury to competition?

Mr. PAULSON. It is illegal without finding injury to competition. It is illegal under the Conduit order per se if you say "match" instead of "meet." * * *

Then you have a situation which violates the Conduit order. That is regardless of whether there may be injury to competition or not.

Mr. PHELPS. I think that was definitely determined in the Morton Salt case. 

Mr. WOODEN. My answer to that as it stands is "No," because I believe there has been a great deal of exaggeration and unfounded assumptions created and built upon the literal interpretation of those two words, "reasonable possibility." 

I believe that even the minority in the Morton Salt case did not properly take account of the context in which those two words were used. 

Mr. BERGSON. Section 2 makes a price discrimination unlawful where its effect may be substantially to lessen competition or to injure, destroy, or prevent competition. Federal Trade Commission v. Morton Salt Co. (334 U. S. 37), held that a price discrimination comes within the ban of section 2 if there is "reasonable possibility" that it will have the defined effect on competition. 

Prior to this decision it had not been definitely determined whether this section dealt with effect in terms of reasonable "probability" or reasonable "possibility.' 

I think it clear that the Supreme Court has now adopted the latter view; but, because of the connotations of the word "reasonable," I doubt that the difference between the two interpretations is, as a practical matter, very great. 

Question No. 3: Is there any yardstick or formula by which a businessman can tell in advance whether his individual freight absorption practice may be injurious to competition?

Mr. PAULSON. Well, he pretty well knows if the Conduit order sticks he cannot systematically receive varying mill nets when matching prices. And that is a rather broad and comprehensive coverage. * * * My answer in the light of the Conduit order, as I read it, would have to be that its practical effect is to require f. o. b. mill selling.

Mr. PHELPS. I would not think so if he had any competition. 

Mr. WOODEN. I do not think there is or can be any such yardstick or formula. any more than there is or can be any such yardstick or formula in the case of violations of the Sherman Act.

Mr. BERGSON. Although certain general standards have already been pointed out by the Court, there does not appear to be any categorical yardstick or formula. 

The problem is not essentially different from that arising under any statute which is designedly framed in general terms in order to avoid the undue rigidity and unanticipated limitations likely to result from detailed specification of the statutory prohibitions.

Under section 2 of the Clayton Act, as under other statutes, the gradual process of applying it in varying factual situations serves to clarify its meaning: and scope.

Question No. 4: If a man in good faith and acting individually and separately uses a freight-absorption practice which is later found by the Commission to be illegal, is he liable to triple-damage suits? Mr. PAULSON. Well, not under the Conduit order. The Conduit order is issued under section 5. The Commission has not gone in its section 2 orders to the limit that it has in the Conduit order. * So I do not see that any businessman has been put in jeopardy of triple-damage suits up to this date unless it be the United States Steel Corp. in Pittsburgh-plus. * * * I presume, if the Commission eventually establishes that Conduit order is the proper position to take under section 2 orders, there would be more danger of treble-damage suits. which seems to be what this question asks.

Mr. PHELPS. He would be liable to triple-damage suits whether the Commission found it to be illegal or not if it is an unlawful price discrimination and if the plaintiff can establish damages with sufficient certainty. 

The triple-damage suit has been so that the damages suffered by the plaintiff,. as a rule, are speculative and they are hard to measure. 

Mr. WOODEN. Of course, opinions might differ as to what constituted good: faith in a particular case, but answering the question without going into that,. 

I would say that he is liable to triple-damage suits if he injures competition by unlawful price discrimination, but actual damage must be proved to the satisfaction of the trial court. Not even reasonable probability of damage would be sufficient in such a case. I doubt that the Commission's findings of illegality: or even its findings of fact would be received as evidence in a private damage suit, though of course the evidence in one case could be used in the other. 

Mr. BERGSON. If a violation of section 2 damages some other person, the violator is liable to a triple-damage suit irrespective of whether his violation was- "in good faith" in a subjective sense.

A commission finding that his pricing practices in absorbing freight were illegal would necessarily include a finding that they were not adopted in "good. faith."

Violation of section 2 is not a criminal offense. Where a noncriminal statute sets up a standard of conduct and imposes some form of liability on those who depart from the standard, good faith is not a defense on the absence of express or implied provision to this effect. It is, therefore, apparent that in the point now being considered, section 2 comports with the usual statutory rule. 

Question No. 5: Does this triple-damage liability date from the time the seller learned of the illegality or does it go back to the beginning of the practice? 

Mr. PAULSON. I think the State laws vary. They have different rules in the different States. Frankly, I do not know just what the State laws for the basis of the treble-damage suit provisions of the Clayton Act really are. 

Mr. PHELPS. I would agree with Mr. Bergson, this morning, that it goes as far back as the statute of limitation would permit. 

Mr. WOODEN. I would agree with Assistant Attorney General Bergson that the liability would carry back to the period of the statute of limitations. 

Mr. BERGSON. The applicable statutory period of limitations measures the period for which the seller is liable unless the injury to the plaintiff occurred at a later date. The starting point of this period is not governed either by the date when the practice first began or the date when the seller first learned of the illegality of his practice. 

Question No. 6: Is it also a criminal offense?

Mr. PAULSON. I believe it is. 

Mr. PHELPS. With reference to unlawful price discrimination, there is a section 

3 in the Robinson-Patman Act generally referred to as the Borah amendment which makes unlawful price discrimination a criminal offense. 

Mr. WOODEN. It is not a criminal offense unless it comes within the language of section 3 of the Robinson-Patman Act, but the language of that section is quite different from the language of section 2 (a) of the Clayton Act, as amended. 

Mr. BERGSON. Violation of section 2 of the Clayton Act is not a criminal offense.

Mr. PAULSON. As a practical matter it amounts to very nearly that, providingand this is rather an important proviso-provided the Commission does not eventually read into the Conduit order the statements it is making with regard to the Conduit order.

I give the Conduit order its strictest possible interpretation. The Commission has publicly given it a lesser interpretation. 

My answer in the light of the Conduit order, as I read it, would have to be that its practical effect is to require f. o. b. mill selling. 

If I answer as against what many of the staff and the Commission say the order means, and their opinion is just better than mine-it is as good if not better than mine-if I answer, then, I say, "No; it doesn't require exclusive f. o. b. mill selling."

But reading it strictly it goes very far toward requiring f. o. b. mill selling. 

Mr. PHELPS. My answer to that is "No." Of course, you can conceive of cases where the absorption of freight costs perhaps would not affect competition, or where there is no competition, but when you have to consider the effect upon your own competitors, and also upon your customers, who are engaged in the resale of the commodity, I should say it is rather hazardous to adopt any system except f. o. b. mill price.

Mr. WOODEN. We have had that question again and again, and I would answer it now by saying that only a price that is nondiscriminatory can be used with complete assurance of its legality. A uniform f. o. b. mill price would be nondiscriminatory and therefore safe. But delivered prices which make due allowance for differences in cost of delivery are also nondiscriminatory and therefore equally safe.

The necessary effect of making due allowances for differences of costs of delivery is to create a uniform mill net. * * * Mr. BERGSON. This question presents another aspect of the problem discussed in my answer to question 3: namely, whether the statute does or should provide a formula or yardstick which will, as to any particular state of facts, delineate what is and what is not forbidden.

It is unnecessary to repeat the reasons for couching the statutory prohibitions in general terms. But with specific reference to this question, it is my view that, while selling at a uniform f. o. b. mill price provides assurance of legality under section 2, there are circumstances and conditions under which other methods of pricing may be employed with substantial assurance of legality,

Question No. 8: Has the statutory right to meet competition been construed by the courts as including only the right to do so in individual cases, and as not including the right to regularly and continuously meet competitiors' lower prices? 

Mr. PAULSON. No, to that question, Senator. The court has not said, as I read the decision, that you can only meet a competitor's price in individual cases. 

Mr. PHELPS. I would say they have held that it does not include the right to do it systematically.

Mr. WOODEN. My answer is "yes," when applied to the circumstances of the 

Staley case and the Cement case, The reciprocal and consistent meeting or matching of each other's prices, whether low or high, may represent a common and calculated consent to a perpetual stalemate on the chessboard of price competition. 

Mr. BERGSON. In the Cement case (p. 725) the Court pointed out that all the producers were using a discriminatory system of pricing. It held that in these circumstances the producers could not show that their lower prices were made in "good faith" to meet an equally low price of a competitor, since the emphasis of the statutory provision is on "individual competitive situations" and it is, therefore, not applicable where a seller determines his prices by means of a "general system" of pricing.

Under this decision, it would seem that a seller who has not adopted a "discriminatory pricing system condemned by section 2" is free to bring himself within the statutory provision permitting a good-faith meeting of a competitor's equally low price. I recognize that there is language in the opinion in the Cement case which might be taken as meaning that it is the Court's view that a seller is using a discriminatory pricing system whenever his method of pricing "constantly results in his getting more money for like goods from some customers than he does from others." The Court's decision might therefore be interpreted as meaning that the defense of meeting a competitive price in good faith is not available to a seller who absorbs freight on his sales in a place in which the current price of others is less than his own f. o. b. factory price plus freight from factory to destination, if the absorption is a matter of practice and policy, rather than irregular and sporadic. I agree that under this view of the facts and the law, the defense being considered would have a very narrow scope.

Wherever you find varying mill nets, you find applicability for the Conduit order.

Mr. PHELPS. Under the doctrine of the Cement case, I think that it is very dangerous for a man to follow his competitors' practices with knowledge of them even though he does not conspire with them. 

Mr. WOODEN. My answer is that mere knowledge that a competitor employs a similar system is not enough, but it is possible that the employment of the system itself is such that it injures competition within the principle of the Beechnut decision, decided by the Supreme Court in 1922, and apparently reiterated by the 

Supreme Court in the Cement case.

Mr. BERGSON. This question seems to adumbrate the issue which has frequently arisen in Sherman Act cases: namely, whether adoption of a given course of conduct, with knowledge that others have adopted the same course, constitutes conspiracy to take uniform action notwithstanding absence of express agreement to do so. Whether it does or does not depends on all the relevant facts of the particular case.

V. INDIVIDUAL DELIVERED PRICING PRACTICES, CONSPIRACIES TO FIX PRICES, AND THE PRESENT APPARENT POSITION OF FEDERAL TRADE COMMISSION WITH RESPECT THERETO 

The subcommittee heard 109 witnesses during the November- 

December hearings on Senate Resolution 241. Twenty-one witnesses were heard during the January-February hearings on S. 236 (two of which witnesses had testified at the prior hearings). Of the 128 witnesses who testified before the subcommittee, only 2 favored required uniform f. o. b. mill pricing, Commission Attorney Wooden and Professor Mund, both recommended to the subcommittee by the Commission. Most witnesses were vehement in their opposition to required f. o. b. mill pricing. The subcommittee frequently urged advocates of that pricing policy to appear and testify at the hearings. No person who asked to appear at the hearings was denied the right to do so.

The Federal Trade Commission has apparently not been oblivious to this wave of opposition to required f. o. b. mill pricing. It appears to be conscious of this public opinion and since the organization of this subcommittee has endeavored to conform its policy to this public demand.

While the witnesses appearing before the subcommittee and in fact, the American public are opposed to required f. o. b. mill pricing, they are even more opposed to any conspiracies to fix prices, regardless of the form the price-fixing arrangements might take or the means used to execute them.

The Supreme Court has found that the cement industry used the multiple-basing-point system to effectuate a conspiracy to fix prices. 

There is virtual unanimity of opinion that a conspiracy to fix prices through the use of any practice or system, is illegal and must be prohibited. The nub of the problem as to freight absorption practices and delivered pricing is essentially the establishment and promulgation of rules that will effectively eliminate price-fixing conspiracies and at the same time prevent sellers from being required to sell at f. o. b. mill prices, when the buyer and seller do not desire to do business on those terms. The consensus is that competitive freight absorption and delivered pricing practices are clearly in the best interests of our economy, when practiced in the absence of conspiracy. 

The real problem is to determine the circumstances under which such practices, when employed throughout an industry, may be found to be conspiratorial, in the absence of direct evidence to that effect. 

Responsive to the testimony before this subcommittee and public. opinion in general, the Federal Trade Commission has somewhat modified its position as to required f. o. b. mill selling. The Commission does not admit that it had proceeded in that direction nor does it concede a reversal of its position. The form of the Commission's current disclaimers is unimportant. It is pertinent only that it now disowns any intention to require f. o. b. mill pricing.

The Commission's first disclaimer occurred on September 18, 1948, when its then-Chairman, in a speech officially released by the Commission, said: * * * basing point and delivered price systems, as such, are under no special attack. In proceeding against collusive price fixing, and injurious price discrimination, the Commission has attacked those delivered price systems where they appeared to be the core of price-fixing conspiracies or destructive of competition. * * * it has been too broadly asserted that all delivered prices are unlawful. No Commission order has yet required f. o. b. mill pricing, nor forbidden freight absorption, except in a context of price fixing or destruction of competition * * *39 

That disclaimer of required f. o. b. mill pricing and freight absorption was limited by excluding cases involving "a context of price fixing or destruction of competition." This represented but a minor deviation from the prior position. A proper inference to be drawn from that statement is that the Commission has required f. o. b. mill pricing where there was "a context of price fixing." This position, however, was later abandoned.

There are now pending both before the Commission and in the courts cases involving freight absorption and delivered prices. It is to be hoped that the views currently publicly announced by the Commission will be applied to those cases. This will give assurance to business that they have actually been placed in effect. 

The Commission has, since the inception of this subcommittee's activity, written off or otherwise disclaimed some of the theories (referred to in secs. The Commission has used guarded language which, while indicating an intention to permit competitive freight absorption, may be susceptible to a different construction at a later date. 

Business can be assured of its right to engage in good-faith competitive freight absorption only by unequivocal assurance of its legality. It is assumed, of course, that the presence of a conspiracy to fix prices negatives the presence of both good faith and competition. 

To assure businessmen of the right to engage in good-faith competitive freight absorption, pending a more thorough study of the problem by the Congress, the enactment of S. 1008 is necessary. 

The duration and extent of the Commission's adherence to the views most recently expressed by Commissioner Davis is, of course, not known at this time. On this result alone, however, the subcommittee feels its work has been worth while.

VI. REPORT ON PENDING LEGISLATION 

The Federal Trade Commission's pricing policies have created a serious question for the Congress to decide. It must find legislative means by which to make certain that all conspiracies to fix prices are effectively outlawed and at the same time safeguard to buyers and sellers the right to use freight absorption and delivered pricing practices when they choose to do business on those terms. As the Commission itself said in 1936, "It is a matter of policy for Congress to determine." 

The Commission appears to take the position in litigation before the courts that it is an arm of the Congress and looks to the Congress for direction and guidance. The Congress cannot avoid this responsibility. 

In 1924 the Commission entered an order on a complaint it had filed in 1921 against the United States Steel Corp. group. Although the corporation did not comply with the order the Commission took no action for enforcement for 20 years. Perhaps feeling a lack of statutory authority, the Commission turned to Congress for new legislation.

On three occasions it asked the Congress to pass laws which would have made basing-point systems and freight absorption practices illegal per se. On each occasion Congress refused to do so. The 

Commission then proceeded with complaints under which it ultimately asked the courts to hold, notwithstanding the refusal of Congress to so legislate, that basing-point systems were illegal per se. It also argued that freight absorption and phantom freight were each equally improper. The Commission cannot avoid responsibility for the briefs filed, and the arguments made, in the courts by its spokesmen appearing on its behalf. The judicial and quasi-judicial proceedings, which have been discussed in this report, have created a situation which requires the attention of the Congress.

Federal Trade Commission Act Amendments 

Section 2 (a) of the bill adds an additional provision to section 5 (a) of the Federal Trade Commission Act. No present provision of that act is modified or deleted.

The bill provides: 

Any illegal agreement, combination, or conspiracy between two or more sellers to employ any pricing practices shall be an unfair method of competition * * *

This clearly reaffirms the purpose that all such practices are to remain illegal. The word "illegal" was added before the word "agreement" to cover fair trade agreements under State laws and which are protected by the Miller-Tydings Act. 

The bill then provides: but no pricing practice employed by any seller shall be deemed an unfair method of competition or an unfair act or practice in commerce within the meaning of this section solely because it involves (1) the charging of delivered prices for goods of like grade and quality uniform to all purchasers of the same class without regard to the place of delivery, or for delivery within any geographic area or zone, or (2) the absorption (in whole or in part), allowance (in whole or in part), or averaging by such seller of transportation charges for the delivery of any goods, or solely because any such practice results in delivered prices similar or identical to those charged by any other seller for comparable goods * * That provision provides only that a pricing practice shall not be illegal solely because it involves delivered prices or freight absorption, allowance, or averaging. The words "to all purchases of the same class" is intended to avoid eliminating differences representing functional discounts.

Witnesses from the Commission testifying on the original bill expressed the fear that it might prevent them from using the identity of prices as evidence by which to prove a conspiracy. Industry witnesses, on the other hand, testified that the provisions of the bill could be nullified through the Commission finding that identical prices were alone proof of conspiracy.

To obviate both fears, the amended bill provides: 

That the use of any such pricing practices in the manner above described shall not alone be sufficient to prove the existence of any alleged combination, conspiracy, or agreement, but may be received as evidence and may be considered, together with other credible evidence, in determining the existence of any such alleged agreement, combination, or conspiracy. 

A vital provision of the bill is the concluding phrase of the sentence: 

Provided further, That nothing contained in this sentence shall be construed as making lawful any agreement, combination, or conspiracy, or any fraudulent, deceptive, or coercive act or practice. 

2 The opening phrase of the proposed amendment is that conspired pricing practices are illegal and the concluding phrase is that nothing therein shall be construed as making lawful any conspiracy. The illegality of conspired prices, regardless of how achieved, is thus made

certain. The proposed amendment makes no change in the law as to what constitutes a conspiracy. It does not affect the recent decisions on the evidence required to prove a charge of conspiracy in American Tobacco Company v. United States (328 U. S. 781 (1946)); and 

Interstate Circuit v. United States (306 U. S. 208 (1939)). The pro posed amendment does make clear, however, that delivered prices and freight-absorption practices which are not the result of a conspiracy, are not unfair methods of competition under the Federal Trade Commission Act. It also preserves the illegality of practices characterized as "fraudulent, deceptive, or coercive." 

Section 2 (b) of the bill adds an additional provision to section 5 (b) of the Federal Trade Commission Act. It has been contended by the 

Commission that it is not effective to merely order conspirators to "cease and desist" from carrying on the conspiracy. The present act provides only for an order to "cease and desist." Conspirators thus sometimes continue their former practices, saying they are no longer doing so by conspiracy. The proposed amendment provides that to dissipate the effects of a conspiracy the Commission may require a seller to establish an f. o. b. factory, mill, or shipping point price and to offer to each buyer the option of purchasing at such f. o. b. prices or at delivered prices which may be less but cannot exceed the f. o. b. price plus the actual cost of transportation. Exclusive f. o. b. mill selling is not required, the seller must merely give the buyer the option of purchasing on that basis. A seller subject to those provisions is barred from charging phantom freight, but is not denied the right to absorb freight.

This requirement may be modified or suspended when either the Commission or the court finds it is no longer necessary to dissipate the effects of the conspiracy.

This provision gives the Commission an affirmative power with which to dissipate the effects of a conspiracy and thus strengthens the antitrust laws. It also assures to buyers and sellers that they will not be forced to transact business at exclusive f. o. b. mill prices when they do not choose to do so. 

As to this provision, Assistant Attorney General Bergson testified:

I am in accord with this provision of the bill. As a matter of fact, in a consent judgment entered last October in a Sherman Act case involving the stainless steel industry the Court at our request included a provision which is identical in effect to that which the Commission would be authorized to impose by this section of the bill. The provision was included in the decree because it was believed that only in this way could the effects of the conspiracy be eliminated. 

Mr. Bergson suggested changes in phraseology which was largely adopted in the amended bill. The Commission objected to a time limitation provision in the original bill which was deleted in the amendment. 

Clayton Act amendments

Section 3 (a) of the bill amends that portion of section 2 (a) of the Clayton Act preceding the second proviso. The proposed amendment is a verbatim repetition of section 2 (a) of the Clayton Act, except for three changes. The three changes are: (1) The present act refers to discriminations, "where the effect of such discrimination may be substantially to lessen competition." 

In Federal Trade Commission v. Morton Salt Company (324 U. S. 37 

(1942)) the Court construed that phrase in the statute as permitting

n order by the Commission when it found a "reasonable possibility" of the required effect.

The Commission says it will continue to prove a "reasonable probability" of the required effect. This position is adopted in the proposed amendment by substituting for the quoted phrase the words 'where there is a reasonable probability that the effect of such discrimination will substantially lessen competition." 

Assistant Attorney General Bergson testified that he saw no harm in the objective sought to be accomplished. His suggestion for accomplishing the change was adopted in the amended bill. Witnesses from the Commission testified this provision did not change the aw as applied by the Commission. (2) It is proposed to further amend section 2 (b) of the Clayton Act by inserting the word "materially" before the word "injure" in the phrase reading "or to destroy, prevent, or injure competition." 

This provision is in line with the Commission's October 12, 1948, statement of policy that it does not consider "any" injury to competition as within the prohibitions of the act, but requires a showing of material injury. (3) The first proviso in the present act exempts "differentials which make only due allowance for differences in the cost of manufacture, sale or delivery." The proposed amendment substitutes for the word "only" the words "not more than." This permits differentials representing not more than differences in the cost of delivery to permit the absorption of transportation costs and at the same time not include charging phantom freight.

Section 3 (b) of the proposed amendment rewrites section 3 (b) of the Clayton Act. Several changes in language are made which may or may not be changes in the present law. According to the views of some Federal Trade Commission authorities, these changes do not alter the present law. A contrary view is expressed by others. The changes in language include the following: 

(1) Section 4 of the Clayton Act authorizes actions for triple damages by persons injured by a violation of section 2. Section 2 

(a) makes certain discriminations in price illegal, while section 2 (b) permits affirmative defenses to such charges. The present act provides in section 2 (b) that such affirmative defenses may be shown "at any hearing on a complaint under this section" the proposed amendment adds "or in any judicial proceedings brought pursuant to section 4 of this act." No witness has expressed opposition to this change and it is not a change in the act as it has been interpreted. 

(2) Section 2 (a) of the Clayton Act makes unlawful discriminations in price "where the effect of such discrimination may be substantially to lessen competition." Section 2 (b) provides for affirmative defenses to a charge of "discrimination in price." One circuit court has said that a reading of the two sections requires a respondent to assume the burden of proving an affirmative defense after the mere showing of a discrimination-or difference-in price, without a showing of the required effect upon competition. 

The Commission disclaims any intention of making out a case by showing a mere difference in price. It concedes that it must also show the required effect upon competition. This view is incorporated in the proposed amendment by providing that the affirmative defenses

of section 2 (b) come into play after a discrimination in violation of section 2 (a) has been shown. (3) The most substantial change in the proposed Clayton Act amendment is the provision that a seller may establish that his prices or practices are lawful by showing the good faith meeting of competition. Obviously, a seller conspiring to fix prices is not acting in good faith. In one case the Commission took the position that good faith meeting of competition was not a defense to a charge of discrimination when it found injury to competition. Although this case is now pending in the circuit court of appeals, the Commission disclaims the rule advocated in that case. 

Commissioner Davis gave the following answers to the following questions: 

Q. Do you believe that meeting competition in good faith should be a defense to a charge of price discrimination under the Clayton Act? 

A. Yes.

Q. Where a seller in good faith reduces his price to meet the lower price of a competitor, do you believe he should be permitted to meet that lower price, when he does so in good faith, even is his doing so may cause an injury to a competitor, or to a competitor of his customer?

A. Yes. If good fatih is present, injury to competition will be rare; and in general the policy of the law should not discourage active competition by preventing the meeting of competition in good faith.

Commission Legal Adviser Dawkins testified that the bill makesthe defense of meeting the prices of a competitor in good faith an absolute defense to a charge of price discrimination. This may or may not be a change in existing law. Personally, I am inclined to believe that it is not a change, but this view is accompanied by a belief that a flexible and vigorous interpretation can and should be applied to the words "in good faith." (4) The bill further provides that good faith meeting of competition shall include doing so regularly and customarily, with or without prior announcement. Dicta of the Supreme Court in the Cement 

Institute case indicates the right to meet competition is limited to doing so "sporadically." Limiting the right to meet competiton to 'sporadic" instances is unrealistic and has the effect of denying all right to meet competition. The evidence is clear that active competition requires that businessmen be permitted to do so regularly and customarily, and that they be permitted to announce their intention to do so if they so desire, assuming, of course, the existence of competition and not conspiracy. (5) The final provision in the proposed section is that meeting & competitor's price shall include not only a like price, but a higher price, and when not unduly oppressive, a lower price. The present statute appears to permit only the precise meeting of a competitor's price. One of the most frequent criticisms of the present Clayton 

Act is its tendency toward price rigidity. Sellers who are found having the same price frequently excuse their price identity by the requirements of this act. Permitting a competing seller to sell at a lower price than his competitor (when not unduly oppressive) not only fosters more active competition but permits a more vigorous enforcement of the antitrust laws by eliminating an excuse for identity of prices.

Assistant Attorney General Bergson testified, as to this section in the original bill: to

If my interpretation of the section is correct, I favor it. It seems to be in harmony with the competitive tradition that new competitors may quote prices to get a foothold in a particular market and introduce their product without running the risk of violating the law by quoting something less than identical prices. I would not like, however, to be considered as agreeing to the express language of the amendment. The present language in the proposed amendment is not too clear and I am not certain of all of the particular situations which it may encompass. Remembering the interpretative difficulties which the Robinson- 

Patman Act has presented, I believe that considerable study should be given to this particular section. In this connection, I would be glad to cooperate with the committee in any way it may desire. 

Section 4 of the proposed bill amends the Clayton Act (as amended by the Robinson-Patman Act) by defining"price." The act is primarily a price discrimination statute, although the act does not define the meaning of "price." The proposed amendment defines "price" as meaning- The delivered cost paid by the buyer for the commodity or product at the time and place such commodity or product is accepted by him, without deduction on account of any transportation or delivery charge incidental thereto. 

The delivered cost to the buyer seems to be the only basis for determining if one buyer is discriminated against in favor of another I buyer. When two buyers each pay the same number of dollars there cannot be a discrimination in favor of one, even though a greater freight cost is involved in the sale to that buyer. The purchase price for goods has long had a well-known meaning. Whenever a buyer fails to pay for goods purchased, the seller has traditionally had the right to sue for the purchase price. The seller then recovers what the court finds is the price. Price is not the seller's mill net return except where the contract between the parties provided that it shall be the price.

The Commission has said that the "true price" or the "real price" is the seller's mill net return. This argument was successfully advanced before the Supreme Court in the Cement Institute case. It appears to be the basis of the Commission's complaint under count II of the National Lead and Chain Institute cases. Under the mill net theory, whenever a commodity is sold to different purchasers at the same delivered price, with different transportation costs involved, there is automatically a discrimination in price. 

The proposed bill does not weaken the antitrust laws. It contains nothing that will permit any combination or conspiracy to fix prices. 

It strengthens the Federal Trade Commission Act by giving the Commission affirmative powers to make the enforcement of its orders more effective. It further adds clarification which inherently strengthens any law.

The proposed bill will not in any manner change the decisions (not including the dicta) in the Corn Products case, the Staley case, the Cement Institute case, or any other Supreme Court decision on this subject. This is the testimony of Assistant Attorney General Bergson as to the original bill. The changes in the amended bill do not alter this conclusion.

The Legislative Reorganization Act of 1946 provides that the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce shall have jurisdiction over interstate and foreign commerce generally and the regulation of interstate and foreign transportation. 

That act provides that the Committee on the Judiciary shall have jurisdiction over judicial proceedings and the protection of trade and commerce against unlawful restraints of trade. The provisions of that act are such as to raise a question as to jurisdiction of S. 236 

by this committee. Amendments to the Federal Trade Commission 

Act are under the jurisdiction of the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce, but amendments to the Clayton Act are under the jurisdiction of the Committee on the Judiciary. B. REPORT ON S. 1008 

S. 1008 is temporary legislation designed primarily to extend a moratorium against prosecution of either complaints by the Federal Trade Commission or damage suits in the district courts, for good faith freight absorption or the use of delivered prices in the absence of combination or conspiracy.

The bill states that it has never been the intent of Congress to deprive individual companies of the right to use delivered price systems or to absorb freight to meet competition in any or all markets, provided such activities are carried on independently, in good faith, and not through any combination or conspiracy in violation of the Sherman Act, as amended. The Federal Trade Commission has only that jurisdiction granted to it by the Congress and damage suits in the district courts for violation of the Clayton Act must, of course, be predicated upon the statutory jurisdiction conferred by Congress. That Congress did not intend to permit such proceedings should preclude the successful prosecution of any such proceedings. Nevertheless, proceedings by the Federal Trade Com-

7 mission, heretofore referred to in this report, have cast doubt upon the legality of such use of freight absorption and delivered prices. 

This bill further provides for what amounts to a 2-year moratorium against the prosecution of those cases. This is for the purpose of giving the Congress additional time to study the need for corrective legislation, and giving the courts an adequate opportunity for judicial review of the arguments, theories, and orders of the Federal Trade Commission. The legislative granting of such a moratorium is clearly and unequivocally not intended as any indication that such pricing practices are now, or hereafter are to be, illegal. 

The subcommittee has, in the prior section of this document, reported on S. 236, which is permanent legislation to dispose of this problem. As more fully appears from the preceding section, the subcommittee finds that jurisdiction of the subject matter of S. 236 is concurrently held by the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce and the Committee on the Judiciary, and has recommended that it be transmitted to the Committee on the Judiciary for further consideration. A considerable period of time may elapse before the 

Congress can give sufficient consideration to S. 236 to enable that bill to be disposed of. This subcommittee is cognizant of the vital and important need for immediate legislation even though of only a temporary nature to clarify the right to individually use freight absorption and delivered pricing practices, when not employed through combination or conspiracy.

The subcommittee believes that S. 1008 effectively grants this necessary temporary relief. A copy of the bill is contained in the appendix.

The Department of Justice appears to favor such moratorium legislation. In a public statement, the Assistant Attorney General in charge of the Antitrust Division said that he opposed S. 236 primarily because he believed it undesirable for Congress to legislate on a question currently before the courts. But he continued:

There has been some mention of the possibility of procuring status quo legislation until the Supreme Court has passed upon the Rigid Steel Conduit case. I would be inconsistent if I were to oppose such legislation, and at the same time urge that the Congress await action by the Supreme Court. However, such status quo legislation should be carefully drawn so that those pricing systems which are clearly unlawful are not temporarily legalized.¹¹

The principal argument in favor of temporary moratorium legislation (as distinguished from permanent legislation) is that the Rigid Steel Conduit case, in which the Commission proceeded the furthest in the direction of f. o. b. mill pricing, is now before the United States 

Supreme Court. There is confidence in many quarters that the Supreme Court will utilize this opportunity of eliminating the troublesome confusion caused by the dicta in the Cement Institute case and clarify the law as to the right of individual freight absorption. 

It should be pointed out that this situation applies only to uncertainty now prevailing under the Federal Trade Commission Act. The 

Rigid Steel Conduit case does not involve issues under the Clayton Act. Both statutes were, however, involved in the Cement Institute 

Commission theories previously advanced, such as that the seller's mill net return is the true and real price, that the right to 

41 Address of Herbert A. Bergson, Assistant Attorney General, before the Association of the Bar of the 

City of New York, February 17, 1949. case.

meet competition is limited to doing so sporadically and other similar contentions, will not be directly before the court in the Rigid Steel Conduit case. Legislation by Congress thus appears necessary regardless of the result in the Conduit case. 

If there is to be delay in passing permanent legislation, regardless of the reason, the temporary moratorium legislation should apply to both the Federal Trade Commission Act, and the Clayton Act. 

S. 1008 does apply to both statutes.

It is the hope of the subcommittee that S. 1008 can be immediately passed by the Congress, while further consideration is given to S. 236. 

The comments made with respect to the jurisdiction of the Committee on the Judiciary as to the subject matter of S. 236 are applicable to 

S. 1008. This bill relates to the "protection of trade and commerce against unlawful restraints and monopolies." That subject is under the jurisdiction of the Committee on the Judiciary. This subcommittee accordingly recommends that the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce transmit S. 1008 to the Committee on the

C. 13), is hereby amended by adding immediately after subsection (f) thereof the following new subsection: "(g) For the purpose of determining the existence of discrimination, the word 'price', as used in this section, shall mean the delivered cost paid by the buyer for the commodity or product at the time and place such commodity or product is accepted by him, without deduction on account of any transportation or delivery charge incidental thereto."

EFFECTIVE DATE 

SEC. 5. This Act shall become effective thirty days after its enactment.

SEPARABILITY OF PROVISIONS 

SEC. 6. If any provision of this Act, or the application of such provision to any person or circumstance, is held invalid, the remainder of this Act or the application of such provision to persons or circumstances other than those as to which it is held invalid, shall not be affected thereby. [S. 1008, 81st Cong., 1st sess.]

A BILL To provide a two-year moratorium with respect to the application of certain antitrust laws to individual, good-faith delivered price systems and freight-absorption practices Be it enacted by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That because of widespread uncertainty resulting from recent administrative and judicial constructions of the Federal Trade Commission Act, as amended, and the Clayton Act, as amended, the Congress hereby declares that it is the sound and traditional policy of the United States that contracts, combinations, conspiracies, or monopolistic practices in restraint of trade are inimical to the public interest in maintaining a free, private, competitive enterprise system; but that it has not been the intent of the Congress to deprive individual companies of the right to use delivered price systems or to absorb freight to meet competition in any or all markets, provided such activities are carried on independently and in good faith, and not through any combination or conspiracy in violation of the Sherman Act, as amended. 

SEC. 2. Until the expiration of two years after the enactment of this Act, the 

Federal Trade Commission Act, as amended, and the Clayton Act, as amended, shall not be construed as depriving individual companies, in the absence of conspiracy or combination or other agreement in restraint of trade, of the right to independently use delivered price systems or to absorb freight to meet competition in any and all markets.

SEC. 3. Nothing herein contained shall affect any proceeding pending in any 

Federal court of the United States on February 1, 1949. [S. Res. 76, 81st Cong., 1st sess.]

RESOLUTION 

Resolved, That the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce be discharged from further consideration of the bill (S. 236) to clarify and formulate a consistent and coordinated national policy with respect to transportation costs in interstate commerce; to strengthen the antitrust laws of the United States and to provide for their more effective enforcement; and to promote competition by permitting sellers to have access to distant markets, and the bill, S. 1008, to provide a two-year moratorium with respect to the application of certain antitrust laws to individual, good-faith delivered-priced systems and freight-absorption practices, and that said bills be referred to the Committee on the Judiciary. 

SEC. 2. That there shall be transmitted to the Committee on the Judiciary an interim report of the Committee on Interstate and Foreign Commerce on said bills and the same shall be printed as a Senate Document. 

SEC. 3. The Committee on the Judiciary will give such prompt attention to the bills as will permit early consideration of the subject matter of them by the Senate.
