BOOK TWO CHAPTER I 

Hello to Hawaii

Two WEEKS after my graduation from the advanced Army Air 

School at Kelly Field, I was in San Francisco going down to the steamship docks with Rex Wilkins. Rex had re-enlisted in the 

Army Air Corps and was stationed at Crissey Field, and during the time I waited for the hour of sailing I had a grand visit with 

Rex and Jerry Blake, who was flying for an air survey company.

Jerry failed to accompany me to the docks under the plea of some flying he had to do, and even at the time I was suspicious of this excuse. Jerry seldom let business interfere with pleasure. 

Rex and I forced our way through the crowd that always gathers when a great liner is to sail, and made our way to the gangplank where we stood around waiting for my new employer, Seymour Huntley, to put in his appearance. As Rex looked at the massive bulk of the liner and considered her destination in the lovely island he succumbed to jealousy. "Lucky stiff," he sighed. "Steel guitars at night and dancing on the beach." "Don't forget I'm going to work," I reminded him.

He gave me a look that said plainly that no sane person would consider flying a ship over the Hawaiian islands as work. Then he followed it up with advice that I should try not to go native and that I should remember Ruth Manning.

"Don't worry," I told him, "she's the kind you can't forget.”

Just then I saw Rex staring off into the crowd, and I heard him murmur: "Halt-one, two."

I tried to see what it was that had attracted his attention, but all I saw was a seething mass of humanity, crowding every inch of the dock. "What is it?" I asked. "Atten-shun! Speaking of girls-look what's walking up the dock! What a stride, what a face-and what a smile!" "What the heck," I said, to be contrary, but I certainly saw the girl Rex meant. She was walking through the crowd with an almost boyish free grace, turning her body to force her way through, but walking right along without the pushing and shoving that distinguished the efforts of lesser people. She was of more than medium height, trimly athletic, but appealingly feminine at the same time. She held her chin high, on her lips was a polite smile, and her dark eyes flashed with a quick, sure interest in everything about her. It was almost shocking to feel the strength of her personality in the competition of hundreds of others around her. Then, taking my eyes from her for a moment I saw that her companion was none other than my new boss, Seymour Huntley!

In a few moments we knew more about her. Huntley introduced her as Diana Dibbetts, daughter of his Majordomo or island foreman, Cary Dibbetts, and informed us that she had been to school in the States and was now on her way home. 

I heard Rex murmur faintly: "Aw, Jimmie, take me with you, take me with you," and I knew that his surrender was complete. Just then the blast of the steamship whistle startled us, the noise and confusion around us grew to a new pitch and I shook hands fervently with Rex. "Write me all about it, Jimmie," he said enviously and then sadly told Diana good-bye.

As we walked up the gang-plank, ship's officers greeted Huntley by name, even passengers called to him, which led me to observe to Diana that Huntley seemed to know everybody. 

Diana looked at me quizzically. "Yes," she answered, “even better than people know him." Her tone puzzled me, but another blast of the steamship's whistle and the resulting shouts of voyagers and those they were leaving behind, made it impossible to talk right then.

During the days when I worked on various coast vessels I had come through the Golden Gate many times and yet the thrill of it never dulled. The great high cliffs on either side of the channel, the picture of San Francisco clinging to its uneven rocky perch, was always fascinating to me, and Diana said that it was to her, too.

She was an ideal companion, and by the time our ship rounded Diamond Head and steamed into Pearl Harbor, Honolulu, we were the best of friends. Yet at no time during the voyage could I learn anything about Huntley from her. When the great ship dropped anchor, Hawaiians appeared as if by magic, in boats, swimming in the water, crawling up the sides of the ship, and the gift of a quarter was sufficient to send them diving from the superstructure.

We left the ship with Huntley in the customs launch, and from there transferred to a skiff that took us to Huntley's flying boat, anchored in the harbor. I had some private doubts of my ability to handle this entirely different kind of ship, but I wasn't going to let Huntley know that. Within ten minutes we were taxiing down the harbor and I achieved a very good takeoff and headed south-east toward the island of Hawaii, where Huntley had his plantation. CHAPTER IX Movie Stunt Flying 

NEAR the town of Santa Ana what had been a peaceful field one morning was miraculously transformed within twenty-four hours into a war-time airport with hangars and pilots' quarters and other buildings that were weathered and storm beaten. 

Such was the magic of a movie company's power. When I reported to Ted Holden with my newly purchased Waco, the activity on the field was terrific. Ships were being camouflaged, a fate that befell my Waco at once; people streamed here and there, studio trucks unloaded cameras and lights and reflectors and scenery, and half of southern California seemed present either in the role of spectators or workers. 

Ted's woes were great in that many of the tried and true

picture pilots could not be located or could not arrive on such sudden notice. Sloan came storming around with a deaf ear for explanations; he wanted pilots and he wanted them at once. 

Where was Snodgrass? Ted explained that Snodgrass was at his place in the mountains near Arrowhead, and that unless he dropped his work and flew up there after him, there was scant chance that he would get the message that had been sent him and report. Snodgrass, I quickly learned, was one of a trio of flyers that Sloan needed desperately for a 5,000-foot dive through a V of bombers that was one of the first shots to be made. In the emergency, I volunteered the information that I could take Snodgrass' place, but Sloan blew up all over again. 

I could never fly for him! 

When he had gone, I offered to fly to the private landing field near the mountain home of the much-needed Snodgrass, and Ted eagerly accepted. My motive was to have the pleasure of flying my new ship, because I was as crazy about my Waco as a kid about a new toy and now that I was renting the ship I would have very little chance to fly it. Consequently, I was in the air without much delay, and by following Ted's minute directions, I found the mountain landing field. I had less luck as regards Snodgrass, however, because his housekeeper told me he had gone on a three-day camping trip on horseback. I 'left word for him to report for work as soon as he returned, and took off for Santa Ana again.

As I neared the field, I had an inspiration. Why not put on an air show for Mr. Sloan? He was hiring men who could stunt, and perhaps I could crack his dislike of me by showing him that 

I had really learned something at Kelly Field. And, in case it didn't work out that way, I would still have had a barrel of fun. The air was clear of ships so I climbed high above the field

and then let loose the whole repertoire. Snap rolls, power dives, wing-overs, loops, barrel rolls-everything I had ever done before and a few innovations. The way the ship handled, the way that powerful motor responded, egged me on to wilder and wilder acrobatics, and I ended the show with a power dive to the field, a quick zoom until the ship hung by its prop, and then a careful handling of it as it fell off, righted, and leveled

The result was a cinch; Sloan informed Holden peevishly that he should hire any pilots he wanted, and Ted triumphantly offered me a job. I took it just as triumphantly, even when I learned that my first assignment was with two veteran stunt pilots, making that 5,000-foot dive through the bomber formation. This was far from a safe and simple maneuver, as it was explained to me. There would be about fifty ships in the air at once, ships of all types. With the other two pilots, I was to be up above the clouds. When we were over a bomber formation, the lead pilot would signal with his wings and fall off to the left into a power dive. The other pursuit job would follow directly behind and I would bring up the rear. With our motors open, we were to dive through the bombers, as the crack pilots of Richtofen's circus had done during the war, since this was one of the most deadly ways of breaking up a "V" flight of bombers. The chances of crashing a bomber if the timing was wrong were very good, but all of us felt certain we could pull off the stunt safely.

We were right. Cameramen reported that the dive was a dream; Sloan was delighted and other pilots slapped us on the back as we stepped from our ships, but I want to confess that I felt like I'd been drawn through a knot-hole. The wild abandon of that dive; the necessity to fight the controls as I battled the wash of the other pursuit ships and the bombers themselves, and the almost sickening moment when we had pulled out of the dive with dramatic sharpness had aged me, and yet when it was all done I realized that I had experienced a wild joy as well. I knew that I was going to grow mighty fond of stunt flying.

The next few days we did routine work, with very little of a spectacular nature mixed in. There was one amusing incident,

however, that I want to relate. Sloan was seeking a pilot to land a ship at high speed and send it crashing between two trees. 

None of the fliers would have directly refused to do the job, but at the same time they were not eager for the assignment. It was my feeling that the shot could be made without involving as much danger as Sloan expected, and I gambled on my judgment to the extent of taking the job, for three hundred dollars. 

The shot was to be made the next day, so that night I enlisted the aid of the property department and we put very life-like wings of paper on an old fuselage. The next morning I got permission from Sloan to go about getting the shot my own way, so I took my ship up and made a landing for the camera directly in front of the "crash" trees.
