FOREWORD 

NE of the amazing facets of modern thought is the revival of interest in the 

American Indian. More and more, adults who a few years ago thought of Indian lore as "kid stuff" are succumbing to the fascination of studying, through books or personal observation, what remains of the

American aborigine. The general knowledge of the subject, however, is still very scanty. 

I once asked a friend of mine if he knew anything about Indians. He said "Yes, I know the difference between a cigar store Indian and a meat 

Indian. The cigar store Indians are cleaner."

This astute piece of anthropology may represent what most people know about Indians. 

We are

like Lorelei in Anita Loos' charming book. Lo relei, you remember, didn't know anything about music, but she knew what she liked, and most of us don't know anything about Indians, but we know what we don't like. 

Another friend of mine whom I once invited to go on an expedition into the Dakota 

Reservations said, "I don't see why you want to bother with the Indians when by going a little farther West you could see the Black

Hills and the Bad Lands and the beauties of na- ture. Such Indians as I have known are somewhat less than satisfying to the aesthetic sense. 

Frankly, they look like the devil, are lousy and a

prolific source of infection. As to conversational powers, they rate next to the Sphinx". In my feeble reply to this outburst, I tried to get my friend to imagine what a wildflower would say which had been picked from its native woodland, transplanted into the stuffy atmosphere of a house, neglected, allowed to wither and finally tossed into a garbage can. If its conversation were 

Sphinx-like, it would be an indication of almost superhuman patience. Any human being that has been treated as the Indian has been treated certainly should be excused for speaking rather volubly and with something less than enthusiastic appreciation. 

Another friend of mine with whom I was discussing the status of the Indian said, "You Indian lovers make me sick. War is tough and the nation that gets licked in a war has a tough time of it. There never was a defeated nation that got treated with the humanity and consideration

which our Government has shown towards the Indian." I mention this simply because it is a widely held opinion that the Indians represent a defeated nation. This is not a fact.

This is not a fact. Some few tribes were defeated in war, but in by far the majority of cases, the land was purchased from them and other land guaranteed to them in consideration of their quiet and peaceful behavior. We, the people of the United States, treated with the people of America as sovereign powers treat with one another. It was a good piece of economy to do so. 

In the movies you can stage a battle and it costs maybe $7.50 a day for a soldier and a horse. And you can shoot him as many times as you want to- -you may have to pay a little extra if he gets shot on his horse and has to fall off, still it's fairly cheap-in the movies. In real life it costs quite a lot to shoot a soldier. During the 

World War we put an insurance price of $10,000. on each enlisted man's head. It probably cost but ΙΟ

that much during the Indian Wars and when the 

Indians started shooting up our soldiers they didn't count the cost. Of course we could have beaten the Indians, we could have massacred them all and wiped them from the face of the earth had we been willing to pay the price, but we found it much cheaper when land was fairly plentiful, to "give" each tribe a few million acres of their own land on condition that they would stay on it and maintain peace and order.

There we have in principle the Reservation System. We made treaties and when it didn't interfere with something more important, we kept our side of the treaties. Then we tried to give the Indians the benefit of modern civilization. 

Everything from overalls to whiskey. We educated their children by teaching them European history and other things that didn't matter. We gave them just enough of the white man's culture to make them incapable of living in the only part 

II

of the world that we would allow them to live. 

What wonder that the noble red men gave way to a feeling of utter frustration. The uselessness of life must have pressed down upon him like a leaden blanket and if he could find escape through peyote, through whiskey or any other drug, who can blame him?

A few years ago I visited an old Sioux chief in his government-built house. It was a frame house with doors and windows, heated by a stove. The stove was evidently put in there to be used, so he was using it. He had a bright wood fire burning in the stove, although it was the middle of August. Every door and every window was tightly shut. There were millions of flies and Chief 

Laughing Bear (that was not his name) had a hawk's wing in his hand all the time that we sat and talked and the hawk's wing was continually in motion to fan away the flies that settled on his hands or face. The temperature must have been 12

120. It was stifling, but I did want to talk to him and I endured it.

Hanging on the wall was a picture of this same chief in the days of his youth. In the picture his hair was long and straight, braided down either side, ornamented with eagle feathers, his breast covered with the beatstplate of deer's shinbones, about his neck the necklace of bear claws. It was a gorgeous picture, but he was ashamed of it, or at least he pretended to be ashamed of it, because it represented him before his conversion to the white man's ways. Now his hair was short, his blue shirt and blue overalls gave the impression of a prisoner's uniform. His back was bowed. He was a picture of what the white man had done to the noble red man that once was.

The experience seemed to symbolize something It did not seem to symbolize the progress of civilized man from barbarism, but rather some horrible nightmare, too horrible to be true. 13

The pages of history alone can tell us about the red man that was and the pages of history are dreadful liars because history is everything that ever happened and the pages aren't big enough to hold everything and so the historians give us what they think we ought to have. Yet we can perhaps salvage enough to give us a picture which will leave us knowing a little more about the Indian than just the difference between a cigar store one and a meat one. 14

Culture Groups 

HERE are some things that everybody knows about Indians. Everybody knows that they painted their bodies, that they wore feathers in their hair, that they treated their women as slaves and beasts of burden, that they lived in wigwams or teepees and that they sailed in birchbark canoes.

But, of course, the trouble is that everybody knows so many things that aren't true. When 

Columbus landed on October 12, 1492 there were thousands, perhaps hundreds of thousands of Indians in the area that is now the United States, who had never seen a wigwam or a teepee, who 15

had never so much as heard of a birch-bark canoe and who, so far from treating their women as beasts of burden, were actually ruled and governed by female chiefs and who recognized a divorce system which was so completely in the hands of the women that any woman might divorce her husband if she so desired by the simple expedient of putting his extra moccasins on the door-step. 

When he came home and saw them there he knew he was finished.

All of this is just a way of saying that there were differences in the Indians of America. 

Learned people who call themselves ethnologists have for convenience divided the Indians which lived in what is now the United States into seven cultural groups. In addition, there were two others to be found in Alaska and Canada and, of course, others in Mexico and parts South. The seven groups have more or less arbitrary names and their distribution is shown on the accompanying map. 16

What do we mean by culture? Well, for the purpose of ethnological study, we don't mean knowing how to speak French, or even being familiar with the proper forks and knives and spoons to use at a formal banquet. In order to get at a difference, let us look at primitive man for a moment. Man has certain fundamental requirements for the preservation of life. He must eat, he must protect his body from cold and injury and he must have shelter from the elements. These are the three basic needs of man's existence; food, clothing and shelter. Then there are secondary requirements, the desire to travel from one part of the country to another, the desire to create articles of utility and beauty, and so on. Now the particular way that any group of men go about satisfying these needs determines the culture of that particular group. 

Naturally, the type of country in which they live has a great deal to do with it, but not everything. Suppose 18

we use this for a definition: Aculture is a manner of life conditioned by man's efforts to satisfy his primary and secondary wants. The Eastern Woodland Culture is represented by the Indians who greeted our Colonial forefathers when they landed at Plymouth 

Rock. They were partly horticulturists and partly hunters. They raised maize, or Indian corn, squash and pumpkins and one or two other vegetables and they caught fish and killed game with bow and arrow or club. 

Birch-bark played an important part in their life. They used it to make their wigwams or shelters which, by the way, were not always conical in shape, although that is the one most commonly seen in pictures. They did not 19

use the skin teepee or lodge, nor did they use the eagle feather war-bonnet, in spite of the temptation which artists cannot seem to resist of showing these features a thousand miles away from where they belonged. The wigwam of the Eastern 

Woodland Indians was frequently tunnel-shaped and sometimes even had the familiar gable roof covering a rectangular-shaped building of sapling frames covered either with bark or thatch work. 

Their cooking utensils were frequently made of birch-bark, the cooking being by the simple expedient of plunging red-hot stones into the water in the vessel. The birch-bark canoe, of course, is characteristic of this type of culture, although dugout canoes made of a single tree trunk were widely used on the Atlantic coast as well as on the Pacific and inland as far as the Great Lakes. 

West of the Great Lake area we encounter the

Prairie and Plains culture. Here very little horticulture was practiced except among some of the 20

Northern tribes. Rather the people were meat hunters and the buffalo was the heart and center of their culture. The buffalo provided food, clothing, lodging, for here we find the traditional skin teepee; the cone-shaped tent characteristic of the 

Plains aborigines. Many of the secondary needs of man were supplied by the buffalo as well. In fact, the old story about the farmer who used everything of the pig except the squeal is aptly illustrated by the Indians' use of the buffalo. 

String, thread, bolstering, rope, were made from the tendons or sinews. The rough part of the tongue was used for brushes for the hair. 

The skin was used not only for lodges and clothing, but for whips, 21

quivers, blankets, cradles, bed and even sleds for hauling things over the snow in winter. Glue was made from bits of the skin of the neck. 

Spoons, cups, dishes, powder horns were made from the buffalo's horns. The sacs of the buffalo's insides, gall bladder, trachea, etc., were used for bags. The bones were used as axes, hatchets, shovels and many other implements. Even the dung was used for fuel and when pounded fine and kept dry ¹"was used to keep small children warm, they being partially buried in the powdery material". Not only the Indians of the Plains, but all of the Indians, hunted and killed for use alone and never for sport. They never killed more than they were likely to need and they used everything possible.

So then, as the birch-bark was the keynote of the Eastern Woodland culture, so was the buffalo of the culture of the Plains.

We have all seen pictures of buffalo hunts, the hunters being on horse-back. Such an accomplished horseman did the Indian become that many people do not realize that there was no indigenous American horse. What the Indian has learned about horsemanship he has learned since the advent of the white man. However, we must remember that the Spanish explorers had pushed across Mexico and to the Pacific and well into the interior of the Southwest of what is now the 

United States nearly a hundred years before the Puritans came to 

New England. Many of the Spanish horses went wild and were caught and tamed by 

Indians. Indians bought others in ex- 23

change for gold or other commodities, and they soon learned how to train their horses to hunt buffalo. However, before the coming of the horse, the Plains Indians hunted the buffalo on foot. 

This apparently impossible feat was accomplished in two general ways. The one was for a few Indians armed with bows and arrows to cover themselves with skins and creep quietly among a grazing herd. The aim of the Indian with a bow and arrow at close range is deadly and it would be possible in this way for each hunter to shoot a considerable number of buffalo directly through the heart, killing them instantly before the herd would take alarm. The other method could only be used when the character of the country permitted. It involved building fences of brush or other material along the slope of a hill leading to a precipice. When a herd of buffalo came near enough to the slope they would be stampeded and forced over the precipice, only to be killed or so 24

grievously wounded that an arrow or bone knife could give the coup de grace. So, long before the horse arrived the Indian of the Plains had developed his culture around the buffalo. 

In the Southwest we find a type of culture far along the road to civilization. The Indians of the Southwest were living in houses of brick or stone grouped into villages or often into apartment houses several stories in height when they were first found by the Spanish explorers. These 

Indians were of a peaceful disposition and raised an abundance of corn, beans, pumpkins and squash, as well as making use of many vegetable foods which grew wild. They were also hunters; making frequent expeditions into the mountains for deer and antelope, even occasional expeditions to the Plains for buffalo. The buffalo was said never to cross the Pecos River, but the Indians of the Southwest crossed the Pecos and carried the buffalo home. For this purpose it was necessary 25

Impression of Taos Pueblo, N. Mex.

to cut up their quarry and dry the meat in the desert sun, after which it was carried home in rawhide bags and used as occasion would arise. These 

Indians are Pueblo Indians, from the Spanish word for "village", derived, of course, from the word for "people" (Latin-populus). They were of different languages and every village differed from its neighbor in customs and traditions. To this day, any one familiar with the pottery of the Indian villages in the Rio Grande valley can immediately identify the town or pueblo from whch a given piece of pottery comes. Basket weaving and the weaving of blankets and other cloths of cotton or wool were brought to a high state of perfection by these people of the Southwest. 

The fly in the ointment was the presence among them of some Plains Indians who had migrated in prehistoric times to the desert country and had retained their old roving ways and war-like habits.

The descendants of these people are the Navajo, 27

the Apache, the Commanche and the Ute. Like the Arab in the Orient, these roving tribes swooped down on the peaceful people of the towns and plundered their fields and their herds. 

Consequently, the pueblo towns were built for de- fense. Although the modern pueblo house has doors and windows in the ground floor, in olden times the only access was by a ladder to the roof of the first story and then through a hole in the 28

roof to the ground floor or through a door to the second story. By pulling the ladder up, every pueblo house was converted into a fort. Underground kivas were used for the storing of food and unless the attack was very sudden, it was frequently posible for the pueblo people to withstand any severe siege, although, of course, their standing crops were usually destroyed. But for this irritating feature of life, there can be no doubt that the pueblo tribes would have attained in pre-Spanish times a much higher degree of civilization than they actually did. 

They remain to this day a people largely unaffected by four centuries of European invasion. In all villages the native languages are still spoken. 

Baking is done in dome-shaped ovens and few concessions are made to the white man's way of life. 

During the last half-century the Navajo and Apache have been tamed and the Indian's worst enemy is now the well-intentioned white neighbor. 29

I have picked out these three cultures of the seven which exist in the United States because I happen to know them best. The culture of the 

Southeast was closer to that of the Eastern Woodland than any other, but it touched the Pueblo culture in the region of the lower Mississippi Valley. 

West of the Pueblo country was the California culture, known to American readers through 

Helen Hunt Jackson's novel "Ramona". North of this was the culture of the Pacific coast which was above all a wood-carving culture. The totem pole might be regarded as its symbol. They were hunters and fishermen and to some extent horticulturists. Their houses were made of wood and their implements exquisitely carved. Between these people and the plains was the Rocky Mountain or Plateau culture. To them the mountain sheep stood in the same relation as the buffalo to the Indians of the Plains.

How easy it would be to study these people if 30

the differences would only stay divided! But many influences made for a mixing of cultures. Chief among these were migration and wars; the migration usually being due to wars or famines or both. One of the widespread customs of Indian warfare involved the capture by the conquerors of the women and children of the conquered. Since the women were the creative workers, it is obvious that such captive women would bring into their newly adopted tribe many of the customs and ideas of their own people.

The Sioux or Dakota Indians during the decades that we hear most about them were more preeminently a Plains tribe and yet it is pretty certain that they were once a Woods tribe and may have lived as far East as the Gulf of St. Lawrence. They were driven West in prehistoric times and still further West in historic times by the Chippewas. 

It is evident, therefore, that their adoption of the Plains culture is a comparatively recent develop- 31

ment and that they had originally a Woods culture. These are only a few of the many illustrations that could be given of the passage of one culture into another or the adoption of some elements of one culture by tribes of another. 32

Language 

NDIAN lovers are frequently asked "What is the Indian word for 'so-and-so"".

I indicated above in speaking of the Pueblo that there are various Indian languages. It is as stupid to ask, "What is the Indian word for 'dog', or 'house'?" as it would be to ask, "What is the Asiatic word for 'dog' or 'house'?", or in fact to ask "What is the Eurasian?" for there were certainly as many different Indian languages in this country as there are today in Europe and Asia combined. 

In some parts of the country over a large area a single language with dialetic differences was 33

spoken. This was the case in the Northeastern part of our country where the Algonquin and Iroquois languages were used as far West as the headwaters of the Mississippi and as far South as Kentucky or perhaps into Georgia. Indians passing from one part of this vast expanse of land to another could understand each other probably as well as, for example, an Italian can understand a 

Spaniard, or a Portuguese can understand a

Frenchman-all four of these languages being developed from Latin. When, however, we get into the Middle West and the Far West, there are languages so completely variant that virtually no common roots can be detected. In the Valley of the Rio Grande two radically different languages are found, the Tanoan and the Keresan. Other

Pueblo people such as the Hopi and the Zuni each have their own language so that we find at least four radically different languages among the Pueblos of New Mexico and Arizona. In addition to 34

this, the Navajo and Apache languages which are similar, differ entirely from the language of the 

Pueblos. And when we go farther West, the

Pima and the Papago and the Havasupai and the Walpai all have their own languages. 

How then can they talk with each other? Nowadays, of course, the children go to school and learn English. In another generation English will be the universal language, but at the present time, most of them know at least the rudiments of the language of the peoples adjoining. In certain cases, however, this is impossible. For example, in the middle of the State of Wyoming is a reservation set aside for the Arapahoe and the Shoshone; two tribes whose languages are so different that they apparently cannot make the necessary noises with their mouths to utter each other's words. Here, as formerly in all parts of the West, the famous Indian sign language is still in use. A hundred years ago an Indian could travel all 36

through the West and make himself understood by members of any other tribe by means of a universally understood system of manual symbols.

The Indian sign language is a study in itself and one of deep interest to any student of philology. 

Its development is in keeping with the development of vocal languages. Some of the symbols are suggestive, others are arbitrary; so it is in spoken language. Words like "Bang!", "Flop!" "Splash!", immediately express their meaning.

Other words are arbitrary noises which must be associated in the mind with a given meaning and may convey entirely different meanings to people of different languages, just as the word "hell" means "light" to a German, but something quite different to us. The word "lego" means "I read" in Latin, but in Greek it means "I speak". This shows that such words are arbitrarily fixed and, therefore, must be learned; so it was with the sign language. It passed from one tribe to another 37

with surprisingly few variations. For example, when the Indian of the Plains wanted to express the idea of "striped," he would move the edge of the right hand across the left forearm, as if sawing the arm at various points between the elbow and the wrist. The Indian of the Southwest to express the word "striped" would hold the hand extended, palm out, with fingers spread and then lower the hand as if drawing a stripe with each finger on some imaginary object. Similarly, the symbol for "purchase" or "exchange" on the Plains was expressed by crossing the forefingers of the right and left hands in the form of an X, while in the Southwest they crossed the forearms. However, it is obvious that the underlying idea was adequately expressed and that was the function of the system. The sign language is dying out today and has become entirely extinct wherever one tribe lives in a large area by itself as among the Sioux in South Dakota, and of course, among the Indians 38

of Oklahoma who speak English and have adopted, for weal or woe, most of the white man's ways.

It persists however, in some of the smaller reservations where different tribes are gathered together and is still used in the Southwest when some of the Pueblo people converse with the Navajo and 

Apache, not knowing their language. 39

§ III Warfare 

MONG the things that everybody knows is the fact that the Indians were very war-like, savage and cruel, killing without scruple and taking pleasure in torturing their enemies. And as I said before, the sad thing about it is that people know so many things that are not true. It is a fact that in almost every case, if not in every case, the first appearance of the white man among the Indians was greeted with friendship, courtesy and hospitality on the part of the Indian. When however, it became apparent to the Indian that the white man's purpose was to take away the Indian's land he did fight. As who would not? It is 40

sometimes alleged that the Indian fought after he had sold his land. This may be or may not be capable of proof, but there is one consideration which is seldom, if ever, taken into account. That is that the Indian had no idea of personal ownership of real estate. Part of his religion was the recognition of the sun as his father and the earth as his mother. If you would sell your mother the 41

Indian would sell the earth. No doubt the treaties by which the land was purchased by the white man were explained in words of one syllable, but just try explaining in words of one syllable, in a language that you know very little about, some economic idea which is totally strange to your audience. No doubt the settlers in New England and elsewhere who acquired land from the Indians by purchase considered that they were actually buying the land, but I doubt very much if the Indian understood it that way. He may have understood that he was granting for a consideration the privilege of settlement, temporary or permanent, but that he was actually giving a title to the land of his forefathers was seldom if ever, I am sure, understood.

The Indian had been war-like and had fought before the white man came. His most deadly weapon was the bow and arrow and this was universal. Blow-guns were used in the South and spear-throwing levers may have persisted in use 42

in the Southwest up to Spanish times. The most gory battles with these primitive implements were far less destructive than any wars of the white man. The Indian never learned, until he had been trained in our own army, to fight in battlearray or even to deploy. His object was the same as that of any fighter, namely, to inflict as much damage upon the enemy as possible while protecting himself from injury as completely as possible. "Wanton atrocities" are told of in every war since the dawn of history. It was always the enemy that was guilty of wanton atrocity and the historian recorded of his own troops only deeds of valor, bravery and chivalry. This is a weakness which can be understood, but the sad part of it is that the Indian had no historians. An old Sioux chief said to me once, "Do you know the difference between a victory and a massacre?". I said, "No. 

What is the difference between a victory and a massacre?" He said, "When the white man won, 43

it was a great victory and when the red man won, it was a fearful massacre."

It probably must be admitted that the Indians murdered helpless women when attacking a village. That is not the standard of the white man's civilized warfare, although we have read of crucifying nuns in 20th Century Europe. One point must be born in mind, that the so-called civilized nations have pension systems for the widows of warriors, but the Indian woman who survived the massacre of her village could look forward to nothing but a life of slavery and drudgery among her enemies. In her despair she would undobtedly taunt and jeer at the victors to such an extent that they would be driven to violence.

The surprise attack is characteristic of Indian warfare. Admitting the lawfulness of warfare, nothing can be more sensible than the surprise attack. 44

As to the cruelty involved in torturing captives,

I doubt if there is any evidence for the torture of any captive who did not enjoy a reputation for bravery. It is hard for us to put ourselves into the place of barbarians, but if we try we may perhaps get some approximate conception of the importance of the virtue of bravery in the barbaric state. When women did all of the work around the house and in the fields and when men hunted and fought, bravery was the greatest of civic virtues. The highest honor that could be paid to a captive was to give him an opportunity to show during his last hours of life the extent to which he had cultivated this greatest of civic virtues. If some notable musician or artist were unfortunate enough to incur the death penalty we might, before putting him in the electric chair call upon him to play for us. It is not too fantastic an analogy, I think.

This puts an entirely different light upon it. 45

It is important for us to try to see things from the Indian's viewpoint, and the too-often ignored phase of this is that cruelty was not practiced for the delight of the tormentor, but as a type of honor paid to the tormented.

That the Indian was a formidable enemy is the universal testimony of soldiers who were pitted against him. We must remember that the Indian was fighting for his land, which is to say that he was fighting for his life. No wonder he fought desperately and fiercely! 

One of the ludicrous inconsistencies of our treatment of the Indians was the way we made use of them in the World War. After more than a generation of telling the Indian to be a good boy and not shoot people and behave himself peacefully, we put brand new muskets in his hands and took him thousands of miles from home to the trenches of France and told him to shoot Germans. 

Many white people were shocked and horrified 46

when the Indians proceeded to scalp the Germans whom they had shot, although no one up to date has shown any particular reason why they shouldn't. When they got back-if they got back-many of them danced the old scalp dance in commemoration of their achievements and victories and again I say, there is no reason why they shouldn't; the inhumanity of it was for us to take them and have them shoot people against whom they had no grudge whatever. 

In spite of what has been said about the ruthlessness of Indian warfare, they were not devoid of chivalrous ideals, and often fought in spectacularly desperate ways. One recognized convention of warfare on the plains was the thrusting of one's spear into the ground as a mark behind which one would not retreat. Catlin tells of a joust a l'outrance between a Mandan and a Cheyenne which might almost have come from the pen of 

Sir Thomas Mallory as an example of Medieval 47

Christian Chivalry. 

The Mandans had pursued a Cheyenne war party; when they found them they saw themselves to be outnumbered, and were inclined to turn back, in compliance with the general Indian rule of prudence. The Mandan 

Chief Mah-to-toh-pa, however, harangued his men to a high pitch of enthusiasm, rode in sight of the Cheyennes and struck his lance into the ground; then, circling around it, attached his scarlet sash to the lance as he rode by. The Cheyenne Chief accepted the challenge, and struck his own lance into the ground beside that of Mah-totoh-pa. The duel was on. At the first exchange of shots the Mandan's powder-horn was shattered; he held it up and threw it away. At once the 

Cheyenne threw away his own powder-horn, and they went at it again with bows and arrows. When the Mandan's horse fell with an arrow in his heart the Cheyenne chief also dismounted, and the duel continued on foot until the Cheyenne's quiver was 48

empty. By this time both had received many arrow wounds in arms and legs, but their shields protected their vital parts. The Mandan at once threw away his quiver and bow, and both reached for their knives; so the fight continued until the 

Mandan chief dispatched his adversary.

Allowing for the embellishment of details, the story is still important as showing the admiration in which they held the ideals of fair play and generosty to a brave enemy. 49

RELIGION AND SOCIAL CUSTOMS 

S IN the case of most primitive peoples, the religion of the American Indian in his primitive state was thoroughly well integrated with life in general. He did not, as we so often do, consider religion as something added to his ordinary existence. He did not even regard it as one department of life. He regarded it as life itself. He recognized that his very existence originated from God and was maintained by God. Religion then was a very real thing.

Among the different cultures and tribes, details of religion differed, but there were many elements to be found throughout. The veneration of the Sun as Father and the Earth as Mother was general. 50

Many tribes, especially the Shoshonean, had myths which told of their ancestors, the first landpeople, coming up from under the earth. Some of the ancestor gods remained under the earth. 

The Hopi snake-dance is only one of many ceremonies built upon this idea-for the priests in the ceremonies tell the snakes what they want the snakes to tell the under-world people (and usually they tell them they want rain-for in Hopi-land rain is always needed!) and at the conclusion of the ceremonies the snakes are released. They go back under the earth, and what is more reasonable than to suppose that they tell the ancestor, underworld people to send rain? Wise tourists take umbrellas with them when they go to witness the snake dance!

The sun-dance was practiced in many parts of the country, and is continued to this day among many tribes, but without the severe infliction of torture which formerly characterized it. It is still, 51

however, an endurance test. Various crises in life -especially puberty in both boys and girs—were marked by religious ceremonies, and the yearly round of corn and bean harvests and the tribal hunts, were always accompanied or preceded by dances and other rites.

Tobacco was used, not as with us but as a religi ous and social tie. Hospitality was cemented with the pipe; treaties were sealed with the pipe; personal prayer, meditation and retreats were invariably accompanied by smoking the pipe. Tobacco was grown wherever possible; often it was mixed with the inner bark of the willow or dogwood. 

Pipes were made of various materials, but one of the most widespread was a type of stone to be found in only one spot in this country. It is named catlinite, for George Catlin who is thought to be the first white man to visit the quarry; actually it is a reddish indurate clay, soft enough to be cut with a saw and shaped with a rasp and sand- 52

paper into any desired form. The quarry, which is located just outside the town aptly named Pipestone, in Minnesota, has been made into a permanent shrine, for it was holy ground to all the tribes which visited it; no blood might be shed in these sacred precincts, but all Indians, though their nations were at war with each other, smoked the pipe of peace together and took home with them what they needed of the medicine stone.

The sweat bath was another religious rite practiced by all tribes. It was something like a Turkish bath. A special lodge was built for the purpose near a stream or lake. The lodge frame was made of saplings bent over and joined at the top like a wickey-up, and this frame completely covered with bark or skins so that no light or air entered. In the midst of it a pit was dug a foot deep or so, in this were placed heated stones. votaries, stripped except for the breech-cloth, gathered inside, while the medicine-man sprinkled The 53

water and aromatic herbs on the hot stones until the lodge was completely filled with steam. Songs and prayers accompanied the rite, and a gourd or other vessel filled with water was passed around, each filling his mouth with the water and then, cupping his hands, drenching his hair and head. At the proper time all dashed out and plunged into a cold stream or lake. This ceremony was carried out not only at certain times of the year, but at various important crises in the life of an individual. Dr. Eastman, himself a Sioux Indian, goes so far as to liken the sweat-bath and the pipe of the Indian to Baptism and the Eucharist of the Christian Faith.

Very early the Indian began to adopt features of Christian lore and to incorporate them into his own scheme of things. This aptitude has made it very hard for scholars to get at the pure and original religious belief of the Indians. Many people have been surprised to find in Indian legends 54

stories like those of Adam and Eve, Noah, the

Tower of Babel and the like, and have even used the presence of such legends as evidence for the historical truth of the stories. But unfortunately it usually appears that the Indian heard the stories from early missionaries, and fitted them into his own folk-lore without scruple. An unwritten tradition can undergo great changes in a generation, and it must be remembered that Christian missionaries were at work among the Indians a hundred years before New England was founded.

For this same reason we cannot be sure that the Indian had a belief in one God, as Fennimore 

Cooper and Henry Longfellow would have us think. To be sure, some individuals and tribes may have gone a long way towards monotheism, but it is not at all established that Gitchee Manito is only another name for the Christian God.

In view of the attitude which some of the stricter Puritanical sects have maintained towards dancing, it may seem strange to treat of dancing under the head of religion, but in order to understand the dance of the Indian, it is necessary for us first of all to forget all about the dance as a purely social pastime, at least so far as the primitive dance is concerned. There are modern Indians who have taken up the Tango and the Foxtrot and even the Big Apple, which we took over from still darker-skinned people, but the character- 60

istic Indian dance was essentially a religious thing. 

Songs usually accompanied the dance and the drum always marked the rhythm.

There is one distinctive difference between the dance of the Southwest Indians and those of other parts of the country. The Southwest Indians dance in unison; every dancer's right foot strikes the ground at the same time. Among the other 

Indians there is no such regimentation and although they all follow the same rhythm, each individual's motions are otherwise independent of the others.

Everybody wants to know about the war dance. 

Seldom does a group of tourists get to witness an Indian ceremonial without someone putting in a request for a war dance. This, of course, reveals at once a failure to understand the purpose of the dance. The tourist thinks he is coming to a show and that the dances which are being performed have no real significance. I once witnessed a cere- 61

monial dance at the Sauk and Fox Reservation in

Iowa where a group of tourists requested that the Indians "put on" a war dance. The tourists were very influential people and the Indians didn't want to offend them. They explained, first of all, that the war dance is forbidden by the Federal Government, that the Indians are not allowed to put on the war dance. The tourists said, "That's all right, we will see that no trouble comes of it from Washington". The Indians had a little conference, but the decision was in the negative. The motive, alas, was an economic one. The chief explained as follows: "There are many tourists here who have come to see our dances, we expect that they will buy many of our trinkets. If we were to put on a war dance there wouldn't be a white man left within five miles inside of ten minutes!" the only chance we shall have of seeing another war dance is if the United States goes to war again and decides to make use of Indian soldiers, No, 62

The scalp dance is, of course, a sort of companion to the war dance. The war dance is a prayer to the war god to give strength for the battle and its effect is to work up a frenzy of blood-thirsty enthusiasm. The scalp dance is performed after the war is over and the trophies have been brought back.

The phenomenon known as The Ghost Dance was one of the most interesting religio-social movements among the Indians after the reservation system had been adopted. Much has been written about it-I had the great privilege of hearing the story from the lips of a veteran missionary in Wyoming, where the hysterical wave began. 

Rumors arose among Indians, who were unable to adjust themselves to the disappearance of the buffalo, to the effect that a Messiah had arisen who 63

was to resurrect the departed heroes, unite all tribes, wipe the white man from the entire continent and bring back vast herds of buffalo. A curious atmospheric condition, due to forest fires in the Yellowstone region, gave impetus to the movement, and dances were held in many places. So long as no overt act was perpetrated, the soldiers could do nothing; and one shot would undoubtedly have started a horrible massacre. The craze spread into the Dakotas, and matters looked pretty bad for the government agents and missionaries. who stood their ground. The break finally came, and the Army, recently equipped with the new rapid-fire artillery, quickly convinced the braves that their cause was vain. A few on the Pine 

Ridge Reservation still cherish the Indian Messiah dream, but they are singularly taciturn on the subject, and most observers credit them as being not much more dangerous than the Stuart sympathisers in England. 64

"Medicine" is an English word which has been adopted by all the tribes to signify something mysterious and supernatural; it may apply to anything from a bit of adhesive plaster or a cathartic pill to the Bible or even God Himself. Both pagan and 

Christian Indians retain to this day a more or less firm belief in the efficacy of Indian medicine. I mentioned above the efficacy of the snake dance as a prayer for rain. Skeptics, of course, always say that the chief medicine men are pretty smart at predicting the weather and as they are the only ones who determine when the snake dance is to be held, they postpone it until their fine senses tell them that rain is almost ready to fall. However, many authentic cases have been recorded of healing diseases by use of herbs and ceremonies, also the luring of herds of deer, antelope and buffalo in the old days by means of the ritual of the medicine men. 

Catlin, who visited the Mandans and others in 65

and later, goes so far as to say that the Buffalo dance cannot fail of its purpose which is to "make buffalo come." Of course he has his tongue in his cheek, and goes on to explain that it cannot fail, because it must be continued, day and night, until the buffalo is actually seen, when official word is given to cease dancing and prepare for the hunt. The example he cites, however, seems not exactly to bear out his skeptical thesis, for he tells of a frenzied dance which was finally called to a halt by the cry from the watchman that buffalo had been sighted; when however the hunters set forth they found that what had been taken for buffalo were Sioux warriors who decoyed their enemies by a clever ruse, and attacked them, killing many. The Sioux were driven off at last, and then buffalo came. In other words, the war party interrupted the dance instead of the coming of buffalo-yet the dance was successful. 

The Mandans, incidentally, though a plains 66

tribe and dependent upon buffalo for meat, were not migratory but lived in permanent villages, and instead of following the buffalo they brought the buffalo to themselves. Hence the importance to them of this bit of medicine.

The contagious diseases which were brought among the Indians by contact with the whites proved too much for the medicine men. Smallpox, tuberculosis, trachoma and even measles worked terrible havoc among them and it was not until the medicine of the white man came among them and great changes were made in their general manner of life that any progress was made against these dread diseases.

Associated with religion, is, of course, the thought of a hereafter and the disposal of the dead. 

We all are acquainted, of course, with the expression, "Happy Hunting Grounds". The expression may not be familiar to all tribes, but the idea is a very natural one. Man in general hangs tenaciously 67

to life and cannot bear the thought of giving it up. When face to face with death, he tries in every possible way to escape the reality of death and is forced to conceive a life after death. The exact form of this life after death varies naturally with the ideals of the people. Even among Christians we find that there is a variation of emphasis. Some people who feel they have worked all too hard in this life conceive the life of the world to come as an eternal rest, but others who are full of energy and untiring in their labors prefer the conception that St. John gives us in Revelation, "Where they rest not, day nor night". So, we can hardly blame the Indians if they made the world-to-come the sort of place they wanted to be in, and, since hunting was their chief delight, it was to be expected that their conception of Eternity would be plenty of deer and buffalo to hunt. 

The important thing is the universal belief in a world to come and the rewards given in the future 68

life to those who lived up to the standard of bravery and virtue which was accepted by the community.

The disposal of the bodies of the dead varied rather widely. On the Plains they used to hang the bodies of children in trees until they were thoroughly dried out before burying them. The bodies of adults were laid out on racks built of saplings and elevated fifteen or twenty feet above the ground. The Navajo people buried their dead in the crevices in canyons, covering them with stones and brush. The Hopi people, believing that the souls of the departed reached the home of their ancestors by way of the Grand Canyon of the Colorado, buried the bodies of the dead in the ground in a seated posture. A long pole was erected between the legs, the top of the pole protruding above the ground. To this pole above the ground, a string or leather thong is fastened which is led in the direction of the Grand Canyon 69

so that at the end of four days, when the soul leaves the body, it can climb the pole and by feeling the string knows the right direction to go to find its eternal home.

Burying food, weapons and other articles with the body was a widespread custom, as was the killing of a favorite dog or horse over the grave.

Whether the idea was that these things would be taken and used in the future life or that they were a sacrifice to the spirits is not always quite clear. 

We must not be too critical in such matters-we too have many well established customs whose origins and purposes are obscure. Probably both ideas existed together; and there may have been the further thought that objects most intimately connected with the deceased should not be used by others.

The Navajo have a superstition about houses in which anyone had died. The characteristic house of the Navajo is called the hogan and it is built 70

of logs, brush and clay, circular or octagonal in shape until it reaches about five feet above the ground and then forming a cone with a smoke hole at the apex. These houses are always built with their doors to the East, not only for the practical reason that the morning sun enters, but also with a religious significance connected with the worship of the sun as their Great Father. However, if someone dies in a hogan, no one must ever live in it again. The hogan is abandoned and the fact of the death having occurred in it is recorded by the breaking of a new door in the North side. 

The nomadic habits of the Navajo result in many abandoned hogans being scattered over the reservation as they go from place to place seeking fresh pasturage and anyone may use an abandoned hogan unless the North door has been broken through. A family will sleep outdoors in the cold and in the rain rather than enter such a hogan. 

Morality is, of course, associated with religion, 71

but here it is necessary for us to remember what was said above about religion not being something just added to one's ordinary life. It would be well if Christians cultivated this attitude towards religion and integrated it better with all other phases of life. It is rather significant that there were no skeptics or unbelievers among the pagan Indians, so far as can be determined now, and I think it was due to this attitude of religion being life and not just a part of life. Morality, therefore, was not considered a department of religion but a department of life. 

Perhaps we might have dealt with treachery under Warfare, because almost always those who have fought Indians have emphasized the treachery of the red man. The Indian was treacherous to his enemy. As long as he was at war, it was part of his warfare to inflict every possible injury upon his enemy. Treachery is just another way of lying. 

Lying and stealing are considered lawful in so- 72

called civilized warfare. It would be foolish, would it not, to tell the enemy what you are planning to do? In private life, however, the Indians were not treacherous and they considered lying as not only immoral, but stupid. If anyone were caught lying he was punished by the judgment of the tribe; the same applied to theft. There was nothing wrong in a tribe taking hunting grounds away from another tribe if that could be done by superior fighting, nor was there anything wrong in a party of young men going out and running off the horses of an enemy encampment, but for an Indian to steal a horse or even a whip from a fellow tribesman was not merely a crime, it was a sign of utter degeneracy.

It's easy to see how a primitive taboo should be built up on this score. When there were no houses with doors to lock there was no way of protecting your own things unless you sat in front of your teepee door all day. Consequently, a rigid 73

convention was established as to the sanctity of personal property. Such a taboo could very easily be enforced because every article of personal property was handmade. There was no mass production of anything. One man's quiver or bow-guard or quirt would be different from those of his neighbors. If, therefore, a thief seized an article belonging to his neighbor, the only way he could keep it would be to hide it and then it would be of no use to him; but if he revealed it, it would be recognized as belonging to the proper owner. 

When, however, the white man came into contact with the Indian and traders brought blankets and trinkets of all sorts which had been produced under the white man's economic system, the old morality broke down.

A story is told about Bishop Whipple, one of the early missionaries to the Dakotas. The Bishop was calling on the chief of a certain village and he left his saddlebags outside of the chief's teepee. 74

Turning to his Indian guide he asked, "Will my things be safe here?", and the guide replied with a smile, “Oh, yes, Bishop, they will be perfectly safe, there isn't a white man within fifty miles of here".

The sexual morality of the Indian was also a matter of strict tribal taboo and whatever the custom of the tribe was that was the custom followed by individuals in it. Some of them apparently allowed promiscuity among children up to the age of maturity, but after that marriage was taken for granted and strict fidelity expected both of husband and wife. Polygamy in some degree was almost universal as we might naturally expect among a people as devoted to warfare as were the Indians. A war-like people always has more women than men and the result is that one man must have more than one wife. Bachelors and "maiden ladies" were virtually unknown. Adultery in women was rare and when discovered was 75

severely punished. Among many tribes this punishment took the form of mutilation of the face by cutting off the ears or the cartilages of the nose. 

Nevertheless, there were some tribes which took all such matters very lightly.

That leads us, naturally, to consideration of marriage customs. These varied, as we may expect, from tribe to tribe. Many of the tribes on the Plains and in the Southwest practiced a rigid system of exogamy; the tribes being divided into various clans within which no two individuals might marry. Other tribes recognized no such limitations.

Marriage was by purchase among nearly all the tribes. When a young man came of marriageable age he went to the lodge of his intended sweetheart with an offering. In the hunting days it might consist of a deer or two or perhaps a given number of ponies which might be represented for the purpose of a ceremonial gift by so many quirts. 76

If the gift was accepted by the father of the young woman, the couple were considered to be betrothed and the marriage rites would take place at once.

Bundling was practiced among many of the tribes in a manner somewhat similar to that of our 

New England ancestors. A young man would come to the teepee of his chosen bride and would lie along side of her couch through the night. He might come quite undiscovered by the parents who would see him in the morning. If he still liked the appearance of the young lady when she woke up and if the parents liked the young man, they proceeded to make out the marriage contract. 

A more common form of courtship was with the blanket and in an Indian village on the Plains in the olden days it was not at all unusual to see what might look like a strange four-footed creature standing covered over with a blanket. 

In reality, of course, it was two creatures. The 77

procedure was as follows: the young man would take one of his best blankets, one that was not too readily recognized as his own, and cut two holes in it to look through. Shrouded in this he would stand near the teepee of his beloved. When she came out he would throw an end of the blanket over her head; if she liked the idea she would snuggle in and they would stand there together for hours, quite unmolested and, of course, quite unrecognized. People would pass by and pay no attention to the couple wooing in this manner. If, however, she did not like the idea, she would throw the blanket off of her and it was considered an unforgivable thing for a young man to persist in his attentions after such a rebuff. 

The status of women in all the tribes, excepting those of the Southwest, was that of a chattel, the husband had absolute power over his wife. However, any unkindness was very rare and cruelty practically unknown. A man might have as many 78

wives as he desired, but usually there were not more than two or three. In the Southwest, the woman had a very high position; she owned the house, although her husband owned the livestock.

This is still the case among most of the tribes of the Southwest. Polygamy was very limited, especially among the Pueblo people who were not war-like and, therefore, did not have the problem of excess women.

Among the nomadic tribes of the Southwest it was the custom, up to recent times, for a couple to be married and to continue in a state of monogamy for approximately twenty years. At the end of that time the woman would be pretty well fed up with housework and, perhaps it may be admitted that the husband's aesthetic sense was not completely satisfied in the person of the woman who had worked for him and with him and, consequently, he looked around for an auxiliary wife who would be about the age of his own youngest 79

child. Such an auxiliary wife was welcomed by the first wife who perhaps felt towards her husband much as he felt toward her and who certainly was glad to have such a young and strong helper. More than two wives was very rare in the Southwest. As I mentioned above, divorce rested entirely with the woman in the Southwest. 

She did not even have to tell her husband to get out. If he came home and found his extra moccasins or his saddle resting on the door-step he knew there was just one thing to do that was to pick them up and go away. This method of discipline had a salutary effect upon a husband's behaviour and it is said that the privilege was very seldom made use of even by the most impatient of wives.

The mother-in-law taboo is one of the most interesting of all the tribal customs of our Indians. 

We use mothers-in-law as prolific material for the comic strip banalities and it is probable that in pre- So

historic times the Indians wrestled with the problem of the interfering, domineering mother-in-law. 

However, they stopped worrying about the problem and cut the knot with one fell stroke of the sword of convention. It became bad medicine as well as bad manners to speak to or look at one's mother-in-law. There was no enmity in it, no hard feeling, no discourtesy; they simply avoided each other completely and entirely. If a woman went to see her married daughter, she would first find out if her son-in-law was at home, if he was she would not go in. If he came home while she was there he would immediately go and wait outside, looking off towards the hills. The motherin-law would immediately throw a shawl or blanket over her head and face and go out. Then the husband could return to his home. She might even work for him preparing his meals during the illness of her daughter, but she could not speak to him or meet him face to face. If there were no 81

one to carry a message to him she would go out to within earshot of where he was working and cry out to the birds or trees, "Tell him his dinner is ready". He would understand.

There is still a firm belief among many tribes to the effect that any one who makes either a painting or a photograph of a person can and does obtain power over that person to do evil. It is a survival of sympathetic magic, common to most primitive peoples. George Catlin, who visited various tribes of Indians more than a hundred years ago for the purpose of painting pictures of them, records the difficulty he encountered because of this belief. The pictures were so lifelike that they must have in them some of the life of the original, whose primary existence was thereby curtailed.

With a movie camera I once tried to take a picture of a group of Pah-Ute women and girls. Before aiming the camera I asked if any of them 82

objected. They giggled a little, but no one refused. To be quite certain, and to be guilty of no breach of courtesy, I repeated "Please tell me if you don't want me to take the picture. I will not take it if you don't want me to." Still no one objected, and some of the younger ones nodded approval. They were so grouped that the younger ones were to one side, the older to the other. I started aiming the camera at the younger ones, and moved it slowly along. But as the older ones felt the dangerous medicine approaching, they covered their heads quietly with their blankets. 

Throughout the Southwest it is still forbidden to tourists to photograph Indians without permission of the individual Indian. In most pueblos you may, perhaps for a fee, take pictures of houses and other objects, but you are explicitly told that the permission does not apply to persons. In at least one pueblo the use of a camera is entirely forbidden. 83

Adaptability 

DAPTABILITY, I have said before, is one of the outstanding qualities of our Indians and one which, so far as I have been able to find in the books which have come to my attention, has not at all been appreciated. When I say adaptability I don't mean merely the ability to change one's manner of life, but I mean the ability to retain one's manner of life in the face of changing conditions and while using new material.

Above, in connection with religion, I pointed out that the Indian could retain a great deal of his old beliefs and practices while accepting many elements of the religion of the white man. This is something that is done in every department of life. 85

Did you ever see a war-bonnet? Did you ever see "genuine Indian beadwork"? Did you ever see a real Indian teepee? If you did, I would like to bet that the war-bonnet had as its basis an old felt hat and that the quills were wrapped with red flannel. If the feathers themselves were not colored with aniline dyes, they were tipped with horsehair dyed a brilliant scarlet through means utterly unknown to primitive Indians. Your "genuine Indian beadwork" was made of beads of porcelain or glass manufactured probably in Massachusetts by white people who probably never expected to see an Indian. This beadwork which we consider characteristically Indian is something that the Indians learned from the white man, but they preserved their own traditions and culture which had formerly been expressed through porcupine quills, through a new and more convenient medium. To be sure, they had previously had beads, but these beads were made laboriously by 86

boring holes in little bits of shell and then slowly grinding down the outside to the desired size. So laborious was the process, that wampum or peag was the substitute for money on the Atlantic seaboard. The "real Indian teepee" that you saw was made of canvas and I am sure the canvas was manufactured in New England from cotton grown in Southern plantations. Originally, of course, the teepee was made of buffalo hide, but it didn't take the Indians long to find out that canvas was better, easier to procure, lighter to carry, quicker at drying out after a storm than buffalo hide and, after the buffalo disappeared, as he did in 1883, they were forced to find a substitute for buffalo hide.

These are only a few of the many instances that could be brought forward. Take dress for example the original Indian dress was of buckskin. In the hunt, when going to war and for many ceremonial dances, the dress of a man was 87

limited to breech-cloth, moccasins and eagle feathers, but at other times, buck-skin leggings and a buck-skin shirt covered, together with the breech-cloth, the entire body. Almost as soon as they found out they could get cloth from the traders in return for a few pelts, they started making their clothes of cloth, reserving the old buckskin material only for ceremonial occasions. To this day, many of the Indians who wear white man's trousers and white man's shirt insist upon wearing the shirt tails outside of the trousers, hanging down in front and behind just as the old breech-cloth did. Such a shirt is usually confined about the waist with an ornamental belt. coat-shirt, buttoning all the way down the front, has for this reason never been popular among Indians.) Even the hogan, considered by many to be the primitive house of the Navajo Indian, was originally circular in shape instead of being made eight-sided and was built entirely of brush and (The 88

saplings. When the railroad started to come through the desert country, large quantities of railroad ties were stacked up at various points, and who could blame the forward-looking Navajo if he at once recognized what help these ties would be in the building of his house? Now he builds his houses that way all the time, even if he has to cut down cottonwood trees to get the logs. 

We have learned a great many things from the 

Indians. The use of tobacco, corn, beans, squash, pumpkin and many other household necessities, not to mention countless healing herbs, were first known to us through the Indian. Likewise the 

Indian has been just as free in adopting our contributions to his own mode of life, but he has had a way of preserving the picturesqueness of his own inherent background even while making use of the white man's material.

The same thing is true with regard to education and economics. The Indians are learning today 89

all the things that the white man's schools have to teach and he is enriching his own tribal life thereby. 90

The Future of the Indian 

T would be presumptuous for anyone to make extravagant prophecies regarding the future of the Indian. Only a few years ago the Indian was regarded as the vanishing American; today he is increasing rapidly. His death rate is still higher than the death rate of the white population, but his birth rate is so much higher that at the present rate it is quite possible to imagine he will some day take his place again as the master of North America. The definite turn came about 

1895. Since then every census has shown an increase. Today there are about as many Indians as there were in 1860, but in 1860 they were "becoming extinct". 91

It is too early now to say very much about the new policy of the Indian Bureau. During the last few years there has been a reversal of policy on the part of the government. The Indian has had a "new deal". Opinions are still very violent on both sides. Up to 1933 tribal lands were being taken away from the Indian rapidly. The reservations had been largely alloted to individual Indians who had the power to alienate their own lands, consequently, Chief Black Snake, if he wanted to buy a horse, could sell part of his alloted land on the reservation. This explains what strikes so many tourists as extraordinary, namely, the existence of farms owned by white men, in fact whole towns and cities inhabited by white men, within the limits of an Indian reservation. 

Since the Reorganization Act greater powers have been given to the Tribal Councils, economic planning has been encouraged and natural resources conserved for the benefit of the reservation In- 92

dians. The Commissioner has been severely criticized both by white men and Indians for his new polices and at the same time he has been warmly commended both by white men and Indians. How the whole scheme will work out no one can tell with certainty, for even though the policies are sound they may come to nothing.

Many Christians have criticized the new policy because it is no longer obligatory for Indians going to Government schools to receive instruction in the Christian religion. Formerly, an Indian child of ten years of age was expected to decide whether he was going to be a Protestant or a Catholic, beyond this he had no choice and usually his decision was made on the basis of a mere whim or after brief consultation with some older 

Indian boy. Now, however, the parents may say that an Indian may continue to be a pagan if he wants to.

It may seem strange for a priest to say that this 93

is a decided advance, but I think that it is, and when I say so I don't want to be put down as a "liberal" who considers that the Christian religion is just one of many ways in which a man may find communion with the Eternal. I am firmly convinced that the Christian religion is the revelation of God and that as our Saviour said, “No man cometh to the Father but by me", yet I believe just as firmly that God is a respecter of human personality, and that no good is accomplished by forcing an immortal soul's decision on so important a subject as the religion by which he is to live. I think that Christianity will mean a great deal more to Indians if it is completely divorced from Government pressure.

A part of the new policy is the encouragement of the Indians in the continued use of their own languages, ceremonials and arts and crafts. That these elements are for the enrichment of our civilization and culture can hardly be denied. We have 94

gone a long way since the slogan "The only good

Indian is a dead Indian" served to steel our Pioneers to a policy of ruthless extermination. 

One parting word. I realize quite fully that this booklet is going to be put down as "just another Indan blurb". I started by telling you about my friend who said, "You Indian lovers make me sick"; may I say very respectfully that love is no more sickening than ignorance or prejudice. Such expressions as, "you act like a bunch of Indians"-"Why not behave like a white man" and the like reflect our unspeakable arrogance and our assumed superiority. 

It is my modest hope that this rudimentary sketch of the 

Indian and his ways will help to make him better known and therefore more deeply appreciated by his White Brothers. Just so, much of the riches of our land are enriched by the heritage of the red man and no harm is going to be done in preserving these riches. 95
