The Commonwealth of Massachusetts CIVIL SERVICE PERFORMANCE RATING. 

SUMMARY OF REPORT. 

LEGISLATIVE BACKGROUND. 

In conformance with the directive of the General Court (House, No. 3158 of 1958), this report discusses the feasibility of instituting a system of performance rating for use in connection with the promotion of civil service employees. 

This subject has been repeatedly raised in the General Court in various forms. Thus, legislative proposals to require, to authorize, or to prohibit the performance rating of state and local employees have been made on 22 occasions since 1938. 

Four legislative recess commissions have rendered reports such as this.

Outstanding among performance rating proposals have been five substantially identical Kirshen-Porter bills filed since 1947 -two of which have been reintroduced in the present session of the General Court (House, Nos. 327 and 437 of 1959). 

These two 1959 pending bills propose a combination of (a) employee work performance reports and performance ratings, (b) seniority, and (c) civil service promotional examination grades, to become the basis of employee promotions in the classified civil service. By statute this combination would consist of an examination mark with a weight of 75 per cent, an employee performance rating with a weight of 17½ per cent, and seniority with a weight of 7½ per cent. 

Such performance ratings could also be used in connection with pay increases, transfer, lay-off, suspension and dismissal of 79,000 state and municipal employees in the classified civil service of Massachusetts. Responsibility for the performance rating system would be vested in the State Civil Service Commission and Director.

Past Commission Studies. Establishment of a civil service performance rating system has been favored by the two legislative recess commissions of 1937 and 1943, and opposed by a third of 1947, while a fourth recess commission, of 1938, remained neutral.

PURPOSES AND TYPES OF PERFORMANCE RATING PLANS. 

Purposes. Performance rating is a tool of management designed to improve the efficiency of employees and thereby increase the return for each dollar expended for personnel. Although not intended primarily as a device to improve employee morale, performance rating may serve that end also. Performance rating may be designed as the basis for one or more of the following purposes: 

1. Pay Increases. For approval of employee step-rate pay increases or "merit bonuses". 

2. Promotion. For selection of employees for promotion, and checking the effectiveness of the promotional system. 

3. Probation. For appraisal of probationary employees, to determine their eligibility for continued employment. 

4. Incentives; Morale. To provide employee incentives and to improve employee morale through recognition of superior work performance; and, negatively, to exert pressure on the inferior employee who does not respond to training or improve the quality and quantity of his work. 

5. Training. To identify employee training needs and improve assignment of employees in terms of their abilities and knowledge. 

6. Order of Lay-off. To determine the order of lay-off of employees when reductions in force occur; and, alternatively, to establish the order of re-employment. 

7. Supervision. To improve supervision and administrative organization.

Seven Types of Rating Plans. This report describes in detail the seven principal types of performance rating plans which have been developed since the initiation of federal civil service performance rating 72 years ago. All seven of these plans seek to identify (a) the superior employee whose performance merits special rewards and better promotional opportunities, (b) the average or satisfactory employee, and (c) the inferior employee who must be trained, transferred to a more suitable position, or discharged if the former two actions fail. Generally, performance rating plans and procedures require the employee's work performance to be described and evaluated solely in terms of the duties and specifications of his position. 

The most frequently used of the seven types of performance rating plans are:

1. Trait Rating Plans. All three major varieties of this most popular type of the performance rating plans usually require the work performance of an employee to be described and analyzed under a small number of job headings using such traits as quality of work, quantity of work, knowledge of the job, work attitudes, etc. The supervisor is directed to indicate on the rating form the extent to which the employee has these traits. For this purpose the trait rating plan uses a graphic so-called trait rating "scale" on which the supervisor shows by adjectives the traits of each employee, ranging from "Poor" to "Outstanding," with one or more intervening adjectives such as "Fair," "Good," and "Very Good." The supervisor must then combine on a numerical basis the grading of individual traits into an over-all performance rating for all of the marks given for the traits pertinent to the employee's job. 

The report must be discussed with the employee by the supervisor. Procedures are prescribed whereby an employee may appeal for a review by a higher authority at which the supervisor must be prepared to justify his rating of the employee. 

2. Analytical Check List Plans. -The major varieties of this type of performance rating also follow the above procedure, but there are two major differences, as follows: (a) In order to minimize supervisor prejudice, the rating form requires the supervisor to describe the employee's behavior on the job solely in terms of behavior and other incidents observed by the supervisor or known to him officially. For this purpose the supervisor is confronted on the printed form with a series of contradictory statements of employee behavior or incidents on the job, and is asked to check-mark on the list those statements which apply to the employee. (b) Under analytical check list systems the supervisor is not requested to evaluate the report of employee performance. He is unaware of the weights or numerical grades of the various statements appearing on the check list and the evaluation of each report which he completes is made subsequently by specialists in the personnel office of the organization who translate the check-marks into a performance rating by means of confidential formulae. 

3. Substantiating Evidence Plans. - These plans provide that the supervisor shall automatically rate all of his employees as "Average" or "Satisfactory," except in those cases which he believes warrant either an "Unsatisfactory" or an "Outstanding" rating. For the latter two non-average types of employees, the supervisor must use a special performance rating form to explain,

[Mar. with substantiating evidence, why he thinks the employee merits a very poor or a very superior rating, respectively. Procedures are provided for a subsequent supervisor-employee interview, and then for appeals by the employee from the action of the supervisor. 

4. Other Rating Plans. — Four other performance rating sys tems are also available though used less frequently than the three preceding plans. They are discussed in the report under the following titles: (4) narrative rating plans; (5) forced distribution plans; (6) field review plans; and (7) the obsolete rank-order plans.

GOVERNMENTAL AND PRIVATE USE OF PERFORMANCE RATING PLANS. 

Performance rating of employees has been used by the Federal Government since 1887, by state and local governments since about 1913, and by private business and industrial firms since the turn of the century. The greatest growth and development in this system has occurred since the First World War. The following summaries of the much lengthier discussions in the main body of this report cover the experiences of: (1) The federal government, (2) State and local governments in Massachusetts, (3) Other jurisdictions, and (4) Private business and industry. 

1. Use by Federal Government. Close to 2,000,000 federal civil service employees are now subject to the provisions of the 

Performance Rating Act of 1950. This statute requires most federal agencies to use performance rating plans for their civil service personnel, which have been approved by the Federal Civil Service Commission. The law provides for the rating of these employees on the basis of not less than three adjective ratings, for which minimum standards are prescribed, as follows: "Outstanding," "Satisfactory," and "Unsatisfactory." The details of rating methods in the individual agencies are left to their discretion, subject only to general supervision by the Civil Service Commission. The statute requires, however, that each federal agency establish a board of review to hear and decide the appeals of employees. Such boards must consist of one appointee of the employing department head, one representative of the departmental employees and one person appointed by the Civil Service Commission. 

Federal Civil Service Rules, formulated by the Commission, stipulate that the performance ratings of employees may be used as a basis for salary increases, promotions, demotions, removals,

The usual experience of federal agencies is that about 99 per cent of their civil service employees receive "Satisfactory" ratings, and that only about 1 per cent of the employees are rated "Outstanding" or "Unsatisfactory." 

2. Use by State and Local Governments in Massachusetts. Per formance ratings are not now used to decide the promotion of Massachusetts state and local employees who are in the classified civil service (under chapter 31 of the General Laws). Such ratings are, however, utilized by various state agencies and local governments for the evaluation of probationary civil service appointees, for the promotion of non-civil-service personnel and for other purposes.

Eight state agencies now make use of performance rating procedures for various purposes, as follows: (a) the Comptroller's

In addition, a ninth agency, the Massachusetts Turnpike Authority, reports that it is exploring the feasibility of performance rating of toll personnel for training purposes, and for promotional purposes. 

Performance ratings are also reported in use by 32 city and

[Mar. town governments of Massachusetts; in 27 of these communities the performance rating is used only by the local school department, mostly in connection with merit salary increases for teachers and the evaluation of probationary teachers. 

3. Use in Other Jurisdictions. At least 22 jurisdictions 

(namely, 20 States, Puerto Rico and the District of Columbia) provide in their civil service statutes and rules for the performance rating of state, and in some cases, local, employees. In 20 of these 22 jurisdictions, performance rating is actually in effect for such employees, while performance rating has been suspended in two others. Eighteen of them employ performance rating as a factor in promotions.

By type of rating systems, the most popular trait rating system is used in these 13 jurisdictions: Alabama, California (68,000 employees), Colorado, Kansas, Missouri, Nevada, New Jersey, Ohio (44,000 employees), Oregon, Pennsylvania, Vermont, Virginia and the District of Columbia; a trait rating plan has been suspended in a fourteenth jurisdiction. 

Three other States report differing practices; thus, a narrative rating is followed in Louisiana (31,000 employees); a substantiating evidence plan in Michigan (29,000 employees); and a New 

York State rating system for 98,000 employees combines trait rating, behavior check list and substantiating evidence techniques in its plan. New York State also provides for performance rating of municipal employees by local civil service agencies: thus, New York City has a substantiating evidence plan for approximately 100,000 city employees.

Information which has been supplied shows that rating methods are employed in the remaining 4 of the 20 "rating" 

States, namely, Alaska, Illinois, Minnesota and Tennessee, and that the method was used and then abandoned by Maryland. 

The information available does not, however, provide details. 

4. Use in Private Business and Industry. An impressive report by the National Industrial Conference Board in 1951 indicated that two out of every five large business and industrial concerns in the nation were using performance rating as an element of their personnel programs. Performance rating systems are currently being used in a number of Massachusetts firms, including the Massachusetts Mutual Life Insurance Company of Springfield.

MASSACHUSETTS CIVIL SERVICE LAW ASPECTS. 

Employees Subject to Civil Service Law. There are in Massachusetts today approximately 36,000 state employees and 132,000 local government employees. Of this personnel, slightly over 

20,000 state employees and 59,000 local government employees are members of the classified civil service and are fully subject to the provisions of the Massachusetts Civil Service Law (G. L. c. 31). In addition, there are slightly under 12,000 employees of state institutions who are "semi-civil-service" personnel with employment and promotion not subject to the Civil Service Law, but with appeal rights to the state Civil Service Commission if they are removed from their positions or transferred, without their consent. 

Bases of Promotion. The Civil Service Law stipulates that promotion in the classified civil service shall be on the basis of "merit ascertained by examination, and seniority of service." It also provides that the selection of persons for positions and employments in the classified service shall be "in accordance with the results of examination, or in order of application, or otherwise." 

Within this general context, and subject to certain veterans' preference and other requirements, the Civil Service Law and Rules make provision for promotions on the two following bases: 

1. Non-competitive Qualifying Promotional Examinations. 

By request of an appointing authority, the Director of Civil Service may hold a non-competitive "qualifying promotional examination" to promote a particular employee to a higher civil service position in the same department. This action may only occur, however, if the following provisions are all applicable to the employee and position, namely, that the new position is in the next higher grade, that the employee has served at least three years in his present classified civil service position, and that he is one of the three oldest employees in length of total state and local civil service. There were 559 such examinations in 1958. 

2. Closed Competitive Promotional Examinations. These ex aminations are open to permanent civil service employees who have served one year or more in their present positions in the department in which the promotion is to occur. If fewer than two such employees pass the examination, a new examination may be held to which like permanent civil service employees of other departments are admitted. If no employees then qualify, an open competitive examination may be held on the same basis as is used

[Mar.

in filling non-promotional positions. There were 363 such examinations in 1958.

When such a closed competitive examination establishes an eligible list, the appointing authority may select any one of the three top men on the list for promotion; if he appoints the second or third man, he must report to the State Civil Service Director in writing the reasons for this choice. 

The promotions of "semi-civil-service" state institutional employees are made at the discretion of the appointing authority, and are not subject to the above examination procedure. 

Performance Rating Possible under Present Civil Service Law.- 

While present practice is to make promotions on the basis of the candidate's examination mark, training and experience, in the official service, and on the basis of seniority and practical tests, in the labor service, there seems to be little doubt that promotions need not be solely on that basis. The statute authorizes examinations to test the fitness of applicants for appointment in accordance with "the results of examinations or otherwise.'

As previously indicated, the Civil Service Law allows a department head to discharge a probationary employee whose work performance and conduct are not satisfactory. If an employee is so discharged, he is not restored to the eligible list and there is an official record in the Division of Civil Service giving the reasons for that discharge. However, if a department head allows the undesirable employee to "resign," no report of the particulars of the resignation and the employee's inadequate performance need be given to the Division of Civil Service, other than notice of resignation. The employee may then be restored to the eligible list, since his separation is officially assumed to be due to no fault of his own. Department heads from time to time allow these "resignations" in the sympathetic hope that the employee will do better next time. The practical and undesirable result of this process is to promote the absorption of inferior and inefficient employees into the civil service system. 

Pay increases for inefficient employees are also possible under the provisions of the State Pay Plan (G. L. c. 30, ss. 45-46). The law provides that the step-rate pay increases for state employees shall be initiated automatically by the Director of Personnel and Standardization on the annual anniversary dates of the employment of employees. The law also stipulates that an appointing authority may veto a step-rate increase of an employee by filing a written objection thereto with the Director of Personnel and Standardization, and this veto remains until the appointing authority removes his objection, or until the veto is overturned by the Personnel Appeals Board on appeal by the employee concerned. As a practical matter the records demonstrate that state department heads rarely raise formal objections to pay increases for inefficient employees.

ARGUMENTS FOR AND AGAINST PERFORMANCE RATING. 

Proponents of performance rating claim that its adoption as part of the civil service promotional process will compensate for alleged weaknesses in the present promotional examination procedure, will increase the efficiency of the government service and will improve the morale of the better employees. Opponents assert that no performance rating system has been devised which satisfactorily eliminates subjective judgments of rating officers, and that performance rating would involve costly administrative

[Mar. burdens not justified by the results. These controversial attitudes center on the following five principal points:

Promotion. - Proponents of performance rating argue that present promotional procedure in the Massachusetts civil service places all the emphasis on (a) seniority and (b) the employee's performance in a single written promotional examination. The least desirable candidates are treated on the same basis as the best, so far as their past performance in the civil service is concerned.

Opponents contend that proponents exaggerate the case. 

They argue that the above type of incident is rare, and that any reasonably competent employee can pass the carefully prepared promotional examination. Opponents note that the appointing authority has an option to select any one of the three top persons on the eligible list of candidates who passed the examination, and that in exercising this option the appointing authority can recognize the employee with the better work history. Finally, the appointing authority can discharge the selected employee during his six-month probationary period if his work is poor. 

Employee Morale. — Proponents of performance rating contend that once an employee has served his probationary period, there is little incentive for him to do more than a minimum amount of work, and that the best employee receives no more recognition than the employee who just meets the minimum. 

Proponents believe that performance rating would identify and reward the superior employees and exert pressure for better work from the marginal employees, to the benefit of both the employees and the taxpayers.

Opponents fear that performance rating would create more morale difficulties than it would solve. They do not believe that supervisor prejudices in rating the intangible aspects of employee performance have been eliminated successfully in existing performance rating systems, particularly in the trait rating systems which are most popular. Opponents believe supervisoremployee relationships would be disturbed by the rating process and that supervisors could intimidate workers with threats of low ratings, to the detriment of morale generally. They do not think that written performance reports, which supervisors must be prepared to justify, would really force them to be less biased. 

Administration. - Proponents believe that the extra work of rating will be more than offset by the major administrative benefits to be derived, namely: (1) improvement of the work effort

They observe that performance rating reports provide a useful source of information for planning administrative changes and reorganization. They insist that performance rating would not be so widely used in federal and state government, and in the business world particularly, if it did not pay for itself. 

Opponents, on the other hand, are doubtful of the administrative value of performance rating. They note that the simpler the rating system is, the greater is the subjective aspect of supervisor ratings, and that the plans which develop the greatest degree of supervisor objectivity are very involved administratively. Opponents believe that because proper attention by supervisors to the preparation of performance reports would take up too much supervisor time, many of them would submit hurried, unsatisfactory reports. 

Appeals. — Proponents do not anticipate a burdensome volume of appeals by employees aggrieved because of their performance rating. Proponents point out, for example, that only about 

1 per cent of California's civil service employees appeal their performance ratings.

Opponents, mindful of the liberal appeals policy of the Massachusetts Civil Service Law and the propensity of employees to exploit that policy, fear numerous performance rating appeals would result. Costs.

Proponents believe that a performance rating system of the trait rating variety would involve a cost equal to about 1 per cent of the payroll of employees rated, or about $560,000 at this time.

Opponents estimated in 1947 that it would be necessary for the State to hire as many as 60 new state employees to handle performance ratings, an additional cost to the State of possibly $150,000 a year. In addition to this new state cost, further expense would occur among the individual agencies of state and local government which adopt performance rating. 

The administrative cost to the New York City Civil Service 

Commission for a "substantiating evidence" performance rating plan covering 100,000 employees is $54,000 a year.

Chapter I. Legislative Background. 

ORIGIN AND SCOPE OF STUDY. 

Origin of Study. 

House, No. 3158 of 1958, reprinted on the inside of the front cover of this report, is the joint order which directs the Legislative Research Council to make this study of the feasibility of instituting a system of performance rating for use in connection with the promotion of civil service employees. Originally it was filed as a Re solve, House, No. 876 of 1958, by Representative George W. Porter of Agawam.

This directive reflects some 20 years of agitation by various Massachusetts citizens urging that the on-the-job performance of public employees be considered as a factor in their promotion in the state and local government service. At present, the promotion of the 

32,753 state and 59,194 local employees who are subject to the Massachusetts Civil Service Law is governed primarily by their competitive civil service examination in the official service (clerical, administrative, professional, and the like), seniority in the labor service and veterans' preference status in both cases; about a third of state personnel is institutional in character and only partially subject to the Civil Service law. In some positions promotion may also be contingent upon physical fitness and professional licenses. 

After public hearings, House, No. 876 with its original proposal for a study on the basis of a Resolve, was favorably reported by the Joint Committee on Civil Service and then by the House Committee on Ways and Means. When the bill was presented to the House for action, Representative Porter moved to substitute House, No. 

3158, a redraft requiring the same study but on the basis of a joint order which requires acceptance by only the two branches of the General Court. This modified proposal was referred to the Rules 

Committees, acting concurrently, which recommended its adoption as was then done by the House of Representatives and the Senate. 

This is the fifth legislative study of civil service performance rating practices authorized by the General Court since 1938.

1959.] 

Scope of Study. 

The specific directive in House, No. 3158 relates only to the feasibility of instituting performance ratings to guide promotion of civil service employees. This is but one of some seven purposes for which performance rating plans are in use. As this study progressed, this single aspect was found to be contingent in part on the more controversial question as to whether equitable and efficient performance ratings for civil service employees are possible in the light of intangibles, such as human prejudices and the attitudes and prac tices of bureaucracies. 

Consequently, it was deemed desirable to include within the scope of this study various performance rating plans which are used for more than the promotion of employees alone, thereby including broad related experience of administrators and employees which throws light also on the more limited question of promotional performance rating systems. However, in view of staff limitations in the Research Bureau, and the numerous variations existing within the major types of broader performance rating systems, this report is confined to the major aspects of such systems and to important differences of policy and practices. Thus, detailed comparisons have been omitted of the provisions of the many federal, state, municipal and private industry performance rating plans gathered by the Research Bureau staff which are available for reference by interested legislators and others. 

Study Procedure. 

To obtain information for this report, the Research Bureau staff solicited facts, statistics and viewpoints on a nation-wide basis. A total of 20 conferences were held with governmental officials and private persons interested in civil service performance rating systems and other aspects of public personnel administration. Included were discussions with: (a) the staff of the Program Planning Division of the United States Civil Service Commission, and other federal personnel officials; (b) various state officials within and without the 

Commonwealth, notably the Director of the Massachusetts State Civil Service Commission; (c) municipal officials in Massachusetts, New York and the District of Columbia; (d) various advocates of civil service performance rating legislation in Massachusetts; (e) the leaders of public employee labor unions in Massachusetts;

[Mar. (f) personnel executives of several private business concerns utilizing performance rating systems; and (g) officers of civic organizations.

In addition, questionnaires were sent by the Research Bureau to four categories of governmental agencies, namely, (a) the civil service or personnel departments of the 49 States, Hawaii and 

Puerto Rico; (b) 63 Massachusetts state agencies; (c) the personnel boards and departments of 110 Massachusetts cities and towns; and (d) the public school superintendents of the 155 largest 

Massachusetts municipalities. File material made available by the 

Massachusetts Municipal Personnel Boards Association and the 

National Civil Service League was inspected. Six large business firms within and without Massachusetts furnished information with respect to their performance rating plans. 

Special acknowledgment is made for the very extensive assistance of Mr. Harold H. Leich and his staff of the Program Planning

Gratitude is also expressed for the valued assistance and co-operation furnished by the other public officials, business concerns, civic organizations and private citizens indicated above in general terms. 

PAST LEGISLATIVE DEVELOPMENTS. 

Over the past two decades there have been presented to the General Court 17 bills and study reports which were directly concerned with performance rating of public employees. In addition, five other bills and reports had an important indirect bearing on the issue. Of these 22 bills and reports, 10 represent the views of proponents of performance rating, three calling for studies were probably also favorable, eight were hostile to such rating practices and one report was neutral.

Performance Rating Proposals for All Civil Service Employees. 

Although the feasibility of civil service performance ratings had been explored prior to 1946 by special recess commissions studying various features of the Massachusetts Civil Service Law (G. L. c. 31), the question was not pinpointed until 1946. In that year 

Simon Kirshen petitioned for a legislative recess study relative to providing an efficiency rating system for the classified civil service

1959.] 

of the Commonwealth. This petition introduced as House, No. 638 was adversely acted upon by the General Court.

Provisions of Kirshen-Porter Proposal. 

The Kirshen-Porter Proposal reproduced in its most recent 1959 form in Appendix A, seeks to provide a system whereby the promotion of civil service employees would be determined not only on the basis of their civil service examination marks and their seniority in service, but also on their performance rating on assigned work. 

The authors, to obtain this objective, would write an entire performance rating system into the Civil Service Law so that no one may evade the intent of performance rating without violating the statute.

Thus, House, No. 437 of 1959 would annually require every state and local department head having subordinate employees subject to the Civil Service Law ("appointing authority") to submit to the State Civil Service Director his performance rating report on each employee under his authority. This report, in a form to be regulated by the Director, would indicate the employee's work quality, quantity, attitudes and habits in relation to assigned duties; and, in the instance of administrative personnel, such judgment on the further factor of administrative ability would be indicated as well. 

This report would be initially prepared and signed by the immediate supervisor of the employee and would then be viewed and signed by the next highest supervisor for submission to the department head who would transmit it to the State Civil Service Director (section 3). 

The bill stipulates that the information included in the performance rating report by supervisors and department heads shall be in accordance with general instructions to be included in a manual to be prepared by the State Civil Service Director with the approval of the Civil Service Commission. The manual must adequately define and outline the terms, standards and procedures of performance rating; must state the purpose and use of performance ratings; and must cite the penalties for fraudulent ratings. It is also to include such other instructions as the Civil Service Director, with the approval of the Commission, may require. A copy of the manual is to be furnished to every supervisor engaged in the rating process; and copies must be made available for inspection by employees at their places of employment (section 3). 

The bill requires that each employee shall be given a copy of his performance rating after it has been received by the department head and before it is forwarded to the State Civil Service Director. 

The employee may inspect the entire record covering the determi-

The rated employee may disagree with the rating given him by indicating to the Director in writing the points on which he disagrees, and may ask for a review of his rating by a review board appointed for the purpose by the Civil Service Commission. However, the employee must file his notice of disagreement within seven calendar days after receipt of his rating, or he shall forfeit his right of appeal of that rating, except in exceptional cases for which the Director grants time extensions. The decision of the review board, which is final, must be reported to the employee (section 3). 

The Kirshen-Porter bill requires the use of performance ratings, in conjunction with other factors, for civil service promotions. It also directs the Civil Service Commission to insert broader provisions in its rules "under which the performance rating shall be the basis for increase in pay within the grade, for transfer to a different type of work within the grade, for temporary layoff, for suspension from the service and for dismissal from the service" (section 3). 

The earlier 1957 version of the bill (House, No. 1045, section 3) also provided that performance ratings could be used to determine decreases in pay; but this feature was eliminated in subsequent drafts. In testimony and discussions before the Joint Committee on Civil Service concerning previous editions of the Kirshen-Porter bill (House, No. 1045 of 1947; Senate, No. 37 of 1952; House, No. 612 of 1957), proponents and opponents have emphasized several "pro"

Advocates of the Kirshen-Porter proposal contended that there is a need for performance rating in the Massachusetts civil service in order to compensate for weaknesses of the present promotional examination process, to increase the efficiency of the service, and to improve the morale of the better employees. They regard performance rating as a tried and legitimate tool of management to improve the public service in the interests of the taxpayer; and they cite the use of performance rating systems by the Federal Government, some 20 state governments and many private business and industrial concerns as a predecent. 

With respect to examination aspects, proponents also argued that present promotional examination procedure places nearly all the emphasis on the employee's performance on one day, -the day on which he takes the written examination. If he is not feeling well, or if he is a good worker who does not express himself too well, the better employee may pass with a lower mark than a less satisfactory candidate who has mastered the art of "examinationmanship". In contrast, opponents contended that (1) this sort of situation is not likely to occur very often, and that the present system provides adequate means whereby the better of the candidates may be selected. Opponents argued that (2) there is a carefully prepared competitive examination, usually in writing, which any reasonably competent candidate can pass, whatever his abilities of expression; moreover, when an eligible list has been established on the basis of the examination, the department head may select any one of the top three candidates on that list, including the lower man who may be the superior worker. Furthermore, observed opponents, (3) the individual so selected must serve a six-month probationary appointment, during which he may be fired if the department head finds his work unsatisfactory; and one of the other two candidates may be appointed, if still available.

In favor of the Kirshen-Porter proposal, proponents asserted that once a civil service appointee has completed his probationary period satisfactorily, there is little incentive for him to do more than the minimum amount of work required by his position. Chapter II. Purposes and Types of Performance Rating Plans. 

PURPOSES OF PERFORMANCE RATING. 

General Aspects. 

Performance rating is a tool of management designed to improve the efficiency of employees of government or business and to promote effective organization. Its objective is to increase the return for each dollar expended for personal services. Although not primarily a device for improving employee morale, performance rating is concerned with employee morale as an efficiency factor. 

Performance rating systems require supervisors to record and periodically to justify their opinions as to the work performance and attitudes of employees under their direction. Normally, a standard rating procedure is required calling for the preparation by the supervisor of a written rating report, ranging from a simple narrative statement to a complex adjectival and statistical document. The report is in turn subject to review by higher authorities in the organization; its content may have to be discussed with the employee who is rated; and procedures exist for correcting ratings which have been criticized as unfair by employees. 

Since many employees cannot be rated solely by their output, performance rating plans involve both tangible and intangible factors. It is the latter aspect which rouses the greatest doubts over the feasibility of particular types of performance rating plans, or of performance rating generally.

Seven Uses of Performance Rating. 

Performance rating of employees may be undertaken by governmental and business organizations for one or more of the following seven purposes: 

1. Changes of Pay Status. Ratings may be used to qualify employees for step-rate pay increases under the salary and wage program in effect. In some cases, these increases are subject to the employee's performance rating; in other cases performance rating factors are only applied to the last step-rate or last several steprates below the maximum pay for the position. Similarly, performance ratings may form the basis of "merit bonuses" paid to outstanding employees.

HOUSE - 

[Mar. 

No. 2655. 

2. Promotions. - Performance rating plans are frequently employed for the purpose of selecting employees for promotion, and for checking upon the effectiveness of the promotional procedures of an organization. Often they are combined with other promotional criteria, such as seniority, to produce composite promotional ratings. 

In regard to the uses of performance ratings for promotion, the Institute for Training in Municipal Administration has stated that A reliable index of employee performance on the job can be of inestimable value as a basis for promotions . . . Employee performance records should be a major factor in the selection of employees for promotion. Although outstanding performance in an employee's current position is not conclusive evidence that the employee will do equally outstanding work in a more responsible position, most supervisors would certainly hesitate to recommend for promotion an employee whose performance record is poor. Performance on the job reveals information regarding work habits, attitudes, ability to get along with others, and related factors that cannot be satisfactorily obtained elsewhere. (In Municipal Personnel Administration International City Managers' Association, 1950, p. 199.) 

3. Appraisal of Probationary Employees. Employment regu lations of governmental and private organizations frequently require newly appointed employees to serve a fixed "probationary" period of employment, during which they may be discharged if their work performance is unsatisfactory. Employers may also require newly promoted employees to serve a similar probationary period in their position before the promotion becomes "permanent." 

Performance rating plans may be employed to ascertain whether the probationary employee's work warrants his continuation in the job on a permanent basis. The rating program thus functions as a check on the effectiveness of departmental or company procedures for selecting new employees. 

4. Employee Incentives and Morale. Management may use performance ratings to give their employees a sense of the importance of their work. Positively, the rating procedure may seek to indentify the superior employees who are to be rewarded for their outstanding service by pay increases or bonuses and better promotional opportunities. The superior employee's morale, and his quality and quantity of work, is improved through the expectation of recognition under the rating plan. Negatively, the rating plan may also strive to identify the unsatisfactory employees who may expect disciplining or discharge if they do not respond to the employer's training program or improve the quality and quantity of their work. TYPES OF PERFORMANCE RATING PLANS. 

Long Period of Development. 

The theory and practice of performance rating of employees have an ancient history, beginning as early as the Third Century A.D. when an employee evaluation procedure was instituted for appointments and promotions in the Chinese imperial civil service. Centuries previously the Chinese emperors had developed a civil service system, complete with competitive written and oral examinations. 

In this country performance evaluation of military and naval personnel was instituted shortly after the formation of the Union. 

Performance rating of federal civil service employees began in 1887, and has expanded with many revisions until it now applies to more than two million employees. 2 It was introduced into the municipal service during the first decade of this century (first in Chicago) and into the state service in 1913 (first in California). At least 20 state governments use performance ratings, in part or all of their civil service systems. Private business and industrial concerns began applying employee performance rating shortly after the turn of the century; and it is estimated that two of every five companies have some kind of employee performance appraisal system in use.

3. Substantiating Evidence Plans. 

The third of the seven major classes of performance ratings being here summarized is the substantiating evidence method. This method is based upon the assumption that the vast majority of employees are doing average, satisfactory work in terms of the job requirements of their respective positions. The task of performance rating is simplified by rating the employee as "satisfactory", or its equivalent, unless substantiating evidence of verifiable facts can be submitted in support of an "unsatisfactory" or an "outstanding" rating. The substantiating evidence plan is devised to avoid the difficulty of fine distinctions which characterize the trait rating plans and analytical check list method. In some degree the substantiating evidence method may utilize the "critical incident" technique of specific employee actions observed by the supervisor. 

The operation of the substantiating evidence rating method was described as follows 18 years ago:

The supervisory official is asked not to rate employees, but to transmit his reports to examiners who evaluate the evidence and determine credits or demerits to be allowed. However, the supervisor makes the determination, for which no substantiating evidence is required, as to which employees fall into the standard group. Since this type of system may not provide adequate analytic evaluations as a basis of discussions with employees to improve performance, it may be supplemented by specialized ratings for supervisory purposes, which are not translated into credits or demerits for the employee's record. 

This type of system is designed to minimize subjectivity by focusing the supervisor's attention on the facts of job performance which can be stated in concrete terms, instead of asking him to estimate the employee's service in terms of qualities possessed in varying degress. In considering whether the subjective element has been largely banished, one must examine the judgmental process whereby the supervisor places an employee in the standard group or elects to submit substantiating evidence of his superior or inferior performance. One must also look at the supervisor's task in selecting and reporting evidence as to unusual performance, and the examiner's task in evaluating the evidence. Variation in the ability of supervisors to express themselves, and in the adequacy of supporting records which they may keep currently, will also enter into the results of such systems. 

These systems have, however, the value of simplicity of administration from the point of view of the supervisor and the advantage of eliminating problems arising from attempts at fine distinctions among the large body of employees whose performance is not outstanding.¹

5. Forced Distribution Plans. 

Forced distribution methods of performance rating, based in part upon the mathematical concept of the Law of Probability, assume the existence of a “normal” distribution of employees from bad to excellent which can be represented by a percentage spread or by a "normal distribution curve" ascertained by formula. The forced distribution technique may be used in conjunction with trait rating scales or analytical check lists; but they contain the added requirement that the supervisor or other rating officers must produce a total distribution of employee ratings consistent with the distribution formula. The objective of the forced distribution technique is to prevent supervisors and rating officers from rating all employees 'satisfactory", or rating too many employees "outstanding".

Thus, the employing organization may have a "normal" distribution curve or percentage spread which anticipates that 16 per cent of employees will have low performance ratings, 68 per cent will have satisfactory ratings, and 16 per cent will have better than satisfactory ratings (ranging from very good to outstanding). 

After all rating reports are in, the rating officials are forced to review them in their totality, and to make readjustments of marks substantially according to the employee performance experience of the organization, as represented or envisaged in the "normal" distribution pattern. Origin and Scope of Civil Service Law. 

In 1884 Massachusetts became the second State to enact a civil service statute governing the selection, promotion, tenure and removal of state and municipal employees (Acts of 1884, c. 320; now 

G. L. cc. 13 and 31). A year previously, Congress had passed the 

Pendleton Act creating the Federal Civil Service Commission, and New York had enacted the first of the state civil service laws. Performance rating of civil service employees was suggested by some of the provisions of the original Massachusetts statute directing the State Civil Service Commission to make rules providing — For open competitive or other examinations by which to test applicants for office, or for employment, as to their practical fitness to discharge the duties of the positions which they desire to fill. For promotions in office on the basis of ascertained merit and seniority in service and examination as may seem desirable. (In all cases where it is practicable vacancies shall be filled by promotion.) . . . For a period of probation before an appointment or employment is made permanent. (Acts. of 1884, c. 320, s. 14.)

The Civil Service Law of Massachusetts, like those of New Jersey and New York, covers many state and local employees. Of about 

36,000 Massachusetts state employees a year ago, 56 per cent (20,817) were reported subject to the full Civil Service Law; 33 per cent (11,936) to certain of its tenure and dismissal provisions but not entrance examination and promotional provisions; and the remaining 11 per cent (between 3,000 and 4,000) were exempt from the Civil Service Law. Of about 132,000 local employees, 44 per cent (59,194) are fully subject to the Civil Service Law.

Promotional Examinations. 

"Promotion" under the Massachusetts Civil Service Law is defined as a change from the duties of one grade of civil service position to those of a higher grade in the same or a different class, as determined by the State Civil Service Director. A "class" consists of a group of positions with sufficiently similar responsibilities to require similar qualifications of incumbents. A "grade" is a subdivision of a class including all positions with substantially identical responsibilities (G. L. c. 31, s. 1).

The Civil Service Law requires that promotions within the classified civil service be made "on the basis of merit ascertained by examination, and seniority of service" (G. L. c. 31, s. 3; Rule 21).

In practice, seniority is not applied to all promotional categories. 

Promotions must first be offered to persons within, and then to available candidates outside the classified source (G. L. c. 31, ss. 15 and 15F). By statute, veterans have a 2% promotional preference (G. L. c. 31, ss. 3, and 21-23A; Civil Service Rule 21). Promotions within the classified civil service are on the three following bases: 

1. Non-competitive Promotional Examinations. 

Classified official service employees, other than policemen and firemen, may be promoted upon passing a non-competitive qualifying promotional examination held by the Director of Civil Service at the request of a state or local "appointing authority."

This method is permitted, when these five requirements are met: (1) for promotion to the next higher grade in the official service; (2) when an employee has had at least three years of employment in the lower grade; (3) when he is one of the three oldest employees in total state and local civil service; (4) when he has necessary physical fitness requirements, approved by the State Civil Service Director; and (5) possession by the applicant of any licenses required by law. Ordinarily there is no veterans preference in this type of promotion. Promotional examinations are generally not used for the labor service. 

The Division of Civil Service held 559 qualifying promotional examinations in 1958, as contrasted with 414 in 1957. Approximately 40 per cent of these examinations related to the state service, and the balance of 60 per cent to the local service.

Open Competitive Examinations when Promotion Fails. 

When a position in the classified civil service normally filled by promotion through either a qualifying examination or a closed competitive examination cannot be so filled because of (a) the failure of any examinees to qualify, or (b) the absence of classified service candidates, then the position must be filled by open competitive examination. Major aspects of examinational procedure are summarized below.

In general, the open competitive examination may be taken by persons of good character who possess necessary technical experience and physical qualifications for the position, as developed by the State Civil Service Director in consultation with the appointing authority, and, in the case of state positions, also the Director of 

Personnel and Standardization in the Commission of Administration and Finance (G. L. c. 31, s. 2A). Examinees resident in the employing city or town are given preference over non-residents; and in tests held for the state service, residents of the Commonwealth are preferred over non-residents (G. L. c. 31, ss. 2A, 17 and 19).

If a state employee who is serving in an "unclassified" position

[Mar. outside the civil service or non-civil-service position takes an open competitive examination for appointment to a position in the classified service, he receives no special preferential credit in the examination for his non-civil-service experience. However, he does benefit from a general credit given all candidates for such experience, including service in private business. This experience factor is weighted by the Director of Civil Service and varies by type of examination. In the interests of fairness, no credit is allowed to a "provisional" incumbent of a position in the classified service for his service while an eligible list was being established. 

In preparing the eligible list of candidates passing an open competitive examination, the State Civil Service Director must observe markedly different preference requirements for veterans and others than hold for the closed competitive promotional examination described above. Briefly, the Director must list the qualifying candidates in the following order: (1) All disabled veterans, beginning with the highest examination mark; next, (2) All non-disabled veterans, in like order; next, (3) Widows and widowed mothers of veterans, killed in action or deceased by reason of service-connected disabilities, in the order of their examination marks; and (4) All other persons, in order of their marks (G. L. c. 31, ss. 3, 23 and 23B).

When the eligible list is established, the appointing authority is authorized to employ one of the top three candidates on the list, according to the order of preference described above. If, however, he selects the second or third candidates he must send a letter to the 

Director explaining his reasons for that choice (G. L. c. 31, s. 15).

Chapter IV. Performance Rating in State Agencies of Massachusetts. 

Letters were sent by the Research Bureau to 63 Massachusetts state departments, boards, commissions, divisions and authorities asking what use was being made of performance rating methods in connection with their personnel. ATTITUDES OF STATE OFFICIALS AND EMPLOYEES. 

At the present time, no information is available as to the general attitude of state department heads, departmental personnel officers, or state employee groups with regard to the desirability or undesirability of civil service performance rating. Of 41 agencies answering Research Bureau questions on this and other scores, only 15 commented on performance ratings, and 26 expressed no opinion. 

It may be assumed that the nine agencies whose performance rating practices have just been discussed above are reasonably satisfied with their present systems in terms of the objectives of those systems. But even here, one of the nine agencies, -the Division of Employment Security reacted negatively to any statutory imposition of a Civil Service Promotional performance rating system.

Only four agencies not now having a rating system indicated to the Research Bureau that general civil service performance rating might have some value.

State employees also have divided views about performance rating. The available evidence suggests (a) that the leaders of state employee organizations are for the most part opposed to performance rating, and (b) that the "rank and file" of state employees is indifferent to the proposal.

Favorable Views.

COST OF CIVIL SERVICE PERFORMANCE RATING. 

Any effort to estimate the probable cost to the Commonwealth of a civil service performance rating system for promotional or other purposes must necessarily be very tentative, because so much would depend on what kind of rating system was adopted, and the extent to which administration was centralized in the State Division of Civil Service or decentralized to the employing departments. 

The 1947 Special Commission on Civil Service, in discussing the Kirshen-Porter proposals which was outlined in Chapter I, reported an informed estimate by "a state official" that some 60 new state positions and between $100,000 and $150,000 in additional state appropriations would be necessary to implement those proposals at the state level; no estimate was advanced with regard to the cost to local governments (House, No. 1830 of 1948, pp. 7-8). 

The Kirshen-Porter bill proposes a plan to be administered centrally by the State Division of Civil Service, with liberal appeal provisions, which is similar to the system abandoned by the Federal Government in 1950.

In conversations with the Research bureau staff, the State Civil Service Director recently estimated that performance rating of the approximately 20,000 state employees and 59,000 local government employees in the classified civil service would require an additional 50 employees in his Division if pending versions of the Kirshen- 

Porter bill were enacted.

The United States Army currently employs a performance rating plan. Under it substantiating evidence is obtained from a narrative statement for civilian employees who are rated "Outstanding" or

1959.] 

"Unsatisfactory." About 429,000 employees are subject to the rating procedure. The Civilian Personnel Office of the Army reports that it must devote about one hour per year per rated employee to this work; supervisors must spend about four hours per year on the rating of each of their subordinates; and the rated employee himself must devote about two hours yearly to work connected with his performance rating, including interviews and appeals. To this expense represented by a total of seven man-hours per employee per year must be added printing and other allied administrative costs. 

This experience, applied to the rating of 79,000 Massachusetts classified state and local civil service employees, suggests that the equivalent personal services of 276 full-time state and local employees would be necessary if the Army method of performance rating were adopted here. 

The State of New York has a rating system which combines elements of the trait rating plan, behavior check list plan and substantiating evidence method. This system, covering 98,000 civil service employees, is decentralized to the departments in large degree. Thus, the State Department of Civil Service requires only a small staff of employees to handle performance rating: two employees working full time on performance rating matters, and five other employees dividing their attention between performance rating and other matters, at a total estimated cost of about $15,000 per year. However, this figure does not include costs incurred in the various departments. 

The City of New York utilizes a substantiating evidence plan applicable to approximately 100,000 employees. The full-time services of eight employees at a payroll and administrative cost of $54,000 are required in the City Civil Service Department in order to administer the plan; and this cost does not include expenses incurred to the various departments under the plan.

Chapter VI. Performance Rating in Other Jurisdictions. 

FEDERAL GOVERNMENT PRACTICE. 

Background of Federal Performance Rating. 

The principle of performance rating was introduced into the federal civil service in 1887, and has been mandatory under federal law since 1912. The use of service ratings received its greatest stimulus and underwent its greatest growth after World War II. Over the years, the federal system has undergone many extensive changes. 

The rating system authorized by the Ramspeck Act of 1940 and the Mead-Ramspeck Act of 1941 and established by the Civil Service Commission in 1942 provided a "Uniform Efficiency Rating system" employing five major classifications, as follows: "Excellent," "Very Good," "Fair" and "Unsatisfactory."

To evaluate employees for rating in these categories, the supervisor was provided with a list of 20 non-supervisory and 11 supervisory trait rating elements, which he was to apply only in the degree that such elements were required in the performance of the duties of the employee's position. At the side of the relevant elements as printed on the standard form the supervisor was required to indicate by a check mark for each employee whether the employee's performance was "Outstanding," "Adequate" or "Weak." These check marks, as totalled up by the supervisor, developed an overall performance rating of "Excellent," "Very Good," "Fair" or "Unsatisfactory" for each employee.

The supervisor's findings were then reviewed, and if need be revised, by a higher-level rating officer in the employing agency. Next, the report went to an Efficiency Rating Review Board in the employing agency for further review and an adjustment in terms of the totality of employees. If the employee desired, he could appeal either to the Efficiency Rating Review Board of his agency, or to the 

Efficiency Rating Review Board of the Civil Service Commission, or both.

All kinds of trouble flowed from this arrangement; and they became especially acute after World War II when demobilization of the armed forces was followed by a massive reduction in the civilian employee force in the various federal departments and agencies. In 

1944 the Congress had enacted a veterans' preference law which

[Mar. specified that such departments and agencies were to be governed by four factors in carrying out their reductions in force: one of these factors was to be the performance ratings of the employees. At this point, two facets of the performance rating system became particularly troublesome: (1) the use of five adjective designations increased the area of controversy between supervisors and employees where borderline ratings were involved; and (2) the appeals system was too liberal and too complicated by "red tape." Departmental officers rated their better employees "Excellent" in an effort to prevent their loss during the reduction in force; and thousands of employees appealed their ratings in an effort to protect themselves from discharge in the reduction in force. For example, there were over 600 appeals in 1948 alone in one large agency. 

Quite apart from these complications, the 1942 Uniform Efficiency Rating System proved ill-adapted to the needs of different agencies, and was burdensome to administer because of the high degree of centralization necessary at the Civil Service Commission level. The system failed to help employees to improve themselves. 

Present Federal Performance Rating Law. 

Out of this unsatisfactory situation came the Performance Rating Act of 1950, reproduced in Appendix B, which departed from the former criteria. This situation, now in effect, attempts to embody new thinking in the Federal Civil Service Law, as follows: 

1. Agencies are directed to establish their own performance rating plans, subject to the approval of the Federal Civil Service Commission. 

2. Appeals outside the employing agency are barred. Employee appeals are to be heard and resolved by a board of review within the employing department, to consist of one person designated by the department head, one by the employees of the department, and one by the Civil Service Commission. The decision of the board of review is final. 

3. The number of adjectival ratings is reduced from five to three, namely: "Outstanding," "Satisfactory" and "Unsatisfactory."

Under the law, each agency's performance rating plan must provide at least these three ratings; but four levels are allowed by Civil 

Service Commission rules. Five departments have established a fourth or "Very Good" level between "Outstanding" and "Satisfactory."

1959.] 

The minimum standards for the three basic adjective ratings as established in the Performance Rating Act are as follows: 

(a) "Outstanding" may be applied to performance which not only exceeds the normal requirements of the job, as they have been made known to the employee, but performance which is outstanding and deserves special commendation. This interpretation is rigidly construed. (b) "Satisfactory" performance is not defined directly, but must be equivalent to an efficiency rating of "Good" under the Veterans' Preference Act of 1944. (c) “Unsatisfactory" performance is that which "shall serve as a basis for removal from the position." No further definition is provided. 

Unchanged by the 1950 statute is the basic criteria for performance rating in the federal service: the officially assigned duties of the position, as they have been made known to the incumbent. 

As in the past, the rules of the Federal Civil Service Commission allow performance rating of employees for some or all of the following purposes: 

1. As an aid to improved individual performance. 

2. For strengthening supervisor-employee relationships. 

3. To recognize the accomplishments and good work of employees. 

4. As a guide to personnel actions such as salary increases, promotion, demotion, removal and reduction in force. 

5. As a means of identifying and keeping employees and supervisors aware of proper job requirements and standards. 

6. To make and keep employees aware of the judgments of their supervisors as to their work performance. 

7. To identify necessary individual training needs.

The Civil Service Commission construes "performance" narrowly. For example, medals awarded to an employee do not count in his performance rating; but credit is given for certain medals in promotions. Employees are given citations under the federal incentive award program, another device used by the Federal Government to recognize and reward good work. 

Experience under Present Federal Performance Rating Law. 

Federal departments do not like to use the "Unsatisfactory rating because of the time lost on appeals. The performance rating plan is not intended as a disciplinary instrument; and an “Unsatisfactory" rating must be preceded by a 90-day warning to the employee involved. The Civil Service Commission encourages the use of regular disciplinary procedures when disciplinary problems arise. Thus, lateness to work is not a rating factor. 

The supervisor must inform the employee of his performance 

---

[Mar. 

rating, so as to give him a chance to discuss the rating and to appeal. 

An "Unsatisfactory" rating may be appealed to the departmental board of review; but there is no appeal to the Civil Service Commission. There have been few appeals since 1950, as contrasted with the appeals which occurred during the previous eight years under the 1942 plan.

About 99 per cent of the nearly 2,000,000 federal employees subject to the Performance Rating Act are rated "Satisfactory."

Many departments record only "Outstanding" and "Unsatisfactory" ratings and the record assumes a "Satisfactory" rating in all other cases. Written qualitative evaluation is difficult in many instances. Some agencies use forms, others do not; very exhaustive forms are employed in certain departments. 

A majority of federal agencies do not review "Satisfactory' ratings. However, the Navy Department requires review of such ratings by the next higher supervisor. Most departments require "Outstanding" ratings to be reviewed by a personnel officer or committee at a higher level above the original supervisor and far removed from him.

Nearly all federal agencies have a personnel officer and a personnel division; and some of the larger departments employ performance rating experts. Many agencies provide training for supervisors in performance rating of employees, through annual training sessions or otherwise. Supervisor training is deemed essential to the proper operation of the various departmental performance rating plans; the department which neglects this aspect is very likely to encounter difficulties. Legislation was recently enacted to promote the training of personnel officers in the departments and to improve personnel management. Experience with the federal performance rating law suggests that some "push" is necessary to keep the rating system functioning properly, and to prevent its disintegration.

In general, the Federal Civil Service Commission has found that a highly formalized performance rating system does not work satisfactorily; therefore, the simpler rating procedures are favored. 

The Commission believes that performance rating is still the best system the federal government has for identifying the superior and inferior employees, notwithstanding the administrative difficulties encountered. 

This viewpoint is not fully shared by federal employee organizations.

The affirmative and negative reactions of business and industrial firms to the use of rating plans were summarized as follows by the report:

Many arguments have been heard against rating programs. Probably a majority are criticisms of the ways such programs are carried out rather than criticisms of the basic purposes of the programs. 

Bernard Shaw once said that his tailor was a very sensible man. Whereas his other friends tended to get a fixed notion of what he was like, Shaw explained, his tailor always took a fresh look at him and made new measurements. Sometimes the new measurements coincided with the older ones, but often there were changes. 

A rating report should give an accurate picture of the individual's current job performance. Few companies would question the usefulness of such a report. 

If it is argued that reports could be obtained from time to time for each employee without the bother of a formal rating program, this point could be granted. 

But the question should then be asked: Is it likely that the reports would be forthcoming without a formal plan? Lacking the stimulation provided by the formal, periodic, company-wide review, the average supervisor would feel no particular compulsion to evaluate his workers in any systematic fashion. Thus, he would be ill prepared, if called on by management, to supply the information desired. Most supervisors need a guide or outline to follow. Most raters also need to be held to some time schedule, or the work will drag out endlessly. One very definite advantage of a formal rating program, according to this reasoning, lies in the system itself.

Two basic values are claimed for rating programs. First, it is said that a company can make more intelligent decisions about its personnel if it has such reports. 

They may be referred to when questions arise as to who should be laid off, who should be advanced, who should be promoted, whose salary should be raised, and so on. Second, it is stated, employees like to know how they are doing. At this point the cynic retorts: "Sure, the fellow who is doing a good job likes to hear about it. The fellow who isn't doing such a good job isn't much interested in hearing about his shortcomings. When all is said and done, the average employee doesn't feel the merit rating program is very important one way or the other." 

The results of a Fortune survey (of January, 1947) . . . bear this out,

1959.] 

at least in part. The following question was asked: "If you work harder than those around you, do you think it'll pay off in terms of advancement and promotion?" Forty per cent of the workers queried said no. More than half of the workers who were union members said no. Nearly one third of the professional workers and executives agreed.

If it is true that so many of our workers do not recognize the relation between hard work and advancement, it is difficult to believe that they would have much confidence in any rating form designed to measure their performance on the job. 

On the other hand, when 828 employees of the Northwestern Mutual Life Insurance Company were asked if they wanted an opportunity to talk over their rating reports with their supervisors, 790 (95 per cent) accepted. A similar finding has been reported by the Southern California Edison Company in connection with a recent attitude survey. One question asked was "What do you think of periodic private discussions with your supervisor to talk over your interests, ambitions, opportunities and how you are doing on your job?” more than 5,000 replies were received and they were distributed as follows:

Other criticisms have been made of rating programs. It has been said that they are very subjective, and certainly many of the traits which are rated do not lend themselves to objective measurement. Hard feelings and grievances have grown out of merit rating reports. In some instances, morale undoubtedly has been lowered. The usual rating form calls for the evaluation of many different traits or characteristics, and the idea that the individual can be broken up into different pieces is, according to the critics, bad psychology. Many union leaders question the fairness of wage differentials based on rating reports for persons doing the same job. And the union is likely to take an uncompromising stand if a member's job is threatened by a low merit rating. 

Still another criticism is that ratings add to the paper work that the supervisor is required to do. The average supervisor, this argument runs, resists paper work and might better spend his time attending to more important responsibilities. 

Finally, many criticisms have been directed to the difficulties of arriving at an exact description of a person's performance. The New Yorker magazine, for example, tells of a mother who was upset when her young daughter brought home a school report marked "Unsatisfactory plus." Later, by dint of great effort the little girl raised her report to "Satisfactory minus," and the mother was overjoyed.

Such exercises in semantics, the critics say, may be tolerated at the juvenile level, but they are out of place with adults. 

Summarizing, it is clear that there are basic differences of opinion as to the values of rating programs in industry. If the purposes of a rating program are clearly stated and well understood, if a form appropriate to the organization is developed, and if those who are to use the form are well trained, some of the objections can be overcome (National Industrial Conference Board, Appraisal of Job Performance, Study No. 121, New York, 1951, pp. 4–5).

APPENDIX D. 

NEW YORK STATE CIVIL SERVICE PERFORMANCE RATING RULES. 

RULE I. DEFINITION OF TERMS. 

When used in these rules: 

1. The term "commission" or "state commission" shall mean the State Civil Service Commission. 

2. The term "agency" shall include all departmental agencies of the State Government, whether known as departments, boards, commissions or otherwise. In accordance with Civil Service Rule 

XIV, Section 5, the provisions of these service record rules and regulations for the State service shall apply similarly to those offices, places and employment in the state courts, and in the county service, to which the State Civil Service Rules and Regulations have been extended. 

3. The term "head of an agency" shall mean that person or group of persons responsible for personnel administration in the agency and working with the Commissioner and the Civil Service Department or its representatives in the establishment and maintenance of work performance standards, and the administration of service rating program of the agency in accordance with the Civil 

Service Law, Rules and Regulations. 

4. The term "Service Rating Board" or "Board" shall mean that group of persons within an agency designated to review the employees' performance ratings and to hear appeals from such ratings. 

5. The term "employee" shall mean a person in the competitive, the non-competitive, or the Labor group of the exempt class holding a position allocated to a salary grade included in Section 40 of the Civil Service Law, or a person holding a competitive class position not allocated to a salary grade in Section 40 of the Civil Service Law. 

6. The term "service rating" shall mean the rating resulting from the written evaluation of an employee's work performance by his supervisor or supervisors.

7. The term "work performance standards" shall mean such guides and objective standards of quality, quantity, and other pertinent job factors to be used by supervisors in evaluating the work of employees under their direction, as approved by the State Commission.

RULE II. RESPONSIBILITY FOR RATING OF EMPLOYEES. 

1. Each agency shall make, maintain a record of, and furnish to the Department of Civil Service at such times and in the manner and form as prescribed or approved by such Department a service rating for each employee who will have completed three months continuous service as of the last day of the calendar year. 

RULE III. SERVICE RATING BOARD. 

A. Membership. 

1. There shall be established in each agency a service rating board consisting of not less than three (3) and not more than five (5) employees with permanent status at least two (2) of whom shall be in the competitive class. 

2. The members of this board shall be appointed by the head of the agency or, if he so authorizes, shall be elected by the employees of the agency for a term specified by the head of the agency. 

3. The head of the agency shall notify the Department of Civil 

Service of the names of the persons appointed or elected to serve as the agency's service rating board. 

4. The head of the agency may designate the chairman of the board or, if he so authorizes, the Board shall elect one of its own members to serve as chairman. 

5. The board shall elect one of its own members to serve as Secretary.

B. Powers and Duties. 

1. The service rating board of each agency subject to the approval of the Commission shall establish and maintain as far as practicable uniform service ratings standards and procedures within the agency. 

2. The board, subject to the approval of the head of the agency and the Commission, shall establish service rating appeal procedures for the agency.

[Mar. 

3. The board shall notify each employee, in writing, of his service rating, at the same time advising him of his right to appeal. 

4. The board, in accordance with the service rating appeal procedures of the agency, shall make a decision on each service rating appeal and advise each employee appealing his rating of the decision of the board within ten (10) days after the decision of the board has been made. Except as otherwise provided, this decision shall be final. 

5. The Secretary of the service rating board of each agency shall report each appeal from a rating of "Unsatisfactory" and the decision thereon to the Commission as soon as practicable thereafter. 

RULE IV. STANDARDS OF PERFORMANCE AND ATTENDANCE. 

1. Each employee shall be rated on the quality, quantity and other pertinent job factors of his work considered suitable for determining the manner in which he performs the duties and carries out the responsibilities of the position which he occupies. 

2. To provide a uniform and equitable basis for rating its employees, each agency shall establish work performance guides and attendance standards, with similar guides and standards being used in evaluating the work of those employees performing the same or similar duties assigned the same or similar responsibilities. 

3. Work performance guides and attendance standards shall be subject to the review and approval of the Commission in the interests of uniformity and equity.

RULE V. EXPRESSION OF RATINGS ADJECTIVE OR NUMERICAL. 

1. Each employee shall receive a service rating expressed in figures from 1 to 100 or in adjective words. If expressed in adjective words, each adjective word shall be converted by the Department of Civil Service to a numerical rating for use in promotion examinations and in determining eligibility for salary increments. The conversion of adjective words to numerical ratings shall be uniform for all agencies.

RULE VI. APPLICATION OF SERVICE RATINGS. 

1. Service ratings shall be given consideration in promotion examinations providing the ratings reported are acceptable to the

1959.] 109 

Department of Civil Service in accordance with the provisions of 

Rule IX of these rules. 

2. Service ratings shall be used in determining an employee's eligibility for salary increments in accordance with section 41 of the 

Civil Service Law. 

3. Service ratings may be used for such other purposes as may be prescribed by statute or by regulation of the Commission. 

RULE VII. UNIFORMITY OF RULES, REGULATIONS AND STANDARDS. 

Rules, regulations and standards pertaining to and used in determining employees' service ratings subject to the approval of the Commission shall be uniform for each agency insofar as practicable. 

RULE VIII. RIGHT OF EMPLOYEE TO BE INFORMED. 

1. Each employee shall be shown the component parts of his service rating by his supervisor as soon as practicable after rating is made and shall be given an opportunity to review with his supervisor such component parts. 

2. Each employee shall have the right to be informed of rules, regulations and standards used in determining his own individual rating.

RULE IX. INVESTIGATIONS. 

1. The Department of Civil Service may refuse to accept service ratings of any agency where the reported ratings indicate that the work performance of employees has not been properly determined, recorded and reported or reports of exceptional or unsatisfactory performance or unsatisfactory attendance are inadequate. The 

Commission or the Department of Civil Service may make investigations of any matters touching upon the service record ratings of any agency.

RULE X. RATING PERIOD. 

1. Insofar as it is practicable, the ratings of each agency shall cover the calendar year.

[Mar. 1959. 

RULE XI. UNSATISFACTORY SERVICE RATINGS. 

1. An adjective rating of “Unsatisfactory" or a numerical rating of less than 75 shall be considered an unsatisfactory service rating. 

2. The head of each agency shall report to the Commission the name, title and salary of each employee in the agency who shall receive an unsatisfactory service rating in the manner and at such times as are designated by the Commission. 

3. Each unsatisfactory service rating issued by an agency shall include written substantiation of the rating and shall be subject to review by the Commission. 

4. Each employee who shall receive an unsatisfactory service rating from his agency shall receive an account of his unsatisfactory performance from the Commission upon his request and he may appeal such rating to the Commission within 30 days after receipt of the Commission's notice that his rating has been filed with the Commission. 

5. The Commission shall conduct such interviews, investigations or hearings as it deems necessary before arriving at a final decision on an unsatisfactory service rating. The Commission shall determine and notify the employee and the agency of its decision on such appeal.

RULE XII. EXCEPTIONS. 

1. The Commission may exempt an agency from these rules providing the agency formally applies for such exemption and there exists in the agency, in the opinion of the Commission, a formalized service rating plan accompanied by appropriate regulations for its maintenance and operation by which the agency may satisfy its statutory obligations as outlined in Section 46 of the Civil Service Law.
