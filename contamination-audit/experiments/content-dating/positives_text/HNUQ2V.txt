TRAILS OF TWO TRAVELERS

CHAPTER I TRAILS OF TWO TRAVELERS 

The trail began June 6, from Plant City, and led to New York, on across the ocean, through the British Isles, and across Southern Europe. Never was weather so warm as it was from Plant City to the mountains of 

North Carolina, except in Southern Italy. From North

Carolina until Rome coats were needed every day. 

This was true even in Paris in August. Rain also was to be reckoned with each day, for tickets not only to cities, but for meals in hotels, on trains, to art galleries, and museums had been paid for in advance. These tickets were good for a definite day. Notwithstanding weather conditions the trail must be followed. 

A brief stop was made at Hotel Holley, New York City, named in honor of the man who is famous for Bessemer steel, then on to the Cunard docks to the steamship Berengaria.

Going Tourist, we had that night our only view of the elegant first class cabins, lovely with flowers, and suites for royalty. The tourist cabin was thoroughly comfortable and pleasant. The tourist passengers were chiefly college groups. Deck sports, dancing, community singing enlivened the voyage. Tourist deck was so popular with other passengers that repeatedly they had to be [9]

ordered back where they belonged. The gaiety of youth always attracts. No passing between cabins was permitted.

Crossing the ocean was calm, lights on the water were lovely. At first sea gulls, sea moss, then “water, water, everywhere." Down in the engine room were machines at work making out of salt water many "drops to drink."

Few ships in sight, until the white coast of England was near. Then craft of all kind. Some so small that a little girl wished to take them from the ocean, for play things. Bird families sailed by, tiny baby birds looking like toys, with their mothers. Osborne House, now a home for disabled soldiers, the Isle of Wight, Cowles of Regatta fame, then Southhampton, where we landed. 

On our return in September the ocean was quite rough. Dark clouds, cold winds, and rain every day.

On the eighth day out, when the end of the tour was near, as sea moss was all around us, sea gulls sailing by and an occasional sail in sight, we were told to go and look for the lost plane "Old Glory." Up toward the cold, stormy north we sailed. Passengers, shivering altho' wrapped in many blankets were forced to remain on deck by ship air, food air, and the constant smoking of men and women. High were the waves, and strong, sometimes dashing open port holes. And all in vain was our search for "Old Glory."

As I stood on deck looking as far as eye could see at the waves lashing themselves in fury, Byron's apostrophe to the ocean came into my mind: [10]

"Roll on thou deep and dark blue ocean, roll”; and then the words of an old poem, "Isn't God upon the water, just the same as on the land?”

Next a vision came to me. I stood, a white aproned child, by the side of my mother as she sang, "Rocked in the cradle of the deep, I lay me down in peace to sleep."

In my heart there was the perfect trust that comes from security in the Everlasting Arms. 

[11]

CHAPTER II THE HIGHLANDS 

Landing at Southhampton not far from New Forest, the hunting ground of kingly oppressor and in easy reach of Stonehedge monument and altar, we were soon on the London Express. We were thrilled at our first sight of English hedges, low roofed cottages, sturdy flowers blooming by the railroad tracks. Daisies, poppies, yellow furze and acres of purple rhododendrum in well kept forests. The estate of Empress Eugenia, last of French Empresses, was noted. Soon the towers of London were in sight. With a recent poet I finished "Going up to London" "Halting on a tall hill 

Pied with purple flowers,

Twenty turrets I shall count, And twice as many towers 

Count them on my finger tip 

As I used to do 

And half a hundred spires 

Pricking toward the blue" "Sing you rhymes, and ring, you chimes 

And swing you bells of Bow! 

When I go up to London 

All the world shall know." [12]

A tour of London was to come later, hence the next morning we were off for Scotland. 

Passing through Nottingham, we thought of Sherwood Forest, Robin Hood and his merry men. Every hotel showed the Nottingham lace curtains made here. 

Carlisle, the ancestral home of Woodrow Wilson and

Thomas Carlyle seemed to open to us the gates of Scotland, as it is near the border. The land was more mountainous, rock walls instead of green hedges, as in Italy. 

In Holland and Belgium the holdings are not divided. 

Every inch of ground is cultivated. In Ireland both green hedges and rock walls are used to separate fields. 

Shepherds coming down these mountains followed by sheep, and the sheep cotes, gave the landscape a 

Biblical air.

In Scotland our first stop was in Melrose. Melrose 

Abbey, a noble ruin, gives a burial place to the hearts of Robert Bruce, and the Black Douglas. Sir Walter

Scott, who has made all of the region famous, is buried in Dryburgh Abbey. These famous ruins can never be restored but will always be preserved by the English Government. A walk all along the banks of the Tweed, by a stone cottage with thatch roof, stopping to pick pink hawthorne, yellow broom, bluebells of Scotland, on across a stile through woods, on to the grave of the "Unknown Soldier," down the street of Melrose, made me feel a citizen of the land.

It was ten o'clock at night when I reached the inn, but broad day light. I sat alone to revel in memories of 

Sir Walter Scott's people; Dominie Sampson, Meg 

Merrilies, the old minstrel and others made me wel- [13]

come to their land.

The next day while we were driving over well swept roads to Scott's home, Abottsford, at one spot a party of red coated hunters dashed out of the woods. At another a little boy stood with his hand on the head of a sheep.

Abottsford is as the Wizard left it. We particularly liked his library, armory, pictures, the paschal lamb carved in wood after the stone lamb in Melrose.

In Edinburg our room overlooked the park beautiful with flowers, and with the clock of flowers, containing statues in marble of the writers of Scotland. There is a similar street near the statues of kings and statesmen. "Our Willie Gladstone" was affectionately mentioned. "Linlithgow Castle" a few miles out, burned by soldiers in the time of Bonnie Prince Charlie, contains the room in which Mary, Queen of Scots was born, and Margaret's tower from which Margaret Tudor watched in vain for King James' return from Flodden Field.

The strength, the immensity of this building surprised me. Some of the towers were seven stories.

Blackness Castle is partially preserved and was used during the late war for supplies for soldiers. The well kept estates with lodge gates, gates of forest backgrounds, led us to the steel bridge across the river Forth. 

This is one of the most famous bridges in the world. 

Edinburg means the Castle Hill, with the little dog cemetery, new war memorial building, the older buildings containing the old crown jewel room, rooms of armor, banqueting halls and chapel. It means "The narrow close," Grays "close," "Weary Awa close," [14]

"White Horse close," (We would say "alley," England "Lane.") Knox' memories in his old home near his church "Giles chapel." The homes of Hume and Burns. 

Not so far away, Holy Road Palace: often part of it occupied by the King and Queen of England. The old part is just as Mary Stuart, the last Queen of Scotland, left it, her room's floor stained with Rizzio's blood. To me it seemed a mockery that portraits of Queen Elizabeth and Henry Eighth hung in Queen Mary's room.

Next the castle are the ruins of the Abbey. Cromwell threw the bodies of royalty from this Abbey. Queen 

Victoria did all she could to replace them. 

Edinburg is a good modern city. Big hotels, government buildings, an immense Woolworth building. 

Woolworth has his stores all over Europe. 2. 

Our first Sunday in Scotland found us booked for Glasgow and the Trossachs. On a directed tour the dates must be met. Leaving our baggage in Glasgow in the only non-temperance hotel we were placed in while in the British Isles, we were soon in Aberfoyle at the inn "Nicol Giles Jarvis," named in memory of the good 

Bailie in "Rob Roy."

After a good dinner eaten in sight of quantities of well cooked meats, we climbed a ladder to get a seat in a coach. The driver wore knee breeches, scarlet coat, top boots. The four horses were big and fat, the blankets warm. [15]

The guide books said the Trossachs are seen at their best in a rain. We had nothing to desire as it rained all afternoon; showers varied by downpour. The tempestuous wind made it impossible to keep up an umbrella. We suffered much pleasure. I thought of the question in my Passport. "Who shall be notified in case of an emergency?" The time for notification seemed to be at hand.

The grandeur of mountains, fern and heather, trickling rills, sheep far from habitation, rock cottages tucked in between the mountains, made it all an unforgettable picture. Memory did her part. The Bruce, William Wallace, and their followers sprang into life. "Fair Ellen" rowed from Ellen's Island in Loch Katrine to meet the disguised James Fitz-James. Rhoderick 

Dhu whistled and "every blade of heather sprang into life, with warriors armed for strife." Passing Loch 

Katrine, Loch Lomond was reached. The majority remained in the inn which was soon filled with smoke puffed by men and women. 

I had noticed a trail marked "To Rob Roy's Can." I had a tryst to keep down this trail. I would see Die 

Vernon riding thro' the forest; I would see "Macgregor on his native heath" and hear him say to Nicol Jarvis, "Of me say what you please kinsman, but of my wife only what it becomes a friend to say and a husband to hear." I would even see the fiery Helen. The tryst was kept for book friends never fail. 

From the many islands of Loch Lomond we caught glimpses of magnificent estates. Night found us back in Glasgow. To my demand "show us the nearest [16]

church," the reply was "it is too late, we have church at six-thirty." I found this was the hour for evening services in England, also. 

Glasgow's shipyards, art galleries, university with its rock garden, and bowling green; the cathedral with its unique background, city of the dead on terraced hills, were all visited. This cathedral is one of the two left standing by Cromwell's army. Glasgow was willing to reform, but unwilling to burn her loved cathedral. 

From the steamship "The Vulture" on the river 

Clyde we waved farewell to Scotland. [17]

CHAPTER III IRELAND 

"Dead slow" read the signs on both banks of the

Clyde, giving us an opportunity to view the crafts of many nations in the harbor, and to note waving fields of grain. We were impressed with the modern air and progressive spirit of every thing, near Glasgow, the Chicago of Scotland. From the gentle flowing waters of the Clyde we were soon on the Irish sea. Here were tumultuous waves, spray lashing the deck, driving wind lifting the ship in its mighty arms, screaming with joy over its power. Passengers were driven from the deck, driven even into cabins to seek forgetfulness in sleep. 

We were told it stormed all night, tho' there was not a sign of it as we docked at Belfast. Hewett's man met us and Hewett saw us through Ireland as Lissone and Sons directed us from the Hook of Holland to Rome, where Pierontti took charge of us. Each rendered excellent service with never a moment's delay. No cause for anxiety, all as easy as going to Jacksonville.

From breakfast we were driven over Belfast in a driving rain. Belfast showed every sign of prosperity. 

Tobacco, and linen factories, the handsome government house adorned with much marble and many portraits, large department stores indicated a city of wealth. [18]

Beauty was not forgotten. In the very large conservatory were many flowers found in Florida, and more flowers entirely new to me, a lover of flowers, than I found elsewhere. 

From the highest hill near Belfast I looked afar and thought, "Who comes not hither ne'er shall know 

How beautiful the world below." "Sparkling water laughed to the hills that seemed to wed the sky."

Nor is religion forgotten as Belfast is justly proud of her cathedral, Romanesque in design. Two pillars were given by all the women named "Anne," one by the "Marys," and so for other names. This cathedral is a fine study for students of symbolism. Good schools are conveniently located.

In Edinburg we left our shopping until Saturday afternoon and found every shop closed. Maddening! 

In Glasgow signs of "Summer Sales" looked pleasing, but "Travelers requested not to come in the First Two 

Weeks," and "Travelers not admitted after eleven

A.M." looked discouraging. I left my indignant and high-minded daughter outside a shop while I plunged in regardless. I came out the proud possessor of three ties, and two pair of golf hose. The latter I gave as from 

Scotland. The recipients found "Made in England" on the marks on the hose. I inspected more closely after that, and in Paris turned down slippers made in 

America. I had not gone to Paris to buy things Amer- [19]

ican, altho' as a patriotic citizen I am sure we have the best. 

We saw the same warning to "travelers" in Belfast.

Our amused guide explained that did not mean tourists, but salesmen. In we went and revelled in the sight of linen of all degrees of superbness and seeing it kept us happy until we reached Dublin. 

The Irish Sea smiled at us, the River Liffey welcomed us as we sped Dublin way past low thatched houses, women with bags of peat, and peat boys, identified for us by an Irish woman sharing our compartment. As we passed Drogheda, she rehearsed the events of the Battle of Boyne, and comforted us when we were told that the customs officer was coming. What a nice Irish boy he was, and how modestly he turned his goodlooking head away as he gently put a part of one hand in our bag. Our Irish lady said that returning would not be so easy; on a platform we would go and show our wears. But we did not. Everywhere they saw "America" on our faces, and promptly checked us. This was noticeable in going into France when battles of words were raging with tourists of other nationalities. Just so, the waiters looked at us, and brought us the natural water of the land. Without question. Why? I do not know. 2We found Dublin in many respects a contrast to 

Belfast. Belfast was rich in buildings, factories, minerals, progressive. Dublin was poor in all save culture and an [20]

intense feeling of "For the Glory of God 

And the Honor of Ireland." 

The Russell Hotel, Dublin, opposite St. Stephens'

Green, furnished the very best food of any hotel on our route. The dinner menus were French. The landlord spoke of Irish patriotism, then said with big eyes full of tears, "but Madame we are so poor."

Ireland has ever been a fighting nation. Fighting England in pride. "A felon's cap is the noblest crown an 

Irish head can wear." Fighting each other. Dublin wore scars from the war of 1921-1922. Then the massive stone government buildings were wrecked, as were the post office and many similar buildings.

The government now uses buildings intended by England for college work for governmental purposes. 

We were in Dublin during the days when De Valera and his followers were refusing to take the Oath. Apprehension was in the air. There was a feeling that the oath meant peace and that Ireland must have. This feeling has won out under President Cosgrave's administration. 

The pity of it is that Mr. O'Higgins had to be the martyr. 

The flag floating over Free Ireland is white, green and yellow. White for purity, green for Ireland, yellow to show friendship for the North.

Free Ireland changed English names of streets, but left monuments erected by England. The monument to 

Nelson is notable.

The bridge across the Liffey is named for O'Connor and is wider than it is long. [21]

Dublin is interesting not only historically but from a literary standpoint. We passed the homes of Sir 

Thomas More, with a medallion of him as a marker, of Wilde, Campbell, Dean Swift and his Stella, and others. By schools of Bernard Shaw, McCormic, Campbell, Goldsmith, Trinity College, massive, old, and also old looking. Examinations were on for the finals, so we saw students everywhere on grounds and porches. 

Fighting Ireland is shown in St. Patrick's Cathedral as it contains tablets and monuments to men of every war. Here were also tablets in honor of Swift, Stella and the faithful servant McGee.

The great park contains what was the residence of the former viceroy; but now the Governor, a Dublin lawyer, lives in it. More interesting we found the magnificent trees, deer, and numerous cows in this park. 

Our guide stopped at the cemetery of his noble dead. 

His eyes showed grief as a few were too indifferent to visit this hallowed spot. We bowed with him in reverence by the resting place of Parnell, O'Connor and others.

Nowhere did we see such poverty as in Dublin. We found a few beggars in Genoa, none in Rome, Mussolini sees to that. But in Dublin! At night the streets were lined with mutilated men, women with unkempt looking hair, babies held out, all begging. Women trying to sell a few apples or oranges. Pathetic groups. Then dear little fresh faced girls saying, "Please buy a flower and I will pray God to bless you." The flowers were willingly bought as the prayer is always needed. Yet, there was another side. Great lines of well-dressed peo- [ 22 ]

ple going to theatres. In Royal Theatre, gilded boxes, soft chairs, much eating and drinking between acts. Men with hats on during the play, constantly going in and out during the acts. Theatre customs in London, Paris, Dublin seemed quite different. 3. 

In going from Dublin to Cork, Mr. and Mrs. D. shared our compartment. They had been in England "following the races." Mrs. D. was very supercilious regarding the "funniness" of Americans. After skirmishing an hour we agreed that all people were much alike. 

Tea and cakes are served at all hours on the trains. 

Our companions graciously invited us to share their tea. The little maid could not understand our refusal, and altho' it was ten in the morning she was more than surprised, when she asked us when would we have our tea, to be told we did not wish it at any time. 

Mr. D. knew his Ireland and pointed out to us the largest bogs, the best peat pits, where the battles had been fought, the destroyed bridges. He told us in England one must not say dogs, but " 'ounds," never hunters in red but "'unters in scarlet." "The 'unting scarlet be'ind the 'unting 'ounds." As a matter of fact we heard little cockney English. The guides prided themselves on "Oxford English."

We looked at times over vast moors, dull, dun and bare; the only interruption to the eye being an occasional stone ruin telling its mute story of monastery or castle of bygone ages. [23]

Then fern, heather, fox glove, furze, stood guarding rippling rills of unsurpassed beauty. The fields were divided by rock hedges chiefly. Few of these fields were under cultivation. 

A golf course caused Mr. D. to say one might play golf on Sunday, but one could not have a caddy. Lost balls made profitable Mondays for caddies.

Reaching Cork we seemed in a new world. The coach which took us to our hotel was primitive, "jaunting cars," two wheels and horse-drawn. Narrow steep streets, women in long blue cotton dresses with black shawls over their heads, yet the ever-present American 

Express, Thomas Cook, Woolworth and Ford signs.

Cork is enshrined in song and saga, set in a beautiful valley of a romantic river. This city by the Lee is the capital of the rich province of Munster. While it preserves the characteristics of an old Irish town, there is much suggestive of foreign scenes, so picturesque its aspect. The houses seem built on terraces, like Naples, and the Lee- "Laughs to the hills that seem to meet the sky."

A fair was in progress, streets crowded with laughing folks with arms full of broom and other flowers.

As I looked down on it all, I thought of Browning's lines: "I leaned out of window for fresh air 

There came a hurry of feet, little feet, Flower o' the broom 

Take away love and our earth is a tomb."

We remember with delight the swans on St. Vincent's, [24]

our stop in St. Mary's Cathedral and our ride on top of the bus to Blackrock Castle. From the elevation we looked down on lawns of emerald green, studded with plants of almost tropical luxuriance and overpowering fragrance. Never were such roses, and lilies! The event however, was our stop at Shandon Church. There the bell ringer took us up into the tower and after playing on his bells many Irish airs, recited for us one verse of 

Father Prouts', "The Bells of Shandon." "With deep affection and recollection

I often think of those Shandon bells 

Whose sound so wild would, in the days of childhood 

Fling 'round my cradle their magic spells.

On this I ponder, where'er I wander, And thus grow fonder, sweet Cork, of thee. 

With thy bells of Shandon, that sound so grand

Over the pleasant water of the river Lee." 4. 

Our firm determination in going to Cork was to have the experience of "bargaining for a jaunting car" to go to Blarney Castle. The persistent rain forced us into a closed motor car.

Going the long way we found ourselves in the Ireland of "Castle Rackrent." Houses with the oldest thatched roofs and dirt floor needed only the evicted tenant to make the scene complete. Then again we were in the days of Cromwell "able, straight-forward and cruel." [25]

Blarney Castle is famous on account of the words of the old song, "The groves of Blarney they are so charming"; and the marvelous qualities of the famous stone. He who kisses the stone has ever the fluent and persuasive tongue. In order to make the kissing as difficult as possible the stone is a few feet below the battlements which only the daring can reach. "Did you kiss the stone?" we asked a number of Boston people. "No, we did not have any one to hold our feet." "Did you kiss the stone?" we inquired of two girls going

Armstrong Tour alone and whom we continually met. "Yes, we had seven people to hold us." Not having their holder we came back free from "blarney," speaking still words "honest and true."

This castle was erected in the fifteenth century by 

Cormac Mac Carthy, the "Laider" whose ancestors were chieftains in Ireland before the English invasion. 

Part of the castle has been completely destroyed and thick ivy covers the ground where once its massive walls stood. Never were there such acres of ivy save in 

Heidelberg. The Girl of My Affections had been searching Ireland for an ancestral home of her dreams. 

Perched on the highest tower she viewed the ivy covered castle, the noble trees, the Shournagh, the vast blue mountains in the distance, dreaming of the long ago and saw. "A thousand vassals mustered 'round 

With horse and hawk, to horn and hound."

Poets and painters have exhausted the resources of their art in the attempt to depict the beauty of the lakes, [26]

and all that combine to make Killarney; including the ancient stronghold of the O'Donaghues, "Sweet Innis fallen" of Moore fame, St. Finnan Abbey, Muckross

Abbey, "The Eagle's Nest" with its echo celebrated by 

Tennyson's "Heaven's" reflects "Killarney." [27]

CHAPTER IV LONDON IS A MAN'S TOWN 

Our first Sabbath in London dawned dark and drizzly, as did every other day of our stay in England. 

An early walk impressed the old quotation on our minds, for "London is a man's town." Strength, massiveness were shown in everything, except a few automobiles, which were so low they seemed built for small children.

As a protection against germs we were to find in Southern lands, but did not find, we stopped for an inoculation at the home of a London surgeon who had studied at Johns Hopkins. He asked if my ever present 

D. A. R. pin was a Rotary pin. "No," I replied, "this pin means that in the Revolutionary war my ancestors fought on the side that whipped your ancestors." "Oh," he said, "you would not say 'whipped.' Not 'whipped' when a child outgrows a mother in strength."

Then the Girl-who-is-always-kind, gently murmured, "America owes much to England which she can never forget" and the gentle voice trailed on until when we left, the surgeon told his neat looking maid to "show our friends our garden." The surgeon stood high in our estimation after seeing the garden as it was de- [28]

signed to give happiness to a child, as well as to produce gorgeous roses.

After breakfast―. But to digress, all meals in the 

British Isles proved that England is a man's land. Such heavy meals, so many courses, so much meat, and boiled cabbage always, fish three times a day, fruits, puddings, everything! One might order beer or wine. "Temperance Hotel" seemed to mean that there was not a bar in the hotel, as there was in our Glasgow hotel. As soon as the big, energetic waiters in dress suits and immaculate linen had finished serving these many course meals, they were busy serving "teas" and cakes in the lounge to many of the same eaters. 

All hotels were well kept. In Scotland, Ireland, Italy, France, industrious maids were forever on their knees mopping stairs, halls, or scrubbing brass knobs.

Keys to rooms were left at hotel desks, in every land except Italy. There you were told to hang your key over your door, and if you did not, you certainly found it there. Was this a Mussolini order?

After breakfast, in a downpour we started for worship in Westminster Abbey. As we sat quietly listening to an exposition of the litany we had time to study room and audience. The latter seemed tourist, the wonderful statues of men famous in war and peace seemed the only Englishmen present. These statues are splendid monuments of the great and heroic. The tones of the mighty organ filled the building, now rising in thunderous triumph, then sinking into notes as soft as the gentlest zephyr. The voices of the choir boys, pure and sweet mingling with organ strains, wafted the souls of [29]

listeners heavenward. It was well that we spent some time this first morning in the Abbey for in Britain's Valhalla the tourist is not given right of way. Once the fact that boys were having a service in the building closed it to us, again a funeral, again a wedding. The building seems democratic. I read that an unusually popular policeman was buried from the Abbey. 

The Chapel of good King Henry VII is especially famous for elaborate sculpture; the "fretted roof is achieved with wonderful minuteness and the airy security of cobwebs." Elizabeth, and the beautiful Mary

Stuart, Queen of Scots, are buried in this chapel, proving Death to be a great equalizer. Here are the lofty stalls of the Order of the Knights of the Bath, reminding us of the Chapel of the Thistle in St. Giles, Edinburg.

In the Poets' Corner while Shakespeare and Addison have statues erected to their honor, the greater number of poets have busts, medallions, inscriptions. Longfellow's bust recognizes his place as America's chief poet, as he is the only American so honored. St. Gaudens' statue of Lincoln stands on the outside of Westminster Abbey but nearer St. Margaret's chapel. Wandering through the vast corridor, everywhere we caught glimpses of quaint effigies of kings, queens, warriors in armor, nobles in robes and coronets, statesmen, prelates with croziers and miters. We paused to look long at the great oak coronation chair with the stone of Scone underneath the seat. It was the stone of Coronation in Scotland. Was it the stone on which Jacob laid his head at 

Bethel the night he saw the Heavenly ladder? The ex- [ 30 ]

tension of this greatest of England's cathedrals is dwarfed by the lofty pinnacle of the Houses of Parliament and is the imperfect conception of "Gothic humour" as Wren, himself, termed it.

In front of the Abbey stands a lofty pillar in memory of "the boys of the Crimean war.” On a later day, when intending to revisit the Abbey we stood near a crowd eagerly waiting the exit of a wedding party from St. 

Margaret's chapel.

And again we came to visit the Houses of Parliament to see the meeting place of the Commons, which would seem regal were it not for the truly regal House of Lords. Both were smaller rooms than we expected to see. No figure of the past seemed more regal to us than that of the Jew who became England's prime minister, who had declared "Though I sit down now, the time will come when you will hear me," who here trod the road from the "Hell of Ridicule to the Heaven of Success."

Not being very well acquainted with palaces, in a palace-contest we would have pinned the blue ribbon on the House of Parliament, supposing it was a palace. 

This entire district is the city of Westminster with its own government.

We gave the afternoon over to seeing the parks, St. 

James, Kensington Garden, Hyde Park, all beflowered and magnificent with oak trees. Labor was having a mammoth parade. Every organization was represented. The ever ready policeman saw us safely through one gate as this procession entered Hyde Park. Here every man speaks his mind. Many mighty questions are openly [31]

aired here at the pleasure of the orators, the policemen keeping kindly guard over all. We felt really in 

England. 

After listening for as many moments as a tourist dared pause, we saw the cemetery for Royal hounds and walked down the famous Rotten Row, preferring to think of it as Route du Roi, then on to Kensington Garden to worship at the shrine of the boy who never grew up, "Peter Pan." The statue with the woodland folk at the base looks just as we would like it to look- 

Peter Pan. 

Back to Piccadilly Circus, on to Holborn Circus. Such queer names for centers of streets. Not shows of any kind, merely street circles.

It was Sunday, still. Where should a dyed-in-the-wool 

Baptist end this day in London? In Spurgeon's Tabernacle. To this large, plain, wide-stepped brick building I went with a worshipping Elizabeth. White haired ushers made us welcome, and we were given seats with a name label; which fact we remember as a boy near us kept telling us the seats belonged to some one else, who fortunately failed to come. With orchestra on first floor, choir, pastor and elder, choir leader on level with first balcony and a well filled second gallery, we felt amid new scenes in the Lord's house. This fact was emphasized by a heart-felt sermon from Dr. Childers on "No other name." 

To the hotel for 8 o'clock dinner; and then, to quote

Samuel Pepys, the gossiping old English writer-to bed. [32]

CHAPTER V OF CASTLES 

Our destination after Oxford was Warwick and Kenilworth Castle. These castles are of two distinct types. Warwick is one of the few fortress castles in 

England that is well preserved, lived in by the owner and showing English life of many periods. A rich 

American once rented this castle for a number of years. 

In some respects it is too much like a small museum for one to feel quite at home in it. 

Kenilworth is an immense ruin since Cromwell's time. Of Cromwell Milton wrote: "To peace and truth thy glorious way hast ploughed."

The time came when he presented this estate as a gift to Henry the Eighth, and rode away on his mule, never to return. This was the most costly gift ever made by subject to a king, but the king would have taken the palace had it not been offered.

CHAPTER VII THE STORY OF A LOST PASSPORT 

On the last day of our stay in London we were free to go as we desired. I planned a regular orgy of Literary 

Shrines. To Chelsea in a taxi, we went to Carlyle House. 

To me there are few writers who compare with Thomas

Carlyle, and few women I admire as I do his Jeanne. 

A small admission gave right of way in this home of theirs. I revelled in Carlyle mementoes, portraits, her letters-such friendly letters; his letters giving a kindlier Carlyle, his big bed, her little bed, his big and warm dressing gown and even his old slippers. 

Into the narrow flower garden we looked. Flowers in corner of grass plots, roses, thyme, daisies, old flowers; rock walls all flower-bordered. 

A sudden thought, "Gray, where is my bag?" A thorough search of house made it evident that the bag had been left in the taxi.

To a long row of waiting taxis, all examined. To the nearest police station. Such interest and attention! Two policemen at 'phones, so untiring in effort. A description of bag and contents 'phoned Scotland yard. The yard replied that lost articles came in 100 percent after twenty-four hours. But the Armstrong Tour was leaving

London in six hours. The bag contained not only all [49]

my money but my passport. That same morning I had taken money and passport from the hotel safe, where I had placed them, being in the habit of losing my possessions.

As all my expenses were paid, and Gray-girl had her own money, I could go on with the party, except for loss of passport. Abroad, or at home, you can no more enter the country without a passport than one can enter heaven without the necessary credentials. The policeman suggested the American embassy for temporary passport. The ambassador, himself, saw us, sympathetically concerned, and phoned the consulate to keep open until we reached there, as only consuls do passport business.

It was by this time after 12 on Saturday. Consulate closed except to us. I knew nothing about my passport except that I got it through Tampa, but the date and number? "No." Now, if the cabman found my bag, it was safe, if a passenger found it, who could tell?

A cable to America was arranged for. The consul did everything except accept an A. B. A. check in payment of cable. Dramatically he said it was not permitted. On my banker's investigation after our return to America,

I found he was correct. Neither A. B. A., nor American 

Express checks can be accepted at the consulate. A wild dash was made to Cook's and to a bank, all closed, as it was after 12 o'clock on Saturday. To Hotel Bonnington for money, back to the consulate, then to Scotland yard, hoping the cabman might have already reported. This place is as imposing as a palace, as it was the former residence of Scotland's Kings when in [50]

England. Mounted police, street police, police at doors. 

We waited while one man told of money stolen from him, while he slept in a park, another of his watch "filched" as he stood in a crowd. Fagin was evidently still in the land. Both were put aside, "Ladies first." 

We were sent to the "Lost and Found Department," of Scotland yard, many miles away, where we were told that at 1 o'clock Monday the bag would doubtless be in. 

And it was.

There were moments of excitement when I nonchalantly announced at two to the Armstrong party, that having lost my passport I would remain alone in London. Miss Ashton was relieved to find I had done everything she could suggest. “Have you any money?" "Yes, I give you my daughter. I keep her money."

Polly-of-the-Great-Heart took charge of the Girl. 

Glorious roses came to my room from Chattanooga friends, and a book from another.

My Monday morning companion at the table was a young Chicagoan who had been in France a year. The bag might be found, it might not. From street hawkers we bought United States flags and saw many wearing them. [51]

CHAPTER VIII CROSSING THE CHANNEL

I paid in being practically foodless until noon the next day.

A young Scotchman and myself were the only pleasant people in this crowded coach. He might not have been so pleasant, as he told me while I had found courtesy all over the British Isle that happy condition would end when I reached the Continent. I informed him that

An elderly man entered the compartment and said to an elderly woman: "Madam you have my seat."

Madam kept it. "Madam, you have my seat, and I request it." Madam sat still. "Madam, you have my seat, and I demand it." Madam left the compartment. 

A young woman insisted upon smoking and desired to keep a window up. A young man not smoking, determined to keep it down.

The Scotchman was pleased that I liked his land, yet had never seen educational and historical Scotland. 

From the train to a boat on the North Sea. 

That particular night the company was short one boat, hence I was in a milling sea of humanity, fighting for seats and berths. My reservation was for July 2, not good for July 4; sitting up all night was before me.

While he explained my book of tickets left with him by Miss Ashton, his clerk drew circles of money and wrote the value in each circle. At one moment Mr. Lissone looked at me for a moment and said, "Did your daughter want to leave you?" 'No," I replied, "she cried. I made her go. 

I always make my children do what I wish them to do." 

I am sure his opinion of my capability increased.

Moody?" I did not recognize the lovely blonde lady speaking and just then we were off.

I was surprised to see so many canals in Amsterdam. 

In Venice the canals are the only medium of traffic; in 

Amsterdam large trees, streets and sidewalks are on both sides of the canals, 500 bridges across them. Our guide did his duty. We were carried across every one of these 500 bridges.

The water of these canals is stagnant, being forced in from the sea, not running through the land. Many flat boats for commerce are used. I particularly noticed flower barges full of cut flowers and potted plants. 

At one point we stopped in front of a tower, called "The Weeper's Tower." Here sailor wives shaded anxious eyes looking seaward for delayed sailors, who might never return. Nieurve Kerk, or St. Catherine's, in which the sovereigns are crowned, with ancient stained glass windows, one modern window commemorates the taking of the oath by Queen Wilhelmina, and the Ryks museum, the greatest in Holland, were noted.

CHAPTER IX A DAY IN BRUSSELS AND WATERLOO 

The motor busses of all lands looked about the same. 

The guides different. Our Brussels guide was a rosy faced boy about fourteen, well groomed and manly. He was guiding to practice speaking English. Seldom did he hesitate for words, always self possessed, though evidently embarrassed.

The Grande Place is the center of city interest. In this quadrangle, our first stop was made by the ever present flower market. The market women with long billowing skirts and queer bonnets would have inspired a Van Dyck or a Rembrandt. Everywhere we saw the peasant women wearing long full skirts, rolling wheel barrows, carrying immense baskets, drawing water and once pulling a boat. The Grande Place is the finest square in 

Europe and the Hotel de Ville, the gem of the place, not even equalled by the palace of any of the numerous guildhouses completing the quadrangle.

The guild halls high towered, woodwork carved and gilded seemed more like palaces than halls of trade. 

Here the merchants, printers, bakers, butchers, brewers had their headquarters. The further evidence of the importance of trade in Belgium is shown in a cathedral, Notre Dame Du Sablon, built by the Medieval guilds. [63]

The building has 48 bronze figures on top of the stone railing, representing practically every trade of the long ago. The same idea is carried out in a Florentine cathedral.

Architectural features were pointed out, especially the guild building with ship design on the top, and history was quoted by the remarkable boy. How Belgium had been oppressed by Austria, Spain and France. 

How from the palace on this square the cruel Spanish 

Duke Alva, looked down on the murder of the Flemish nobles and on the martydom of the good Egmont, the patriotic Hoorn, Lamoral and the noble Montmorency. "See, there is the statue to these patriots." How Brussels led in driving out Spain and in the crowning of Belgium's King, in 1831. The flying flag showed that the king was in his palace. This closed it to tourists. 

From the "old town" by devious ways winding up and on, by terraced gardens-Belgium is low but there are elevations-by a park surrounded by trees, cut and trained to resemble vines more than trees, trained as are the trees bordering Queen Mary's walk in Hampton Court, and trees in a part of the Luxemburg garden. To me the trees seemed mutilated and I could not admire them.

Our destination was the Cathedral of Sainte Gudulfe and St. Michael, towered and Gothic; noted for its carved pulpit representing the Expulsion from Pardue, superb stained-glass windows and rare old tombs. 

Down wide streets to the Palais de Justice, covering a greater area than St. Peter's at Rome, costing nearly five million dollars. The high domed building is superb, [64]

and from its elevated site fine views of the lower town are obtained. Persistent sellers of post cards, chiefly grown men and women, gave one little time for anything except their wares.

A stop at a lace factory to see the women making by hand, lace like cobwebs. We were permitted to see specimens of lace. Handkerchiefs, collars and bridal veils, all wonderfully beautiful, hence very expensive-yet about half what one would pay in America. 

A stop was made at the National shooting gallery, to see the spot where Edith Cavell was shot, and many others besides this English nurse. In a tiny garden an iron disc in the ground marks the spot where all the martyrs sat when the fatal shots were fired. Edith 

Cavell postcards were on sale. The picture showing 

Edith to be a mature woman. Not so La Petite Gabrielli, a Belgian girl, likewise shot as a spy. We had passed the winsome statue of this Belgian maiden, as well as the famous statue of the Mannekin. This statue of the lost two year old boy, is endowed, and loved, even beyond the statues of King Leopold and others. 

The only museum we visited here was the Wiertz Museum, containing fantastic compositions of a "mad painter Wiertz." Those who were objectionable to him, Wiertz placed in hell. He so placed Napoleon, and a pope. He looks down on both, from the heavenly group. 

There was a touch of human kindness in him, shown in his animal pictures. A dog in the kennel we all loved. 

On returning to the hotel we found the dining rooms crowded with men from the stock exchange. We did not object to the delay in service, as it gave us an oppor- [65]

tunity to observe types. Flemish and Walloon seemed in evidence, as blonde and dark heads were observed.

America owes much to the Walloons, as many of these people settled in our thirteen colonies.

We could not understand one word, as conversations and knives and forks flew rapidly. The men were all tall and some one said, "upstanding." I do not remember a thing we ate, except quantities of fruits of various kinds. The Anspach is a small hotel, good service, with curb service, also. 2 After lunch we were told we would be taken to art galleries or we were free to do as we pleased. Freedom was always the choice. To the majority it meant shops. 

To Elizabeth Hoover and myself it meant the battlefield of Waterloo.

Just how to get there, Nelle's beau had explained to Elizabeth. We soon found ourselves confused, and as the plaza in front of the Stock Exchange was jammed with citizens, soldiers and policemen, we tried our French impartially and were met with amused head shakes, as each man thrust another forward. A sign, "New York Herald," was just across the street. In that direction we started, when we saw Nelle and the beau. 

We followed them at a distance until we reached the right car.

Through many small villages we passed until we reached the Waterloo village. A man in uniform jumped [66]

on our slowly moving street car to extol his tea shop and museum. As we left the car a swarm of tea shop, cards, and determined souvenir people surrounded us, urging us to buy their wares. The women were especially insistent, "Americans rich, buy." It was like running a gauntlet.

Well, there was the famous battlefield, there was the mound, the giant lion made of French cannons on the top of the mound, and there were 256 steps leading to the top. The day was intensely warm. Up went the young people. Should I? With a mind wavering between going and not going I started up and was soon at the top. I passed many who were sitting on the steps, others gasping for breath, yet I remember a young couple, the father carrying a small child, who almost ran laughingly up.

This mound 200 feet high, was made by cart loads of earth, being hauled chiefly by women. Its sides are covered by wild flowers, small shrubs, vines, grass. Only two stone monuments are on the battlefield. Elizabeth historically studied the landscape, as she did the ghastly panorama at the foot of the mound, a panorama of lifesize men and horses, in every position of agony. 

After visualizing Victor Hugo's Waterloo as an immense letter "A," with Hugoumont, Mont St. Jean and La Belle Reliance, at each point of the A; after seeing Napoleon, Ney, the Old Guard and the approach of Blucher led by a cowboy; and listening to Elizabeth's historic dissertation, I gave myself over to feeding my soul on the beauty of the new, old world. As far as eye could see was waving grain, out to meet waving pop- [67]

lars. Near us daisies, poppies, corn flowers, other friendly flowers. Waterloo!

Returning to Brussels we drove by the Bois de la Cambre, the Bois de Boulogne of Brussels; by small cottages protected by neatly trimmed hedges. A bed of immense pink summer blooming begonias, a queer garden fountain of a donkey, braying; a flying Mercury bearing a torch of flowers, soft immense pink roses, gorgeous geraniums, made the drive a delight.

It was too late for anything except dinner and window shopping. Brussels is the Paris of Belgium. Its shops seen from the sidewalk seemed equal to the shops of the Rue de la Paix of Paris, in beauty and price. 

Many diamond factories are in Belgium, hence windows glitter with diamonds.

The best collection of instruments for studying the heavens, I saw in Brussels.

Never have I seen so many young and beautiful women wearing the heaviest of mourning; and never have I seen shop windows filled with such handsome and exclusive looking mourning gowns and bonnets. 

Occasionally we passed an old mother, swathed in black, and our hearts gave an extra throb of sorrow. 

The next morning we were given the regular Continental breakfast at five, as we were to leave at six for Cologne. The Continental breakfast of fruit, cold bread, butter and coffee or chocolate, we were to become accustomed to, during the remainder of our tour. 

Occasionally honey, or a jam, was added. The bread though cold, in the Netherlands and Germany and Switzerland we liked. We never did get accustomed to [68]

French bread;

and Italian bread was much like French bread. 

Even at the early hour of five our Lady of Flowers, Mrs. Burlback of Chattanooga, found time to run to the flower market and return with arms full of carnations. Generously distributed they were, and the remainder graced our tables in Cologne.

Even in leaving Brussels facts pursued us, the last being that the Rue d'Anspach down which we were driving was built over the Senne river.

Flanders Field, for devastation from the Great War.

Clusters of small houses built wall to wall, to economize in ground. A few of these houses extended into the hill side. Our lady from Texas settled herself comfortably, with a book, saying "Belgium is all right. She can care for herself." [69]

In the hotel in Cologne the first thing we noticed was cabinets for cologne; next, the German beds. In all the hotels of Germany and Switzerland, there were soft beds with many big soft pillows and much cover. 

Always across the foot of the bed was what seemed to us to be not a comforter but a small feather bed, extending about halfway the length of the bed. Our chairs were hidden each night by discarded pillows, bolsters and covers. The hotels everywhere were freshly papered. 

Often we could tell that there were or had been doors in the room, by the cuts in the paper. We wondered if the freshness of it all was a result of the Great War. 

Miss Ashton promptly took us to that marvel of Cologne, the Cathedral-the grandest Gothic building in the world—and then left us to the delight of exploring as we desired.

The tower of this Cathedral is 500 feet high. There are 7 bells, one of which is the largest and heaviest bell found anywhere. The interior is cross shaped, the shrine of the Three Kings of Cologne, adorned with gold and precious stones, is the chief treasure. The kings were the Wise Men Kaspar, Melchior and Balthazar, who came from the East to worship the baby Christ, and [70]

whose bones were brought from Milan by Barbarossa, the great emperor. Wherever we walked that afternoon, we came again to see this more than magnificent building, viewing it at last by moonlight, when it was truly a dream of beauty.

Cologne is the largest and most important Rhineland city, great even in the middle ages due to her trade. Her shops are the best in the world. The prices so very moderate, we pass windows with reluctance. Determined was I to buy cologne. Equally so was the Graygirl in her assurance that I had no use for it. Finally she relented and gave me a bottle. 

From modern streets we went down "tortuous, dark and narrow streets of the old town," seeing many relics of medieval greatness, of ancient fame, or infamy. In the cloister of St. Ursula is the tomb of that unhappy princess, who with her 11,000 maidens was massacred at Cologne, on her return from Rome.

Alone we stood on the great bridge across the Rhine.

CHAPTER XI VENETIAN DAYS 

Someone advises that Venice should first be approached by sea and at midday, so that the glory of this unparalleled city "throned upon her hundred isles" with golden spires, gray and silver domes, campaniles outlined gracefully against sky of blue, the soft coloring of old reds, rose and silver in buildings might bring to reality a dream of beauty.

Another suggests that the Bride of the Adriatic should first be seen by moonlight when Venice seems a fairy city between sea and sky. We reached Venice at night and in a driving rain. 

There was "musketry in Heaven" when we went from the train to the Canal and into waiting gondolas. 

Gondolas in poetry had sounded gay and picturesque. 

We were far from joyous as we trusted ourselves to the frail crafts, rocking on the water and with boatmen whose language we could not understand. Hoods over part of the boat protected us from the rain; our gondoliers skillfully took the waves and soon we were landed at the steps of an ancient palace, now a hotel. This building was rich in marble columns and stairways, in mosaics, pictures and tapestries. The Texas girl, always ready for adventure, and the Gray girl, not to be left [84]

behind, followed me out on a narrow bridge which led into complete blackness. We next tried narrow passages, rather than streets, leading by dimly lighted small shops. Not a soul was in sight and as the winding passages seemed a fitting place for a hidden hand and a stilleto, again we turned. If we had only known it we were about a five minute walk from St. Mark's 

Square, which is the center of Venetian life. 

As our room overlooked the Canal, we could hear the gentle waves beating against the walls, the call of boatmen, an occasional song. A pleasant memory of this room is an immense double-meshed mosquito net, hanging in voluminous folds from a ring near the ceiling, completely enveloping the bed. 

Our first excursion was to St. Mark's Church. As we entered the large square which is completely surrounded by old palaces-now shops, we faced St. Mark's. This square is full of pigeons fed once a day for 700 years by the state; and now many times a day, by tourists. 

The pigeons of St. Mark's are better known than the 

Church. The Church is low, of Byzantine architecture, and unique in every way. It is not a cathedral, for it was the private chapel of the Doge. You get from all these places only a deepened impression of what you take to it.

The building is of brick encrusted with colored marbles and precious stones, brought from the then known world. [85]

Venice was once a world power, her fleet her strength. Every ship was under command to return with beautiful gifts for St. Mark's. The many pillars are inlaid with porphyry, jasper and other semi-precious stones of various hues, and shelter five vaulted porches ceiled with mosaic, with fantastic sculpture of flowers, birds, grapes twined in endless profusion and over the central porch "the glowing steeds of brass" stand. Doge in 1204, taken to France by to France by Napoleon, returned to Venice in 1815. During the World 

War they were taken down and hidden. Near

Sarcophagus of Daniele Manin, last Liberator of Venice.

In this hall are frescoes by renowned artists, of the

before shrines, on sumptuous chapels, on the colored marble pulpit.

Ruskin emphasizes the fact that while figures of the "Mother of God" are seen continually, yet the Cross is first seen burning in the center of the Temple, and a figure of Christ hangs in the utmost height of every dome.

The fight between Savonarola and the Medici for the soul of Florence grew. CHAPTER XIII A SUNDAY MORNING IN ROME 

We reached Rome on Saturday afternoon, in time for a walk, and an evening at a band concert in one of the large parks. The size of the audience reminded us of Central Park, New York. Italians are music loving people. Their concerts are the very best.

The scene was modern, with no reminder that the remains of many cities might be under us, with a history running through nearly 27 centuries. Broadly speaking you find in Rome whatever you seek. You may drive over Rome in two hours, Dr. Whittinghill told me; and archeologists and historians say, you may study 

Rome three years and still not see Rome.

St. Peter's, the Forum, the Coliseum, are the three most prominent objects of interest for the tourists. We devoted our first day in Rome to the three, and incidentally fixed much of Rome in memory, as in time the Tiber, Hadrian's Tomb, the imposing palace of justice seen for the first time that morning, became familiar objects, as well as numerous fountains, bridges and the statue of Victor Emmanuel.

St. Peter's is the largest church in the world, but remember America is planning a larger. It is also the most imposing, I think. There is a gradual approach to [102]

the open piazzi, or square, containing the impressive semi-circular colonnade of marble pillars, the Egyptian obelisk and fountains. This obelisk has a history older than the church. It has witnessed heathen ceremonies, Christian tortures-and St. Peter's burial. 

The colonnade of pillars are like great arms of the basilica. Even on Sunday you were interrupted by sellers of post cards and pearls, nearly up to these steps. 

The guard in gorgeous uniform, the bronze doors, particularly the Holy Door, Porta Santa, never opened but once in twenty-five years, arrest your attention. Inside so gorgeous are the ornamentation of walls and columns that the vastness of the room is not noticed. 

It is said 1000 people would not be noticed in the building, that 50,000 could easily be accommodated. 

The virgin "released from the block of marble" by 

Michelangelo, the column from Solomon's Temple, the fresco showing St. Peter stopping Attilla by causing the cross to appear in the sky; the monument of 

Matilda of Tuscany, whom you met in Florence, the bronze statue of St. Peter with foot shining from much kissing, sitting by one of the piers supporting Michelangelo's great dome; the statue of St. Helena, Constantine's mother (Constantine founded St. Peter's), the handkerchief of St. Veronica "given by her to Christ to wipe the sweat from His brow as He bore the cross to Calvary and returned to her with the imprint of His face upon it"; the altar, the high boxed-in choir and the golden tomb of St. Peter, must be seen. The tomb is under the great dome in the center of the building. 

There are many mural paintings, and some wall pic- [103]

tures in mosaic, as fine as the most delicate painting. 

There is also the pier containing the relic of the true cross, which St. Helene brought to Rome. 

We were in the building at the hour of high mass. 

Few people could be seated, but hundreds stood looking at the gorgeously robed prelates as each served in turn, and listening to the choir responses, and the tones of the organ. One of the most beautiful in the world.

The tower disappeared years or centuries ago, the foundation remaining seems in memory to have been 30 or 40 feet deep and 90 or 100 feet in diameter, the bottom of this space contains shrubs, blooming oleanders and syringa, under which numerous cats walked, slept, or frolicked. They [105]

are fed meat by the city from butcher shops and by cat friends. Every stray cat is disposed of by being put în this space. When they become too numerous some are killed. We saw comparatively few cats. 

Our next stop was made at the most magnificent of Rome's fountains. It is in tiers, the clearest, coldest water gushing out of many spaces all flowing into an immense basin. Drop a piece of money into this basin and you will be sure to return to Rome, and you will always have good luck. The practical Girl thought it wisdom to remain in the shade of the carriage in comfort, but I am a venturer and seeker of good luck, so out into the scorching sun I darted and in my money went.

Small boys clustered around, watching for the coins, for which they dive.

Who has not pictured the Forum, the great meeting place of men who once upon a time framed laws that we have copied? In that hot July sun it looked prosaic. 

Tourists were aimlessly strolling around, families seeking a holiday diversion, children romping over pavement which dated years before Christ.

Slowly the Forum took form as we walked down the Sacred Way (Via Sacra). As we sat by the columns, marking the temple of Castor and Pollux, twin sons of Leda, whose wooing by Jupiter in the form of a swan, is celebrated in painting and marble, Elizabeth, with canny historical knowledge, made it all live again though she did say her Forum maps were not like this Forum.

The Sacred Way is twenty feet below the street and for centuries this entire space was covered by debris, [106]

over which finally grass and trees grew. The remains of a still older civilization are doubtless under the exposed Forum for there have been at least nine domes. 

The Forum is at the foot of the Palatine hill. On this hill Romulus lived when he invited the Sabines to come down from the Aventine Hill for games, in the space now occupied by the Forum. Here the Roman men in need of wives stole the Sabine women. In Florence on the piazzi of the Uffizi Galleries, a group marble, "The Rape of the Sabines," commemorates this event. 

The Sabines furnished the next king of Rome, Numa, the peace king. A mighty builder was Numa, as remains of his peace temple, "The Temple of Janus" stands. 

He wanted the Temple closed always, but Rome was so war-like only twice were the doors shut. Numa built the "Temple of Vesta," and under the guidance of Egeria, a goddess, instituted the order of the Vestal Virgins. Remains of their temple are well preserved as are portions of their house. In the temple the Sacred light must burn. They must be chaste. Should the light go out they were scourged, and if the vows of chastity were broken they were buried alive.

The small cells in which they were buried alive were found under the pavement. The Vestals came here at ten years of age and doubtless romped as were the twentieth century children doing that afternoon. It is said a flower garden was made for them. They had great influence politically, and some of their statues still stand near the ruins of their house. Such battered looking ladies! Numa also built a house for the Pontifex Maximus into which Julius Caesar was carried after his [107]

assassination. The remains of this house shows bath, bake and servants rooms, with much polished marble of great rarity.

The halls were paved with marble and porphyry. 

The floors were tessalated mosaic. Sand is kept over the mosaic, a boy with broom stands ready to sweep a space clear for you to see, for a lira or two. 

Part of the walls are covered with many layers of building material; the last, brick, stuccoed and crudely painted. These paintings are protected. The building runs back into the Palatine hill on which the royal palaces stood.

The ruler lived near the legal and religious life of his people.

The Via Sacra was lined with ruins of other temples of glittering marble, holes in the pavement showed where monuments once stood, the patches were of different dates. An ancient well was uncovered by the guide, the much decorated Arch of Severus shown, also remains of the aqueduct of August, the Tarpeian rock on the Capitoline not far away, the place where Virginia was killed by her father, (you have thrilled over the poem), the site of the rostra where Mark Antony, Cicero and many another orated. Then the Arch of Titus built by Titus in A. D. 70, with the labor of Jewish slaves after he had conquered Jerusalem.

On the Palatine Hill stands the House of Livia, mother of Augustus, and a stairway shows the great height of the palaces. Augustus ruled the world from his palace on the Palatine. Leaving the blistering heat of the baked stone and marble, after passing the most beautiful of Roman arches, Constantine, we were on the Appian Way. This road is an example to all time of good road building as it is in perfect condition after 20 centuries of use.

It seemed to us all too short a drive before we were out again to see the Baths of Caracalla. Thick walls, shrubs growing on the top of some of them, large pool spaces, "lockers" cut in rock, gymnasium spaces, for men and women. The Girl thrilled over having at last found kindred spirits. The mosaic floors are easily seen here, many uncovered, a few bits of mosaic scattered about. 

The baths at Pompeii were in every way similar. This spot in flower time is said to be "odoriferous" with blossoming trees and flowering plants, rhododendrum being specified. This is a spot that Shelley loved.

We made no attempt to "do" the Seven hills of Rome. On the Capitoline Hill stands the museum, Conservatore, the Appian Way mile-stone, Marcus Aurelius on his bronze horse, and the ghost of Rienzi, doubtless. 

Rienzi was persistently in our minds since Mussolini's star was in the ascendant. "What do you think of Mussolini, and what do you think of your king?" We asked the Italian guide. "We love our king," he replied. There was silence until the brave soul said, "And you fear

Dr. Whittinghill, who has been in charge of the Baptist work in Rome for many years, was our guest for dinner one day. Yet in the lobby of the hotel there hung a bulletin of various Protestant churches and the hotel men gave us every aid in locating them.

The campanile of this church is the tallest in Rome, and it is the largest of the 80 churches dedicated to the Virgin.

CHAPTER XV POMPEII & NAPLES 

As soon as Monsieur Pierontti had introduced us to our hotel, we went at once to the windows and looked down on the most picturesque flower garden one could imagine. You get from Pompeii only a deepened impression of what you take to this "most advertised spot in the world."

The place was a Greek colony before it fell into the hands of Rome.

Only misfortune made Pompeii famous. In A. D. 63, an earthquake nearly destroyed the town; in A. D. 79, [123]

an eruption of Mount Vesuvius completely covered Pompeii with ashes. For 1700 years the place was forgotten. In 1748 unusual traces were noticed; in 1763 in tunneling for an aqueduct to carry water to Naples it was found that under vineyards and mulberry trees were marvelous remains of a buried city. In 1863 the Italian government began systematic excavations which continue.

The first impression as one passes over the hot rough stones is of another Roman Forum. A mass of stone walls, pillars, narrow streets not over six feet wide, sidewalks and all; huge rough stones for walks, very large, very rough. Such an amazing mass of stone balustrades, columns, and walls! 

We felt the presence of disaster when we saw two skeletons under an arch where, fleeing for life, they met Death.

This theater was recently used when the King and Queen visited Pompeii.

This city had been compared to a very beautiful woman with slatternly ways so sure of her "natural beauty that she does not mind being dirty." This indifference has made the Beautiful Lady the prey of Greeks, Romans, Arabian pirates, Norsemen, Saracens, Lom bards, Bourbons, Bonapartists, until Victor brought about a United Italy. For 2700 years no Neapolitan ruled Naples.

Our first stop was at the National museum which contains the finest of bronzes, ancient mural decorations from Pompeii, 1800 bronze objects from Pompeii, collections of ancient glass, terra cotta and a picture gallery representing various nationalities of artists.

sky. People as small looking in the distance as dolls walking up the steps.

The Neapolitan speaks in song. In song he sells his wares on the streets, he drives his horse, or cow in song.

At a customer's request he milks his cow at his door. 

In song he takes orders. These rough narrow streets were filled with "joyous noises."

Leghorn and Pisa were the chief cities passed on our way from Rome to Genoa. Leghorn is one of the chief Mediterranean ports. It was founded by the Medici family, is fortified, well built and modern.

We saw the ramparts which defend Genoa. 

As our fat horse ambled along, going up a narrow steep street (all of the streets seemed narrow, steep and crooked) we came up with a funeral procession. Milan is remembered as one of the most beautiful of European cities. 4. 

While Milan dates from 400 B. C., it is chiefly 14th and 15th century history that is remembered. This is the history of the Visconti, the builder of the Cathedral; and of the Sforza. 

About the time of Dante one of the Visconti proclaimed himself Perpetual Lord of Milan. This selfelected ruler was of lowly birth and to keep his power he ruled that Milan should not declare for Guelph, meaning Pope; nor Ghibbeline, meaning Emperor. He ruled further that if any man discussed politics his tongue should be cut off. He hired soldiers to do fighting and he saw to it that his people worked in industrial and commercial arts so the Milan state was the most secure and prosperous in Italy. It was the student Visconti who was the founder of the Cathedral and came near doing for Italy what Victor Emmanuele II did, unifying her principalities.

When victory was in his hands they were powerless to close upon it, as he was overtaken by the grim

Francesco rebuilt Milan, fortified it with enormous walls; then died leaving Milan to a 22-year-old soldier son. Going down the hill we left the business streets to stroll among the residential section, where women [180]

The cemetaire is at the foot of Montmartre. Here rests that beauty, Madame Recamier, Heine, Greuze, Violet le Duc and others as famous. 

The most famous Cemetaire in Paris is Père laChaise and it is at the same time the largest and most beautiful in the world. All the cemeteries are used as parks by women and children.

Our first point was the Jewish section where "Rachel" rests, then to the Gothic marble canopy under which those renowned lovers Abelard and Heloise sleep. Thru devious paths we found the graves of Chopin, Moliere, Balzac, Corot, Daubigny (Barbizon painters), La

Leaving the cemetery we dined at a small workingman's restaurant by way of contrast to the more expensive places. The entire force came out to serve us very good food.

and if we had only known it one leads to Cemetaire de Picpus, burial place of Lafayette, which it took us another day to discover.

In search of Chateau atmosphere we went to the practically dismantled Chateau of Vincennes. Here

Here the eminent artist Charles Le Brun lived and designed magnificent royal furnishings.

CHAPTER XXI PARISIAN PEREGRINATIONS 

The walk from Gare St. Lazare to Notre Dame was long on that August Sabbath, yet who would drive when there was so much to observe. 

A pause at the Madeline to hear the organ, a longer one at the flower market, where only on a Sabbath are posies displayed in this particular spot, down Rue Faubourg Saint Honore with its many small shops showing even on Sunday in windows, shawls, old cameos, coral, garnet and antique jewelry; of course, hats and gowns.

Old St. Roch came next. To me the altar in this church is the most impressive in the world. Mary and 

Joseph bend in adoration over the reclining, smiling

Child, one at the head, the other at His feet. Far in the distance hangs the Crucified Christ.

There are many Sunday markets where flowers, and miscellaneous objects are sold. I did not see but one market just for birds, which is near Notre Dame. Birds of every description, and food for birds. To make the scene complete a few cats. After lingering at this market for a short while we approached Notre Dame, which we had visited before. [184]

Paris has two islands. The Ile de Louis, and the Ile de la Citié, both small islands in the Seine, connected by a bridge, cow pastures in the long ago, I read. From the small Ile de la Citié, this Paris of wealth, learning, sophisticated civilization, of over 3,000,000 has grown. 

Notre Dame is the treasure of the island. 

The Cathedral is not as impressive as other cathedrals probably because the towers were never finished. The picture of these belfried towers, flying butresses, gargoyles silhouetted against the sky makes the Cathedral well known.

On this Sabbath we entered with the throng. The majority were tourists evidently. As we sat attempting to listen a constant stream of humanity passed to the 

Treasure Room. Here, for a few cents jewels worth a king's ransom, coronation robes (Napoleon's of heavy gold embroidered satin particularly interested us) are displayed.

As the kings were born in palaces, consecrated at 

Rheims, buried in St. Denis, they came to Notre Dame for coronation. In the long line passing in a vision none was more impressive than Napoleon, standing for coronation, but taking the crown from the Archbishop's hand and placing it on his own head. The Little 

Corporal from Corsica, poverty-hounded student, had made himself Emperor of France, and the world must know it. Then the regal Josephine kneels at his feet and he crowns her. These were trying days for Josephine. She had failed to furnish the desired heir; would she share in the culmination of Napoleon's greatness? This was her answer. Her Gethsemane came [185]

later in Fontainebleu when she was divorced to make way for the youthful Maria Louisa. 

We sat through two services trying to catch the feeling of Sabbath devotion. On leaving the Girl said, "Never will you get me in a tourist church on Sunday," and I squelchingly replied, "You worship in your heart."

A pleasant lawn is just back of Notre Dame. There we sat to watch the folks go by. In front of the building is now an open space. In this space was once many small buildings in one of which was the mother of the Gypsy girl, the heroine of Victor Hugo's "The 

Hunchback of Notre Dame."

On the Ile de St. Louis is a very ancient cathedral, quite small. I had a mind to see it on that morning. It has been said that this island rests upon the bosom of Paris as a beautiful cameo would rest upon the bosom of a lovely woman. I had tried several times to catch this view without success until that day. The place is quiet without street cars, streets narrow, buildings old and grey. That morning the streets seemed deserted, a few shops open. In a home shop a woman sold flowers made of ribbons. The cathedral was closed, an old woman guarded the door. A picture for an artist. We passed wonderfully carved old doors, and caught sight of entrancing stairways and small fountains playing in tiny courts. All things of loveliness—and an inspiration to artists, antiquarians, writers.

Even here one sees Napoleon, as it was to the Ecole Militaire on the Ile that the shy lad from Corsica first came. We left this isle of enchantment for the modern [186]

Rue de Rivoli for a dinner at expensive Cafe Rumpelmeyers. One must eat everywhere to appreciate Paris.

There is one spot in Paris of which the Girl never tired, La Sainte Chappelle. La Sainte Chappelle was built by St. Louis on a return from the Holy Land to shelter the crown of Thorns, a fragment of the cross and a nail from the cross, all too precious to be placed in Notre Dame, hence this jewel of a casket was prepared for it. The Palais de Justice nearly conceals the small cathedral, which has been secularized, although few meetings of any kind are held in it. There are two stories. The first floor rather unadorned. The second a gem of the rarest rays. The most beautiful windows ever seen. The room is empty except for post card venders. It is said only reverent souls willing to be struck dumb before transcendent loveliness should enter this jeweled chamber, which is "awesomely beautiful.” 

Narrow winding stairs lead to this room. At the head of the steps is a narrow slit cut in the wall, through which cruel, crafty Louis XI peered to see who was worshipping.

The towers are beautifully proportioned. Many years were spent in building other cathedrals, but St. Louis was so impatient that his precious relics might be worthily housed that La Sainte Chappelle was finished in three years.

On a week day we entered the Palace of Justice and walked through the building watching courts in session, lawyers and others at work. The building contains many statues none so beautiful as the memorial to the soldiers of the world's war. [187]

Round "pepper pot" towers flank the entrance to the

Conciergie, the prison of the revolution from which 

Marie Antoinette, Madame Elizabeth and many others were carried across the pont au Change (the bridge of the Money changers) to the guillotine. How gloomy the cells seemed! In one corridor the friendless, moneyless prisoners were herded. In another, now the Museum, the 21 Girondists were kept. 

Marie Antoinette's cell where she was under constant surveillance, her small altar are shown, as well as the stairway she and others ascended to trial and descended to doom.

Robespierre, Marie Antoinette's foe, occupied later a cell near hers and from it went to his reward. In this building the public prosecutor, Fouquier-Tinville prepared his lists; in one of the towers was the torture chamber where terrible agonies were inflicted to force confessions. 

You may not like Marie Antoinette; but you love

Madame Roland, who went to her death from the Conciergie exclaiming "Oh, Liberty how they have mocked thee," and Charlotte Corday who gave her life for 

France. Madame Lafayette was imprisoned here when

Robespierre fell and the madness ceased, and she was freed.

eray, and many as well known, studying your guide book as you go until St. Germain of the Field is reached. 

This church was once called the golden basilica so splendid were the gifts of kings and queens to the relic housed here, the tunic of St. Vincent. One fragment of the "old church" destroyed by the Normans stands. The present building is modern being only 900 years old. 

The murals, altars are interesting, but the object of interest are the exquisite fragments of the Lady chapel with its setting of green, so peaceful that Sabbath day that one could scarcely realize that Charlotte Corday, Madame Roland, Madame Elizabeth were beheaded just across the street and their bodies thrown into this yard. Most cathedrals, possibly all, have a Lady chapel, in honor of the virgin.

The other side of the church is a Palissy nook. A monument to the noted potter standing by an oven, a tall broad pillow covered with plaques, showing his glazes. You see examples of his work in the Louvre and the Cluny, but here Palissy seems real. His reward from his day was imprisonment and death in the Bastille because he was a Huguenot.

A number of beggars stood around the building into whose hands you were glad to place a franc or so. 

Diderot, of dictionary fame, guarded in bronze the section of the street.

"Les Miserables" and made three meals on one mutton chop.

CHAPTER XXII FUGITIVE SKETCHES OF PARIS 

In the vicinity of Rue Faubourg Montmartre you will find the homes of Liszt, Meissonier, Halevy; Folies Bergeres and somewhere near, Grevius', of wax figure fame. 

To understand history see Grevius. We followed the early Christians through the Catacombs (Paris has catacombs as well as Rome) witnessing weddings and funerals. France's distinguished sons stood before us, a few Americans as well.

The history of the revolution was given. A wax Charlotte Corday in her peasant dress, slew a wax Murat as he sat in his shoe-like bath, and was dragged away by wax revolutionists. 

The Royal family in splendor, in prison. Who can forget the scene where Marie Antoinette, being called to the window by the mob, has the head of her best friend, the Princess Lamballe, on a long spike, thrust in her face. "Does this mean danger to my family?" asked the king. The reply, "It is a warning."

The saddest scene was the queen, so regal in Versailles, and Fontainebleu, so gay in the Hameau, now in a narrow cell robed in black. Queen no longer, just the "widow Capet" on her way to the guillotine, from whence her body was thrown in the yard of Expiatoire [196]

Cardinal Richelieu in 1624 built the palace here on the site of an older palace. The powerful cardinalminister stronger than King Louis XIII, ruled here in despotic state, and at his death left his palace and gardens to the king. La Palais-Royal was the playground of Louis XIV, in his troubled childhood during the wars of the Fronde. Henrietta, widow of Charles I, of England, found refuge here. Peter the Great and three Americans were guests here, Paul Jones, Franklin and Payne. In memory, the figures and the small bits of royal humanity are seen in the gardens. The Bois du Boulogne touches la Champs-Elysees and covers several thousand acres. It is a forest and a recreation park. Approached at one angle we passed the Arc de Triomphe at the moment that the mayor of Paris and high dignitaries were bearing flowers to pay tribute to the Unknown Soldier whose grave had been decorated by a communist. This approach led us to the jardin of animals, an immense zoo. Platform cars always crowded, dashed at this point through the forest. 

We liked best the way by the ball park. Watch the players, then continue our walk down the road and enter the candleberry-bordered entrance near the rose garden. In August these roses were beauties, and the dahlias beyond compare. Losing ourselves in the depth of the forest in the distance we saw horse back riders and comfortable couples in fiacres; nearer a few traces of ruins, forest everywhere. The grass was as soft as [203]

velvet, and the trees-only God can make such trees. 

We thanked Him that France has kept her forests. 

A ride of about twenty minutes in a train took us to 

St. Denis.

The streets and shops were full of interest as we walked in search of the Cathedral. St. Denis is an industrial town, giving little thought to the tourist. 

A funeral was in progress in the church, and as we waited in the side aisle we could not but notice the difference in funeral customs as shown that afternoon. 

After the talk, and song, men in blouses, looking like working men, placed the small casket on their shoulders of unequal height and absolutely trotted out of church. 

The friends stood in line on either side of the aisle down which the bereaved passed. Many sympathetic greetings were exchanged as the procession moved slowly on.

A description of the many tombs would be futile. 

Louis XII and Anne of Brittany have most magnificent tombs expressing French sculpture of the renaissance period. Then the crudeness of early sculptures on 

Sarcophagi is in contrast. Many tombs were marred by being chipped and even initials of the thoughtless carved on some. This was a slight desecration for few bodies or bones are in any Sarcophagus. The terrorists saw to that, marching to St. Denis and throwing out all the bodies. The body of Henry IV was so skillfully embalmed that his wounds received in the peoples' cause stopped them for awhile. The heart of the grand monarch Louis XIV was sold to a painter who bought other royal hearts from which he hoped to secure a new [204]

shade for his palette. The Revolutionists venerate the body of Turenne, which later Napoleon had buried in the Invalides.

It is written that although quick-lime was put on the bodies of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, and the few bones not destroyed are in L'Expiatoire, yet the dust coffined from where the bodies had rested and buried in St. Denis, places more of the remains of these monarchs here than that of any others. Notwithstanding gloom and dust, the place was impressive. 

Paintings of historic events cover the walls. Imagination does the rest, even to showing us Joan of Arc, as a worshipper here.

It was Andre Lenoir who preserved these tombs and many, many antiquities from revolutionary destruction. 

At one time his body received the thrust intended for the object he was protecting. 

The two cathedrals of the name St. Germain had interested us. We were anxious to see the Chateau St. 

Germain. It is this chateau which the Indian Maharajah has recently bought for his American wife. We selected a morning for our visit. Nature was in her loveliest mood, and we were charmed with our drive through country roads past princely looking residences. In fact 

I believe we would call it "millionaire drive." The lawns, forest background, and the Seine bordering the drive completed a scene of beauty.

Unintentionally we left the car before reaching our destination, and in trying to find just where we were [205]

called on the village mayor. Never were people so friendly, or as anxious to speed you on your way.

For eight hundred years a castle has been at St. 

Germain. Francis I was married here to Claude, daughter of Louis III and Anne of Brittany, so soon after 

Anne's death that Louis required the bride, bride groom and court to wear black mourning. 

Chateau to me meant beauty which I did not find in this building. A huge pile of little adorned brick, and remnants of a moat. We saw few of the rooms except the museum of antiquities dealing with the conquest of the Romans by the Gauls. 

The chapel across the way is 13th century. James II of England when exiled lived in the chateau and wished to be buried in this chapel. There are remains of the chateau in which Louis XIV was born. This chateau was built by Henry II and IV. It is now the Pavilion 

Henri-Intre where one may dine well, and then enjoy an unrivalled view. For here as elsewhere the grounds are all that one could desire. Forest, drives, walks, flowers, and more flowers.

We walked until we reached the balustrade of the Seine. Looking across the Seine we imagined St. Denis, the perpetual sight of this burial place, inspired Louis XIV to build Versailles. He wished in life to put Death aside. We will put all thought of kings aside and watch crafts on the yellow river. We see the buildings of 

Paris, Eiffel tower soaring over all, and white Sacré 

Coeur pointing the way heavenward. [206]

Both Malmaison, home of Josephine Bonaparte, and Reuil, her burial place, are on the way to St. Germain, yet we selected an afternoon for our visit to these spots. 

The most interesting small town we passed through was Neuilly where we changed trains. Here lives 

Madame Pointenac, president of Paris U. D. C. Loui siana has a monument in honor of General Pointenac. 

Carol of Romania, the much discussed, once lived in Neuilly. A street fair was in progress, occupying the length of several blocks. It is customary to bargain for whatever one desires to purchase. 

Certainly we talked of Josephine the beautiful 

Creole from Martinique, wife of General Beauharnais, a splendid man, murdered by the revolutionist. 

The young son Eugene went to Napoleon and asked for his father's sword. Napoleon returned the sword to 

14-year-old Eugene. What more natural than that his mother, Josephine should go to thank the young Napoleon. She was much older than he. He had not given much thought to women at that time. His struggle had been for food sufficient to sustain life, and means to assist his mother in the support of a large family. He was charmed by the fascinating widow, and they were married. To her two children, Hortense and Eugene, he was always friend and guide. His fondness for the mother seemed sincere, he divorced her with regret. 

Ambition usurping the place of heart. Some one said that had Josephine taken the right road out of Paris, instead of the wrong road, when Napoleon returned [207]

victor the divorce would never have occurred. She missed him, instead of meeting him.

Malmaison was always a favorite home of this couple, and after the divorce in Fontainebleu, this palace was given Josephine for her home. An artistic gate opens into the lawn. On the right is the inevitable souvenir shop, and back of it the old carriage house which has some of Josephine's numerous carriages still in it. On the left of the gate is Napoleon's work shop. Prints of 

Napoleon in youth, of his campaigns are on the wall and a picture nearly life size of a youthful Napoleon. 

As we approached the house we walked through the rose garden, kept up in memory of the flower loving empress and full of Malmaison roses.

The palace is grey, by no means large. Guides take many tourists from room to room, Hortense's, Eugene's, Josephine's room, dining room, library and all. 

One room is given over to glass cases of wearing apparel. Josephine's coronation robe, heavy white satin, gold embroidered, the baby clothes of the king of Rome, child of Napoleon and the new wife, Maria Louisa. To us it seemed mockery to place these things in Josephine's room. She is said to have loved the baby, as he was sometimes brought to see her. Napoleon came here occasionally for council. Back of this palace is a large lawn and forest. So many shrubs familiar in the United 

States are here. Butterfly bush, lavender, "candleberry,” lilac. The former shrubs were all in bloom. There are several statues on this lawn of Josephine, Napoleon, and others. A small stream, a rustic bridge lending added charm. [208]

Across the road is the Mausoleum of the Prince Imperial who was killed in Africa, son of Napoleon III and the beautiful Eugenie of Spain. This mausoleum is in the forest, alone. The young man gave his life for 

France, and so France honors him. 

In returning we stopped at Reuil to see the burial places of Josephine and Hortense. The way to the church was through narrow cobble stone streets, a few French words at small shops would get directions. In front of the city hall we saw a monument to the Unknown Soldier, who is honored everywhere. 

We found the old caretaker just ready to close the small church, but he was willing to direct us to the tombs. Eugenie gave both white marble figures of bending angels life size. The tombs are on either side of the altar. The bodies rest in Sarcophagi in the crypt below. Napoleon III gave the handsome organ in this church, buying it in Italy.

We were fortunate in our day in being in Reuil. The day before was the annual Napoleon day, when orations are delivered here in his memory. [209]

CHAPTER XXIII A DAY OF ADVENTURE IN PARIS 

During the last days of our month in Paris, the lure of rates to Deauville led the Girl and Gladys into Normandy. There the horses at the races, the people on the beaches, the queer, ornamental-roof houses filled them with delight.

I had a sinister purpose in remaining in Paris. There was the big church of St. Eustache, the Bourse, and

Market to be discovered, a pilgrimage to be made to the grave of Lafayette, and I had the firm intention of sitting on a marble bench overlooking the Tuileries

Garden and eating all les petites gateaux (little cakes) and brioches that I could devour. I intended doing it all dressed in my new Paris gown and chapeau (hat) and gloves. 

The day was Sunday. Very decorously I made my way, so attired, to the church of the British Embassy. 

There the sermon from the text, "We Are Debtors" brought a lesson of stewardship and was soul satisfying. The hour was twelve. Now for the feast. The shop was found on Faubourg street. "No, I did not want a table, nor wine, nor chocolate, just a dozen fat little cakes on a paper platter." I had hoped to find a seat alone, but a number of small boys were playing tennis [210]

near the marble bench.

Few other people were in evidence. 

Before me was the Place de la Concord, bronze statue in center. Marble figures representing France's large cities, placed at intervals around the Place. 

Champs-Elysees; gay with horseback riders, little fiacres, people and children; extended to Arc de Triomphe and on as far as eye could see, a tree lined vista. 

On my right were the Tuileries gardens, and then the Louvre. This view made my feast, as I sat alone, all 

I could desire.

In going to the Louvre I rested near a tea pavillion, and in looking at the crowds coming and going I forgot the Paris gloves until when near the Louvre I missed them. Hastening back I found a man on my last seat very, very fast asleep. No gloves anywhere.

In the Louvre I met the Cathedral-touring girls whom we last saw on the streets of Milan. They looked at me in surprise asking, "Are you lost again?"

A walk to Place de l' Opera, a tram marked Point de Picpus, started me on the pilgrimage to la Cemetaire 

Picpus where Lafayette is buried.

Started me-mind you, for I could not make the tram driver understand what I wanted. He put me off at the end of his line, and waved me on. I soon found myself in Bois de Vincennes which we had been told to avoid as it was the dangerous gathering place of the communists. Never did I behold a scene as pleasant, unless it was a Sunday school picnic. The children! The children! Children playing everywhere. Fathers rolling baby carriages with mothers in attendance. Families under [211]

the noble forest trees spreading supper. I walked on to the lake, which was filled with boats and swans. I would gladly have remained in the rustic Chateau had it not been for my quest.

The Garden of Agriculture is across from Bois de Vincennes. After an examination of labelled plants, I approached the guard and in French asked for Cemetaire Picpus and the grave of Lafayette. He understood me. I could not understand him; nor would I pretend to understand him even as much as I did really understand him. Finally the long-suffering guide took me outside the Garden on the walk and pointed as he drew a diagram. A woman told him his drawing was not correct, so he wadded it up and threw it away. As he held my arm he asked every one passing if they spoke English. As French heads said, "No" French eyes gazed at the American woman who would see Lafayette's grave as though she were the communist. Her perturbation of mind was over the size of the tip she should offer the so gracious guard. The amount mentally increased each moment. In the end when the handful of money was extended, the guard with hand over his heart bowed low, and courteously refused it all. "The pleasure was his."

The interpreter was finally found, a taxi secured and we were off. We entered a deserted street, the driver soon stopped at the door of one of the small houses on the street in search of information. He stopped at a gate in a high wall for a moment then drove on and around this wall which surrounded the block. He was a puzzled driver, as he again stopped at the gate for no [212]

cemetery had he seen. This time he pressed a button on the gate. A pleasant woman appeared. Yes, this was 

Cemetaire Picpus, and here Lafayette was buried. 2 Entering the gate we faced a small chapel from which a priest and nuns of the Perpetual Adoration were coming. A convent stood near the chapel. 

Of course you are remembering "Les Miserables" and you know that it was the walls of Picpus that Valjean climbed, holding a stout cord by his teeth which he had placed tenderly under the child Cosette's arms, and so drew her into the safe shelter of Cemetaire Picpus, when he was pursued by the Paris police. 

It was from this cemetery Valjean was carried in a coffin, buried, later resurrected by his friend to enter the gate of Picpus as gardener's assistant. Cosette, beloved of his heart, was placed in this convent. 

As my guide conducted me through the gardens, she seemed proud of the pears, and vegetables. I was interested in the white robed sisters. There are two gardens, both narrow, not over 40 feet, I think, with a flower bordered path along the side. In the second garden is a small stone house, hardly larger than a dog kennel which was Jean Valjean's hiding place. "It is small,” I said. "Ah, madame, he was so poor." The second garden was divided by a stone wall and a gate led into the cemetery where rest the true nobility of France. Lafayette's grave was easy to find, as two American flags [218]

float over it. By his side rests his wife, and their son, George Washington Lafayette sleeps at their heads. A wall with locked gate separated this space from a restful spot covered with soft waving grass, a few drooping trees and a few old monuments. Standing by this gate the French woman feelingly in broken English told me the story of Cemetaire Picpus. 

At the close of the revolution a French princess returned to Paris. She knew her mother had been a victim of the revolution, she wished to find her grave. 

Inquiry disclosed the fact that a seamstress went each day somewhere to pray. A visit to the seamstress revealed the prayer place as being the place where her mother's body had been thrown in a ditch with hundreds of others, among them the princess' mother. The seamstress had followed at a distance the tumbril containing the bodies. The princess bought this ground, for a burial place. The families of other victims asked the privilege of being buried near their loved ones. Madame

Lafayette's mother and aunt were both beheaded and their bodies thrown here. The spot seemed sacred to me. 

Leaving Cemetaire Picpus I walked a few blocks to Place de la Republique. It was late. The trams dashed by me crowded to the limit. A few friendly souls waved to the woman waiting alone.

As I looked the Column in the Place de la Republique vanished. I saw the guillotining of the long ago, the headsman Sanson, come from his red house near for his gruesome task. (Sanson's family had been executioners for 200 years). All noises ceased. I heard the fall of the axe, the thud of heads falling into baskets, and [214]

then the rumble of tumbrils on the way to the trench which is now Picpus Cemetery. My quest was ended. 3A tram ride finally brought me to the vast Market place, which I found closed at this hour, to the Bourse nearby, and to the great Church of St. Eustache, also closed as it was after six. Many ragged folks, beggars 

I presume, slept on the steps of St. Eustache, opposite the Market. None awoke. There was little life in this place usually so crowded and stirring. It is said that the Market folks give much trouble as they led in every 

My objective next was Gare St. Lazare and Asnieres, as it was nearly seven. I decided to cut across a few streets to save time, and soon found myself in a large circle alone with a huge bronze equestrian rider.

I held a conference with this rider as to whether 

I should keep on "cutting" or find a taxi. As two taxis passed just then I presume he advised taxi. 

I soon reached the Gare. I had given all my change in fares and tips, leaving me only large bills. I gave a bill to the driver. He filled my hand with small money. 

I knew it was not sufficient, so asked for the return of my bill. A policeman moved a few steps nearer. The driver then gave me bills, and he was one astonished man when I gave him as a tip all the little money he had given me. I had no idea how much it was. I was leaving Paris in a few days. He had my thank offering. [215]

I entered the pension (boarding house) just as the bell rang for dinner, thoroughly satisfied.

The day of our departure came. With sorrow we bade our many new friends adieu. At Gare San Lazare a last dash was made to Perrine's for more gloves.

Then came the ride through the country. The following extract from a French paper under the headline: 

A COUNTRYMAN'S DIARY 

Cloud and Shine.

August 23.-The slopes of the hills are like the harvest plains below-scenes of colour, ever changing as the clouds pass over the face of the sun. On those high places the dwarf furze is in full flower, and the bright clumps of bell-heather, and the darker more spacious purple of the common heath, as splendid as ever they will be. So the clear shine upon those broad, bloomy slopes renders them visible and vivid afar, a delight to all who sojourn near them. 

The day is one when every spot seems to get a little sunshine, and none holds it long. Now it is you who are shadowed, a grey rain falling about you, while you mark the sunlit gold away up the valley and the purple and gold of the distant hillsides. A quarter hour later that glad light has reached you and a mist of rain dims the far prospect. All is shine and sparkle, color and comfort, here, with impetuous butterflies hither and thither and the bees humming along their busy ways.

P. W. D. I.

The train stopped at Rouen in Normandy long enough for us to see the Cathedral in front of which Joan of Arc was burned. A statue of the Maid stood before the Cathedral, a monument to the fickleness of man, as well as to the Savior of France. 

The Cathedral is notable for the dissimiliarity of its spires. 

The Carmania was slow in coming in. We were at last on board. The wind, rain, coldness near icebergs of the return voyage has been described. 

In thinking of our landing in New York I paraphrased part of a verse from "Moods and Melodies." "We admit the heart's turmoil

As our feet press America's soil;
