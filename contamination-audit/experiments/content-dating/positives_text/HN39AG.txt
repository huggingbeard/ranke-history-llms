TO THE STUDENT 

This is to be your book. At present it is only a framework - a pattern of what your finished work will be. When you have completed it, you will have written a book of your own - one which will reflect your own individuality, and which will measure your understanding of our world today.

That is the object of this book-to help you see how our present world has come about; to understand it better; to recognize its problems; to realize how far men have advanced and how much further they need to progress. It may also help you to see more plainly what part there is for us to play in the great adventure of the future. 

The plan of the book is simple. It consists of eight units. Each unit deals with one subject of major importance in modern times. There is also a preliminary survey unit, for those who are not acquainted with the story of men's earlier progress. 

The tools for your work consist of books, magazines, papers, maps, pictures, the radio — in fact, anything which helps you understand how the world of today has come about and what your relation to it is. Your textbook itself is a whole collection of tools, as we shall see in our first assignment.

The Aims of each unit help you to keep in mind what you are trying to accomplish. 

Suppose you are given some boards, a saw, a hammer, and some nails, or a piece of cloth, scissors, needle, and thread—and are told to go to work. Naturally, you will want to know what you are expected to make. It is important to know, for example, if you have been supplied with lumber and tools, whether you are to build a table or a garage. It is just as important, before starting on a new history assignment, to know what you are expected to accomplish - in other words, to understand the Aims of the unit, and to keep them in mind while preparing your lesson. You would not be likely to forget whether you are making a table or building a garage; it would be just as foolish to forget what you are trying to do while working on a history assignment. 

After you have decided to build a garage, or to make a dress, as the case may be, you will be obliged to follow a plan or pattern, or your work will not be likely to turn out very well. In working out the Aims of your history unit you will likewise need a plan or pattern if you wish to accomplish what you set out to do. This is the purpose of the Study Guide and the Written and Oral Topics. Your teacher will help you alter this pattern, if need be, to suit your measurements.

TO THE STUDENT 

To gather together all the material necessary to complete a unit may take several weeks. Look over the whole unit assignment in both textbook and workbook before you begin to work at it in earnest. See what its main topics are, and the period of time the unit covers. Find out from the maps where the action takes place. 

After glancing over the whole unit, you will begin work in earnest. Read all of each assignment before beginning on the workbook. Do not read to remember; read to understand. After the first reading you should be able to discuss the questions at the beginning of each section, and to write the brief summaries that are called for. 

Then, when your teacher thinks you are ready, begin to fill out the spaces in your workbook. Nearly all the questions can be answered from your textbook, but if you are a quick reader you will find it worth your while to hunt more complete answers in the other books mentioned. 

Fill out every space carefully! You are writing your own book. Let it be worth preserving.

Make your own list of new terms: they may be different from the ones in the workbook. The meanings of new words can often be found in the textbook. If not, use the dictionary. Make out also a list of questions you would like to have answered, and bring them to class. Put in this list only questions you are sure you cannot find answers to. 

Prepare carefully for the floor talks and map talks. Listen attentively to those given by others, and keep brief summaries of the ones you think worth while. You may have questions to ask, or something additional to contribute. 

After completing as much of the work as your teacher thinks best, choose an assignment from the supplementary work listed in the back of your book. Consult your teacher about your topic. Sometimes the whole class can work together on a project—such as an exhibit, or a dramatization. But there is a large enough variety suggested so that you will have a chance to exercise your special talents.

The library may not have all the books listed in your text. It would be a good idea to appoint a committee of the class at the beginning of each unit to check up on the material to be found there. Probably other books, not mentioned in your text, will be found. The list prepared by your committee can be posted on the bulletin board for the benefit of the class. This work constitutes a good supplementary project. 

If any part of the subject interests you especially, consult one of the longer and more detailed works on the list. You may find good material for a class report. Be able to give the author and the title of the book you have consulted. Keep brief notes or summaries of what you have read.

Often a book of fiction gives a correct impression of the period, and a more lifelike one than many volumes of facts. If you have time to read one of the books on the fiction list,

iii notice carefully whether the account is true to the historical facts as you know them; whether the author is showing only one side of the story; what new ideas you find; what references to facts you have studied, which help you to understand them better. 

Some of the most important tools for the work in modern history are magazines and newspapers. These will help you to "follow-up" the accounts begun in your textbook and in the library books. No matter how up-to-date a book may be, nothing between covers can keep up with the times. New "last chapters" need writing every day. Keep your work up to date with reviews of magazine articles, or clippings from newspapers. Arrange and mount such clippings carefully with explanatory notes of your own. 

The Tests are suggested and are to be used as your teacher thinks best. 

It is our hope that this year's study will help you gain in knowledge and understanding of the nations of the world.

3. To gain a clearer understanding of the definition of history as "the common adventure of mankind.”

UNIT II

EVEN before the Renaissance, rulers had criticized the Church when it interfered too much with government. The Renaissance, which started so many new ways of thinking about life, gave some people new ideas about religion. They became discontented with the Church, and often discontent grew into revolt. Differing opinions at first gave rise to persecutions and religious wars; but in the end these very differences led to religious toleration. The world has taken a long time to make this adjustment, and it is not complete even yet. We ourselves have to be careful not to fall into the errors of three or four centuries ago.

UNIT III HOW NEW NATIONS DEVELOPED AND BECAME RIVALS FOR WORLD POWER

DURING the sixteenth century the Tudors had succeeded in making England an autocracy in fact, if not in name. The beginning of the seventeenth century saw the English people bent on recovering their "ancient rights" to tax themselves and to determine England's religion. Thus began a long struggle between those who believed that Parliament should rule and those who supported the claim of the Stuart kings that they ruled by "divine right."

On the continent of Europe, the theory of "divine right" was accepted by nearly every country, and a few powerful families had come to control the affairs of most of Europe. At home they seized every chance to increase their power over their subjects, and abroad they lost no chance to extend their boundaries at the expense of their neighbors. Now began the mad race for territory in the New World, in which England joined, and rival powers soon came into conflict in America and in the Orient. AIMS 1. UNIT IV HOW A STRUGGLE FOR DEMOCRACY BEGAN WITH THE FRENCH REVOLUTION

THE benevolent despots of the eighteenth century had proved unable to solve the problems of their day. Now the peoples themselves tried to solve them, and democracy began to take the place of despotism. This change began in France, so we know it as the French Revolution. Much was accomplished peacefully; but such great changes do not happen easily or all at once. People with privileges struggled to keep them, and the oppressed fought for their rights. Other countries feared the spread of new ideas, and the people themselves quarreled over their new liberties. 

At first the people's cause seemed lost. For a time Napoleon ruled all 

Europe with a strong hand; and after his overthrow the former rulers did best to seize back their old power. But the people had tasted freedom, and could not be kept down. Divine right monarchy gradually gave way to rule by the people.

4. To understand the changes made by the Congress of Vienna and to see why attempts to restore divine right monarchies failed to crush democracy.

UNIT V HOW ENGLAND DEALT WITH PROBLEMS OF INDUSTRY AND GOVERNMENT

WHILE the political and social revolution which began in France was spreading throughout the world, another sort of revolution was taking place in England - a complete change in methods of work, which transformed men's ways of living. This Industrial Revolution, too, spread around the world, carrying tremendous benefits to mankind, but bringing great problems as well. At first reform was slow, for the English government had come to represent mainly a small class of landlords, who feared change of any kind. Then gradually changes took place in government, to be followed by social and economic reforms, until England became a leader among the progressive countries.

At the same time Great Britain had been extending her empire around the world. As she became more democratic, she tried better ways of treating her subjects in other continents. Finally, in the twentieth century, she contributed to the world a new form of empire - the British Commonwealth of Free Nations destined to play an important part in the world of today. 

AIMS 

1. To learn how the Industrial Revolution changed ways of working and living. 

2. To understand the new problems that arose and to see how England tried to solve them by gradual reform. 

3. To understand how the action of governments affects our everyday lives. 

4. To trace the growth of the British Empire, and its development into a Commonwealth of Nations.

UNIT VI HOW CONTINENTAL EUROPE PROGRESSED 

AFTER 1848

THE nineteenth century was marked by democratic, scientific, and industrial progress. The seeds of democracy sown by the French Revolution had kept alive, in spite of all the attempts to root them out. The spread of the 

Industrial Revolution to the continent hastened their growth. The spirit of nationalism grew also, and new national states were formed. 

Progress itself gave rise to problems many of them unsolved today. 

Wherever possible, this unit brings the story down to the present. Reference books, magazines, and newspapers will be needed to keep the work of this unit up to date.

UNIT VIII HOW THE TWENTIETH-CENTURY WORLD IS TRYING TO SOLVE ITS PROBLEMS

In many ways the world at the beginning of the twentieth century was a better, a more comfortable, and a happier place to live in than the world at the beginning of the nineteenth century. Scientific invention and discovery had advanced, and the progress of democracy and the growth of human sentiment was very apparent. With their advance, however, they had brought new world problems, problems the solution of which had not been reached in the nineteenth century, which still puzzle us in the twentieth century, but which must be solved if civilization is to continue. All nations are bending their efforts to such a solution. AIMS 1. To learn how the nations blundered into war in their attempts to keep peace. 

2. To learn how this war, and the peace treaties which followed it, not only failed to solve international problems, but set up new ones. 

3. To see how the world today is attempting to solve these problems.
