ENERGY COMMISSION What It Is, What It Does 

Twenty-fi wenty-five years ago, atomic energy was a subject discussed only by scientists. Today, this force is a significant part of our culture and has profound import for all mankind.

Atomic energy provides strength for the defense of the Free World. It is used for such diverse tasks as the generation of electric power and the treatment of disease. It has improved agricultural production, increased efficiency of industrial processes, and broadened horizons in nearly every area of scientific endeavor. 

Future applications will be limited only by man's ingenuity.

The Atomic Energy Commission is the agency of the United States Government responsible for the development of this energy, for defense as well as for peace. 1

The Atomic Energy Act of 1954 contains the following statement of purpose: "A. The development, use, and control of atomic energy shall be directed so as to make the maximum contribution to the general welfare, subject at all times to the paramount objective of making the maximum contribution to the common defense and security; and "B. The development, use, and control of atomic energy shall be directed so as to promote world peace, improve the general welfare, increase the standard of living, and strengthen free competition in private enterprise."

This booklet undertakes to explain the Atomic Energy Commission-its organization, its activities, and its role in man's search for knowledge.

ISTORY 

In 1946 Congress enacted legislation to place control of the nation's already vast atomic energy program in a new civilian agency, and on January 1, 1947, the Atomic Energy

Commission officially came into existence. 

Thus, control of nuclear energy passed from the Army Corps of Engineers, which had operated the Manhattan 

Project during World War II, to the new agency. 

The authors of the Atomic Energy Act declared that "never before in the peacetime history of the United States has Congress established an administrative agency vested with such sweeping authority and entrusted with such portentous responsibilities." 

The physical facilities and the complex organization, representing an investment of 2,2 billion dollars, constituted one of the nation's largest enterprises. Involved in the transfer were 37 installations in 19 states and Canada, employing nearly 45,000 people.

The facilities included small laboratories, huge plants, a great variety of unique equipment, and the world's only nuclear reactors. Historically, the AEC has conducted much of its work through contractors. Under a typical arrangement, the AEC owns the physical plant and equipment and pays the cost of operation; the operation is carried out by a private corporation or scientific organization under a cost-type contract. 

About 130,000 persons work in AEC projects, but the AEC itself employs only about 7000. 

AEC Headquarters staff determines policy and the field office personnel implement it in administering contracts. 

There is, however, direct Headquarters administration of work at six AEC laboratories. 

The portion of the AEC staff which is headed by the General Manager has responsibility for all operating activities, including production, research and development, and training. 

Regulatory Staff

The AEC regulatory organization is responsible for licensing the civilian use of atomic energy materials. The 

Director of Regulation and his staff have offices in Bethesda, Maryland, a Washington suburb.

The regulatory staff issues licenses for the use of radioactive materials and for the construction and operation of reactors and other facilities. It also formulates regulations and inspects licensed installations.

Before it issues a license, the AEC determines that the applicant has the personnel and equipment to handle safely the material he wishes to obtain, or that the facility he plans can be built and operated with safety. 

Some license applications are considered at public hearings, either before an AEC examiner or before a threemember board.

After a license is granted, the regulatory staff conducts inspections to assure that the licensee complies with regulations and the provisions of his license. The Director of

Regulation has five regional Compliance Offices, in New 

York, Chicago, Atlanta, Denver, and Berkeley, Calif.

The AEC is assisted in performing its regulatory functions by the Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards. 

This independent group, established by law, advises the 

AEC on the safety aspects of nuclear reactor design and operation, and on proposed reactor safety standards.

The regulatory staff also administers the AEC transfer, by agreement, of part of the atomic energy regulatory authority to state governments. 

Reporting to Congress

The Commission is required by law to report regularly to Congress on all atomic energy matters. A special committee of both houses of Congress- the Joint Committee on Atomic Energy-maintains surveillance of AEC activity and all other matters related to the development, use, and control of nuclear energy.

The AEC submits a detailed report and numerous special reports to Congress each year. Through these and through hearings conducted by the Joint Committee, Congress is kept informed. Subcommittee of Joint Committee on Atomic Energy holding a hearing. 10 100

RODUCTION Raw Materials 

Uranium contains an enormous amount of energy. If all the atoms in one pound of uranium-235 (a fissionable form) were fissioned, the energy released would equal that obtained by burning 2.6 million pounds of coal. Faced with an urgent military need, the AEC in 1948 launched a large program to procure uranium by purchase from foreign and domestic sources.

The resulting search for uranium ore deposits-together with development of improved processes for recovery of uranium from ores- assured the United States ample supplies for military requirements and for use in civilian electric power producing reactors. 

By the late 1950s steps were taken to limit purchases. A plan to spread out procurement through 1970 was announced in November 1962. That arrangement assures steady production from domestic mines and mills, at a slower rate, but extending over a longer period than originally planned. Demand for nuclear-produced electricity is expected to provide an increasing market for uranium during the 1970s. 11

Some ore concentrates are purchased from South Africa and Canada, but most uranium used by the AEC comes from domestic sources. 

Domestic uranium mines are in western states; mills are located nearby. Both mines and mills are privately owned.

Feed Materials to

Both domestic and foreign uranium concentrate is shipped an AEC "feed materials" plant at Weldon Spring, Missouri, for refining.

The Weldon Spring Plant refines the concentrates into high purity uranium trioxide (UO). In the normal production chain, this trioxide then takes one of two paths: 

1. It is used either at Weldon Spring or in another feed materials plant at Fernald, Ohio, to make uranium metal for fuel elements to be used in plutonium production reactors; or 

2. It is sent to the gaseous diffusion plants for the production of enriched uranium. As it occurs in nature, uranium is a mixture of three different forms or isotopes: uranium-235 (U-235), about seven-tenths of 1%; uranium-238 (U-238), about 99.3%; and uranium-234 (U-234), a trace which for practical purposes may be disregarded. The term "enriched uranium" refers to uranium which has had some of the U-238 removed, leaving the mixture richer in U-235. Uranium-235 is useful in atomic energy operations because it is fissionable, that is, capable of sustaining a chain reaction. (In 1964 the Commission reduced uranium concentrate processing operations because of lower requirements.) 13

Fernald is the National Lead Company of Ohio. Uranium-235

The AEC produces uranium-235 in huge gaseous diffusion plants at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, Paducah, Kentucky, and Portsmouth, Ohio. Usually the uranium trioxide produced at 

Weldon Spring is sent first to a "feed plant" at Paducah where it is converted to uranium hexafluoride (UF), the material used in the gaseous diffusion process. (Because of the availability of sufficient feed, the Commission decided in 1964 to place the Paducah feed plant on a standby basis.) 

Since all forms of uranium react the same chemically, they cannot be separated by ordinary chemical methods. A separation process based on a tiny difference in weight between atoms of U-235 and those of U-238 is employed instead. This is the gaseous diffusion process, in which uranium hexafluoride-in a gaseous state-is compressed against a barrier containing innumerable holes less than twomillionths of an inch in diameter. The molecules of UF。 gas with U-235 atoms are lighter than the UF, molecules containing U-238 atoms. Hence, they are more active and bounce against the porous barrier more frequently. Because of this greater activity the U-235 has a slightly greater chance of penetrating the barrier. The gas mixture that passes through the barrier holes, therefore, is slightly richer in U-235. This procedure is repeated thousands of times, each time with a small gain of U-235. Enrichment by the gaseous diffusion process usually results in products containing up to 93.5% U-235, but enrichments up to about 97.5% can be achieved. Gas of any desired enrichment below the normal top product can be withdrawn. 16

The gaseous diffusion plants are among the largest industrial installations in the world and cost about 2.5 billion dollars. Processing buildings at the three sites cover more than 600 acres. The plants use about 4% of all electric power generated in the United States. 

The end product, uranium enriched in U-235, is used in weapons or as fuel for nuclear reactors. 

The Oak Ridge and Paducah plants are operated for the AEC by Union Carbide Corporation, the Portsmouth plant by the Goodyear Atomic Corporation. Both the feed materials and the gaseous diffusion production activities are administered by the AEC's Oak Ridge Operations Office. Plutonium

Plutonium-239 is a man-made fissionable material which, like uranium-235, will sustain a nuclear chain reaction. It is used primarily in weapons, but research has been conducted into its feasibility for fueling nuclear reactors. 

Plutonium-239 is produced in large reactors by the following process: The reactor fuel contains both U-235 and 

U-238. When an atom of U-235 fissions it gives off neutrons. If a neutron is absorbed by a nearby atom of U-238, this atom is converted to U-239. Uranium-239 is unstable. 

By a process known as beta decay, the U-239 atom forms an atom of neptunium-239, which also is unstable and becomes a plutonium-239 atom.

The Hanford reactors are cubical stacks of graphite containing horizontal tubes loaded with uranium slugs clad in aluminum. The Savannah River reactors are large stainlesssteel tanks filled with heavy water (water containing deuterium, a heavy isotope of hydrogen). Fuel elements are in vertical tubes.

The AEC has 14 production reactors -nine at Hanford and five at Savannah River. (In 1964 the Commission decided to place one of the Savannah River reactors and three of those at Hanford on a standby basis.)

The work of these installations is supervised by the Richland Operations Office and the Savannah River Operations Office, respectively. 18

The AEC, in cooperation with the Department of Defense, performs the research, development, testing, and production necessary to provide the United States with nuclear weapons.

Design and development of weapons are conducted at three installations-Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory at Los Transonic wind tunnel to test scale models of weapons and research rocket vehicles.

The AEC manufactures nuclear weapons to meet national security requirements, and retires obsolescent weapons from national stockpiles. These activities are carried out in a complex of government-owned facilities operated by private industry under contracts administered by the Albuquerque Operations Office and the Oak Ridge Operations Office.

Plowshare The Plowshare Program was established to develop peaceful uses for nuclear explosives. Many have been proposedranging from canal construction and the excavation of harbors to the recovery of minerals and the production of new elements.

Of all potential applications, the one most likely to have large economic value is earth moving. The technology for excavation is being refined through AEC-sponsored field work and laboratory studies. 20

A nuclear explosion underground provides a unique opportunity for basic research by releasing more electromagnetic radiation, nuclear particles, heat, and pressure than are available from any other source. Men, right, are dwarfed by the Sedan project crater at the AEC's 

Nevada Test Site. 

Applications in mining and oil and gas recovery are being studied. 

Industrial-scale chemical reactions and production of radioisotopes which are important for research are other possible benefits. These possibilities are subjects for continuing research.

In December 1961 a multipurpose confined explosion called Project Gnome was set off underground near Carlsbad, New Mexico. It was the first nuclear explosion set off specifically for the Plowshare Program, although scientists already had obtained considerable data from military tests. 

The second Plowshare experiment was Project Sedan, an excavation experiment at the Nevada Test Site on July 6, 1962. The explosion, equivalent to that produced by about 

100,000 tons of TNT, displaced 10.6 million tons of earth in a few seconds, leaving a crater 1,280 feet across and 320 feet deep.

By the end of 1964, there had been nine other experimental devices exploded as part of Plowshare. 211 21

EACTOR DEVELOPMENT Civilian Power Reactors 

Because modern civilization has a tremendous appetite for electricity-the electrical output of the United States doubles each 10 years-man constantly is searching for new sources of power. Generation of electricity is therefore one of the most important uses of nuclear energy. 

Although America has large reserves of coal, oil, and gas, these "fossil fuels" are well suited for many uses other than production of electricity. Therefore it is appropriate to use nuclear fuel to supply some of the demand for new power. 

The AEC works in cooperation with industry to develop commercially attractive nuclear electric power. Already large nuclear plants, with capacities in the range of 300,000-500,000 electrical kilowatts, are considered competitive in high fuel-cost areas, and are expected to become competitive in most other sections of the country in the next few years.

The AEC program concentrates on developing and improving reactors and associated equipment, processes, and materials.

Its report suggested (1) early construction of plants of the most competitive types, (2) development, construction, and demonstration of advanced converters, and (3) intensive development and demonstration of breeder reactors. 

Converters produce some nuclear fuel, but less than they consume, and breeder reactors produce more fuel than they consume. 

Nuclear Desalting

The AEC is investigating the use of large nuclear reactors for the desalting of sea water. In response to a request from the President, the AEC and the Department of Interior in 1964 submitted to the White House a report outlining a program to advance the technology of desalting. 37

The possibility of dual-purpose nuclear plants which would produce electric power and also desalt water is under study. One important approach examined the feasibility of plants which would produce 150 to 750 electrical megawatts of power and 50 to 150 million gallons of water daily. 

In addition to these domestic studies, the AEC is cooperating with several other nations in joint studies.

Reactor Safety 

The AEC has established rigid controls to assure safety in U. S. reactors.

Safety efforts begin with the design of the reactor, emphasizing particularly safety of the core structure and the control systems. The purpose is to anticipate possible hu- 43

Engineers study heat transfer coefficients of reactor fuel elements under burnout conditions. man and mechanical errors in operation and to guard against them. Control rods, for example, are designed on a "failsafe" basis so they will shut down the reactor automatically, if necessary.

The next step is to make certain that in the unlikely event of a reactor accident all radioactivity will be contained within the reactor building. Several barriers against the escape of radioactive material are provided. The first is the cladding around the reactor fuel. The second barrier is a steel pressure vessel which encloses the reactor core. 

The next is a shield of dense material surrounding the pressure vessel. Finally, in most civilian power reactors, the entire reactor system is enclosed in a gastight steelcontainment vessel engineered to withstand any conceivable pressures which might develop.

All details of site location, design, construction, and operation of a proposed reactor are subject to review and approval by the AEC regulatory staff. Each power reactor construction permit application is considered at a public hearing. A second hearing may be held when application is made for a license to operate the reactor. Reactor opera- 44

tors must pass comprehensive examinations before receiving AEC licenses. All phases of reactor construction are inspected by the regulatory staff, and are subject to review by the AEC's Advisory Committee on Reactor Safeguards. 

The AEC conducts intensive research to obtain information on the safety of reactors and the effectiveness of safeguard devices. Activities include the deliberate destruction of experimental reactor cores to study control mechanisms and to help determine safe operating limits. For example, in one project, more than 2000 power bursts have been conducted. In 1965, the AEC was building additional facilities to acquire more safety data.

ADIOISOTOPES 

The AEC conducts broad research to advance the technology of using radiation and radioisotopes, the radioactive forms of elements.

Radioisotopes already are used in many ways in medicine, engineering, industry, and scientific research. Work is under way to extend their usefulness by applying them in control of air and water pollution, oceanography, meteorology, space exploration, crime detection, and national defense.

The development of processes for the radiation pasteurization of foods has important economic potential. Low-level írradiation of fruit and fish can increase their refrigerated shelf life, reduce transportation spoilage, and lengthen the marketing period for these and other food products. 

The use of large radiation sources for production of new chemicals and plastics, sterilization of medical supplies, and other industrial processes is continuously investigated. 

Some radioisotopes are obtained by irradiating target materials in a nuclear reactor; others occur as by-products of the fission process in a reactor.

Two other Navy navigation satellites wholly powered by SNAP radioisotope generators also have been orbited. 

The principles underlying radioisotopic power are simple. 

Heat is generated when radiation from a radioisotope is absorbed in surrounding material. The heat produces electricity directly by activating an energy conversion device, either a thermocouple or a thermionic unit. In a thermocouple, two metals at different temperatures produce a voltage, and a thermionic device collects electrons emitted 50

by a hot surface. Several hundred watts of electrical power can be generated in this way. Since radioisotope generators have no moving parts, they are ideally suited for use in satellites and in unattended installations such as remote weather stations and light buoys. Industrial Participation and Diversification 

As a matter of policy, the Commission encourages maximum participation by private industry in nuclear energy affairs. All AEC industrial activities are reconsidered periodically to determine which can be transferred to industry.

U. S. industry now can provide most of the materials and services for the civilian application of atomic energy, with one exception-the production of enriched uranium-235. 

The Commission hopes this activity can be placed on a commercial basis in the future. A technical worker adjusts thermocouples on an isotopic electric generator, using cerium-144. 

A pulsing reactor emits a bright blue flash at the instant of intense radiation. 56

As commercial capability grows and technology advances, realignments are necessary in atomic energy work. Some changes, such as the cutback in production of plutonium and enriched uranium announced in 1964, may cause major readjustments, particularly in communities which grew up in support of atomic energy plants. 

Recognizing a responsibility to these communities, the AEC has adopted a policy of assisting local and state groups in economic development efforts to lessen the impact of cutbacks. This cooperative effort has been helpful in preparing communities for the shutdown of some facilities. Its progress is being closely observed by industry and government.

In addition to the regulatory measures whereby the AEC assures safe handling of radioactive material not under its direct supervision, the AEC has extensive programs of industrial health and safety, fire protection, and radiation protection at all of its own sites. These are designed to protect workers and the public. 

The AEC has an impressive safety record. For example, since the beginning of the atomic energy program in 1942, there have been only seven deaths from nuclear causes among atomic energy workers in the United States. Histori- 60

cally, the atomic energy program has been one of the safest industries in the nation, and radiation accidents represent only a minor fraction of the Commission's low accident rate.
