AMERICA BOASTS THE WORLD'S GREATEST SHAKESPEARIAN COLLECTION 

LACING the Folger Shakespeare 

Library in the national capital has made it the nation's monument to the dramatist whom we rightfully feel is ours as well as England's. Folger's two deepest regards were for America and Shakespeare. He once replied to Dr. S. Parkes Cadman:

I did think of placing the Shakespeare Library at Stratford, near the bones of the great man himself, but I finally concluded I would give it to Washington; for I am an American.

The collection is a very great one, in conception as well as in size and richness. That the most complete Shakespearian collection of all times should be brought together in less than half a century, and by one man's efforts, is

proof beyond words of Folger's enthusiasm and energy.

Folger's active interest dated from his college days at Amherst, when he belonged to a club of students organized to read the plays of Shakespeare aloud.

Among Emerson's works, to which he turned after hearing him lecture, he found particularly appealing the tercentenary eulogy of Shakespeare. Fifty years later this same essay was read at the dedication of the Folger library. 

While in college Folger read Shakespeare in Routledge's "Handy-Volume" series. His interest in editions dated from his purchase for a dollar and a quarter of a Halliwell-Phillipps small scale facsimile of the First Folio, a book which influenced him so much that he made a practice for many years of buying all the copies he saw, to use as graduation gifts. His first collector's

item was a copy of the Fourth Folio 

(1685) which he purchased for $107.50 in 1889, asking for thirty days' credit.

Later purchases included seventy-nine of the two hundred known copies of the First Folio, but the fact of the extraordinarily large number-the British Museum, with the collection next in size, has only five-meant hardly more to him than his ownership of one particular item among them, the identical copy used for the Halliwell-Phillipps facsimile. 

Folger's life was a busy one, but the business career to which he turned after his admission to the New York bar could not keep him from Shakespeare.

Long before he retired, even at the height of his career in Standard Oil (of which he was President from 1911 to 

1924), whatever leisure he could take was pervaded by Shakespeare. Mrs.

Folger, a Vassar College graduate and a former teacher of literature, was a constant help to her husband in his collecting. With her, he made eleven trips to England, going each time to

Stratford-on-Avon. There they were well known, though they made no parade of their collecting and it is recorded that at a time when Folger possessed fifty First Folios, Richard Savage, the custodian of the Shakespeare library at 

Stratford-on-Avon, could not believe that he had even five.

Huntington, the other great collector in Folger's field, was also unobtrusive, and the two always acceded to each other's wishes, never bidding competitively. Rosenbach, who had both men in mind in his purchase of the library of Marsden J. Perry, gave first choice to Folger, because his interests were more specialized than Huntington's.

Folger chose the unique complete copy of the 1619 binding together of nine quartos, the first semblance of a collect ed edition of Shakespeare's works. 

An even more important unique copy is the 1594 quarto of Titus Andronicus, purchased by Folger under romantic conditions. It had been discovered in a peasant's cottage in Sweden, according to an announcement in the New York

Sun in December, 1904. In telling the story of its purchase, Folger, twenty years later, recalled the very weather of the day because he walked the streets for three hours before making up his mind to specify two thousand pounds as the highest price he was willing to pay. In accordance with cabled instructions, his agent started at once from London to Sweden, where, the Sun reported, the book had been deposited for safe-keeping with the Librarian of

the University of Lund. By chance, or perhaps it was not chance but shrewd judgment in current values, two thousand pounds was the precise amount which had been named to two other agents. All three arrived at once. But

Folger's man, who actually had the cash in hand, obtained the book. In 

1929, Folger said that he thought ten times two thousand pounds would not be too much to expect, should the item be returned to the market.

There is no chance of that, however. 

Folger amply endowed the library and left it to the nation under the care of the Board of Trustees of Amherst College. Housing it suitably in Washington was a problem which was skillfully solved. The building is constructed of marble, simple in its lines, harmonizing with the classical architecture of Washington. The exterior is decorated with

scenes from the plays, done in relief by John Gregory. Both outside and in, the walls bear inscriptions by and about Shakespeare. The interior is sugges tive of Tudor English. The Reading 

Room, in the style of an old English hall, has a high roof trussed with wooden beams. Its fireplace is very large and its stained glass windows show tracery similar to that of Trinity Church where 

Shakespeare is buried, but the old glass of that church having disappeared, a new subject was chosen, "The Seven 

Ages of Man" from As You Like It.

Book shelves in two tiers, the upper served by a continuous balcony, accommodate 20,000 books, and from the room there are entrances to vaults containing 18,000 of the most valuable folios and manuscripts. Stairs lead directly to stacks below.

The public is admitted freely to the

Exhibition Hall containing many relics of Shakespeare's day, but entrance to the Reading Room, as in the Huntington Library, is restricted to qualified scholars. The Folger Library and the 

Huntington Library are not public libraries, but gentlemen's private libraries, both of which were perpetuated primarily to preserve rare books for special use. The Folger Shakespeare 

Library is so comprehensive and so beautifully housed that it is not undue boasting to proclaim it as the world's greatest monument to Shakespeare.
