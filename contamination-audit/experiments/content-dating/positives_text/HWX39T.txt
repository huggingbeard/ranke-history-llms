PREFACE 

The present study was prompted, in the first place, by seminars conducted during the summer session of the George Washington University, on the subject of neutrality. In these seminars specific emphasis was placed on the United States Neutrality Act of 1935 and the embargo restrictions on exports to Spain during the Spanish 

Civil War. 

This study was prompted, in the second place, by the opportunity to read the letters that poured into the office of Senator Henry Cabot Lodge, Jr., during the first 2 weeks of the special session of the 

Seventy-sixth Congress. Later I had access to similar correspondence in the office of Senator James J. Davis.

From the opinions of the academicians and of the general public I turned to those of leaders who testified before the Committee on Foreign Relations, and to the legislators themselves whose views were revealed in the printed record. 

My own opinion changed and changed again, yet always I was conscious of a desire to review conditions in past centuries regarding sales of arms and to study the actual attempts at control under both national law in the United States, and under international law. 

This thesis then is submitted primarily as a collection of facts which tell this story, rather than a presentation of arguments and opinions.

THE CONTROL OF SALES OF ARMS, AMMUNITION, AND IMPLEMENTS OF WAR (1545-1939) INTRODUCTION 

The factual data collected for this paper on the control of sales of arms have been organized under three chapter headings-namely, International Law Regarding Sale of Contraband, International

Supervision of the Sale of Arms, and United States Supervision of the 

Sale of Arms. 

Chapter I reviews international law governing the sale of arms as it had developed from the sixteenth through the twentieth century. 

This review includes the various definitions of articles considered by both belligerents and neutrals as arms and ammunition during these centuries, the rights and obligations of the neutral state in selling munitions to belligerents, and the rights of neutral citizens to trade in arms and munitions. 

Prior to and including the period of the World War, international law permitted neutral citizens to trade in arms at their own risk. 

Following the war, in line with efforts toward disarmament and the establishment of a lasting world peace, nations began to realize a serious danger in this uncontrolled trade which international law permitted. Chapter II of this paper deals with the many efforts made under the League Covenant and in international conferences to set up international supervision and control of the traffic in arms and munitions.

Failure to establish a generally accepted international treaty leaves supervision and control of the traffic in arms in the hands of individual nations. Extensive neutrality legislation was recently enacted in the United States, especially in the years 1935-39. Chapter 

III first briefly states the earlier acts, and secondly gives a detailed presentation of American legislation in the years 1935-39. This final chapter closes with the legislation on the statute books as of November 1939. 1

CHAPTER I. INTERNATIONAL LAW REGARDING SALE OF CONTRABAND DEFINITION OF CONTRABAND GOODS 

In any discussion of control of the traffic in arms, ammunition, and implements of war, it is first necessary to define terms. Grotius, the 

Dutch publicist, statesman, and jurist divided all articles into three groups: 

Objects which are of use in war alone, as arms: others which are useless in war, and which serve only for purposes of luxury; and others which can be employed both in war and in peace, as money, provisions, ships, and articles of naval equipment.¹

The Grotian classification was accepted down through the seventeenth, eighteenth, and nineteenth centuries, yet not without differences of opinion in regard to the contents of each class of objects. 

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries it was common to list the articles to be considered as contraband in bilateral treaties. 

A summary of provisions in these treaties laid down during the seventeenth century shows that ideas of contraband varied considerably. It is true that in the majority of treaties munitions of war solely were classified as prohibited goods. Gradually in individual treaties of friendship or alliance, additions, changes or exemptions were made. Three seventeenth-century treaties added horses and soldiers; two, money and ships.2 A treaty of 1626 added metals, money, timber, provisions.3 Several treaties included as contraband, provisions, materials of naval construction, and naval stores. Other treaties were made excluding naval stores, materials of naval construction and provisions, naming them rather, on free lists.5 

In 1625 the Anglo-Dutch treaty of alliance against Spain was promulgated. This agreement is often cited as the first to use the term contraband in the modern sense. Article XX of the treaty presents the contraband concept in full development:

All contraband goods, such as Ammunition and provisions, ships, arms, sails, cordages, Gold, Silver, Copper, Iron, Lead, and the like, design'd to be carry'd by any one from any Port into Spain, or into any other Dominions under the Obedience of the said King of Spain, or his Adherents, shall be Good Prize, together with the Men, and Ships that carry them."

England, even as early as the sixteenth century, began a policy favoring extension of contraband lists. Sir Leoline Jenkins, an English authority on international law, explains that articles of direct use for warlike purposes were considered contraband but that each state should possess the right to draw up at the opening of war a list of articles to be contraband during the war's continuance.' The trend of later centuries has been to increase the number of articles named as contraband. Modern statesmen have by no means completely solved all the difficulties surrounding the definition of contraband. The writings of such publicists, as Kent, Wheaton, Manning, Ortolan, Bluntschli, and Heffter not only set forth lengthy enumerations but attempt to argue logically that contraband of war cannot be limited to munitions.19 Hall, himself an eminent authority, in commenting on the writings of these men, states: 

It appears to me that, though no great precision can from the nature of things be obtained, articles other than munitions of war may to some extent be classified according to the greater or less intimacy of their association with warlike operations, and consequently according to the less or greater urgency of circumstance under which a belligerent may fairly prevent their access to his enemy; it being nevertheless in all cases understood that usage shall receive its full value insofar as it weighs in favor of a particular custom.20 

Long-continued practice and treaty definition however have generally limited for inclusion within the category of contraband of war commodities exclusively devoted to warlike functions.

Absolute contraband at the outbreak of the World War consisted of goods exclusively used for war. Then on August 4, 1914, the British issued an order in council which proved to be the first of a long list of contraband decrees made by them. On September 21, a revised list was published, with many articles which were formerly conditional contraband named as absolute contraband, with articles on the free list of the Declaration now being named as absolute contraband. 

Just as the English had been proponents in the past of the inclusion of foodstuffs and naval stores, now England was the first to stamp metallic ores and ingredients of explosives as absolute contraband.

After continual revision and the publication of many lists, the distinction between absolute and conditional contraband was disregarded entirely. British lists were including as many as 181 items as contraband of war. The British claimed that modern science could so utilize raw materials that the innocent free import finally became part of the finished weapon of destruction. Cotton could be used for explosives, and lard to make nitroglycerin. It was almost correct to say that any commodity which could by any possibility be deemed to be useful to the armed forces or the government or the people of an enemy was ipso facto contraband.23

SALE OF CONTRABAND GOODS 

In discussing the law governing the sale of contraband a fine distinction must be drawn between sales made by a neutral state itself to a belligerent state and sales by citizens of a neutral state to a belligerent state. Before explaining this distinction it would be wise to set forth the right of the belligerent state in regard to these sales of contraband to the enemy.

It was early recognized that the belligerent state had the right to intercept absolute contraband and that it had the right to capture, confiscate, and punish through the decisions of its own prize courts. 

As early as 1545 an English proclamation read: 

Her Majesty thynketh and knoweth it by the rules of the law as well of nature as of men, and specially by the law civil, that whenever any doth directly help her enemy with succours of any victell, armor, or any kynd of munition to enhable his shippes to maintain themselves, she may lawfully interrupt the same; and this agreeth with the law of God, the law of nature, the law of nations, and hath been in all tymes practised and in all countries betwyxt prynce and prynce, and country and country.24

OBLIGATIONS OF NEUTRAL STATES 

It has previously been stated that in any discussion of neutral rights and obligations as they concern contraband a distinction must be drawn between sales of contraband made by a neutral state to a belligerent state, and sales by citizens of a neutral state to a belligerent state.

Sales made by the neutral state were largely dominated by the binding provisions of treaties during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. Some of the general principles later accepted by states in their relations with each other were evolving, but always the fulfillment of a prior treaty obligation dictated the position to be taken by a given neutral and a belligerent state in a given war. 

Treaties of simple peace and friendship and treaties of defensive alliance (made prior to the war) stipulated that the contracting states would not assist the enemies of the other with forces, or subsidies, or by indirect means. The Duke of Brunswick promised Louis XIV in a treaty of 1675 not to assist any enemies of France directly or indirectly, not to permit any levy of troops to be made in his states, nor to permit passage of troops through his states.28 

Neutral states in other cases pledged to the signatories actual aid in troops, money, munitions of war, the purchasing, lading, and equipment of war vessels, the raising of soldiers and seamen, and the hire of ships. This is an example of the fact that it was permissible to assist one belligerent without abandoning neutrality when the state had entered into a treaty prior to the outbreak of war.

Later more emphasis was laid on the duty of a neutral state not to give assistance to warring nations. In defining neutrals Bynkershoek held- I call those non-enemies who are of neither party in a war, and who owe nothing by treaty to one side or to the other. If they are under any such obligation they are not mere friends but allies-Their duty is to use all care not to meddle in the war—If I am neutral, I cannot advantage one party, lest I injure the other-It is more essential to remain in amity with both than to favour the hostilitise of one at the cost of a tacit renunciation of the friendship of the other.29 

Neutral states adhered to neither side, did not mix themselves up in war, were in amity with both parties, and furthermore, owed to each other only what was due in time of general peace. 

Vattel, writing in 1758, went farther perhaps than any writer of the century. Neutrality consisted inan impartial attitude so far as the war is concerned, and so far only; and it requires: 

1st, that the neutral people shall abstain from furnishing help when they are under no prior obligation to grant it, and from making free gifts of troops, arms, munitions, or anything else of direct use in war. I say that they must abstain equally, for it would be absurd that a state should succour two enemies at the same moment. Besides, it would be impossible to do so equally; the very same things-the same number of troops, the same quantity of arms, of munitions, etc., furnished under different circumstances, are not equivalent succour. 2nd, that in all matters not bearing upon the war a neutral and impartial nation shall not refuse to one of the parties, because of the existing quarrel, that which it accords to the other.30

Gradually the roots were being laid down for two principles which were accepted later. These were those of impartiality and of abstention. Under these principles the neutral state treated both parties to the war alike and refrained, as a state, from giving active aid to either belligerent.

Substantial agreement on neutral rights was reached by many of the leading nations and was embodied in the clauses of multipartite declarations and conventions during the century prior to the World War. In concise language the Hague Conventions in 1907 stipulated for the neutral state these principles of impartiality and abstention: 

Every measure of restriction or prohibition taken by a neutral power * * * must be impartially applied by it to both belligerents (art. 9, fifth convention). * * *Seeing that it is, for neutral powers, an admitted duty to apply these rules impartially to the several belligerents * * * (preamble, thirteenth convention).

A neutral power must apply impartially to the two belligerents the conditions, restrictions, or prohibitions made by it in regard to the admission into its ports, roadsteads, or territorial waters, of belligerent warships or of their prizes (art. 9, thirteenth convention).

The supply, in any manner, directly or indirectly, by a neutral power to a belligerent power, of warships, ammunition, or war materials of any kind whatever, is forbidden (art. 6, thirteenth convention).³¹

In the light of these Hague Convention articles it is a comparatively easy task to draw conclusions. The neutral state regardless of treaty, sympathies, or views as to the justice of the quarrel must refrain from giving aid to either belligerent. Sales of munitions by a neutral government to either belligerent are universally condemned.

RIGHTS OF NEUTRAL CITIZENS 

This strict abstinence from sales of contraband by a neutral state was not necessary where sales of contraband were made by citizens of the neutral state. It became a well-established fact that the individual citizens of the neutral state had the right in time of war to carry on commerce as in peacetime. The theory was that trade on the part of neutral individuals was a commercial act; that trade was not directed against the belligerent. It was carried on rather for business and for profit and not to harm a particular individual or nation. Writing to Hammond in 1793 Jefferson claimed: 

Our citizens have always been free to make, vend, and export arms. It is the constant occupation and livelihood of some of them. To suppress their callings, the only means perhaps of their subsistence, because a war exists in foreign and distant countries, in which we have no concern, would scarcely be expected. It would be hard in principle and impossible in practice. The law of nations, therefore, respecting the rights of those at peace does not require from them such an internal derangement of their occupations.32 

It was not the duty of a neutral state to prohibit either the manufacture of or the sale of arms by private citizens, nor was it illegal to permit a free and unrestricted supply of arms to all belligerents by neutral citizens. International law, as laid down in the Hague Conventions fully set forth these doctrines in the following article: 

A neutral power is not called upon to prevent the export or transport, on behalf of one or other of the belligerents of arms, munitions of war, or, in general, of anything which can be of use to an army or a fleet.33 

Though national and international law established the legality of sales by citizens of the state, the neutral state itself completely refused to take the responsibility of protection for its nationals against capture, punishment, forfeiture, or confiscation of contraband goods. The 

United States Neutrality Act of 1793 set forth the obligations this country believed to be incumbent upon neutrals; in the main the law followed the standards set by international law, yet stated that law with strength and firmness. The scope of the act was most comprehensive. It defined the acts which the neutral government is bound to prevent and the acts in which the neutral government withholds its protection from citizens who suffer penalties. The President's proclamation and Hamilton's instructions to the collectors of customs of the same year made it clear that American citizens could sell merchandise to belligerents, yet at the same time, American citizens were warned that they need not expect the support or protection of their government if they were penalized by punishment or forfeiture.34 

To draw a conclusion in regard to definition and sale of contraband is to indulge to some extent in repetition. A law of contraband existed, notwithstanding the fluctuations and inconsistencies in its execution. England continually opposed nations in continental Europe, for she favored wide restrictions on trade and her courts broadly interpreted not only the character of the goods carried, but their ultimate destination directly or by continuous voyage. CHAPTER II. INTERNATIONAL SUPERVISION OF SALE OF ARMS 

It would be difficult to determine just when it was first realized that trade in arms and munitions constituted a serious danger and that supervision and control should supersede freedom of trade. An early convention, known as the General Act of Brussels, was drafted and ratified. The act signed at Brussels, July 2, 1890, provided: 

for the repression of the African slave trade and the restriction of the importation into, and sale in, a certain defined zone of the African continent, of firearms, ammunition, and spirituous liquors.¹

The idea of supervision and control, however, has been given worldwide discussion since the close of the World War. The statesmen who drafted the Covenant of the League of Nations in article XXIII provided the constitutional basis for the many League efforts toward control that followed during the years after the war. 

Subject to and in accordance with the provisions of international conventions existing or hereafter to be agreed upon, the Members of the League will entrust the League with the general supervision of the trade in arms and ammunition with the countries in which the control of this traffic is necessary in the common interest.2

Not only was this proviso laid down for supervision and control of the trade in arms, but an even broader, more far-reaching article dealing with the manufacture of arms was embodied in the Covenant: 

The members of the League agree that the manufacture by private enterprise of munitions and implements of war is open to grave objections. The Council shall advise how the evil effects attendant upon such manufacture can be prevented, due regard being had to the necessities of those Members of the League which are not able to manufacture the munitions and implements of war necessary for their safety.3

A convention for the control of the trade in arms and ammunition was signed at St. Germain en Laye and Paris on September 10, 1919, 2 months after the signing of the Treaty of Versailles. Though this seems to indicate a keen awareness of the need for control, followed by quick action to implement that control, the reverse has proved true. Many commissions, special committees, and conferences, drawing up as many conventions and draft treaties fill the record of League action from 1919 to 1934. The student feels utterly baffled with the volume of effort, with the pages of hearings, debates, and proposals. The most organized method of presentation is to outline these major organs, set up under the League Covenant, together with their major proposals and their real importance. The first major convention drafted after the war was the Convention of St. Germain. It was drafted on the assumption that it would be universally accepted by the governments of the countries assembled to draw up the Treaty of Versailles. Twenty-eight powers signed the convention, including the United States, but the convention was never ratified during the succeeding 6 years. The League adopted strong resolutions in 1920 and 1921 to secure ratification and letters were written to governments asking for opinions, objections, and action. Eleven nations then ratified the convention, making their ratification contingent, however, upon that of the other signatory powers. The United States refused to ratify, and principally because of the action of this Government the leading arms-producing powers likewise refused ratification."

The convention had never been formally submitted to the United States Senate. After the Senate had refused ratification of the 

Treaty of Versailles it was not even transmitted to that body by the President.' The secretary-general of the League addressed a note to the United States Government on November 21, 1921, seeking to secure favorable action by the United States. The third paragraph of the reply of the Secretary of State, addressed to the secretary-general of the League, under date of July 28, 1922, states the position taken by the United States: 

In reply to the inquiry made by the secretary-general in pursuance of this action, whether the Government of the United States of America is prepared to ratify the Convention of St. Germain, the Secretary of State begs to state that the terms of the proposed convention have been carefully examined and that while the Government of the United States is in cordial sympathy with efforts to restrict traffic in arms and munitions of war, it finds itself unable to approve the provisions of the convention and to give any assurance of its ratification. 

The answer was a complete refusal and the United States is held responsible for the failure of the Convention of St. Germain. Eleven governments had ratified the convention and four others were willing to adhere. CHAPTER III. UNITED STATES SUPERVISION OF SALE OF ARMS 

The first pieces of United States legislation relating to the traffic in arms were a series of joint resolutions passed by Congress to control the sale of arms to Central and South American countries. During the latter part of the eighteenth and the early part of the nineteenth century civil strife in these countries had been frequent and disastrous, and the United States wanted rebels and insurrections suppressed. 

United States recognized both the independence and the rights of these American republics and entered into diplomatic relations with their established governments. Congress enacted the joint resolutions to prevent the export of arms intended for the use of rebels against authorized governments. The earliest of these resolutions was approved on April 22, 1898, in connection with the War with Spain and conferred upon the President the power to prohibit the export of war materials "within his discretion and with such limitations and exemptions as would seem to him expedient." President Theodore 

Roosevelt used the power conferred upon the Executive by this act in a Presidential proclamation of October 14, 1905. In the proclamation he forbade "the export of arms, ammunition, and munitions of war of every kind from any port in the United States or in Porto Rico to any port in the Dominican Republic." 2 1 March 14, 1912, Congress enacted a joint resolution which among other things provided:

That whenever the President shall find that in any American country conditions of domestic violence exist which are promoted by the use of arms or munitions of war procured from the United States, and shall make proclamation thereof, it shall be unlawful to export except under such limitations and exceptions as the President shall prescribe any arms or munitions of war from any place in the United States to such country until otherwise ordered by the President or by Congress.3

The resolution and a later proclamation by President Taft warning against the commission of unneutral acts attempted to suppress the traffic in arms between the United States and Mexico during a 

Mexican insurrection.¹

The May 1937 Neutrality Act in addition to vesting discretionary power in the President to lay an embargo in case of civil strife, prescribed when the President found restrictions on shipment of articles or materials in addition to arms, ammunition, and implements of war were necessary for the security and peace of the United States, he had the right to forbid the carrying of these articles on American vessels either directly or for transshipment to a belligerent. The act required the President to enumerate these articles. Once enumerated it was not only illegal to transport them on an American vessel but all right, title, and interest must have been transferred to a foreign government, agency, institution, association, partnership, corporation, or national before the goods are exported from the United States.49 

Upon the outbreak of war in Europe in September 1939, President Roosevelt invoked the Neutrality Act of May 1937 and proclaimed embargoes on shipments of arms to France, Germany, Poland, the

United Kingdom, India, Australia, New Zealand, and Čanada. How ever, during the previous summer Cordell Hull, Secretary of State, had sounded the keynote of the administration's views on arms embargoes. 

The Secretary contended that at the time the arms embargo was originally adopted he had called attention to the factthat its enactment constituted a hazardous departure from the principle of international law which recognizes the right of neutrals to trade with belligerents and of belligerents to trade with neutrals. "A continuation of the trade in arms," he claimedis a clearly recognized and traditional right of the nationals of a neutral country in time of war, subject only to effective blockade and to the right of belligerents to treat any such commodities as contraband.50

The legislation enacted by the special session of the Seventy-sixth Congress held intact the administrative organs of the National Munitions Control Board and the Division of Controls of the State Department and the administrative functions of both.51 On the other hand, after long and stormy debate Congress, in the act passed November 4, 1939, permits Americans to sell arms and munitions to all belligerents with these restrictions: 

1. No American ship may carry these goods into combat areas (these defined by the President) or to any state named as a belligerent in Presidential proclamations. 

2. There must be a complete transfer of title to foreign ownership before these goods leave the ports of the United States. In other words all right, title, and interest in the arms and munitions for export to a belligerent must be transferred to foreign ownership at the port of lading in the United States before they are exported.52

The pattern for the latter restriction, popularly known as the cashand-carry clause, was found in the act of May 1, 1937, for the President then had been given power to place restrictions on the shipment of articles or materials in addition to arms, if necessary for the peace of the United States or for the protection of American citizens. 

Thus to write the final chapter of the history of the embargo under the neutrality laws of the years 1935 to 1939 is to return again toward tradition and international law, but with greater government control and supervision.

CONCLUSION 

This study has attempted to present the major accomplishments toward both the international and national supervision and control of the sale of arms.

It has been widely recognized that no effective control of the traffic in arms can be established except by international agreement. But international supervision and control of the traffic has not yet been effected.

In the absence of an effective international convention the establishment of a measure of supervision and control by domestic legislation has been successful. In the United States control was resorted to only in exceptional circumstances and on a restricted scale until after the year 1935, when it became a regular and continuing function of the Federal Government. Today the Nation is in possession of complete and current information in regard to the exact quantities of arms which leave the United States; information is available concerning the country of destination and the identity of the exporter. 

The National Munitions Control Board feels that it is of particularly vital importance to publicize this information at a time like the present when many of the principal nations of the world are at war and when arms are being purchased in the United States in unprecedented quantities. The fact that exportation is subject to license at all times and that customs authorities regularly supervise the exportation renders more certain and thorough the enforcement of restrictions imposed by law and the close observation of the effect of policies adopted in regard to the exportation of arms. 

The groundwork for supervision and control of sales of arms has been laid. The problems incident to that control have been recognized. The administrative machinery necessary for control and supervision has been set up. It is my belief that through the steps outlined in this study the nations of the world have begun to formulate a system of arms control which may in the future prove to be a valuable guide in establishing supervision and control of the traffic in arms. 26
