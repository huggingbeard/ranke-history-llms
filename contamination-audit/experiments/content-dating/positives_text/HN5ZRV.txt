PREFACE BY THE AUTHOR 

Can any normal human being turn to the newspapers today and realize that he is a member of the human society whose actions are illustrated in the news of the day?

Can the reader imagine that governments who are organized for the protection of their nationals, indulge in blackmail, arson and robbery of their own minorities?

Who would think that in the twentieth century citizens by the thousands would be driven from their native lands, penniless, homeless and with no place to go?

Can the few democratic nations left in this world permit these conditions, which are threatening their very existence to continue? 

Are we facing a dictatorship of mankind by ruthless leaders, who deny our birthright of free speech and expression and burn the very books that still enlighten us? THEY CAN

Every thinking man, women and child, knows that peace on earth and good-will toward mankind is our solution-but what can we do right now to obtain it before it is too late? 

The political theories we are faced with do not offer much assistance. Democracy, which claims that all men are created equal in the pursuit of happiness, is somehow not functioning that way. There are in these so-called democratic countries millions of unemployed people and millions working at sub-standard wages, who by reason of reduced or eliminated incomes are not equal to the remaining citizens in their pursuit of happiness. 

Socialism presents some fine teachings, but no plan that the people can absorb to remedy their economic injustices, without first accepting the political theories, which would require time and mass education. 

Communism has been associated in our minds with political and military revolution against existing governments—and that is abhorent to the senses of liberal men. Revolution against government destroys all that is good in the present age before the new organization has the chance to function for improving conditions, and in so doing brings further suffering while adjustments are being made. Fascism has proven to be the creed of the dictators who buoy up their followers with teachings of hate, envy, treachery, even murder in the vilest of forms, to spread these poisonous doctrines among any whom they can contact.

What teachings are there in the world today for the liberal man to turn to for a solution?

If there are none, can we just sit down and await the world catastrophe that must come? In the light of the expenditure of twenty billions of dollars in one year for armaments by an impoverished group of nations, the hate and envy that is being fomented in every nation, and the hidden propaganda that is being spread among the democracies designed to divide their own nationals on religious and racial issues-could we expect peace?

Is war the solution? We know the dangers of revolution of the people against their government in case of war; desperate and disillusioned masses trying to remove a yoke that they themselves do not understand. In the sudden turmoil of destruction, hunger and confusion that war brings, will an atmosphere prevail conducive to cool, calm and balanced leadership; or will the arm of a tyrant intoxicated with sudden power strengthen his position by destroying all criticism? No, war is not the solution.

Yet we must do something. We cannot harden our consciences to the facts and shut our eyes to the future. If we do, it means that our intellects, the most wonderful creation that providence has given us, have also been paralyzed. 

The author cannot admit to that as yet. He has therefore given you his analysis of the menaces that are gnawing at our very vitals, and a practical solution for our ills, without political change, without regulation of Private Industry, but with a plan that eliminates the waste of our present economic and social system. 

Please read this book not with the thought in back of your mind that you are in a safe financial position to weather the storm that may befall you, as history teaches us that mobs have no reason and draw no lines; but read it as a means of enabling you to right a desperate wrong before it is too late. 

I dedicate this book to the millions of suffering humanity in every nation who have no one to turn to in an embittered world. 

THE AUTHOR.

CHAPTER I WHAT'S WRONG? 

Civilized countries arming to the teeth for the destruction of human beings, going bankrupt to do so. 

Strikes of workers all over the world for some security and a decent living.

Increase of taxes and levies beyond human endurance, breaking the backs of the majority of people. 

Dictatorships denying the right of free speech and expression to millions of human beings. 

Persecution of the minorities and forcing these people to leave their native homes to start all over again in lands no better off than their own.

Honest industry infested with racketeers employed to maintain false price standards.

Crime expanding, and the very youth of the nations becoming hardened criminals.

Insanity increasing because of the complexity of making a living in a merry-go-round of uncontrollable factors. 

Ignorance of the people encouraged by means of lies and false propaganda.

Unemployment being satisfied by the grant of small wages from governments, and the workers' labor wasted in expenditures for armaments and war machinery. 

All this has made hate and envy the by-word of most of the nations. One has only to read the daily newspapers to realize the dangers of war and revolution that exist in all parts of the world today. Our modern means of destruction consisting of bombing airplanes, high speed explosives, and poisonous gases threaten the destruction of our very homes and families unless some definite plan can be developed to stop this crazy inhuman whirlpool of present day existence.

The leaders of political parties who seek our vote promise us that they will eliminate unemployment, they will raise the standard of living, and they will at the same time reduce taxes. When they are questioned as to a definite plan for bringing this about, none is forthcoming, because they have not discovered the basic cause of the world's madness. Until they do, their remedies are surface cures that disrupt more than they cure. They seek to regulate industry without a knowledge of industry. They tax profits, but do not replenish losses.

They spend public money entirely out of proportion to what the public can afford to spend. They pauperize the poverty-stricken unemployed by assigning the majority of them to useless tasks and wages that do not assure a decent living, but just a mere existence. 

They fail to give any employment to those who are willing to work but who haven't as yet reached a desperate and pauperized stage. 

They use public money for a haven of refuge for their political henchmen. They give the public flowery speeches instead of facts. They juggle the currency so that the cost of the necessities of life are not constant. They complicate administration by a subterfuge of myriads of departments, criss-crossing each other. They fail to perform as 1

representatives of the people the things which we seek. These are peace, sufficient food, clothing, shelter, education, and security for all those who are willing to work and earn these necessities. 

We are over-awed, over-manned, and overcome by leaders who grope in the darkness, hoping for miracles that do not happen. What is the cause of all this madness and rush to nobody knows where? 

Let us, fellow citizens in various fields of endeavor, get together around a large table and analyze the problems we individually have. 

CHAPTER II 

The most important figure is our youth. Upon him depends the future. So first around the table is a high school graduate, fresh from school, aiming to go out and find in the practical world the additional experience he requires to provide for his needs, and to develop him into a future citizen.

He has, in a general way at school, found the work that he is inclined to do. It may be mechanical, artistic, commercial or academic work. He has in his mind a belief that the whole business world is open to him, so that he can seek the employment that he likes best. 

He commences his search via the usual medium of "Help Wanted" columns in the newspapers. He meets with the usual greeting: “What experience have you had?" Replying in the negative, he is shown the door. After a number of interviews, he realizes that he cannot obtain a position without experience, and that without a position he cannot obtain experience. So he decides to lie, and his mind conceives mixing dates, and visions of former employers, all for the opportunity to work.

He finally obtains a position. After a period, either through lack of adaptability—physical or mental, or because of conditions of depression, seasonal activity, or a dozen other factors over which he has no control, he is discharged to go out and find another position. 

He follows the usual routine and finds that the practical reasons for his discharge are common to the other concerns engaged in the same activity and that are governed by the same market conditions. 

After the failure of many attempts to procure employment in the same capacity, he finally takes a job in another field. His previous experience serves but little purpose towards higher income. A period elapses; he again finds himself out of a job. He repeats his search all over again. A lapse of years and continued changes force him to accept any position that can be obtained. He is now a full fledged member in the ranks of unskilled labor, his earning capacity too low for a decent income on which to marry and raise a family. 

This discouraged youth, who spends weeks, months, and possibly years hunting miscellaneous jobs is ripe for any scheme that spells easy money-a straight path to rackets and crime. Multiply the number of wasted hours of each discouraged youth looking for a job, by the millions of youths in each country, and you will realize the tremendous cost that has been included in higher prices to cover this waste. If there was a plan whereby they could be trained for the work in which they are naturally adapted-with an opportunity given for steady employment in their particular field, this wasted time could be utilized towards the production of their necessities. 

The college graduate, our second member around the table, has 2

had a few more years than the high school youth, in which to learn more about his qualifications. He also enters a business world that hires help only when needed. He alone bears his loss of income and the discouragement which follows. The employers who are themselves subject to uncertain markets with its indefinite income cannot assume the responsibility of steady employment. 

College men spend years of preparation as a foundation for building up a future as leaders in their chosen field. After they are graduated, due to the uncertainties of employment, it is difficult for them to find work that will give them a broad business training in their specific field. How few of them ever receive the necessary broad experience in any one field is attested by the fact of the scarcity of executives who can manage branches of industry. No enterprise can prosper where qualified management is lacking. This destruction of the skill of potential leaders on the rocks of unemployment, in lack of direction and uncertainty of outlook, is a terrific waste of the productive facilities in any country. This waste is included in the cost of the articles the people buy. 

CHAPTER III 

Our third member around the table is the small retail business man. He may be a retail grocer, dry goods dealer, furniture dealer, or one of the thousand others that crop up on Main Street, “Open for Business". With a few thousand dollars, generally the surplus he has saved from the guts of his family and himself-denying everything but a mere existence for years, he appoints himself as a committee of one to decide whether Main Street needs a new retail store. 

He has no data as to whether the store is superfluous or not, or whether the proprietors of the stores already existing are making a living. He just plunges and takes a chance. 

At first, his specials of items at cost price attract purchasers from the other stores. This influx of customers inspires him to immediately add to his lines, duplicating stock that neighboring stores are carrying. His capital is gradually frozen in merchandise that must be moved in order to operate on his limited capital. 

Following measures universally employed, he cuts prices generally below cost, to move his stock, and in the process, receives calls for merchandise other than what he has. He obtains these items from the wholesaler on credit, on the basis of the magnificent sales he is making. A period of juggling in this manner finally discloses that he needs more capital to cover his losses. 

Faced with the prospect of going out on the almost hopeless task of finding a job in the ranks of the unemployed, he experiences a feeling of despair, and becomes desperate to retain his business. Having exhausted his own capital, he commences to borrow from his intimate and also distant relatives, squeezing every penny he can lay his hands on. As his money disappears, he becomes lax in the payment of his bills, and before long, after much struggle and worry, he goes bankrupt. 

The losses incurred through his bankruptcy, and the cost to the government of carrying him along until he is in a position to find a job, is an obligation that the public is forced to assume and is included in the price of the articles sold.

There are many stores that purchase expensive fixtures and spend a considerable amount on advertising when they open up, only to find that either the rent is too high for the volume they do or the poor location forces them to close up. These losses over their own lost capital are some of the costs that are included in the price of the products you purchase. Is it any wonder that you pay in many retail purchases a gross profit of over 40 per cent above the actual cost of the merchandise?

Many stores today are surviving only because they pay next to starvation wages to their employees. A number of them substitute seconds and shoddy merchandise and sell them at first grade prices. 

A large number of stores pay too much overhead for the small volume they do. They manage to exist by having installment sales to consumers who live from hand to mouth, and who are compelled to pay the exorbitant charges requested.

Think of all the duplicated stocks carried by stores in the same neighborhood catering to the same public. Those stocks represent tying up of capital that bears interest and has to be paid for by the consumers who buy from these stores.

This waste in retail merchandising has precipitated into the market chain stores. They are better organized in the data of picking the proper location, but, in turn also have to compete with other chain stores seeking that same location. One of them takes second, third, or fourth place and the cut-throat competition continues. 

Their losses are not assumed by relatives as the small man's are, but are taken in a dignified manner from ignorant investors, who are cleaned out of their investments, robbed of the little security they have been able to accumulate over a period of years. 

There are some chain stores that have managed to remain on a profitable business basis, but at the cost of squeezing the manufacturers to the extent of what the traffic will bear. These manufacturers in turn have to squeeze their employees who cannot resist. 

The small proprietor, who cannot make a living and who has too much responsibility, would be only too glad to have an opportunity to work in the capacity for which he is fitted. Especially is this true if he is sure of making a good living with security of employment and an opportunity to raise his standard of living if he so desires.

CHAPTER IV 

He

The fourth man around the table is the small manufacturer. may make dresses, handbags, neckties, or any other item that we use every day. These enterprises are generally founded by one who has been employed in some branch of the business previously, perhaps as operator, executive, salesman, etc. The founder usually seeks a partner or partners, who can supplement his knowledge of the business, or who can furnish capital. 

They start off in this new venture for one of many reasons. 

Either because they were dissatisfied with their past working conditions, or because they desire to raise their previous incomes as employees, or perhaps they want to feel independent, and be their own boss.

The first difficulty they encounter is in the selection of a design

or a model that will take, so as to enable them to book orders. There are but few gifted men in any industry who can design an item that has popular appeal from the standpoint of fashion, coupled with economical manufacturing and good sales value. These men are gobbled up by the large manufacturers who can afford to pay the price of their services, due to their enormous volume of sales. 

The small manufacturer, therefore, has to copy these creations, possibly with some slight revision, but nevertheless the same idea. 

With the copied designs, they interview the store buyers who would rather do business with the large manufacturer, because of his dependability. The buyer, therefore, becomes a sharp trader with the small manufacturer, demanding concessions of price, shipments, and conditions that the small man cannot afford. He has to pay at least the same prices as the large company for raw materials, supplies, and labor. In fact, his expenses are proportionately higher, for he has to have in his small working space a similar showroom to the one of the large manufacturer. He has to have a bookkeeper to record his accounts. He generally has to pay more for his materials and supplies than the large manufacturer, as he purchases in smaller quantities. If the pay of labor were equal with both the small and large manufacturer, the only concession the small manufacturer could make to the store buyer would be a sharing of all profits. The profit is a very small percentage in the manufacture of necessities, so a sharing of this would not be much of a concession. 

He is therefore compelled to resort to sharp practices. First he tries to conserve on working space by crowding into a small area the largest number of workers he can get away with within the law. 

He next attempts to find sub-contractors. These generally consist of a man and wife, and a few non-union help. By chiseling these contractors down to a bare existence, he manages to cut his labor costs. 

This results in cheap workmanship. These forced savings, plus a good percentage of his profits, if not all, make up the concessions that the small manufacturer must give to the buyer in order to exist in business.

The poor working conditions of the small manufacturer precipitate a lot of dissatisfaction among the workers, thus causing continual strikes in labor which endeavor thereby to force changes. This waste is generally too much for the meager profit of the business, and the small manufacturer goes bankrupt; after he has, however, inflated his credit to the maximum with the firms that have supplied materials to him.

All these small manufacturers, I am sure, would like to drop their nerve-racking problems. It is not pleasant to have to serve as a combination purchasing agent, sales manager, credit man, strike mediator and complaint clerk. It is not easy to have to contend in addition to these other duties with the financial maneuver of present day small business. These manufacturers, I am sure, would rather change to a position where they could function in the capacity for which they are adapted at a decent living wage, decent hours, a higher standard of living and security of income. 

The public would also save the expense of each small enterprise losing generally more than its own capital going in and out of business. It would save the waste of labor being thrown out of employment every time a company is started and bankrupt. It would

save the waste of constant strikes due to uncertain conditions that labor has to contend with in the fight for existence between these small enterprises.

The fatality among these businesses is tremendous. One of the main reasons a few of them have survived and grown large was because they happened to copy a couple of items that had sensational appeal. The profits resulting from them were sufficient to raise the small man to the class of the large manufacturer. Very few of the small manufacturers, however, have managed to survive in business for any long period of time.

CHAPTER V 

The large manufacturer is fifth around the table. He may be the chairman of the Board of Directors of a large corporation, or one of several partners in a large limited company, or a sole proprietor. He is engaged in the production and sale of one or more items on a large scale, that the public needs. He may have formerly owned a small company that survived the pitfalls of small business, or may have purchased several small companies to eliminate duplicate operations. 

By combining manufacturing facilities he was enabled to cut down space, reduce inventory, simplify overhead expenses, and obtain more efficient management, the combination of which would serve to reduce the sales price of the product. In this way he can maintain the proper designers who can produce products that appeal to the consumer and have good sales value. The large company is now given preference by the store buyers over those of the small businesses. 

However, on account of their size, they find that they must have tremendous volume of orders to cover their overhead expenses. How is this volume determined? By territory? No! By the needs of the people? No! It is determined by the amount of high pressure sales and advertising that each individual company can afford to expend, and the price it can afford to meet in competition with other similar companies. All the individual companies in a certain industry soon adopt the most efficient methods of production, due to the standard machinery developed for that industry. The cost of labor generally becomes standard, and the price of competing products should be about the same. In order to exact a saving, and be below competition, each concern strives to do the maximum volume, irrespective of whether the market is there or not. They build up advertising and sales organizations to feature the slightest difference in design as a change of revolutionary magnitude, which serves only to confuse the buyer, in the long run. The public generally buys the most advertised brand, whether or not it truthfully represents the best value. These high pressure sales and advertising programs result in consumers buying. The mortgage and installment plan creates the desire in the individual to buy things, whether he can afford them or not. The installment system makes every purchase easy, and causes people to mortgage their future income, which is indefinite, with obligations that are definite. All is well until recessions in the business cycle create additional unemployment, and people find that their obligations are difficult enough to settle, without incurring new ones. A sudden drop in the demand for all goods, with a further decrease in

production; the cycle continues, bringing in its path worry, discouragement, poverty, hunger and increases in taxes due to the government's obligation in giving some subsistence to the unemployed. 

Large manufacturing units are therefore always in the process of expanding when times are what they call good, with more buildings, more machinery, and more employment, and are at a loss to know what to do with them when times become bad. If it were possible to pay employees sufficient in these good times to balance a good income for all times, there would still be a waste of idle machinery, plants and equipment. But, the wages of the employees do not even warrant a good standard of living at the height of our so-called prosperity, let alone affording them a surplus to cover poor times. 

The difficulty of employees in making a decent living even in the best of times creates great dissatisfaction, and causes strikes, bringing additional unemployment and waste of idle plants to an already overburdened structure of business. 

Think of the losses that are caused by the waste of labor in large armies of the unemployed, the waste of strikes and lockouts, the waste of duplicate sales effort and advertising, that cater to the same market. All these wastes have to be borne by the public in the cost of manufacture and sale of the items they buy. 

The large manufacturer today is paying dearly for the right to say that he is in business. He has one royal headache trying to guess his next move. In idle times he has too much inventory to move, more help than he can use, more expense than he can carry, his investments dropping in value, a constant red balance, which makes him worry, lose sleep, and become nervous. 

In active times, due to the sudden increase in demand, he cannot obtain inventory quick enough to move with insufficient help. His expenses will rise out of proportion unless carefully checked up. The previous depression makes it doubtful whom to trust, and how much. 

The constant fluctuations in prices of materials are factors over which he has no control. The lack of trained executives in his field place a heavy burden on his shoulders, making him prematurely old. 

The insecurity, worry, and concentration necessary to make a profit today in any business except a virtual monopoly, robs the business man's energy to such an extent that he cannot spend the time with his family. He cannot relax and enjoy the profit that he does make.

With all this, his surplus that is invested for future protection is not secure because of changing values. His family at any time may find itself poverty-stricken, the little temporary luxury a passing dream. How many business men secretly wish they could throw the whole thing over, if it were possible for them to do so. 

While problems make life interesting, they are only so when you understand and recognize the factors entering into the solution. 

When a problem is such a gamble that any move may spell disaster when life savings can be lost at a wrong guess, where the other fellow's mistake may mean that a mature business man is thrown into the ranks of the unemployed-living on a bare subsistence wage, this gamble loses interest.

The business man developing an ego from being looked up to by many employees as the boss, generally does not realize how insecure is his position. One look around at the fatalities of large industries

in only ten years of past history will soon convince him that he is only a log that can be seen floating on the water, but subject to the direction of the river tides at will. 

CHAPTER VI 

Sixth around the table is the farmer. He may be a man who works with the aid of his family, cultivating a small tract of land that he owns, or one who has hundreds of acres, using hundreds of workers, or he may be just a tenant on the land, leasing it from year to year.

He decides that his land is specifically suited to produce a certain crop or group of crops. At the time of cultivation, he has no definite knowledge of what the demand will be, or what he can obtain in exchange for the crops he is going to raise. He therefore sows as intensively as he can on the amount of land he owns. He invests in seed, fertilizer, labor, and finally produces his crop ready for market. 

It is natural for him to want to sell his products in the nearest market, to save the added cost of crating and freight to distant points. 

The products being perishable, he must dispose of them quickly. He finds that the local market cannot absorb but a part of his crop, and as he must dispose of the balance he becomes a subject of barter for a commission merchant (a man or group of men who purchase from the farmer to sell to the wholesaler). The entire business of a commission merchant is based on speculation; for he also has no way of determining the market demand, or the price that commodities will bring. He therefore buys at the lowest price that open bidding will permit. The farmer who is also in no position to determine the demand, generally is forced to accept the lowest price offered, even if it represents a loss.

The commission man may be hundreds or thousands of miles away from the farmer, and the crating and freight is a considerable item entering into the excess cost of the product. The local wholesaler solicits the market of the commission men, and pays the price that is requested, whether the products merit it or not. 

The wholesaler generally trucks the products to his store or warehouse. He adds his distributor's cost and profit, and retrucks it to the retail merchant, who in turn sells it to the public at large, adding his overhead and profit. The public pays the freight and cartage from farm to commission men, a speculator's profit and overhead, the wholesaler's profit and overhead, and the retailer's profit and overhead. How can the employee who is paid by the farmer in terms of his cost purchase the product on the basis of the greatly increased price of the retailer? He cannot!

How can the industrial worker who also earns at the rate of the cost to the manufacturer pay the greatly increased price of the retailer? He cannot!

The result is that millions of dollars of crops are wasted every year for lack of demand. Yet millions of workers go hungry because they have not the sufficient means to pay the increased costs to the retailer! 

This same waste applies to all the necessities we need for the sustenance of life. Waste that is a parasite on our living organisms

that cry for the need to exist on the food that Mother Nature has so kindly prepared for our free use. Nature has given plenty for everybody, and we destroy and waste in our every path. 

CHAPTER VII 

The seventh man around the table is the employee. He may be occupying some unskilled job as an ordinary factory worker, or he may have acquired the knowledge that raised him to a skilled mechanic, or he may have been willing to accept responsibility, and was promoted to foreman, superintendent, or department executive. 

Irrespective of the position he holds he is the necessary means of transforming raw materials in the state that Nature has provided with the natural forces that we have so far recognized, into the articles we buy for our every day needs. Each employee and his dependents consume the article that he produces or helps to produce. They require certain food to keep them healthy, clothing to keep them warm in winter and cool in summer, a home that has sufficient room that can give them the privacy of sex, sufficient space for the movement of their limbs. A house that is warm enough to keep out the chill of winter and the heat of summer. The home must be furnished in a manner permitting them to sit, relax, and sleep, so that they can do their work efficiently. His children must have sufficient education to understand their relationship to other human beings. They should have an understanding of the needs of people to enable them to decide what work they can do best with the natural abilities they possess. They must also have such education in art, music, and literature that they can understand and appreciate these cultural arts for the inspiration to bring forth sympathy, tolerance, and kindness towards their fellow men. Their education must further teach them to think independently, so that they can visualize problems and plan a means of carrying them out. 

They must also understand the various natural elements and forces that have so far been recognized, so that they may draw from them the aid necessary to afford better standards of living. 

The family must further have sufficient recreation to give relaxation to their minds, so that they are fertile for new impressions and thoughts. Physical recreation is also essential for a sound healthy body. They must also have the things that Science has so far developed for our daily use. These contributions broaden our powers of thought, vision and education. They in turn serve to contribute towards a recognition of more natural forces waiting only for discovery-the greater the development of natural forces the higher the standard of living.

The newspapers, magazines, radio and advertisements repeatedly illustrate the needs of the people. By the finest expression in language, art, and music they impress upon the public the thousand and one reasons why the people cannot exist without these advertised products. They, however, omit to tell the public how and where to obtain the necessary means to make these purchases. The head of each family is therefore left to his own devices to find out how he can obtain employment that would give him and his family the means to buy.

He finds that his income starts only when he is employed in some

capacity. In order to get the most out of his work, he endeavors to obtain employment at the work he is best fitted to do. A large amount of time is spent in replying to the necessary Help Wanted ads, and seeking interviews with the various employers to obtain the best proposition. In the meantime, however, his family must live. If his personal capital, consisting of previous savings is exhausted, or if he has not been able to accumulate any savings due to previous poor income, he must accept the first proposition he can get, whether it is the work he is fitted for or not. There are always millions of workers in this position looking for jobs without resources, and bidding against each other for the jobs offered.

The employers who in turn have to compete with each other to produce at the lowest cost or go out of business, are compelled against their human feelings to accept the man who can do the work at the lowest wages. This wage has no relationship to the prices the worker and his family have to pay for their needs. These prices paid by him have to cover not only the total wages paid to all workers participating in their production and distribution but, in addition, includes waste of bankruptcies, taxes for the subsistence of the unemployed, taxes for past wars, duplication of efforts and overhead in trying to sell the same markets, overcharges in interest and finance, losses because of strikes and idle plants, loss of efficiency due to untrained workers and lack of proper management, dormant labor and machinery tied up in the manufacture of armaments, loss of labor of those enrolled in the army and navy, who, while they consume, do not produce, also the value of tons of products dumped overboard that have no ready market, and that cannot absorb the expenses of storage for future markets. These are just a few of the avoidable wastes too numerous to mention here.

These wastes increase the cost of our necessities so out of proportion to earnings, that only the highest salaried workers can really have the comfort and happiness of a decent living. The majority of workers have to do without any luxuries, being able to afford only the bare necessities. Every expenditure has to be carefully watched, lest they go into steady debt. Millions of workers just earn sufficient to exist from day to day, with little food, and of the poorest quality, clothes that are shabby and offer no warmth, and homes that have poor ventilation, are over crowded and filthy, all reflecting the misery, poverty, and hopelessness of their slavery. 

These wastes, feeding on wastes, place a greater burden on labor in all countries of the world. Is it any wonder that it is getting more and more difficult to obtain the means to exist, let alone live in comfort?

CHAPTER VIII 

The eighth member around the table is the banker. He may be the chief executive of a savings bank, commercial bank or trust company, engaged in accepting your deposits for safe keeping until you withdraw them for your use. These deposits consist of money received by you for your services either in the form of currency or checks that can be exchanged for currency. 

While you receive this money for your labor, your labor does not represent money-and for this reason. The wages you receive 10

for working depends upon the number of people willing to accept this work at the lowest wages, your labor therefore has no constant value. Further, you may be discharged and become unemployed, and then your services have no value at all. Because of these conditions it was found necessary to find something else besides labor certificates to represent money-something that had an almost constant value. 

Gold, a metal that was valued by people because of its scarcity and could be handled indefinitely without losing its deep yellow color, seemed to be the first choice. Another metal, silver, that was more plentiful but also had the quality of retaining its color became second choice. So these two metals that in themselves did not serve people other than being used for jewelry, became the power of the means to live in the guise of money or standards of coinage. 

Gold and silver were too heavy to carry in bulk, so paper certificates were issued by the various governments, certifying that there was a definite amount of gold or silver held in the national vaults for each certificate issued. These certificates formed our currency. 

As gold became the power that could buy labor and everything produced by labor, it was sought after by all nations by every means, fair or foul. Man's labor, the only power that could transform nature's elements and forces into the things we need to live is therefore the only real wealth that exists. This wealth is wasted and used for plunder and murder under the guise of war. Gold, an inert material could buy people's labor, even as slaves, and compelled large nations without gold to submit to small nations with gold. 

However, the real wealth of labor cannot be substituted, and even gold proved its weakness. During the last war, the countries of the world destroyed their man-power by wasting their labor killing other men, and being killed in return. The cost of food while they fought, the cost of armaments they used, and the value of property that was destroyed, made it necessary for the nations involved to obtain more money to continue. They borrowed from their next richer ally until they all were in debt to their richest ally. The gold of the poorer nations was first exhausted as they used it for cash purchases to buy materials in the open market at the lowest cost. When this was gone, they had to accept war materials at any price from the nations that would extend credit to them in these materials. When the war was finally ended and millions of men were released for useful work, they found that on account of the nations' waste of all materials during the war, a terrific shortage existed in food, clothing, and raw materials without which they could not proceed to work. The poorer nations without gold could not purchase their requirements from the nations that could furnish these materials; and the wealthier nations who had already extended enormous credits to them did not want to sell without receiving payment. As they did not sell, they did not care to buy, and gold became a frozen asset. These wealthy nations, however, had built up their production to include foreign trade, without which they could not keep large numbers of their own people employed. When they found other nations could not buy, they collected all the gold coin from the people, put it in national vaults, and deliberately reduced the value of their currency so that the other nations could buy. This process was known as going off the gold standard, and was only a shot in the arm, because most nations followed suit, and the relationship between them existed as previously. 11

This devaluation of money increased the cost of all articles of the nations participating, but did not increase the pay of labor in the same relationship. By this means, workers are paying for the waste of destruction of the war, in addition to the taxes that they already recognize for that purpose.

In order to procure the funds to cover up the losses of the war and the aftermath of unemployment, that followed and still exists, the various nations solicited their bankers to transfer your deposits in their banks to the government account and substitute government bonds instead. "Is there anything better than a government bond?", they cry, and with this cry the governments of the world are continually taking the payment that you received for hours of work and sacrifice and placing it where they see fit to cover up the deficiencies they know exist, but are too blind to eliminate. The bonds that are substituted are collateral backed collectively by all the people of the nation as a whole, but must be made good by taxation if they are to remain good collateral. If not, they can be deflated to a lower and lower value, which means that the currency issued by the government on the same basis has less and less value. You can withdraw your deposits at any time. However, if taxes are insufficient to cover government expenditures, the currency really has less value than when you deposited it. If the government raises the taxes to equal its expenditures, the added taxation makes your purchases higher in cost. Therefore, either way, you are out. Any money spent by governments in eliminating waste, and thereby raising the standard of living is not in this category, but how many governments are eliminating waste today? The mad race of armament building is only an indication of the path we are heading for. 

In addition to losses through government, let us not forget the private losses through the failure of banks during the depression. 

These failures represented the over valuing of property and stocks during an inflated period.

These losses are the aftermath of every depression. Every depression is the result of the accumulated wastes of unemployment, speculation, improper planning, duplicate operations, duplicate inventories, duplicate overheads and public spending beyond our means. 

As man is a constant consumer, he must be a constant producer. 

His labor is the only power that added to nature's resources produces the things to live and therefore, is the only form of wealth. Currency must be based on his wealth to be a true medium of exchange. 

If our currency was constant the banker would not have to guess at values, manipulate foreign exchange or risk the incomes that you have labored for and placed in his care.

The poor woman inspects these apartments and finds that they are poorly ventilated, have no steam or direct means of heat, contain only the simplest of sanitary facilities and have dark corridors that are traps in case of fire. Many other thoughts grip the mother who had hopes of giving her children a perfect upbringing. Nevertheless, she has no other alternative and so she rents this apartment, hoping that her husband can at least retain his job to pay this small rent. 

Her next concern is the food they must have. This expenditure she must reduce to at least half of her figured decent minimum. What to cut down on is a problem, for they must eat. She begins by disregarding a balanced diet, buying what can do the most filling job at the cheapest cost. Eggs, meat and milk become luxuries which can only be used sparingly. Bread, potatoes and cereals become the main diet. The children soon reflect in their physical development this deficient diet, stunted for the rest of their natural lives. The mother is now called upon for clothes-the little left her for this item can only buy sufficient to proverbially cover their bodies. Ripping, patching and altering of old clothes gives her additional work, robbing her of the little leisure she otherwise could devote to the companionship of her husband and children. The reduced budget has to practically eliminate the thoughts of entertainment, both in and out of the home. Annual vacations are out of the question-even occasional daily outings have to be carefully figured out. 

Illness, with some cost for doctors and medicine is a family catastrophe, which means further stinting of the already reduced existence. Items like insurance, saving for unemployment, saving for higher education, are entirely neglected. 

The mother must be a genius to keep the home in good cheer under these conditions. The same old furniture and furnishings must serve, for these things cost money that is not forthcoming. The constant tension on every member of the family, striving to get what they require and cannot obtain, leads to quarrels and unhappiness. 

Poor father is up against a brick wall, trying to better his position. without daring to sacrifice his present income which just cannot stop. 

Mother, with her nose constantly to the grindstone, watching every penny, working continuously to satisfy all, has no control over the cost of the necessities they must have to live. 

The children soon become indifferent to the guidance of their parents, whom they feel are responsible for the personal denials they are constantly compelled to make. Higher education, individual study of music and arts become a vacant dream. For even if they can obtain some public education in these arts, they will soon have to try to get employment to pay for their increased upkeep as they grow older, which father's constant unskilled wage will never pay for. 

The amount of work that has to be done by unskilled young people to earn even a small salary, does not permit the physical and mental effort of evening study. The children constantly hear of the difficulties of finding any sort of job. Can you blame them if they become discouraged and disgusted with their lot?

This family and millions of others like it all over the world, are paying in their deficiencies of existence for all of the waste, duplication of efforts and the extravagance of our operating method in producing, distributing and administrating natural resources for our use.

CHAPTER X 

The inventor is tenth around the table. He may be an employee that has discovered a simpler, yet more effective method of performing part or all of his work. He may be a farmer who has found an improved method of cultivating a crop. He may be a manufacturer that has discovered a new use for his product. He may be a merchant who finds a better way to service his customers. In fact, it may be anyone who is satisfied that the present way of doing something is not the best and discovers some way of doing it better. 

The seed of invention is in all human beings, for we are constantly trying to save ourselves physical labor. This quality in all of us is continually being expressed in our work and therefore is evident in every field of endeavor. However, our present methods of earning a living make it very difficult for new ideas to bear fruit. 

The inventor who desires to make his invention practical has to find the time and the money to develop his idea to the point of acceptance. 

When he does, he discovers that he has to be a first class salesman to sell it to some corporation or individual who has capital to exploit it. He also at the same time, has to be shrewd enough to at least get 18

some reward for his original idea and succeeding efforts of development. In his search for capital he constantly faces the risk of having his ideas stolen or infringed; for the obtaining of a patent does not mean protection unless one has enormous funds for counsel, courts, and preparation to stop an infringer and prove his patent claims in court. There are so few inventors that have the combination qualities of salesmanship and shrewdness, the leisure time for development of the invention and the independent finances for protection, that it is only in rare instances that an inventor shares in the commercial success of his invention.

There are thousands of patents purchased at a very small cost from discouraged inventors, because corporations desire to pigeonhole them to protect their own markets for existing products that they manufacture. 'New ideas may also make their present equipment obsolete, endangering their investment. 

The successful inventions that we hear about are the ones that have managed to survive the many hardships that our inventors have to contend with in their struggle for recognition. 

There is in addition, the loss incurred by hundreds of thousands of patents being taken out by people who cannot afford the patent fees, let alone the cost of development and protection. There is the additional waste of time and money for legislation of infringement on patents, some of them dragging for years. Many patents are not worth the paper they are written on, because there is no organized way of reviewing new ideas by specialists in that particular field, to see if they are practical for design, application and cost. All these wastes are not only paid by you in one form or another, but rob you of the use of many inventions that the public could enjoy for added leisure or a higher standard of living. 

The hardships suffered by inventors destroy their initiative, discouraging their continued efforts. 

Just think of the wonderful benefits derived by the human race from the inventions of the telephone, radio, photography, and application of electricity. You then can appreciate the loss we have from the lack of organized cooperation we should be able to offer those who desire to improve our present methods of doing things. 

Why is it necessary to stifle initiative by placing the complete burden of accomplishment on the shoulders of the progressive individual and at the same time have him worry about capital. Is it not enough that a progressive individual has to formulate an idea, concentrate to make it practical, without having to worry about capital to operate on, markets to educate, and exploiters to beat and make a living at the same time? What incentive is there to encourage invention so vital to human needs, when every potential inventor knows from the sad experience of others the difficult road he has to travel to even repay his original expense of patents and experiments. 

CHAPTER XI 

The eleventh member around the table is our representative in government. He may be our representative in the city, county, state, or Federal Government. We voted for him because we believed he had the necessary qualifications and experience to carry out our ideas of good government. 19

What are our ideas of good government. We can all agree that every man and woman that desires to work should have that opportunity, for otherwise their idleness is waste and their existence a tax upon others. It is a common understanding that all people must have food, clothing, sheiter, education and recreation. These of course come from the resources of the country and science has transformed them for our use. Otherwise without these essentials we must accept the social dangers of an undernourished and discontented class of our population and the crime that follows in its path. We further agree that no activities shall be carried on to curtail the freedom of thought or expression of any of our people. It is also acknowledged that our industrial as well as government activities should be carried on with the minimum of waste, so that all of us benefit by the savings thereby. We also recognize that our first essentials of living must be enjoyed by all before indulging in extravagances for a few. 

We know that the youth, aged and feeble must be given their requirements of living, even though they are unable to earn these by their own labor. We also agree that all encouragement be given to those whose intelligence, imagination and labor reduce our physical work, increase our knowledge, protect our health, and inspire us to better thought.

All our people agree that we must as a nation act towards having peace on earth, for otherwise no nation or family is safe from the murder and misery that war decrees. No progress is lasting where the very foundations of civilization are uprooted. No family life is secure when your children can be snatched from you, to become cannon fodder for the destruction of some other human being desiring the same peace and happiness that you seek.

We all realize that the one reason for war is because the nations of the world refuse to recognize the fact that every human being on this earth must have the necessities of life and the freedom of opportunity to work and earn these necessities, whether his own country has the natural resources or not. Before any nation can subscribe to a plan for other nations, she must set her own house in order and establish the same principle for her own citizens first. 

Your representative in government must work actively towards the carrying out of these policies if you want good government. However, he is only an individual. His views may be prejudiced by his own social position, or by previous business connections. all times, subject to active pressure by well organized minorities seeking special privileges, or trying to disguise them as benefits to the majority. You therefore, cannot expect him to act honestly and without prejudice for your interests, unless he is made to realize that with the acceptance of a public position he accepts a definite obligation outlined in a written plan of the consecutive steps that are necessary to obtain good government. This plan is signed by him as a condition towards the holding of his job as your representative and the failure or deviation of these steps give you the power to dismiss him upon a hearing of charges. This is the method that will tend to eliminate from our midst men who seek political or judicial position with the one thought that it offers them the opportunity of increasing their own wealth and security at the expense of the millions of poverty stricken and hopeless citizens not organized and too ignorant to know what it's all about. 20

We will attract men that seek public service because they have a heart and a head to direct it. Men who want responsibility for its opportunity for leadership and service. Men who have the foresight to realize that their own happiness and security is not tied up in the little temporary protection of money that can be wiped out of their grasp over night, but that is tied up with the security against economic want of all people in the nation. They in turn as a nation to see that the people of other nations are so provided for. 

The plan, step by step, that you must provide as a condition of election is not simple; for, you must realize that it be definite, workable and understandable, so no questions as to its meaning or application can be misconstrued. Let us, gentlemen around the table, who have our varied interests, discuss this plan and see if we can formulate one that will work. Let us all be frank enough to be fair to see the conditions as they actually exist. We will also call for discussion from those not engaged directly in industry, but who form an important part of our population. 

There are the teachers who direct our education, but who like our government representative, have at all times felt the attempt of minorities to control the freedom of knowledge they wish to impart to their pupils.

We have the composers, musicians, and actors, who are responsible for our entertainment. These men have seen thousands of their kind on the rocks of unemployment. Their talents being wasted in idleness instead of being utilized to brighten the lives of a good portion of our population that cannot afford to pay the price of private entertainment. There are the sculptors, painters, and writers, that inspire us with the beauty of nature. How many of them live in the direst poverty, struggling from day to day, to obtain the opportunity to show the public what they have to offer. But they continue to fail because, like the inventor, they must have finances to support themselves, sales ability, and shrewdness to buck those who make it a business of exploiting talent to their own advantage. 

There are the doctors, dentists, and other similar professions who administer to our health. Their ethics call for them to treat people of all circumstances with the same degree of attention. Their stomachs are constantly resisting these ethics for they cannot exist unless their fees cover their expenditures.

The independent overhead that each professional man has, irrespective of his volume, the idle hours that could be cooperatively employed in conjunction with other similar professional men, but are wasted as an individual, increase his fees so that he may make sufficient to exist, but limits his services to those who can afford them. 

The attorneys, brokers, real estate agents, and other similar professions that have undertaken to interpret our rights and secure our investments are compelled to defend and represent us whether we are right or wrong, for they too have their own existence to seek. 

As there is always some right in every wrong, their consciences soon justify their position, and we soon learn to our sorrow, that our justice is relative to the finances we can expend to obtain it. 

These men should be interested in a government that makes it possible for them to earn a living at their natural talents in a constructive way and be independent to accept only such missions that call for honest effort. 21

We also invite our newspapers, magazines and advertising representatives. They know what has happened to their industries in countries where dictatorship has become a power. They certainly should be interested in good government. 

There are many others engaged in vocations other than these mentioned in servicing our needs. They are also invited to participate in a plan for good government, because a government properly functioning is a benefit for all.

CHAPTER XII 

When the last speaker had finished there was a lapse of deep silence. Every member around the table began to realize that he was responsible as a member of our society for the conduct of that society-that while they knew that poverty, crime, hate and war were wrong, they were supporting the conditions that bring them. about.

Yes, some of them may have kidded their consciences by giving to some charitable request, but actually by so doing they admitted that they wanted to buy their peace of mind to temporarily rectify what they were permanently supporting-a poverty-stricken individual in a world of plenty. Some of them smoothed their consciences by supporting a peace society to spread peace and good will among men-but what did their efforts comprise? Were they a definite attempt to eliminate the basic causes of war or just a pacifist's reaction to thinly veneer a core of rottenness in humanity that he himself was supporting? Why was he a hypocrite by denouncing the result without changing the cause? There were some members around the table who really thought that things had to change of themselves—and their action would just be utter inaction. 

Would these members change places with those who were suffering from their inaction? Those who were now going through a living death with worry, fear and hunger. Would they take this suffering with a smile of anticipation that things would eventually change of themselves? Are they foolish enough to imagine that changes without direction will find the proper path to follow? Are they hiding from themselves these very facts that confront them as members of human society?

Of course, there are the members who actually feel that their existence and every action upon this earth is the work of God, and by appealing to "His" mercies the power above will rectify all injustices and direct us upon the paths of righteousness. In their appeal to the Almighty, do they justify the prostitution of God's gifts to man that they daily participate in? Do they see that the Almighty powers and forces show us humans how to act and live if we honestly observe their application? Do they make a jest of supernatural gifts and guidance by not using the reasoning faculties given us to attain the standard of living they desire for themselves? Are they asking God's mercy for destroying and wasting natural resources designed for distribution to all people of all nations, creed and color? Are they in their prayers sincere in the fact that they recognize the equality of men as children of the same creator to share in the fruits of nature without exploitation and hindrance? Do they think that they have 22 22

a particular pull with the Almighty to cancel all of their obligations to others that they may receive mercy for hindrance of nature's own laws?

There were others around the table who were filled with their own egotism of financial success. Their own personal comforts became the prime objective of their lives. Their consciences were satisfied in their belief that as an individual they were powerless to change conditions that they knew were wrong. They further satisfied themselves by saying that if the other unfortunates had only applied the same effort that they had to their work, they too would be in financial comfort. They, however, made no analysis of their efforts, for if they had, they would find that not only all of their physical and mental qualities, contributed to their success, but also the factors of production, distribution and markets had made their efforts successful and were entirely beyond their control as humans. It is not the individual alone, but merely his contribution to society as a whole, which combined with the forces of nature have made his efforts a temporary success.

There were some members around the table who did realize that their individual problems concerning health, happiness, work and security were common to all members of society, irrespective of their position in our economic sphere. These members were now all sympathetic to a plan that could eliminate the wastes of our present activity and divide the gain to increase the standard of living for all. 

Finally, the government representative spoke up. "Gentlemen," says he, "by this time you probably realize that in a world where our nations have spent in one year over twenty billions of dollars for armaments for the destruction of human beings-that in a world where a man's religious beliefs have been made a basis for persecution and blackmail-that in a world where countries have been demoralized and dismembered at the point of a gun-that in a world where no one has any security from one minute to the other that in a world where there is constant turmoil, hate and envy preached by the leaders of nations against one another—that the people of this world are groping for a plan that will permit them to live in accordance with natural laws of peace and good will towards mankind. 

We have around this table representatives from all phases of our modern economic life. I feel that these members by the proper thought can make suggestions that will improve their lot and the lot of all others. You all realize the seriousness of our present situation, and you can all give us some real thoughts upon which we can distribute progress. Let us again start with the youth of the country-the high school graduate-and see what suggestions he may have."

I also pointed out to you the outgrowth of crime that enforced idleness, due to unemployment causes. The cost of crime administration is a large item in our public taxes today. My recommenda- 23

tions, therefore, will deal with the solution of this unemployment. 

The faculties of high schools have already recognized that vocational guidance must be given to students, so that they may choose a trade or profession in which their natural abilities may have full scope, because a man works best at that for which he is naturally fitted. 

This vocational guidance has no value to the student unless he can continue in this class of work after he graduates, provided he so desires. It is therefore, necessary if youth must be continually employed, to have some vocational centers connected with groups of high schools, where the youth who cannot obtain employment in Private Industry can if he so desires enter and have the opportunity of earning for his needs at the work that he is trained to do. Every encouragement would be given the youth for the fullest development along his chosen field. He would have better guidance than if he were unemployed in Private Industry, seeking the companionship of others also unemployed. These small groups, with time on their hands and no direction of purpose, requiring some income for their immediate needs, are the ones that form the nucleus of our criminal classes. Vocational Centers will remove the inferiority complex common to all those forming our unemployed class, by providing them with useful work, good income and direction towards public achievement.

The question of course, is how the Vocational Centers can be organized and operated. Please bear in mind that the unemployed youth must eat, wear clothes and have the shelter he needs whether he has funds or not. Instead of taxing the public for their support, these unemployed youths would work in these vocational centers, producing for themselves not only what they require to live, but in addition, earn suffcient to carry their share of local and government administration.

The terms and conditions under which they produce for themselves must be an entirely different basis as far as wages, purchases and production are concerned than our present arrangement in Private Industry. Otherwise, they will soon be relatively in the same uncertain position that people in Private Industry find themselves today. These Vocational Centers would cover the main branches of industry, science and art, so that any member would find within its scope the work of his choice. Even if a member found it advisable to change his original selection of a career, he would have the opportunity to do so early in life. I appreciate that there are quite a few details in working out the organization of these vocational centers. 

These details I leave to the more experienced members around the table if they feel my suggestion worthy of adoption." 

CHAPTER XIV 

The college youth was now called upon as the next one around the table to speak. "It seems to me that all of our waste and difficulties show up in unemployment in a world of plenty-show up in the lack of a good standard of living for a substantial part of our people, and also in the fact that all people have a lack of security. I feel that unemployment, being the major problem, should be treated first. 

Private Industry, being based on operation for profit, cannot 24

absorb the unemployed unless there is profitable work for them to do. Useless employees therefore have no value and become a burden that must be removed. However, government must accept the responsibility for the unemployed, because millions of starving people would be quite a menace to the rest of the country. Governments, however, not wishing to compete with private business, use the unemployed on projects not warranted by the workers' immediate needs. 

There is no effort made to determine just what the worker can afford for other items in addition to his daily existence. The result is that we see million dollar Post Offices being erected in the midst of dirty unlivable slums. We see beautiful highways with no provision made to definitely insure all workers decent shoes to wear. We see skilled and highly trained mechanics working in ditches at ordinary laboring tasks that could be performed by the untrained worker. We see workers who barely earn sufficient to live, faced with the prospect of further taxation, because most government incomes do not cover government expense. And we do nothing, because many of us still feel that private business based on profit will eventually remedy this condition. But, can it? We have seen what moderate regulation by some governments does to private business. It immediately stops expansion of capital improvements, the backbone of our present system of Private Industry. These capital improvements must continue in order to absorb the unemployed in ordinary production. The attempts of the government in trying to adjust this failure by the increase of taxes needed for this maneuver, brings dissatisfaction to many of us.

However, not knowing the constructive thing to do, the dissatisfied population becomes an easy target for all sorts of false propaganda. This is an open door for dictators who appeal to the dissatisfaction and ignorance of the masses, by promising Utopia with no facts to support it. We also see a world arming for destruction. 

We do not know what to expect in national or world affairs, from one minute to the other. This all causes a stagnation of Private Industry, also beset by its own uncertainties. Anyone can therefore, see that under these conditions, there is no way of improving private business, unless these uncertainties are removed. No one to date has been able to formulate any balanced plan for removing these uncertainties in Private Industry. Regulation by governments of some parts of Private Industry seem to distort the complete relationship still further, instead of actually improving it. 

As we are in a competitive arrangement, it seems that the only way that Private Industry can justify its own distorted formation, is to compel it to compete with a system planned for the welfare of the people as a whole-a system that is just, honest and in accord with nature's laws. There will be a good many members of our society who will immediately say that two systems cannot exist side by side. But, we must find a way or accept what Private Industry brings forth intolerance, war, hunger, poverty, crime and loss of freedom. 

The unemployed whom we will consider as the garbage of Private Industry can be gathered together to serve as a nucleus of a new society that will show us if Private Industry deserves to exist. By this means we can immediately use a waste and convert it to a gain. 

I therefore must also be in accord with the high school youth 25

and state that Vocational Centers can be introduced to absorb unemployed in a new system that cannot repeat the mistakes that are choking Private Industry today. We must in this organization develop a new understanding of capital, labor, production, distribution and currency. We must examine these divisions without prejudice, seeking a way without destructive criticism. Do not become influenced by previous doctrines and experiments that have been tried by upsetting an existing order without first establishing a new one. 

Wealth or capital, as we understand it, consists of natural resources, such as land, mines, etc., and excess labor. By the term excess labor, I mean the labor that is left over once the people's needs have been produced. For example, if it takes a man thirty hours to produce sufficient for his needs and he is willing to work forty hours, the ten hours become excess labor. This excess or surplus labor can be used and converted into the form of machinery or building of factories, etc., or could be exchanged with currency if other people wanted to utilize this excess time. 

If the country's needs are small, the surplus labor is large; and if the country's needs are great, there will be a small surplus of labor. 

These needs of the people are what is known as a "standard of living". It is the aim of civilized man to continually increase this standard of living, and at the same time decrease the number of hours needed to produce it. In this way there will be a large surplus of labor to further produce a still greater standard of living and also permit more hours for leisure and education. 

At the present time, due to the tremendous waste in our operations of government, industry and distribution, the workers receive but a small part of what they actually produce. The results are that while their potential needs are large they can only afford a small portion of them. This causes a large surplus of labor which should be used as capital or wealth but instead under Private Industry becomes unemployment and therefore waste. The problem of what to do with it then arises. The unemployment of this surplus labor further causes keen competition for existing jobs, still further lowering the wage scale and still further decreasing the demand for commodities. 

If a decent standard of living as determined by the science and knowledge of the country is enjoyed by all, then all surplus labor is really capital that can be constantly utilized for common gain. However, if this surplus labor results in unemployment, those unemployed cannot afford to purchase the things that they formerly produced which in turn necessitates a decrease in the volume of production. This surplus labor then becomes a social menace. 

While employers recognize capital or wealth today as a surplus of what is left after what they pay out, it really is not, unless the workers enjoy a standard of living commensurate with the best that science and knowledge has made possible, because capital or wealth accumulated any other way will act as a boomerang in the form of disturbed economic conditions. This chaos will eventually not only absorb this wealth, but substitute losses in its place. It follows therefore, that if in our Vocational Centers we do not wish to repeat our present errors, capital or wealth should be understood in its true definition, and that is, surplus labor over the labor that is required to produce a good standard of living for all of its members. 

The next item we have to define in our Vocational Center is labor. 26 26

In Private Industry today we recognize the value of man's labor by the wages that are paid him. Supposing that a friend of ours named 

Smith is an executive of a large corporation and is earning $15,000 per year. Sometime later Smith loses his job, because of depressed conditions. Unable to obtain a similar position, he is compelled to accept a job as a clerk at $2,000 per year. New friends of Smith will regard him as a little better than an average white collar worker. 

Again a period elapses and Smith finds himself out of a job, because of poor business conditions. After struggling until his savings have been exhausted, he finally accepts a job as a road worker at $18.00 per week. His new friends will then regard him as an ordinary unskilled laborer. Now mind you, this same Smith, with the same ability, because of conditions, which are no fault of his own, has dropped in value from $300.00 per week to $18.00 per week. What would you say is really his true worth?

Now supposing he lost his last job and could not obtain another you would say that he has no value at all. Yet we do know that Smith has a definite value to the community-again, what is it? 

Now, during all of his positions and during his unemployment his knowledge was the same and therefore constant, so that was no censideration of his value. His effort was undoubtedly sincere in all of his positions, for if he was not sincere he could not have risen to his first position. Therefore, his effort was constant and was no consideration of his value. Let us now examine his responsibility, for that was different in each position. In the highest position he accepted responsibility that few men would have cared to take. This responsibility did undoubtedly consume a lot of his personal time and his mental activity was greater than his physical effort. We therefore can justly state that his responsibility in the higher position merited him a greater return than in the lower position. So it follows that knowledge and effort must have the companion responsibility to make all three really effective. Furthermore, a market must also exist for the combination, or otherwise it is of no value at all. 

I therefore suggest that in our Vocational Center, if we wish to avoid the errors of Private Industry, that labor should be valued according to knowledge, effort and responsibility, with a free opportunity for all members to exercise their application at all times. 

Production as it is now understood, is the land, capital and labor, necessary to convert materials in their basic form, to the state in which they can be used by the consumer. Land which was given by nature for the benefit of all human beings as absolutely necessary for life, was originally taken by land grabbers. Grants were given either by rulers or by manipulation of the igorant public. The land was subsequently divided, subdivided and transferred to so many holders through the years, that no attempts have been made to right the original wrong of land seizure, and we now accept land ownership as the proper thing.

However, to partly compensate the public for the original seizure, the people still have through their government, the right to condemn and take any land, providing the owner is compensated for its fair value. The original wrong is also somewhat compensated for, by the fact that land is taxable, or in other words, the owners of the land are compelled to contribute to the public expense even when the land is idle. The ownership of land is therefore an unprofitable obliga- 27

tion, unless it is being maintained for useful purposes. If that useful purpose is necessary for the conservation of the community, no owner has the right to object to its use if he is fully compensated for it. 

If the public should therefore decide that Vocational Centers are necessary to absorb the unemployed, in a manner that will prevent the present forces of disintegration of social and economic independence, the use of land required by the Vocational Centers can be carried out by the state, as representatives of the people, the way it is done at the present time. This method is classified as condemnation proceedings, protecting the owner and community in accordance with their vested rights.

Now that the Vocational Centers can obtain the use of any land they require by present legislative processes, the next step is to obtain the necessary capital. Capital, exclusive of land, as we have already defined it, includes superfluous labor converted into various forms, such as machinery, factories and inventory. 

Now as heretofore mentioned supposing you have to work a given number of hours for your needs and you were also given the opportunity to work extra hours. These extra hours would be your capital or wealth. You could, if you were a farmer, produce additional food in these extra hours to be stored as a surplus. If a miner, you could mine in these extra hours a surplus of coal, iron, etc., to be used for future use. If a mechanic, in these extra hours you could take what the miner has mined, apply your labor and produce some tool or machinery that would serve you both to make your work easier. If all workers cooperated, they could, by working extra hours, accumulate capital or wealth that could create, build and produce anything that they would have the ability for. 

Each human being has therefore within himself the power to use his surplus labor to accumulate per week as capital or wealth. 

For instance if the Vocational Centers required twenty hours of your labor per week for you to produce a good standard of living, and you had the opportunity to work say forty hours per week you can readily see that the twenty surplus hours could be contributed by you to accumulate wealth for the centers, which could be used for machinery and factories to produce the things that you need. This same wealth could also be applied to study and invention of new methods that would continually reduce the effort necessary to produce your needs. This saving would give you more hours to produce a still higher standard of living, or to use at your discretion as leisure or study for self-improvement after your obligation to the Vocational Center has been satisfied. A part of these surplus twenty hours could be contributed by you, for your share of support to the local and federal governments. A part of these extra hours could be contributed by you towards public health, education and welfare. Some of these hours could be further given by you to support those Center 

Members who became ill or aged and unable to work. You could budget these hours to the demands made on you, and either limit these demands as unworthy and unfair, or increase your hours to take care of those things you deemed worthy and just. You would soon see the true relationship between the actual amount of hours of your labor that you spent to produce your necessities as compared to the hours of labor that you need to contribute to the administration of the Vocational Center, government and public welfare. You would 28 888

soon put a stop to extravagances that you would not be willing to work for. The capital or wealth of the Vocational Center would therefore be voluntary contributions of labor by its own members once the Centers have been established. 

Labor as we understand it is a physical or mental effort expended by an individual to accomplish a definite task. This labor can be either productive, unproductive or destructive. If it accomplishes a task that is of benefit to someone, it is productive; however, if it is idle it is unproductive, and if it lowers or destroys present standards, it is destructive. Labor is the only effort that we can use to convert nature's resources into the things we require to exist. Therefore, waste of labor is the destruction of the means to live or in other words—our wealth. Unemployment is destructive to wealth, because it is wasted labor. Skilled labor used for unskilled tasks is a destruction of wealth because it is a waste of effort. Labor performing duplicate tasks for a common result is destructive to wealth, because half of the effort begets no gain. We, therefore, at the Vocational 

Center, will exert our cooperative efforts to see that labor is not wasted.

The first move will be to determine what constitutes a minimum or a fair standard of living for our members. We now in Private 

Industry have a fair standard of building construction for safety and fire protection, and we have standards for education, standards for traffic guidance, standards for legal procedure, etc. But the most important standard of all, a minimum standard of living somehow seems to be overlooked. Our Vocational Center will look to the housewife again to rectify this omission to give us a fair minimum standard of living as regards to food, clothing, shelter, entertainment, luxuries, and percentages for transportation, taking into consideration the number of dependents and their ages. 

The second step will be to determine from Private Industry just how many hours, in terms of ordinary labor, skilled labor and overhead labor it takes at present to produce these commodities. 

The third step will be to establish a labor rating for every commodity. This can be calculated in accordance with the facilities that the Vocational Center has for producing them, as compared with the facilities in Private Industry, that is at present producing the commodities. This adjustment may not be accurate at the beginning, but can be adjusted periodically in accordance with our experience. 

The fourth step will be to determine the number of labor hours of ordinary labor, skilled labor and overhead labor necessary to produce the minimum standard of living that we establish for our members.

The fifth step will be to classify our members as unskilled, skilled or as administrative labor by examining their education, ability and experience and their willingness to assume responsibility. These examinations will be formulated by the members of the Vocational Center who are educators or experienced in Civil Service procedure. 

All records of the results of examinations to be open for inspection by all members. Any member who questions the classification can appeal to an arbitration board appointed by the members as a body for that purpose.

Ratings will be based on a minimum standard of living, an average standard of living and an executive standard of living, similar to 29

the standards of living reviewed in the chapter on the housewife. 

However, the differences in return will be really enjoyed by the possessors, for it will represent actual immediate benefits, instead of the theoretical ones as in Private Industry-such as life insurance which may, or may not because of fluctuating values benefit the possessor's family after his death; the higher levying of income taxes which gives the contributor no immediate benefit; the higher cost of medical attention because the individual seemingly can afford it; excessive contributions to organizations and charities which the individual must subscribe to, for otherwise a false reflection as to his earning ability will be assumed by his neighbors with the possible results of hurting him in his earning of income. He will also have the satisfaction of knowing that his added return is not at the expense of the ordinary worker who will also have a good standard of living. The ordinary worker will respect the higher ratings, because they will be based on merit and responsibility, the opportunity always being open to him to qualify for a greater income when he so desires. 

The sixth step will be to determine a budget of working hours for all members of the Center to provide for their needs and the capital hours to cover all other contributions as outlined under our description of capital. 

You can readily see that Vocational Center Members, constituting their own market, can plan their needs, and by their own efforts raise their own standard of living as they see fit. They can increase their efforts if they so desire and accumulate more capital and utilize it for more education and self-improvement. There will be no destruction of wealth, for their object as well as their effort is under their control at all times.

There will undoubtedly be a number of items that the Vocational Center will not be able to produce efficiently through their lack of skill or facilities, and it will in these cases be necessary to purchase them from existing Private Industry. This leads to a discussion of currency and the means of exchange necessary between Vocational Centers and Private Industry, because otherwise two systems cannot function together in the same society. In the chapter discussing the banker, the evils of our present currency were reviewed. The fluctuations in value and the unstable conditions that follow are just some of the results of the currency based on gold or monetary substitutes instead of the only real wealth that we have, which is human labor. 

We, in our Vocational Centers, not wishing to repeat the errors of Private Industry, are going to make the basis of our currency human labor, instead of gold, silver or substitutes. We will have a unit of one dollar the same as Private Industry has, but it will be equivalent to one hour of time of ordinary labor, instead of a questionable amount of gold or silver. If an ordinary worker spends forty hours of labor at the Center, he will receive forty Vocational Dollars. 

These dollars will be used as currency to purchase any commodities at Vocational Center distribution depots and will serve as the currency for all transactions at the Center. It will always have a constant measure because one hour of time of ordinary labor is constant. 

It will reflect any variation in the price of commodities as progress or retrogression, and immediately disclose to all members the actual hours of labor entering into the production, service or distribution of any commodity. Cost will represent labor expended, and the mem- 30

ber's income will automatically serve as a true budget of his expenditures, for there will be no indirect or hidden costs to confuse him as to the true cost and value of the article he consumes. There will be no inflation or deflation of values disguised by cheaper and fluctuating currency. 

The worker will know exactly where he stands and will not be the tool of unscrupulous governments, covering their deficiencies by the manipulation of the value of currency and discharging these false obligations by the issuance of cheaper money. 

The worker in Vocational Centers of one country will regard other workers in Vocational Centers of other countries, in terms of what they really produce, for the difference in price in Vocational Center commodities between one country and another will reflect only the difference in labor hours. Vocational Center workers of all countries will therefore strive to exert their best efforts to reduce the number of hours to a minimum on all products produced. This will lead to a better understanding between workers of all countries and will make them see that they are all members of one society, striving for the highest standard of living, instead of enemies stealing each other's bread.

Exchange of products by Vocational Centers in various countries can be easily arranged for as they will all have the same basis of currency.

To convert these constant Vocational Dollars to the fluctuating inconsistent private money in use at the present time, is a problem that we will have to leave to the banker. It will probably give him only part of the headache that Foreign Exchange is giving him today, because he will have the advantage of making the conversion with a currency that is constant.

With true definitions of land, capital, labor and currency, the Vocational Center will at least be organized to proceed to the other problems that have to be solved. Our retail business man, who has had far more experience that I have in problems of distribution, I am sure, will be glad to make any criticisms or suggestions that he sees fit, so I leave the next discussion to him."

CHAPTER XV 

"I know," says the retail man, “that if a request was made for me to turn over my store to the Vocational Center as a distributing depot, and provision was made for me to work so that I could enjoy a decent standard of living, I would be only too glad to do it. This happens to be my third location in business. Each time I was compelled to move, almost using up my last dollar. The first forced change was due to a large chain store opening three doors away and immediately lowering prices on a few leading products that were widely advertised. My customers did not realize that the average of prices they paid on all of the items were about the same as they paid in my store. 

They were fooled because the chain store could afford hand bills, window displays of specials and newspaper advertising featuring these leading products which I could not afford. The result almost led to my bankruptcy. My second location was in a new neighborhood where customers were few and far between. I had counted the fami- 31

lies in the neighborhood as a basis for taking the store, but I had no means of knowing that they were in the habit of shopping in a small town about two miles away, where they thought they were purchasing bargains. After thinking, working and eating my heart out, trying by word of mouth to drum up trade, I finally had to give up practically penniless. My present location is good, but my rent and operating expenses are so high that the margin is barely enough to carry me through. I have been fortunate to be able to manage so far, because business has been good, but the first slump will break me as I have not been able to put away a penny in reserve for bad business. Can you blame me for my feelings in the matter of giving up my business? You say that the Vocational Center is thinking of inviting me to join and contribute my store and services? It is too good to be true. However, if it really is true, can you appreciate the savings I can make by having a definite market. 

I wish to suggest how I would then operate. I would operate a

Vocational Center distributing depot, equivalent to our retail stores. 

There will be a definite number of Vocational Center Members in every Vocational district whose approximate needs will be known. 

The minimum, average and executive standards of living will have a general outline of the commodities the members will probably use. 

Knowing definitely the number of customers I am going to have and also knowing exactly what districts they live in, I can plan the location of my store in a business district most convenient to service their needs. Knowing before hand the approximate commodities that 

I must distribute to the Center Members, I can plan my store with the assistance of the architectural department of the Center. 

The store could either be arranged as a specialty store distributing one class of merchandise, or as a department store distributing all classes of merchandise. This of course, would depend on the number of Vocational Members, land available in the community and a study of what type of store could give the most efficient service in that locality. The Architectural Division of the Center would have charge of this study.

The cost of any products distributed through the stores will consist of the total cost of production plus the overhead expense as determined by the Vocational Members as necessary and the cost of the store building. This overhead expense will be determined in the following manner. The Vocational Center Members would determine the number of hours or corresponding Vocational Dollars they would be willing to work or spend to have their commodities distributed. 

This unit multiplied by the number of members in any given territory would determine the limit of overhead expense. The cost of the store building will be determined as follows. An estimate will be made by the Architectural Division giving the requirements of the store as determined previously together with the approximate life utility of the store. The cost of the building can be taken temporarily from the capital account of the Vocational Center and replaced gradually with the distribution of the products. 

Now for the procedure of distribution—each store manager, knowing the living standards of the members who will patronize his store, will immediately requisition from the Vocational Center warehouse, a sufficient amount of commodities that will be required for one, two or three days, depending on the delivery schedule from the warehouse. 32

For commodities that are slow-moving, or of undetermined call, samples alone will be carried, the actual orders being shipped from the warehouse direct. This will effect a great saving in inventory for the stores and a saving of space. At the same time, however, it will offer the members more efficient delivery. The warehouses, centrally located and catering to a large number of these stores, will be able to route their deliveries more often at far less expense. Each item will will be priced and displayed without discrimination to any particular product. The help, having no ax to grind, or premiums to collect, for pushing any particular brand, will not hinder this plan, as all products will be Vocational Center merchandise.

Store catalogues will state the definite merits of each commodity in an unbiased manner. These will be prepared by the Vocational 

Center Merchandising Division. Each catalogue will serve as a guide to all members who seek to know which product will best serve their interest. Requests and suggestions for products not listed in the catalogue, will come from Vocational Members desiring them, and will be submitted by the store manager to the Merchandising Division for study, to determine whether a demand can be reached to warrant production.

Any new products or improvements to present products will be featured in the Vocational Press (the cooperative newspaper), so that all members will have the opportunity to inspect them at the stores. 

If group members find that the distributing depot is not giving them the proper service, complaints can be made to the store manager and if it is beyond him, to the Merchandising Division, to determine what the difficulties are.

They may find the manager or assistants lax in carrying out the instructions issued. If the fault is theirs, they can be brought up on charges and penalized by working additional hours towards the accumulated capital account, varying in number with the degree of seriousness of their neglect. If their laxity is due to ignorance or lack of skill, the store help can be instructed in the proper procedure. 

If the fault is improper building facilities, the Architectural Division will make the necessary alterations to eliminate difficulties. If complaints are due to faulty manufacture, delivery or warehousing, they can be traced and remedied in a similar manner. If the fault is apparently insufficient help, Vocational Members knowing the cost of distribution of the most efficient stores, have a comparison to guide them as to whether the budget should be increased for more help or not. In other words, the Merchandising Division of the Vocational 

Center sets a standard of operation costs and personnel for its establishments, based on a definite market, definite budget and highest type of service. All commodities are presented on their real merit with their true cost. The income of the store manager and assistants will be determined as all other members of the Vocational Center will be. 

This will undoubtedly be discussed by our friend the employee when he is called upon."

CHAPTER XVI 

The small manufacturer was hoping that he too would fit into the vocational picture. His troubles the past year were almost at a breaking point. What with labor strikes, poor business, keen com- 33

petition because of the reduced market and heavy taxes, he did not know where to turn. He knew that all business was in the same fix, and even if he thought that some new venture might be profitable, he could not free the capital he had tied up in his present business to start this new venture. The few dollars that he was drawing at the present time from his business could not stop if he wanted to at least feed his family. The presentation of his business to the Vocational Center would be a relief to him, but what could the center do with an unprofitable small business? They would surely turn it down, especially if they had to guarantee him a good living out of it. 

He, however, had overlooked some basic facts that made a vast difference.

The Vocational Center would not have to seek a market-the 

Center members themselves constituting that market. Secondly, the fact of having a market and definitely knowing the standard of living of the members, would make it possible for the Center to determine what his factory output should be and have him produce this steadily throughout the year. Labor would not strike, for they, as well as he, will be cooperative owners of the business. His executive work would continue as every business needs management. He will really be of far more importance to his business than he is now, because his mind will be clear of financial problems, sales problems, designing, purchasing and advertising problems. All of these functions will be taken over by men who are specialists in these particular fields. His duties will consist of supervision of the men under him and production of definite quantities only. 

In planning his production, he will receive a standard specification sheet covering the description, design, grade and quantities he is to produce, taking into consideration his facilities for producing them. He will also receive a budgeted labor cost and overhead cost, and the raw materials will be sent to him in the proper quantities he will require. He will also receive standard inspection sheets to check on the quality of the finished products. 

The Vocational Center warehouse, knowing his output and also knowing the demands of the stores, will know when and how to order his products. Workers will be free to make suggestions for any improvements. These suggestions will be submitted to the Merchandising Division who will cooperate to see if the suggestions are practical to carry out.

The small manufacturer began to see daylight now. He saw an opportunity to effect innumerable changes that he had in mind in the past, which he had not been in a position to carry out. 

There was another small manufacturer in the town who was producing the identical line that he was. Why could they not both join up with the Vocational Center and eliminate duplicate inventories that they were carrying. Mr. Jones, the other small manufacturer, could take charge of all of the machine operations, while our small manufacturer could concentrate on assembly and finishing. 

Closer cooperation of these operations was necessary, for there were considerable backcharges from the stores for shoddy merchandise in both companies. They could eliminate both of their showrooms, using the distributing store of the Center to display their merchandise samples. This saving of space would permit them to move into the same building instead of occupying two separate ones. The Archi- 34

tectural Division of the Center had already suggested that his present location could be used as a service building for the maintenance of members' cars.

Mr. Jones could dispense with his two nephews who were his salesmen. They destested his garment business anyway, having studied engineering at college. He knew it, but kept them, because they could not obtain jobs in the field in which they studied, or in fact any other jobs. The Vocational Center is so organized that it can use every available man in his own particular field, for it aims to produce a higher standard of living for its members, than even the wealthy man enjoys in Private Industry.

The small manufacturer reflected again—I could also dispense with some of the men who were never born to be garment makers. 

Take Sam, for instance—he is a mechanical genius. It breaks my heart to see him pack garments when he could probably develop an automatic machine to do the job if he had the time, money and shop in which to experiment. There is Arnold, operating the press iron. 

Did you ever see his work in planning houses? No one would hire him in that line, because he is not a college graduate with years of experience. Yet he has won several prizes in exhibitions for his work. 

I am also going to suggest to Mr. Jones that when we have both joined the Vocational Center we can operate our machines steadily throughout the year. We could not accomplish this in Private Industry for even our largest customers refused to order ahead of season-preferring to rush us out of our wits with last minute orders during the season. Idle machinery produces nothing but expense and idle employees nothing but heartaches.

The Merchandising Division who has the responsibility in the Vocational Center of predetermining what the approximate demand will be for successive periods will cooperate with the store, warehouse and factory for continued production throughout the year. 

Besides this, our salesmen who sell the same customers have overdrawn heavily on their drawing accounts and have wasted a lot of valuable time going over the same territory. Now, we have nothing for these salesmen to do, because the Vocational Center has nothing to sell; but as we already mentioned before, the Vocational 

Center can absorb all men. 

The small manufacturer also realized that under this cooperative plan designers of new products could institute an organized effort to obtain suggestions and criticism direct from the public. 

Think of what an advantage it would be to the designers of the Vocational Center as compared to those in Private Industry wherein the only criticism obtained was a loss of sales or rejected merchandise. 

The unscrupulous methods we all used to try to copy the other fellow's merchandise and take away his customers reminds me of men rowing in opposite directions in a boat with many leaks. Is it any wonder that all of our old competitors are applying to join the Vocational Center-those old rogues have since become so friendly that one would think they were bosom friends all of their lives. Come to think of it-why not?-with no labor or financial trouble they could have a wonderful life. Can you blame them for feeling so kindly towards all especially now when we are all prospective brother members? 35

CHAPTER XVII 

The large manufacturer saw how practical the Vocational Center could be for the small manufacturer, but he felt that his business was too complex for a simple solution. His subconscious mind reviewed the mesh of detail that had him going, not only during the day, but even at night. He spent sleepless hours trying to find a way to standardize and simplify operations. It seemed that as soon as he solved one phase of the problem, conditions would suddenly appear to upset it, and start him off again on another tangent. This happened so many times, that he figured that if it was really beyond him, the expert and founder of the business, to solve these constantly recurring problems, how could a change of organization help matters. But, nevertheless, if someone could show him ways of more positive control of sudden fluctuations that made useless so many of his plans, he would welcome such assistance-it would at least mean greater peace of mind. He was now willing to discuss some of the conditions that upset him during his business career.

His first setback was the lawsuit brought by the "H" Co. for patent infringement. This dragged for years. He had to bond his customers for their protection in order to get some of their business, for they were also threatened as a party to infringement of the patents. The many conferences with his attorneys took a great deal of his time, in addition to his other duties, and the cost of their services made quite a dent in his capital. He finally reached an agreement with the "H" Co., which somehow was not profitable to either party, and both agreed that the entire fight was ridiculous and regretted that it had ever started. Yet right now the "W" Company was contemplating a suit against him on another patent situation, and he was wondering on what terms he could settle it for his company. 

The second setback he had was the strike of his workers which tied him up for ten weeks. He was almost frantic to get that settled and even with his large surplus of cash he could not have stood it very much longer.

The third setback he had was the dumping on the market of a large amount of foreign merchandise at less than his cost prices. 

This left him holding the bag with a large inventory, and the cost of keeping his organization that he could not dispense with, if he was still going to remain in business. 

The fourth setback he had was the price war with the other competitors in his field. It lasted for one year due to the slack in business. He had slashed wages right and left, cut down every conceivable item and even then he could not meet the competition. The worse actor was the "S" Company, who had borrowed a million and a half dollars on a bond issue (first mortgage) and was trying to use this money to get control of the market. This enabled them to steal the march on him. Now with the poor showing of the last year the only money he could raise to fight the "S" Company was from bank loans. The interest on these would be high and add to his present losses. The bank loans had to be gradually liquidated and could not be repaid from a business that was losing money. Of course, besides these problems, he always had to meet the same ordinary problems of capital, labor and markets that the small manufacturer had. 36

Let us now see whether the Vocational Center would have eliminated his serious setbacks, over the ordinary ones we have already solved for the small manufacturer. 

That patent suit would not have been necessary with its loss to all parties. It was not even a gain to the attorneys who could be doing constructive work, instead of receiving wages for wasted work as neither of the parties in the suit profited. There was a loss not only of time and money of these clients, but also a loss in public money. The total cost of the administration of the courts in trying these cases for these companies was far greater than what was collected in expense from the losing client commonly known as court costs. The Vocational Center Members are all cooperative owners of the Center and as such would submit their ideas to the Patent Division of the Center. This department, in cooperation with the inventor, will experiment and develop the commodities for the ultimate use of all Vocational Members. The inventor, when called upon, will undoubtedly enlighten us as to the best procedure for this setup. 

Some members of Private Industry, not in sympathy with our plan, may try to embarrass us in the beginning, by bringing law suits against the Center, for infringement on some of the products that we attempt to produce. If this happens it will be necessary for our Patent 

Division to solicit all labor, retail stores, small and large manufacturers in Private Industry who are sympathetic with our plan, to give us their cooperation and aid. Our members in the Center who are attorneys, will at minimum cost to us cooperate to make these suits expensive to the plaintiffs, so that only the most hardened plaintiff will carry on the suit. However, our Patent Department will be very careful to protect the rights of others on the products we produce, and offer a fair settlement in case we must utilize the original ideas of others, who are not members of our Center.

The large manufacturer's second setback was the labor strike that almost made him bankrupt. As we have previously stated, labor in the Center being cooperative and therefore owners of the enterprise, determining their own income, by the number of hours they are willing to work, will therefore, have no occasion to strike, as they do in 

Private Industry. 

His third setback was the dumping of foreign goods. Our Vocational Members who constitute their own market and produce their own goods, are not interested in foreign merchandise unless it is produced by Vocational Centers in foreign countries. They will trade commodities on the basis of Vocational Dollars. These dollars will represent the actual labor hours entering into the production and therefore a true cost. If labor hours are as efficient in production in all countries, the exchange can take place on any equal basis-and if not, the country having the most efficient labor is not going to desire to exchange products for those of a lower quality. The Vocational 

Press will illustrate to Vocational Center Members, all over the world, all new items as well as improvements on existing items, and their cost in Vocation Dollars. This standard will act as a guide to those 

Vocational Centers in other countries that are less efficient and encourage them to expend extra effort to reach the highest standard, so that they may obtain the advantages of equal exchange in products. 

His fourth setback was the price war between competitors and the juggling of finances by his worse competitor to gain a strong hold 37

of the market. We have already shown that the market of the Vocational Center consists of the members themselves, directly producing for their own needs. Therefore, no condition to corner this market can exist, as it does in Private Industry. One cannot compete against himself. The juggling of finances is in the same category. All of the capital of the Center is controlled by a capital account, and as stated previously it represents the surplus labor of the Vocational Members, who in turn form their own market. It is not a fund created to make one member and break another as capital acts in Private Industry. It is cooperative for the good of all members. 

The large manufacturer was still not convinced for he saw his personal power reduced to that of any executive status, the same as hundreds of others. He was different from the retailer or small manufacturer, inasmuch as while he had problems, there were periods when he had a lot of money at his command and absolute freedom in acquiring anything that could be purchased with his money. This he considered was independence and an incentive for him to carry on in spite of all obstacles. No, he thought, the Vocational Center may be good for ordinary people, but not for me. I am considered a success. 

It is difficult to reason with a gambler who will plunge all of his fortune on a lucky card, knowing that in the event of loss, his family will go begging. It is almost as difficult to reason with a man who has been a financial success in Private Industry. He cannot be made to understand that his personal talent which he himself cannot account for, has contributed but slightly towards his success. He does not realize that the people who work for him, the public who constitutes his market and the wonders of providence in furnishing the raw materials are contributing factors to his success. He also does not think of the heat, light and power which transforms raw material into various forms. He does not appreciate the factor of human effort which for thousands of years has plowed into improvements typified by modern machinery, buildings and methods. Time, temperament of the people, economic conditions—all had a bearing on his personal success. His health, family heritage, friends, neighbors, even his enemies, all contributed their share, either directly or indirectly. In view of these facts, a man's egotism must certainly be exaggerated to consider himself the big shot of the works. It is further very difficult to reason with this individual, that his economic freedom is only possible, if he has with it political freedom. This cannot exist very long in countries that deny a great percentage of the population a decent standard of living. He cannot see that peace as well as plenty must exist, for even his own family is not safe from the horrors of war, irrespective of the size of his fortune. 

Can he forget the many recurring periods since the Great War, when the entire world was again on the verge of an even worse catastrophe. Does he remember the frenzy and confusion of the nations desperately purchasing temporary peace at any price-even to the extent of sacrificing the independence of democratic countries? 

Does he realize the torture of people all over awaiting the minute for the call to become cannon fodder; their homes to become targets for bombs and poisonous gases? Does he know that armaments, in spite of temporary peace still are increasing? This may be old stuff to him, but unless he is open to reason, he is a contributing factor to these conditions and on account of his position, a very important one 38

His eyes may be larger than his stomach in thinking that his money will buy him a better living than he could obtain if he were an executive in the Center. The executive standard of living in the Center (eliminating waste and at the same time cooperating for the attainment of the highest standard of living for all its members) ought to be as great and possibly greater than his standard. His real wants are limited by his consumption of them and is no greater than those of even the poorest individuals. If individual gain is the reason for his incentive, how can he account for an American soldier, who risked his life capturing single-handed a machine gun and sixty men of the enemy. What did he get for it? A small congressional medal of no real value except the honor of it. A large manufacturer's incentive is not due to his desire for wealth accumulation, even though he thinks so-for if it were-why do also our millionaires who have more than they can ever consume during a lifetime, accept some of the positions carrying the greatest responsibility in the country? Why do these same millionaires on vacation wear the simplest of working clothes and look for their pleasure in fishing, hunting and golf-sports open through public parks to all members of our society, irrespective of their financial position. Man by nature obtains a real pleasure and incentive in life by being of service to others, if at first their immediate families are well provided for. What finer provision can a man give his family than that of a good standard of living, security and peace. This service to others is a real incentive to all human beings, though in Private Industry it is disguised with masks of false definitions. Our reactions to labor, land, capital, production and consumption are so distorted, that exploitation, dishonesty, ignorance, intolerance and wastefulness are regarded as incentives, if a particular individual profits by any one of them. His social position is not even jeopardized by an analysis of his income if he manages to keep within the law-itself full of trickery and loopholes for the wealthy offender. 

The large manufacturer finally began to awake to the logic of these facts and realized that he too must assist all other people to attain a decent standard of living, if he wanted to continue to hold on to his own. This goal must be his incentive to succeed. What can be do to assist? "Well, gentlemen," says he, “I will admit that if

I operated my plant as a unit of the Vocational Center, I could effect a number of economies. Every dull season I have to discharge hundreds of workers only to rehire all over again when I have a busy season. These workers cannot sit at home and wait until business picks up, as few of them even have a week's wages in reserve. Therefore, they accept whatever job they can get. Some of them move out of the city and some of them go on relief. The result is that when we rehire men we have to take on many new help. These have to be trained and while they are being broken in they are a loss to the company. The Vocational Center would do away with this waste, as there will be no guess as to a market for its products. The Vocational Members who will have a secured standard of living, as long as they are willing to work for it, will have no need to fear depressions, so they will not let fear affect their purchasing power. They will not be influenced by high pressure advertising, showing preference to one class of merchandise, at the sacrifice of another, as all commodities will be truthfully presented in stores and catalogues as to cost and utility. The members will further have the opportunity 39

to work extra hours at all times, should they desire additional luxuries. There should be no need to forego any item that was in their original budgets. Constant budgets will serve as true indicators for determining production schedules. Arrangements will undoubtedly be made, so that the currency of the Vocational Center will be issued annually and must be spent during the year. This would encourage the members to spend all they earn except the amounts that they set aside annually, toward purchases like a home or an automobile. Items that could be paid for over a period of their normal lasting qualities-for example, a home could be paid for in twenty years, or an automobile five years or sooner, depending on the agreement among the Vocational Members themselves." "Another economy that could be effected," said the large manufacturer, "would be a reduction of my inventory. I, as well as my competitors, am compelled to carry a large stock of raw materials, semi-finished parts and finished products, for we must at all times be prepared to meet a sudden increased demand, or lose sales. The 

Vocational Center warehouses would eliminate all duplicate and excessive inventory of a number of manufacturers, now catering to the same market in Private Industry, for the Center would have a definite account of the demand of their entire market. This knowledge will enable the warehouse to carry only such inventory that will meet this constant demand, the buying needs of the members not being influenced by the sudden and economic upheavals current in Private Industry. The enormous sales expense that manufacturers have to contend with at present in trying to capture the same trade with but slight differences in product, will be entirely eliminated, for Vocational Centers have no need for salesmen, other than stores, which serve all members equally. A large number of smaller factories could be merged with not only a saving of space, but a saving of management. Cooperation for mutual savings will give the Vocational 

Center Members goo value and improved products. The suggestions of members to the managers of stores will receive direct attention from the Merchandising Division, who will have the responsibility of using these suggestions towards improvement. In Private Industry, most manufacturers make changes only when they are practically faced with a loss of markets, for while there are profits, they concentrate on volume and delivery only. There are very few individual manufacturers who can carry on independently expensive research, unless they virtually have a monopoly of a given product. All manufacturers know there are dull times, as well as good ones, and they conserve their profits as a reserve to tide them over, instead of expending them in organized research. 

Another economy that Vocational Center membership will permit is standardization of operations of an entire industry to save the utmost in labor. The greater the saving, the more hours they will have to devote to the manufacture of additional products that will add to our standard of living." "I now can truthfully say," said the large manufacturer, "that I am sincerely in favor of Vocational Centers where each member will know just what he needs and proceed to work to produce it without waste, guess or interference-where the fear of unemployment, hunger or poverty, will not exist in human relationship-where a man can make machinery his slave, instead of being enslaved by machin- 40

ery. One cannot expect these Vocational Centers to solve every problem immediately. There will always be new problems in production and distribution presented by continual progress. That, however, is where our incentive comes in.

Nature decrees by her system of evolution an inborn desire in all human beings to raise themselves to the highest standard that their intelligence portrays.

When we cooperate the efforts of all human beings to strive for the highest standard of living, we are presenting a portrayal of activity that the simplest mind can visualize. This then arouses the natural desire of that person into play to exert his efforts towards the higher standard advocated. 

If we are cooperatively organized to continually increase these standards, the individual will to establish them follows."

CHAPTER XVIII 

The farmer was the next around the table. He thought that what he might say would affect the food supply of the nation. He knew that the world understood his problems as there had been attempts to assist him in showing him the best methods of production. There also had been some sort of cooperative effort on the part of the government, to make possible low cost, power and irrigation for his use. However, no one had been able to do much for him on his labor problems or on his uncertainties of production due to the uncontrollable elements. The lack of price control of his products and the difficulties of distribution were still questions that had no answer. 

He was very much impressed with what the previous speakers had mentioned in regard to the basic fundamentals of demand, supply, labor and living standards, which past proposals to him in Private Industry had only partly touched. All proposals that he had received generally dealt with inflation, deflation, curtailment, dumping and premiums for non-production. These measures were not effective, because his markets were guesses, his costs were a gamble and his trading was trickery. "I am trying to think," said the farmer, "in what way the Vocational Center could assist me and if there are any reasons why I could not cooperate with it in solving at least some of my major problems. Of course, my work, you appreciate, is entirely different from the previous speakers. I can see the definite advantages they outlíned. My work, however, is individual and subject to elements beyond my control. I would like to visualize the advantages the Center would offer me and that could be applied to my farming. Pardon me if I think aloud and try to follow my thoughts.

One of my problems is to know how much to raise of my various products to meet the market demand. In the past, I have tried to raise all I could on my land within the limits of my capital and then attempted to dispose of it in the most profitable manner. Whatever 

I have not been able to dispose of at decent prices, has been naturally disposed of at the best prices I could obtain. This same activity on the part of all of the individual farmers has created a large surplus of commodities, at sacrificed prices during the periods when the market demands have been reduced. I therefore, can see that if the market demands could be predetermined it would at last set a standard for us to follow. We could then try to produce to meet these markets, making allowances for certain reserves to substantially com- 41

pensate for losses due to the elements as experienced in past years.

The previous speakers have already shown how the Vocational Center assures a definite market as the members are the market. 

They can plan a budget and have the free opportunity extended to them to earn sufficient income to cover it. Their needs can be carried out as planned for there will be no high pressure advertising to make them sacrifice one need for the attainment of another. The Vocational currency will be of constant value to further guide them as to the true cost, assuring in all a true balance which cannot be obtained in Private Industry. 

Under present Private Industry, the market is only partly affected by the needs of the people, because of their fear due to insecure employment, unstable foreign conditions, fluctuating stock market prices. 

The effect of advertising pressure for one product against another bidding for the consumers' limited dollar, also leads to an unstable market. 

With a predetermined market, I would also be in a better position to plan my labor requirements. My help instead of receiving wages that are based on the smallest amount they can possibly be hired for (irrespective of whether or not they can exist on them) will under the Vocational Center plan receive wages equal to the hours they put in without profit to anybody except themselves. Their standard of living will be equal to those of the same skill that are employed in other classes of industry, working the same number of hours. This will encourage the services of those that enjoy farming, in preference to industrial work. Nature has endowed all of us with varied natural abilities. There are a large number of workers that enjoy labor in the open air instead of factories. A number that love to take care of cattle instead of machinery. There is a large group who love the soil instead of the city. These workers could be trained and made far more efficient, than some of the present farm labor, that is forced to work at something they do not enjoy. They either work or go hungry.

The condition of workers producing for themselves, in work that they enjoy, would eliminate dissatisfaction and strikes with its waste and ill-will. With efficient and satisfied labor at my disposal, I can then advise the Vocational Center, by the records of my past experi ences, as to the quality and quantity of products I anticipate producing on my land, also the approximate labor and hours that I think I will need to do this work. The Vocational Center then allocates for my use an amount of Vocational Dollars that I may draw against, equal to my estimated labor hours required times the rate of the various classes of labor I will utilize, including my own. I will be at liberty to ask for suggestions from farm experts of the Center as to the best methods of producing my products. These men have this data based on experiences of other farmers who produce the same commodities as I do. The raw materials will be sent in to me as I require them. I then proceed to farm and try to exceed my original estimate of what I calculated I could produce on my farm. If uncertain elements of hurricane, flood, frost, drought, insects and a dozen and one other conditions over which I have no control limit my production, I will advise the Center as soon as I am aware of the existing losses. They in turn make whatever arrangements are necessary to assist me, and at the same time do all they can to increase 42

production, of the same commodities, at some other farms, to make up my shortages. If they cannot do this, they draw on whatever reserves are set aside for this purpose. As the Center knows what to expect in the raising and producing of farm products, by the reports of the various farmers, they will advise the Vocational Members through the Vocational Press, to make substitutes in their purchases so as to maintain a balance between production and consumption. 

This also eliminates the problem of financing, that I have as an individual farmer under Private Industry. Not knowing my market, the ultimate prices and the uncontrollable elements of nature, it is difficult for me to know just how much to borrow, even if I had the necessary collateral on which to make a loan. The Vocational Center will arrange to make such mergers with other smaller farms, that will reduce my costs, simplify transportation and improve the quality of my products.

The next problem I have is sales. Being a farmer and not experienced or organized for selling, I am at the present time at the mercy of speculators. Especially is this true in bad times. The Vocational Center will take that problem entirely off my hands. The 

Vocational warehouse will requisition my products with instructions, to either ship direct to distributing stores or warehouses, depending on whether it is slow or fast moving. merchandise. The Center has no products to sell, but only to distribute, as the market consists of the requirements of their own Vocational Center Members. The

Vocational Press will advise all of its members just what products will be forthcoming, the prices, and suggested purchases for proper standards of living.

Continued experience will adjust these standards to a proper balance between all divisions of the Vocational Center, as there will be constant earning power to meet constant demand and increased earning power to meet increased demand. 

The next problem I have is transportation. Now in Private Industry, I have to plan my shipments as an individual, yet fit into transportation arrangements that are organized for all general demand. 

If I have to ship less than a carload, to a given point, it means more expensive crating and longer delivery schedules. I have no way now of combining my shipments with other farms, for they are my competitors, trying to sell the same customers. I therefore, would not wish to disclose my customers to them. I have the same problems in trucking shipments. If I own my own trucks, then I am almost in the trucking business, with its own individual problems of maintenance, help, observance of interstate laws-problems entirely aside from those of my farming. My market being uncertain, I cannot regulate my trucks to proper capacity schedules. I therefore, have an investment in trucking equipment that is not always in use. This ties up some of my limited working capital in idleness. If I contract to let others do my trucking, my shipments do not always fit in with their schedules, and they must go out and seek other business to keep their trucks in constant use. I therefore, cannot depend upon their services for ready deliveries. Partial shipments by truck also compel me to go to the expense of extra crating, to protect them from other classes of merchandise in the same truck. 

The Vocational Center will have their points of distribution all over the country. They will determine the quantities required and 43

the time they are required. They can therefore combine shipments to all of their distribution points to get full carloads, quick schedules and minimum packing. These matters will be in the hands of men who are up to the minute in this specialized work, cooperating with me for our mutual gain. 

Chain stores in Private Industry have attempted to obtain efficiency in transportation and some of the other phases of distribution. They, however, use this efficiency to exact a low price from the farmer who is dependent upon their purchases. These prices are so close, that in a number of instances we are compelled to become Simon Legrees to our labor and chattels to our bank, in order to exist, with a threat of closing our markets if we strike. 

These low prices permit the large class of small wage earners the opportunity of buying at least some necessities that they must have to live. However, if these prices do not cover the costs of production, some other class of our population is paying the difference from their impoverished existence. Prices must at least equal costs to enable all of us to exist. However, these costs should be reduced by the cooperation of our intelligence, resources and labor to attain the objective of the highest standard of living for all. 

The Vocational Center cannot eliminate the uncertainties of farming, due to the action of the elements. It will, however, be organized to cooperatively tackle those conditions, far more effectively than Private Industry can, individually controlled. Where labor, capital, production and distribution can function as one unit for the Center's economic good, they can certainly accomplish more than these same units competing against each other, not only as classes, but also as sub-classes and individuals. 

Of course, in a discussion of the advantages of operating my farm as a cooperative production unit of the Vocational Center, I did not take into consideration my personal position. I can assure you that it is no different from the previous speakers', inasmuch as I too, require but three things. They are first, the finest living that the national resources, science and labor can produce, cooperating for my benefit and using my services to the best of my natural abilities-second, the security of income for my natural life, if I am willing to work as long as I am physically and mentally able to-third, good will among all of my fellow human beings, so that I can respect them and receive respect from them for my individual rights as to freedom of speech, pursuit of happiness and spiritual liberty." 

CHAPTER XIX 

The employee, who was next in turn, represented the large majority of workers. He was, by this discussion, receiving an education in the various phases of his economic existence. Conditions that he was not even aware of affected his very existence, and he felt ashamed of the fact that he had given them so little thought. When he periodically lost his job, it was a case of tough luck. When he could not provide for his family, he was considered a bum and a lazy good-for-nothing. He inwardly knew that those insulting him were wrong, but he had no defense. The usual statement of "I cannot obtain work" was generally replied to by the usual answer-"Any man who is willing to work at anything can always get a job”, and 44

to substantiate these statements, numerous examples were given of this and that person refusing to work, after being offered a job. The fact that the pay offered was insufficient to provide even a meager living, or that the job was of such a nature, that he was either physically or mentally unsuited for it, did not alter the situation and the reaction as to his unemployment was the same. The shifting of the blame to his shoulders justified the consciences of those who had what they needed, from sympathizing with those who had not. No consideration was given to the fact that the worker, by tying himself down to a job with insufficient pay to exist on, or one that he could not hold for any length of time, was killing his chances of getting the proper job, both as to pay and ability. The result was that the idle employee was just a lonesome soul, fighting against a hardened world.

The strange thing, however, was the fact that even when the employee was fortunate in obtaining the proper job, he too became hardened. He felt that it must have been his personal qualities of determination and persistence that persuaded the employer to hire him. He developed the ego that if these same personal qualities existed in others who were unemployed, they could also obtain a position.

Employees who did look through the want ads, never analyzed why there were one or two columns for regular help wanted on a salary basis in comparison to approximately ten columns for sales help wanted, on a commission basis. If employees did study this condition, they would soon recognize that the big problem in Private Industry consisted in finding a market for the commodities produced. 

This market not only required buyers that needed commodities, but those that could also pay for them. The lack of buyers that could afford to purchase, was the fact that made for unemployment. It was not just a case of tough luck, as was the impression of the employee.

Now that the previous speakers had disclosed a plan of Vocational Cooperative Centers, in which this employee was a very important factor, it was really up to him to speak his mind and state his reaction to it and how he would care to function as a member. "Gentlemen, being an employee, I have always been interested in one result-good wages and steady employment. I consider a wage as good if it can buy my family and myself a decent living, judged by the standards of the other fellows in the neighborhood who have similar jobs to my own at steady work all year round. I would only expect more luxuries if I were to be promoted to the position of foreman or manager. Personal politics played an important part in our factory, and interfered with my effort to prove that I was worth a better job. There was also such preference shown to neighbors and relatives of the boss, that I knew I had very little chance of becoming foreman or manager. I could not force the consideration of any of my suggestions for improvement because the favored ones could have me fired. A steady job had to be my first consideration. 

You can therefore appreciate that when a cooperative organization such as the Vocational Center offers me the opportunity of steady work and a good living, I would naturally be interested. When it further assures me of the chance to qualify for any responsibilities I wish to assume with an increase in income and in my standard of 45

living, I cannot hestitate even for a moment. If I am a member of the Center, I can work as hard as I please and know that my total labor is repaid to me in the food, clothing, shelter and luxuries that my family need, without any profit going to others. Those who don't work will not be a burden that I have to carry. Everybody associated with me receives a return only in accordance with their real knowledge, effort and responsibility. The knowledge that no one can hoard their earnings to take advantage of my work at their price will make me realize that I am truly a free man.

This is the reason. Under the Center, currency issued in a current year will have no value when the year expires. Therefore, members will spend their income. It will not be necessary to save money as capital to later enable them (as it would under Private Industry) to control other workers in a private business, as all members work cooperatively for the benefit of all. Further, the right that I have in cooperation with other members, to determine the rating of all Vocational Center Members including those that have even the greatest responsibilities will give me the control I should have for my own security. True, I realize that these ratings must be fair, but I will have access to the facts to be considered. I will have the free opportunity at all times to accept equal responsibility with the highest executive, if I can pass the tests to prove my ability and effort. Every encouragement will be given me by the Center to qualify for these positions. A steady job which is so vital to me in Private Industry, will be an assured thing at the Vocational Center, because as a consumer I can always work to produce my needs. For the first time in my life I will feel that I am supporting directly my government, both local and federal, by my own labor. I will therefore, take a new interest in how they operate. Government extravagances will be my sweat and I do not intend to let them dissipate my labor without just cause. They will arrange my affairs as a citizen, as to what I can afford to pay in relationship to my needs, an entirely different basis than that which exists in Private Industry. Our currency, represented by Vocational Dollars that are equal to hours of labor, is currency we can understand. The results will be entirely different from what workers have to contend with today. 

Wages are the same as they were several months ago when our necessities were far lower in cost. We do not know why, but it so happens to be. For example, if Vocational Centers in two different countries had similar machines to produce a given commodity (these machines as standard equipment for an industry had an equal output), the only difference in production between the two would be the amount of labor necessary to operate them.

It will either have to add some other commodity that it can produce for $2.00 to its original offering to make the value equal, or it will have to increase its labor efficiency to match that of Center No. 2, or it will not manufacture that particular commodity at all. In the first two cases, Center No. 1 will recognize that it must either be satisfied with a smaller return-with a lower standard of living for the worker or an increase of output per worker which means a higher standard of living. But, Center No. 1 will undoubtedly confine its efforts to the commodities it can most efficiently produce. 

Compare this with the methods used in Private Industry. The banker was now called upon for his practical suggestions of how the currency of the Vocational Centers could be organized and circulated so as not to conflict with present Private Industry. "Gentlemen," said he, "in my previous discussion I pointed out the difficulties ensuing from a currency that is not based on the wealth of human labor. I, therefore, recommend that in the Vocational Center the currency should be based on the time of one hour of ordinary labor. The unit for this hour shall be known as one Vocational Dollar. Otherwise, our present method of handling currency could be incorporated into the Vocational Center with but little change. The Centers could issue a supply of certificates in denominations of 1, 5, 10 and 25 dollars, sufficient in number, that will afford free circulation of their requirements for a definite period, with allowance for the time to reprint them. These certificates would have the day, month and year of printing, plainly visible, and a serial number for identification and classification. They could also be arranged in different colors or effects for the year in which they were going to be issued. The seal of the Vocational Center could be engraved, embossed or perforated, similar to check protection, so that counterfeiters would have difficulty in reproducing them. These certificates could be further identified as capital certificates, issued for payment of capital accounts, and purchase certificates, issued for payment of wages, to differentiate between their purposes, similar to reserve or currency notes in banking.

Members would be given these purchase certificates in accordance with the hours and rating of their work. Each member's signature and membership number to be machine duplicated on the certificates, when they are issued to him weekly or monthly. This signature and number converts these certificates to currency. They will further identify the issuance and prevent the unsigned certificates from being used as currency.

Members then pay monthly, or have charged to their account, all Vocational dues, payable as their share of the services and commodities, the Center assumes as a body. For instance-support of government, welfare, education, insurance and similar assessments. 

There will also be issued at the same time, his contribution to the capital account, which is organized for the payments of land, machinery, loans and other similar expenditures. A statement will be rendered to each member for these individual assessments on monthly or semi-monthly basis. 

The member then proceeds to spend his currency, as he desires, in proportion to what he himself budgeted as to his allowance for food, clothing, shelter, transportation, luxuries, etc. He also pays into an annual fund, any payment he has obligated himself to, in the purchase of more than annual expenditures, such as a home, automobile, or some other capital expenditure.

Each member can deposit any Vocational Dollars that he desires to use later into a Vocational Bank as a basis for a checking account. 

Identification by his membership number and signature will make his check acceptable in any Vocational store or service depot. 54

There will be an exchange rate given daily in the newspapers of the relationship between the Vocational Dollar and its denomina tions and Private Industry currency. 

The operation of the exchange rate will be treated as the operation of foreign exchange in various countries is at present. 

Arrangements will be made so that banks in Private Industry will accept Vocational money from certain businesses connected with Private Industry, such as theatres, restaurants, hotels, public conveyances or any other similar businesses not engaged in the sale of merchandise that can be exchanged or traded. These businesses, of course, have the option of spending the Vocational money directly in the Vocational stores.

The exchange rate between the Vocational Dollars and, for instance, the currency of the United States, will be worked out as follows: The Vocational Dollar is based, as already discussed, on one hour of ordinary labor. In order to have a comparison in other currencies, we must determine what one hour of ordinary labor earns in Private Industry. The government of the United States has the data on hours and wages which they obtained by taking the average earnings and hours of all ordinary workers in the twelve leading industries of the country. It may be, for instance, $.50 per hour, then actually one Vocational Dollar is equal to $.50 of Private Industry currency. But, you will say, supposing that the Vocational Center labor cannot produce in one hour, what the ordinary labor can produce in Private Industry. In that case the value would not be true. 

You are right, and it will automatically adjust itself in this way. 

Efficiency of the Vocational Center Members will be expressed in the merchandise that they produce and distribute, through the Vocational stores. Now supposing that you were engaged in business in Private Industry that permitted you to have some Vocational 

Dollars that you wanted to convert into private money. You note that the exchange rate in the newspapers ordinarily give it as one to two. You decide that maybe you can obtain better value by spending your Vocational Dollars at the Vocational stores, to purchase the things you require that are there on display, instead of converting it through the bank into Private Industry currency. Now, if you obtain better value you will not exchange that currency at the bank.

Now, supposing when you shop you note that the value of merchandise in Vocational stores represents one Vocational Dollar to four dollars of Private Industry currency or double its previous value of one to two. You would seek to obtain all the Vocational Dollars you could get hold of. In fact you would offer up to 334 of Private 

Industry dollars for one Vocational Dollar, because at that figure it would still represent a $.25 profit in the currency of Private Industry. Others would do the same and there would be a sudden demand upon the banks of Private Industry to obtain these Vocational Dollars for exchange. As there will be only a limited number of Vocational Dollars in circulation in Private Industry the banks will offer the biggest premium that their customers are willing to pay. Their willingness was represented by 334. Therefore, the exchange rate becomes one Vocational Dollar to 334 of Private Industry Dollars.

Now supposing on the other hand, that the value at the Vocational stores disclosed that the value did not warrant the one to two 55

exchange rate. You would then dispose of your Vocational Dollars at the bank immediately. Other people having Vocational Dollars would do the same. The banks would have more Vocational Dollars than people want. The rate of exchange would then fall lower and lower, giving the Vocational Dollar a very low value as compared to the value of Private Industry dollars. It would reach a point where at that exchange rate the merchandise at the Vocational stores (expressed in Vocational Dollars) would represent a much greater value than what you could buy with the Private Industry dollars (you obtained for that Vocational Dollar) in Private Industry stores. A demand would then be made by Private Industry people for Vocational Dollars again so that they could obtain the merchandise in Vocational stores. This demand would be reflected in the banks again as explained above and the exchange rate would soon follow suit until it reached a high point wherein it was not profitable to seek any Vocational merchandise. 

While the relationship between Vocational currency and Private Industry currency varies, the Vocational Dollar in itself is constant for all times as to what it can obtain in exchange for merchandise at Vocational stores. The prices are true costs, and will not be subject to the variation that the prices of merchandise in Private Industry have to contend with which is due to fear on the part of consumers to purchase because of bad times. They will really enjoy the fruits of their labor without fear of future want or a guilty conscience of a spendthrift. 

The reduced costs of maintaining this simplified Vocational banking system as compared with Private Industry will mean a large saving to the Center Members. They will be free from the interest charges that Private Industry has to pay for the extravagant maintenance of its banking system, and also the losses incurred by banks in the uncertain speculative means of evaluating investments that have no constant worth.

CHAPTER XXI 

The housewife by this time was also beginning to grasp the functions that production, distribution, consumption and currency perform in our everyday existence. In Private Industry, she did not pay any attention to these matters other than asking John, her husband, for his pay. When it was not forthcoming because of unemployment, she knew the hardship and suffering to expect. John did not even know why he was laid off. All he received was a pink slip stating, "Your services are no longer required". However, when he questioned the foreman the only reply he received was, that business was bad and no future orders were on hand. 

Why? How? When? Where?-no one seemed to know.

This happened so many times in his working life that he questioned no longer, but took for granted that all he served as was a tool to be laid aside when not required. His poor wife was the goat, for she could not appear to be discouraged for fear of affecting his morale, as he immediately had to start hunting for work and present a brave front in competition with the others.

The whipped dog expression of the men he had seen apply for the many jobs which he had obtained, was the result of either a critical wife, or a long layoff. His wife remembered his recital of these conditions and tried her best to hide her real feelings. 

She had always secretly prayed for steady work for her man, and now that a movement was being organized to give him not only steady work but work that yielded as good a wage as he was willing to work for, it as with difficulty that she could control her emotions. "Gentlemen," said she, "I realize now what an ignorant fool I have been for not studying our means of existence as a member of the human race, but concentrating instead on my own existence as an individual. If I had, I would soon see that my lot as an individual is entirely dependent upon society as a whole, not only in this country, but society of all of the countries of the world. 

I have paid in suffering for this neglect-the death of my brother in the world war-and now one of my boys in prison for keeping bad company. This was a result of his lack of purpose in his long layoff from his last job.

I have the constant fear that my husband will lose his new job, one of several since our married life. The knowledge of the poverty and misery that always come when he is discharged is almost unbear- 58

able. But now that is all over-because I can see that the Vocational 

Center gives my man the opportunity to work at something he enjoys and therefore, is fitted to do, at a standard of living only limited by his own efforts.

When I receive my Vocational Press every morning, I will not be compelled to read pages full of murders, rackets, prostitution, distatorships and wars-which I now know is 90 per cent the fault of the system, that not only causes unemployment, but shrugs its shoulders at the social menace that it brings. 

When the government of all of the people of a country is compelled to offer some assistance to its outcasts, to prevent total disintegration of the system, it is accused of extravagance, regulation and even hypocracy. I know that these accusations are made, because public relief increases taxation.

It seems a paradox, but public relief begets more relief instead of eliminating it. The reason for these opposing conditions is, because the relief which is paid for by increased taxation is indirectly added to the cost of commodities. This increased cost further reduces demand, which in time creates more unemployment with its resultant additional relief. Private Industry, therefore, does not like relief. 

Why do they not eliminate it then. The truth as I see it now is, that they cannot, for the entire arrangement seems to be lopsided." 

This housewife may be a young newlywed, a young matron or the wife of a man over forty. She receives in Vocational Dollars the return for the hours that her husband worked, at the rate of his services determined by responsibility, knowledge and effort. She knows about how much she can spend for food, clothing, shelter, amusements, travel, etc. She can visit the Vocational stores in her neighborhood, which function as either specialty or department stores and select the commodities she requires. She then makes disbursements for her expenses that are necessary.

If her requirements are more than her husband is earning, she urges him to either put in additional hours of work, or study to pass tests for a higher rating. The free opportunity is there, as in Vocational Centers there is no limit to the hours he wishes to work, except by limitations of his physical and mental powers. Periodical examinations of Vocational Center Members will establish these limits, both for active and inactive work. If a man's physical or mental condition is such that he is not in a position to do the work he has selected, proper recommendations are made by the Vocational Medical Division and if he is entirely incapable of work, he is admitted to the retired or sick list. In any event, this does not stop his regular income. 

Of course, if a man deliberately refuses to work and is physically able to, his family if it has no other means of existence has a right to demand forcible work for him. The Vocational Center then uses the influence of friends and neighbors and other Vocational Members, to make this man realize his responsibilities, as a provider. If this fails, he is separated from his family without privileges, until he is made to realize that idleness is more of a care than work. 

However, there are very few healthy men, who having the free opportunity to work at a vocation they enjoy, would deliberately loaf, especially after pressure is applied both by his own immediate family and neighbors.

The housewife, whose income in Private Industry did not even 59

cover half of her needs, will be in an entirely different position as the housewife of a Vocational Center Member. Insurance, medical attendance, musical and even college education, the large items of any budget, will be taken care of by the Vocational Center in their capital account and managed by experts. The costs will be paid for by her husband's labor. All efforts will be expended by the Center 

Members cooperatively to reduce these costs to the absolute minimum. Any misfortune to the provider of a family does not affect the income, for the Cooperative Insurance that he supports while working permits a continuance of the income until such circumstances arise that would call for its discontinuance. In this way innocent children and helpless wives are not subjected to misfortune beyond their control.

The funds that will be forthcoming for their support can in no way be taken from them, as it many times happens in Private Industry by swindling racketeers, who act as leeches upon the innocent. 

Any advice the housewife requires on housing or home planning will be given free of charge, by the Architectural Division of the Cen- Questions of education, social, musical, scientific, or academic education for the training of the child or adult, will be answered by experts in those fields. Her outlook on life and sphere of acquaintance will be enlarged, for now she is on an equal plane with any other Vocational Member and can obtain the free knowledge to place her mentally in the same position. 

This enlightened sphere will make the housewife tolerant of other people's beliefs. This tolerance, in combination with her economic independence, will fortify her against false doctrines of hate and envy. She will love and admire people for their contributions to public welfare. She will appreciate her fortunate position of living among peace and plenty. 

Human nature will now signify balance to her, not instability as she had previously interpreted it. Occasionally she had marvelled at the examples of the accuracy of natural laws. There was the instance when the astronomers foretold almost the exact minute of visibility of a comet even though there had been a lapse of seventy years since the previous appearance of the comet. She also marvelled at the unconscious working of her own body when she was in good health, so complicated yet so smooth. Still the obtaining of food, clothing and shelter for the average worker under Private Industry did not seem to follow the natural laws of order at all. In fact the complaint seemed to be aimed at the surplus of our resources rather than at the scarcity. Nature had apparently been too good to us.

The Vocational Center now indicated to her what really could be accomplished if we followed natural laws and put order into our efforts to seek a means of livelihood. She now saw that her duty as wife or mother was to acquire and distribute such knowledge that would make her neighbors also visualize that a higher standard of living was possible under the Vocational Center plan. 

Her conception of true religion always had been the relationship of all people living in peace and goodwill to further their common interest. She previously, however, was in no position to live up to it. John, her husband, asked no questions when he was given a job. 

He may have suspected that he was replacing someone else at a lower 60

wage, but he had to live and could have no scruples about the other man's discharge. It was a case of dog eat dog so he might as well take anything that came his way. Sunday school taught them both the Golden Rule, but it seemed to apply in their life as a "Gold Rule". 

The standard of living of any worker under the Vocational Center plan is entirely up to the ambition of the worker himself. Therefore, it will not be necessary to revise the budgets as previously outlined by the housewives when they determined their basic needs. The only fact that the housewives will be concerned with will be the classifications that their husbands come under as the ordinary worker, skilled worker, executive or director of a complete industry. 

An important factor of a household is the consideration of domestic help. Those housewives in Private Industry whose husbands made a good income and who could afford help, could procure the services of a woman and sometimes a man to assist them. The work being rather unpopular because of its confinement and long hours, made it difficult to obtain proficient and permanent help. Nothing is more. aggravating than training a servant to your household routine, only to have that person leave, forcing you to start training someone anew. 

The little relief that household help offers in some cases is not worth the trouble. 

How will the Vocational Center take care of this type of help?

Domestic help is considered at the Vocational Center no different from any other labor. Any person seeking that work and liking it can be trained to proficiency in that field, and will be paid at the same rate as all labor.

A Vocational Member family requiring domestic help would apply at the Domestic Division of the Vocational Center and state under what conditions she would desire this help. These conditions could be either sleeping in or out, the number of hours, size of family and similar information that is ordinarily given to an employment agency. The Vocational Center has in its membership classification the history of all of its members and approaches those whom they think would quality.

If the demand for domestic help is greater than the supply, hours would either be reduced or a higher rating given so as to make the terms more attractive. The applicants by accepting the conditions of the job voluntarily, will at least assure the family employing them that they will stay.

If If either party decides that the arrangement is unsatisfactory, she will apply to the Domestic Division again stating the reason. it is found that the fault is because of the short-comings of the domestic, she will be trained to overcome her deficiencies. In this case her income will not stop. If the fault lies with the housewife the regulations of the job will have to be adjusted. In calculating the compensation of domestics, the meals received by them will be charged against their earnings. The cost of these meals given by the family will be determined the same as are the meals in a Vocational Center restaurant. 

Of course the question arises-how can a member of ordinary income use the services of a domestic of an equal rating, or possibly higher. The answer is that the ordinary worker will only be able 61

to use this domestic on a part time basis. The meals given and the 

Vocational Dollars he can afford for domestic help determine the number of hours of use.

However, if the family can prove to the Domestic Division that help for the family must be obtained for the reason of poor health of the housewife, an application can be made to the Medical Division, who will assign a nurse to the family, instead of a domestic. This cost is included in the capital account that all members subscribe to for medical appropriation. To offset part of this expense, the family engaging the nurse will furnish her meals. 

There may be other services required in a household besides domestic help for which the family seeks temporary additional income. To provide for this, the worker is given the opportunity to work extra hours over his usual routine. Of course, he must be physically capable to do so. 

If a member is engaged in a cooperative task, it will naturally be necessary for all those contributing their labor to work the same number of hours. Any member, therefore, desiring to increase his income by additional hours, must permit the Vocational Center the right to place him at individual work so as not to make it necessary for the others to work too. The compensation for these extra hours is equal to the amount paid other members that perform these same tasks during normal hours. 

Therefore, the provider will be in a position to support his family without being forced to have his wife assist him. In case the wife wants to work, she would assume the same relationship as her husband does to his work at the Vocational Center. She will receive the income that is warranted by her work rating and number of hours she is willing to work. Of course her net return would probably not exceed that of the housewife, as she will be obliged to pay a domestic, to take care of her home duties.

There will be very little desire on the part of a woman to work for the additional income that it gives her, as her husband has every opportunity to earn what is required for the home budget. 

The housewife then began to reflect on the biggest force of destruction-War. When the war was declared in which her brother lost his life, the reason given by the countries that engaged in it was that they were fighting for their very national existence. The methods that they desperately used to avert this war at the time convinced her that they only faced the final issue because they were driven to it by the aggressor nations. Of course she did not understand why the nations that feared this war actually loaned money and sold armaments to the so-called aggressor nations almost to the day that war was declared. Thereby, building up the strength of the future enemy. She did not at the time try to find out why the economic distress of one country was no concern of the others, but their armaments were. The people of all countries hated war, and yet the leaders could not seem to prevent the periodic wars that were waged. 

Why didn't the leaders of all the nations get together and make satisfactory arrangements that would prohibit war? Why did not these leaders then recommend that Private Industry be abolished, and a system arranged whereby there would be no private profits involved? They probably would have, if they knew how the changes could be gradually incorporated, so as to eliminate the disturbance and confusion of a sudden upset of an existing system. The efforts of those sincere leaders, who did some thinking along these lines, were always bemoaned by others who kept repeating that Private Industry is the only arrangement that can develop individual incentive. If 

Private Industry were destroyed there would be no progress and we would all go back to the caveman period. The strange thing, however, is that the armies and navies that were organized for the protection of Private Industry and supposed to be the most efficient force in the country, are not in themselves operated for profit. Yet in the face of that, the leaders dared to tell the people that Private 

Industry and incentive were synonymous.

It just seemed to complex for her at the time, but now she saw through it all. She now felt that the leaders probably did know that production for use without profit by the nations would make the world one free market and eliminate war, as there would be no exclusive market or profit involved for any nation to start one. This, however, would tend to make all nations equal in power and of course that was a horse of a different color. Power by the sword, commanding thousands of men to die for no good reason, was much simpler for the leaders, than power earned by intelligence and merit. Housewives also proved contrary to the teachings of Private Industry, because they have no profit motive to give them incentive to perform the most difficult work of the family. Their incentive is love and duty and they should demand of their husbands that they support those leaders, who will have the same incentives-love for the people they rule and duty to guide these people in the proper path. Evidence of this will be furnished when these leaders advocate a system of cooperative efforts for common gain within a democratic structure for all nations. Vocational Centers are the embodiment of this arrangement.

CHAPTER XXII 

The inventor, the next around the table, could by his natural bent visualize things a trifle more clearly than the others. He noted that none of the previous speakers had mentioned one very important service that the Vocational Center could render their members, as compared with Private Industry, and that was the encouragement to every Vocational Member to develop as a part of his personality the incentive to get things done.

In Private Industry the one great bid for incentive was, as most people thought, the desire for wealth or the increase of income. Because of this anticipated financial gain, the entire burden was placed on the individual to prove himself equal to the reward offered for his success. The inventor who had devoted his efforts to new commodities found that the obstacles thrown in his path by capital, producers and consumers until the new product had proven itself, were hurdles that very few individuals could overcome. The knowledge 63

of these obstacles to those that attempted to face them became public information and discouraged all but the most persistent of individuals, who at the same time had to have trading genius, leisure, and financial means, to last through the long seige. 

The disastrous results of this difficult fight for recognition was the callous reaction on the part of most people to any new idea. 

The reading of "business opportunities" in the newspapers, disclosed that advertisers seeking purchasers for their present enterprises or those seeking business connections, generally stated that no patents or new ideas would be given any consideration. These advertisers as well as most individuals were well aware of the large fatality of new ideas before getting to first base. 

The attitude on the part of the public towards any new idea submerged the initiative of people including even the brainiest. It discouraged constructive work along original lines and compelled those with initiative to express this natural function in other ways. They turned instead to speculation, gambling and trading-none of which activities contribute at all to a higher standard of living for the nation. This misdirected force of initiative substituted false hopes of imagined wealth, instead of honest effort for real wealth. This weakness on the part of people made them targets for stock selling by many corporations seeking additional capital but not deserving of it. 

The worthy corporations suffer with the unworthy in the depression following every era of over-indulged speculation. 

Irrespective of all of this manipulation, Private Industry points to the accomplishment of making a large number of commodities available to the public of late. It fails to disclose that these innovations are only a handful as compared with the lost ideas that have been buried in the Patent Offices and suggestion boxes in the world of Private Industry. The argument that a number of them would not have been commercially profitable may be true. But, the procedure followed in the marketing of patents could never bring this to light. The tests that have been made are on an entirely different basis. They have been applied as hurdles in the path of the inventor to overcome. He must fight single handed and generally empty handed a system that talked initiative and incentive, but was not even organized to give either a fair hearing. "I can see," said the inventor, "the way in which the Vocational

Center could not only make it possible for people of initiative to have a fair opportunity to determine whether their ideas are sound but also a way in which they may reap their rewards for honest effort. 

This information made public will encourage every individual to exercise his natural function of initiative in the direction where it will do the most good.

Vocational Centers will have a Patent Department consisting of experts in various fields of endeavor, who will constitute a board of examiners of all ideas submitted. Instead of an inventor applying to the Patent Office direct, all Vocational Members who have some new thought or plan will describe it as best they can, or enclose a sketch similar to what one sends to a patent attorney in Private Industry. The examiners in that particular field will review it with the one purpose of seeing if it has any merit and what the preliminary costs of production would be. 64

If in their opinion there is no advantage over the existing method, a report is given to the member that subscribed it covering the examiners' findings and the reasons for them. If the member still feels that his idea is right and the examiners' corrections wrong, he makes application for another hearing and is convinced or convinces them that they are wrong.

This procedure in no way discourages the potential inventor, for it is constructive criticism that is being given him. In fact the suggestions disclosed may even enlighten him as to their necessary adoption and urge him to resubmit the idea in a different form. Now, so far he is not put to expense of applying for patents as he is in Private 

Industry. 

If the examiners decide that the idea has merit, they formulate a plan of preliminary tests. This may involve investigation or experiment. They arrange, as experts in their respective fields, the necessary measures that will satisfy their own requirements. This will take away from the inventor the problems of proof, that he is generally not familiar with, has no time for and for which he lacks funds. 

If the preliminary tests disclose further possibilities, it is carried on through the various steps of development until it is in its completed state. Of course, the inventor is consulted for any suggestions he may offer, in addition to those of any Vocational experts, who can by their past experiences assist the development. 

The cost of this is taken care of by the capital account of the Center, because the ultimate product is made available for the use of all Vocational Members. The original inventor is credited with the accomplishment. He immediately receives a membership in the Inventor's Club, a Vocational Center Division that not only sponsors the rewards to their fellow-inventors, but encourages those who have proved their ability, to spend a greater portion of their time, if not all in further research for the Vocational Center. 

A complete report of the successful invention is given to the Inventor's Club at the time the inventor becomes a club member. 

The importance of his invention is evaluated by his fellow members on these principles. Will it add to the standard of living or social progress of our members? Does it replace some of our physical effort? 

Will it offer increased utility to our present uses of products or services? These considerations in addition to the effort and ingenuity of the inventor form a basis on which a reward can be given. His fellow club members then decide upon a suitable reward as evidence of the appreciation of the Center. These rewards will be given besides the inventor's regular income which already affords him as nice a standard of living as he is willing to work for. The reward therefore would be a personal gift such as an extended travel vacation, a more luxurious automobile or house, etc. As all members of the Inventor's 

Club may be candidates for these rewards for other inventions at any time, they have to be fair in their awards. 

Many inventors may desire to spend all of their time following up further research in their chosen field. By consulting with the examiners who conducted the original selection and application of his ideas, a program of procedure can be established. The predominant factor in all of these matters is the gain to the Vocational Center. 

The members irrespective of the work they do are provided with the same opportunity for income. 65

The international admiration given to all members of the Inventor's Club as men and women of unusual ability, makes it a much sought after honor. Those members who have received this honor are publicized in the Vocational Press. The ones who have repeatedly contributed inventions become national heroes, for their efforts truly represent progress by the practical application of them towards procuring for all members a higher standard of living. 

The inventor in the Vocational Center is respected by all the members, instead of being referred to as a crackpot and nut as he generally is in Private Industry. These epithets are given him not because he deserves them, but because the struggles he is compelled to make for recognition generally leave his family and himself poverty stricken, disillusioned, discouraged and ridiculed. The popular reaction being that only a crackpot and nut would face all of this, knowing in advance what has befallen almost all of his fellow inventors.

Can you appreciate what the difference in popular reaction would do to men of original thought and action in the Vocational Center? 

There is so much to do in comparison to the little that has been done in the development of nature's resources—the fight of man's hidden enemy, disease—the enlightenment of education-the mystery of our solar system and the improvement of man's own faculties, that it is a crime to waste our endurance and energy to overcome selfish and petty restrictions forced on us by a system that continually threatens our existence by starvation. Is it necessary to fill us with this fear of hunger to force us to do our daily work? The answer is self evident.

What has been the financial position at the time of accomplishment of those who have really done worthwhile things for humanity? 

Take our artists, writers, musicians, philosophers, statesmen, engineers and inventors. Their best work was performed when they had a clear mind to concentrate on the work at hand. Many have worked with hunger and fear constantly at their side, but these torments cannot by their very nature, permit the free and clear mind which is essential to the solution of even the most elementary of problems. 

The system that practices it is bound to doom millions of its members to ignorance and superstition. This is a fertile field for the absorption of hate, envy, lies and injustices, that the tyrants of the world are forcing on the human race, even at the point of a gun. 

The Vocational Center recognizes that man must work to live and places no restrictions on the opportunity to work. All are members of an enlightened community not interested in each other's destruction but in each other's construction. Every member is respected for his responsibility, merit and effort. The principle that all people must have the individual freedom to permit each the opportunity of reaching for the greatest attainments is respected. 

Trickery and treachery are considered a disgrace and not signs of success under the mild name of shrewdness. Men are organized to cooperatively eliminate all duplication of effort, and convert the gain from this saving of labor to improve their own livelihood. The 

Vocational Center can accomplish all of these things, because it fits in with our human nature, which tends to make us accomplish what 66

we visualize. There is no human being that is honest and sincere with himself, who can visualize any image other than Peace and Goodwill upon this earth.

CHAPTER XXIII 

The Government Representative was the last member around the table. He now realized that he apart from the others must function not only as a representative of the Vocational Center, but also as a representative of Private Industry-for it will exist until Vocational Centers have proven their supremacy. "Gentlemen," said he, "I hope you realize my responsibility not only to you as Vocational Members, but to all of those engaged in Private Industry who are so constituted that they cannot take advantage as yet of the offer to join our organization with its apparent definite advantages. 

I, as your government representative, know by actual experience the difficulties I have had to encounter to pass laws that would mean justice and security for you. In the first place, as your representative, I came into a government that was functioning along certain lines of procedure, the outgrowth of many years of existence. I could not suggest any drastic changes, for even if they were just, I would be called revolutionary and radical by those of a different opinion, and all the pressure that they could exert would be applied toward my defeat.

The result was that I was compelled to only register such slight changes that could muster the large majorities that would offset the antagonism of the minorities opposing them. I was also subjected at all times to pressure from selective groups who had great financial power behind them. They could with this power gain supporters towards influencing the majority of the representatives to approve their legislation. Each one of us soon found out on which side our bread and butter came from, and we tended to support all of their measures whether they were just for the people or not. 

New legislation was introduced not with any definite plan in mind for a complete accomplishment, but thrown in piecemeal, as and when some representative felt that he was suddenly inspired by his own feelings, or so induced by the pressure of others. He decided that the contribution he was offering was the one measure that had to be passed to keep the country functioning. 

This intermittent form of new legislation kept me at my wits end to try to fit it into some definite scheme of what I secretly thought should be a program of accomplishment. Hundreds of measures, each in itself requiring months of study, were thrown at us for almost immediate decision. Is it any wonder that you received so many laws inconsistent with each other and therefore so difficult to observe? 

I have always secretly wished when I was elected, that a plan that was prepared by the voters, outlining their wishes in a complete sequence, would be given me and others in the same political party so as to guide us in what to follow. The recognized term of "political party platforms" was generally but a vague expression of a movement, to rectify all of the wrong that the other party in power was accused of, and served no other purpose than its use for speechmaking with its empty words. Now for the first time in my political 67

life, I hear of a definite plan of action. May I interpret in my own way the views of the previous speakers that I have been pleased to hear. 

There is now to be organized a Vocational Center, which is a cooperative movement among all those who are seeking employment at the present time, and also those who do not feel that they are receiving the benefits of security and a decent standard of living from Private Industry. I understand it also includes all businessmen, who desire to turn over to this organization their present enterprises in return for a good income and security. It also includes all professional men who desire to serve the entire group in return for economic security. 

There will also be included all those who believe that elimination of war depends upon security and plenty for all people. In fact, it will include all those who wish to join for their own good reasons. 

The membership in the Vocational Center should not be confined to any religion or race, but should be open without restrictions of any sort, to those who are willing to work and have the physical and mental qualifications for the type of work that they select. 

That the Vocational Center is organized to create, produce and distribute all commodities and services that are necessary, toward the highest standard of living for its members, without profit to any individual, group or corporation.

That all members receive in compensation an equal amount to what they produce, directly or indirectly, at a rate that is based on responsibility, effort and merit. These ratings to be established through tests, by the members as a body. 

That all members subscribe to a capital account a definite number of their hours of labor. This capital account being the fund accumulated to pay for all materials and services required by the Vocational Centers as community expenditures. The number of hours subscribed by the members should be decided upon by the members themselves to meet the cooperative services they require. 

That members permit their Vocational Centers to buy or lease land, buildings or factories, etc., required to carry on the cooperative efforts of all members.

That no restrictions be placed on any member in the number of hours he or she desires to work, providing either meets the periodical physical and mental examination required, thus assuring the Center of the member's ability to carry out the work scheduled. 

The Vocational Center reserves the right to choose such work that will not disrupt the planned schedule for any extra hours of work that are required by Vocational Members.

That the currency of the Vocational Center will be based on a Vocational Dollar for one hour of time of ordinary labor, decimals or multiples of this base being arranged for exchange purposes. 

That a new issue of currency be distributed annually voiding the value of all previous issues of currency. 

That each division of the Vocational Center can exchange commodities or services within their own divisions, or those of other countries, but Vocational currency only must be used in the exchange. 

That the Vocational Center assumes its obligation for government support, but reserves the right through its representatives, to demand a budgeted account of all anticipated government expendi- 68

tures and any past records of purchases it deems necessary to inspect. 

Its purpose being to advise the Vocational Members to consider the necessary expenditures and protest against any extravagances that cause them to work additional hours as their share of the cost. (These hours instead could be applied to improve their own standard of living.)

That the Vocational Center protests against all forms of relief for active workers-such workers being offered membership in Vocational Centers to earn as they produce, without profit to anyone. 

That Vocational Members urge the government to eliminate all departments performing duplicate functions and retain only those employees who are absolutely necessary. All those discharged employees being offered membership in the Vocational Center to earn for their needs.

That Vocational Members urge their government to make every move possible by radio, press and theatre to appeal to the people of all nations to eliminate armaments and warfare, limiting their armies to international policing methods only. Vocational Members in all countries to follow suit to show that there is no need for national armies and navies, now that Vocational Centers eliminate all need for new territory on the part of any nation-for there is now a free trade and an exchange policy between all Vocational Centers. That there is no unemployment, as any able bodied worker can become a member, producing for his own needs. There is no poverty because a good standard of living can be earned by all Vocational Members of all nations willing to work for it. 

That the Vocational Center, by its very nature, is a cooperative movement against the practices of all forms of religious or racial prejudices. It reserves the right to bar anyone from its membership who is proven to have acted, either by written or spoken word, in any way tending to create differences between Vocational Members on racial or religious grounds. 

That no youth, either male or female, can become a worker of the Vocational Center until he or she is eighteen years of age. 

That all Vocational Members adopt the slogan of the Center- "Peace and Goodwill to Mankind."

The government represenative hesitated and said, "Gentlemen, I have interpreted your thoughts to the best of my ability, and as the representative of at least twelve million unemployed in one nation I feel that I must do something to assist them in their organization. 

Especially so as to immediately take away from us the burden of the tremendous outlay for relief to the unemployed. They can assume their own responsibility as citizens for the support of the government once they are cooperatives. These citizens who comprise almost one-fourth of the total employees of this nation are all prospective members of your Center.

If they are organized to accumulate capital funds by surplus hours of work of each member, why cannot we lend them part of the money that we spend on relief on which we have no return, as capital to get them started in their venture? The Vocational Center to repay us by their currency that is represented by a definite number of extra hours on the part of all Vocational workers until this loan is repaid. 

We can make it payable over a period of years, so as not to burden them at the beginning when they are just getting organized. The 69

government can exchange this Vocational currency for merchandise or the currency of Private Industry and act as the final exchanger outlined by the banker. By lending them this money they can start production of commodities. They can immediately effect the saving of duplicate operations, wasted effort and unbalanced production and distribution, that they have outlawed by their method of cooperative activity.

As soon as these loans are made, we could publicize the methods and policies of the Vocational Center. We could then offer owners of business enterprises, factories and farms who are beset by the difficulties of Private Industry, the opportunity of transferring their interests and services to the Center for the definite income they will secure as active members.

Democratic countries already have legislation that provides for loans to Private Industry. Billions of dollars have been advanced in part to banks, insurance companies, railroads, manufacturers and even small stores, to enable them in some possible way to stimulate industrial activity. The government in making these loans was sincere, but it did not actually question whether the money would eliminate the wastes of industry, manufacturing excess products over the amount that could be absorbed by the market. It did not question whether the money loaned would be used for duplication of sales effort to the same customers. It did not question the risk involved in loans to corporations. These corporations with the borrowed money could undersell below their own cost, competitors, thereby leading into a position where they might not be able to repay the loan in full. 

It did not question whether the money loaned actually increased the employees' income to give them increased purchasing power. 

Surely, if the government loaned money to Private Industry under these undetermined conditions, there cannot be any objection to loaning the unemployed money (that is actually being given them today as an outright gift as relief.) Especially, in view of the fact that this loan will not only enable them to provide for themselves, but aid them to assume their share of the support of the government itself. The government must also consider that the Vocational Center is set up to pay the loan advanced in a positive manner of surplus hours of their own workers, instead of the uncertainties of payment of loans by Private Industry. The members of the Vocational Centers constitute their own certain market, while those in Private Industry have to depend upon an uncertain market, to furnish the funds to repay their loans. Surely the unemployed as citizens are entitled to the same benefits of government that the citizens of private business are.

Private Industry should not even seek to object. It probably will offer its fullest cooperation when it considers the stupendous amount of relief they pay out. What a terrific tax burden on the part of 

Private Industry this is!

The unemployed and relief workers individually receive such small incomes that they can only purchase the barest of necessities. 

Even if Private Industry did make a small profit on the increase in volume of necessities purchased by relief workers, it can only realize but a small percentage towards its return in taxes to pay for the entire outlay of relief. These small profits on the sales of these neces- 70

tities to the unemployed are further absorbed by the increased tax for overhead required by the government to administer relief and also to collect the taxes levied. 

Private Industry should further give its cooperation because it is not set up to help itself. It cannot absorb the employees that it discharges, because of its lopsided formation. The government must accept the responsibility of providing for those discharged, or face the serious consequences of millions of starving men, women and children, dying of hunger in the streets of our cities. 

You do not think that Private Industry would last very long if it ignored these conditions-do you?

Private Industry will naturally wonder what is going to happen to all the stores, factories and farms now catering to the production and distribution of the commodities absorbed by these unemployed. 

All of these will have the choice of either joining the Vocational Center or take their chances of still remaining in Private Industry. 

I am sure that most of them will prefer to turn their enterprises over to the Vocational Center for the return of a good living and its security; for they will have far less worry than most business enterprises have today. What with limited markets, labor trouble, heavy taxes, uncertain world outlook, business problems are not simple. 

One private bank reports that between 1930 and 1936 Private Industry expended sixteen billions of dollars more than it took in. One cannot say in the light of these figures that Private Industry is really on a profitable basis. Even the largest of companies may be tempted to make the change. At least they should welcome the Vocational 

Center for relieving them of some of their tax burden for relief. 

They will soon recognize other merits of these Centers and cooperate with them, to really solve the problems of balance and make real its slogan-"Goodwill and Peace to Mankind."

Labor unions should not object to the Vocational Centers, for they, like Private Industry, also cannot absorb the unemployed. They too are not set up for it. Their attempts to end unemployment by reduced working hours so that all can find employment, may be a charitable idea, but a very impractical one. They are with this one move raising the cost of production and forcing a reduction in demand because of the increased cost. The gain in wages will be of no value to the workers when they are thrown out of employment by the decreased demand.

Increases in wages or shortening of hours or both can only come from one source and that is from the increase of purchasing power on the part of the consumers. The only way to increase the purchasing power is to make available all for the same cost additional commodities. This can only occur when production is increased without any reduction in wages or the discharge of any employees. For the minute that wages are reduced or employees discharged, their purchasing power is immediately cut, because of the fact that we are all consumers, as well as producers.

Union leaders undoubtedly work on this premise they think that by forcing wages up, employers will find ways and means of increasing production by the use of labor-saving devices, and thereby the workers will not only benefit by higher wages, but increased purchasing power. This theory is fine, if unemployment does not result from these labor saving devices. It does, with marked increase, be- 71

cause Private Industry individually operated cannot regulate itself as a body to immediately balance production and consumption. 

While these labor saving devices eventually reduce the cost of specific products, they upset the entire relationship of labor to consumption. This creates large groups of unemployed workers who cannot be absorbed until a state of normalcy is obtained. As efforts to reduce labor are being made by employers constantly a state of normalcy is never reached, and unemployment is always with us to a lesser or greater degree. So, union leaders and organized labor should soon realize that Vocational Centers which are not individually owned or operated can act as a body to utilize these labor saving devices for common gain. In fact these efforts are greatly encouraged for the labor saved is immediately converted to making additional commodities. The market is always there for it depends upon the needs of the members instead of what they can only afford to buy. 

Will the government object to a change in its setup of relief? 

I can answer that as a government representative. The government is your collective agent and is there to do your bidding, once your plans are known to it. 

What I suggest therefore to you who want the formation of Vocational Centers and all its represents, is to act without delay. 

The dictators of the many nations of the world brazenly hold out the economic problems of the remaining democracies to their people, not as an example of the deficiencies of Private Industry, but as a weakness of the democratic form of government. 

The actions of the democracies in not being able to offer shelter to all oppressed minorities, as they did in past years because of their own unemployment, gives strength to the false propaganda of the dictators. They make up this deficiency in their own countries by the manufacture of armaments on borrowed money or increased taxes. 

While there is work, the reduced living standards that follow are blamed on minorities one day and foreign countries the next. 

Their people are forcibly inoculated with hate and envy to serve the military aggressive purposes of their cruel, dishonest rulers. They even do not stop with their own people, but by clever propaganda inject this hate and greed and create internal strife in all other countries, especially the democracies.

This propaganda and military aggression is threatening our very existence as a democratic country. There is no time to lose. Organize immediately your friends and neighbors into Vocational Center groups. Wire, phone or write your representative in government to read this book, so that he may understand your viewpoint. Reprint the basic principles of Vocational Center organization and distribute them to all prospects of your club.

Advise your government representative to use the unemployed in classifications such as Works Progress Administration in the United States as a nucleus of Vocational Centers, capitalizing on the money and experience already expended on this organization. 

Petition your government representative to extend the present policy of lending capital to Private Industry to include a loan of capital to those unemployed, who desire to organize themselves into cooperative Vocational Centers.

The Reconstruction Finance Corporation in the United States and similar government departments in other countries are already organ- 72

ized for that purpose. Let your representative know that these Vocational Centers will repay these loans by issuing Vocational Dollars, earned by the surplus labor of their Vocational Members. These 

Vocational Dollars will enable the government to purchase for its consumption, any of the commodities produced by the Centers until the loan is repaid in full.

As the last speaker around the table, let me suggest to you all, that as we are now assembled here together, we would make an excellent start in forming the first club which will eventually become a Vocational Center.

As I do not hear any objections, I take it that you are in accord with the suggestion. What is that I hear?—my nomination for President of the First Vocational Center Club?-and what's more-my unanimous election!

I certainly do appreciate the honor, ladies and gentlemen, and assure you that I will endeavor to justify your confidence in me—your government representative.

CHAPTER XXIV 

As your first President, I do not want to make a lengthy speech. 

I must, however, emphasize the importance of preparing a definite program if you want to accomplish the solution of our economic and social problems by democratic means-while there is still an opportunity to do so. 

You must, as I previously stated, start the ball rolling by forming Community Vocational Clubs. Build up your membership and immediately petition your government representative to carry out the suggestions already made, regarding the extension of loans to prospective cooperatives and using the present organization of unemployed as a nucleus for the Vocational Center. After this preliminary, it is essential that you prepare facts and figures, so that you may determine just how much capital is necessary to get these Vocational 

Centers started.

I fully realize that this work is going to take time and thought, and that most of you are busy enough as it is trying to make a living in these times. Nevertheless, the work must be done. Leaving matters to the other fellow is what caused the housewife so much grief and will cause you more, if you let matters drift for themselves. 

My advice is to accept the responsibility of progress for all as your own individual problem. Give the movement all the time you possibly can now-and the results will take care of you later. 

Certain committees will have to be appointed for both local and national work. Here is a committee plan I have in mind.

The members of each individual club will elect the following local committees Membership, Budget, Industrial, Agricultural, Distribution, Housing, Standards and Appropriation. They will also elect a Vocational Center Director who is qualified by training and experience to act as a coordinator of these committees. The Local Housing Committee will obtain from all of the prospective members of the Center information as to the type of home they live in, room area, number in family, ages, and the condition of the home, etc. Those unfit for habitation will be reviewed, to see if they could be made modern with a reasonably lower cost than comparable new construction, considering the age and condition of the structure. The national committee will find substantial data in the various government bureaus, the National Association of Manufacturers, Chambers of Commerce, Railroads, etc.

Similar codes can also be prepared covering the warehouses and distribution. The Local Standards Committee will also assist the corresponding national committee in the preparation of codes pertaining to medical and dental service for all members; a currency code concerning all currency exchange procedure and banking, and any other codes that will be necessary for a complete understanding by members of the routine covering the operation of all divisions of the Vocational Centers. These codes can be arranged, so that supplements can be added periodically, as warranted by the actual progress of the various Vocational divisions. 75

This committee, will determine the cost of new enterprises in cooperation with the Industrial, Agricultural and Distribution Committees. A survey of all existing large enterprises in

They will break down the present existing costs of Private Industry into the actual material and overhead cost and also deduct the amount of profit. They will extract from these present costs all charges that Private Industry is compelled to make, but which Vocational Centers eliminate, such as interest on loans and bonds, also dividends, both on common and preferred stocks, cost of high pressure advertising in all forms, association fees, legal fees, excess salaries, duplicate inventories and warehouses and duplicate sales costs catering to the same markets. They will deduct the taxes paid for the support of the unemployed. 

The resultant cost from this survey will enable the Appropriation Committee to definitely calculate what capital will be required to produce and distribute the needs of the Center Members as efficiently as exists today in Private Industry.

They will analyze the relief costs for comparison. In America alone it has exceeded two billions of dollars annually, which in itself places it as being almost the largest enterprise in the country. Only a portion of these funds will make it possible for the Vocational Centers to have sufficient capital to produce commodities in mass production, especially when they eliminate the superfluous costs that Private 

Industry has to include.

The reports of the local committees will enable the national committees to prepare a program of organization. This program should assure the government that any loan made to the prospective Vocational Centers will be repaid by the actual labor of the members expressed by its Vocational currency. The National Appropriation 

Committee through the National Vocational Director can suggest that these loans applied for can be taken from the relief appropriation. The banker's arrangement of Vocational currency exchange will enable the government to obtain for the payment of its Vocational Dollars, commodities of equal value to what it receives for the currency of Private Industry. The national committee will further guarantee the government a definite contribution on the part of their Vocational Members as their share of support. 

The schedule of operation of the Vocational Center will assure all interested groups such as labor unions, government, large industrial enterprises, etc., that all Vocational Members will enjoy the full advantages of a cooperative balanced program, to produce their needs for their own consumption, without infringing on the rights of others. 

The Vocational Center will accept with thanks the contribution of the experience and benefits of Private Industry that will help the organization and operation of these Centers. In return, the Vocational Centers will completely relieve Private Industry of its taxes 76

for the support of the unemployed. This will further gradually reduce the cost of government, because these unemployed can become happy citizens contributing their own share of government support. 

The government's problems will be simplified for it will not have to indulge in any projects designed for the employment of labor other than those of definite need at the present time and of the type that the people can afford. 

The people of every nation will be happier, for they can now solve their economic and social problems by democratic means, without interfering with their existing political beliefs or disrupting present industry and commerce.

Vocational Centers will show the way to welcome every person that is oppressed by governments for any reason. The Centers will now make it possible for these oppressed people, not only to produce for their own needs, but also assume their share of government and community expense, thereby enriching the country of their adoption. 

The Vocational Center movement will be the positive answer of the democracies to the dictators and prick their bubble of blown-up importance.

This knowledge will light the candle of truth, so that all who care may see, and constitute a silent threat to all those who deny the truth and preach hate and war against their human brothers. 

CHAPTER XXV 

When the government representative finished, every member around the table knew that the plan and the program that they had mutually worked out was practical. What troubled them was how their fellow groups not around the able were going to be convinced. 

Their reading of newspapers and listening to the radio had most of them confused as to the real issues confronting the world that even the proper plan might not be recognized. Depressed economic conditions and periodic wars have given rise to many reformers. Each had his own special program and swore by all that was holy that his was the one that the public must follow. Most reformers in building up a program did actually no planning. They coined meaningless phrases and by publicity expanded them to include everything the public would accept at its face value. Will the public realize the thought, vision and sincerity that inspired the Vocational Center movement and the absolute need for its adoption or will it consider it in the light of another ballyhoo. In what way can it be identified as to its true worth. If we could manage somehow to get the public to distinguish between a planned program with a definite objective as compared to an outburst of abuse against others because of personal motives of the speaker, the people could at least find a start towards a proper analysis of what they see and hear. When the individual listener develops a curiosity as to what is in the speaker's mind instead of his mouth, he can detect whether the presentation is fact or farce.

There is a vast difference between a program that is planned as compared to one that is not. The first requires research, thought and the ability of the planner to visualize a complete picture of the com- 77

position of all the forces proposed to build the structure. 

The planner next attempts to wreck the very structure he has completed to discover any weak points. He applies revised thought and action to overcome these defects, and repeats this process over again and again until he sincerely believes he has done everything in his power to anticipate any weakness. One can therefore see immediately that a planner must be impartial to anything but the existing facts. 

Prejudice, envy or hate will prevent his acceptance of these facts. 

Therefore, when any individual represents himself as advocating human progress and at the same time attacks the nationality or religion of people, he is not only acting contrary to the elements of planning but is a destroyer of the progress he supposedly advocates. 

His mind is prejudiced and covered by the fog of intolerance. One can immediately tell that he is an opportunist lacking sincerity and has no plan. Watch these fellows, for their object is to create discord among common interests so as to weaken resistance toward outside forces. If you are not careful to detect the duplicity of these reformers, you may find yourself as their tool attacking your own position of safety. The tyrants of the world choose just this method employing as their representatives the most convincing orators. 

The Vocational Center in contrast is definitely a planned program—a structure that was built up as an answer to conditions that exist and that nobody denies. One may not agree with the group around the table on every detail of arrangement, but that is immaterial as the setup permits flexibility. The group, however, developed their suggestions from a complete picture of what could be gradually accomplished to obtain democracy. They as a group did not advocate hate and malice to any person, group or nation. In fact they showed that peace and goodwill on earth were not mere words but the foundation of the entire Vocational Center society. The plan takes into account a gradual and peaceful change to rectify recognized faults of Private Industry. Vocational Centers appeal to your logic for they are reasonable in their demands. They appreciate that human beings cannot always respond to calls of decency and justice immediately. 

A fog of prejudice and superstition may hinder the passage. However, when these calls can be voluntarily answered, the traveler may perceive that the fog has lifted. The atmosphere becomes bright and the direction clear. The Vocational Center appeals for equal opportunity without restrictions to all people, because it is the only way civilization can function. You cannot extract blood from a stone, neither can you expect progress from poverty. 

Mass production has offered the world social utility-mass consumption must take place for social stability. One cannot proceed very far without the other. The richest individual with great temporary power must understand this truth and not restrict its gradual fulfillment. It is the height of foolishness to feel that a denial of a fact means its end. When truths are constantly denied they persist in seeking each other's company until they crystallize into a powerful mass too difficult to control. It has been this resistance to facts that caused the sudden revolutions and upheavals in history. A position in society can be no safer than the pillars supporting that very society. 78

The Vocational Center establishes definitions of true values. In 

Private Industry the individual in his rush to seek fleeting security neglects the factors that mean permanent security. That is why it is commonly known that wealth is just a passing fancy. 

The plan of this group around the table is offered to its fellow groups as the first attempt in their knowledge to provide within our society the peaceful means of civilizing that society. No one can by any stretch of the imagination call our society in its present state of periodic wars-civilization. No one can see the rights of man trampled upon in a maze of prejudice and hate and call it democracy. "Democracy Wake Up"-let your radios broadcast construction, not destruction-growth not deterioration-opportunity not restriction.

Let your papers illustrate progress not poverty-movement not manipulation-friends not enemies health not disease-industry not crime-peace not war.

Let your mind be open not closed-your finances tools not weapons—your heart sympathetic not petrified.

Let your education be liberal not dogmatic—your opinions flexible not rigid-your belief humanity not mechanism. 

Think, work and live to make democracy a reality not a camouflage for everything that people do not want to understand. 

Give the Vocational Centers and all it stands for your encouragement, for it represents a definite plan to make the changes that must come in an orderly manner. 

The Vocational Press will reflect in its pages these orderly changes. There will be a gradual elimination of the word foreigner. 

In its place will exist the term of Vocational brother or sister as we today recognize our fraternity brothers. Extensive travel by even ordinary workers to other countries will undoubtedly bring about a demand for an international language similar to Esperanto. This will be taught in our schools and permit a common appreciation of international Vocational plans and problems. Mutual understanding will lead to a basis of friendship between the peoples of all nations. 

As there will be free trade with no restrictions, the latest methods in design, production and distribution in all countries will be disclosed and illustrated for the benefit of all Vocational Members. 

Science, art and music will have international scope without the discrimination that exists today because of national economic policies. 

They will bring increased happiness to all people, and lead to establishment of cultural centers even in the most backward countries. 

The newspapers of the Vocational Press will have certain noticeable differences from the ones in Private Industry. There will be no individual advertisements as there will be no individual producers in Vocational Centers. There will be no high pressure selling of one class of merchandise, at the expense of another, for Vocational Centers are interested in producing the best in each class of merchandise and in a proper balance, for a good standard of living. Another difference in the paper will be the absence of the number of financial items which are printed in the newspapers of Private Industry. I have in 79

mind news of bankruptcies, stock transactions, interest rates, forced sales, mortgage foreclosures, tax sales, liens, financial rackets and all of the termites of financial juggling that serve today to impoverish the weak and enrich the strong. 

Another gradual omission which will occur in the Vocational Press will be the activities of crime and prostitution. These will eventually be corrected in institutions organized for reform and not for punishment. Experts agree that most crimes originally start from economic want either on the part of the individual or his family. Vocational 

Centers by extending the opportunity for all to earn in accordance with their needs, will kill the start of many a criminal career. Prostitution will be gradually eliminated, because young men and women will not have to be denied a natural relationship on account of economic want. The means and opportunity will be available to them at an early age to produce and earn what they need. 

The Vocational Press will reflect the absence of resulting evils from a faulty arrangement and substitute in its place, constructive progress by a balanced cooperative system.
