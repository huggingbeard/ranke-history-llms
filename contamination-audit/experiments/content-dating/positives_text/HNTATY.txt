C479.808.65 You remember the France you read about in 'The Tale of Two Cities'? 

That was an unhappy land with the red hand of terror raised over hill and vale. 

Then a foolish king and his glamorous con sort were about to ascend the steps of the guillotine; thousands of others who had lived and profited by that dying régime were soon to follow these majesties into eternity. New rude rulers would rise into brief power; new ideas that were neither liberty, equality nor fraternity would come to the fore. Such rulers and their ideas would be tried out and prove quick failures. 

Into this unhappy time the Providence of God brought a baby. That baby was destined to span a long life and from his deathbed leave a brotherhood which would continue the teachings of its Founder even into our own days and into our own broad breasted land.

For the baby born into terrified France on May 20th, 1789, in the little village of -2-

Marlhes, a town in the diocese of Lyons, was to be baptized Joseph Benedict Marcellin Champagnat. Before the baby was six weeks old the populace of distant Paris would storm the grim Bastille and The Reign of Terror. would be on. Little of these political troubles disturbed the quiet of Marlhes, and the babyhood of this ninth child, born to parents who were to have ten. HESE parents were peasants and the wew little Marcellin grew familiar with holy things in the atmosphere of that Godfearing home. His earliest recollections were of the family kneeling, while Father or Mother recited the Rosary of the Fair 

Queen, whose protection was mighty.

Thus began that love for Mary which became an all-devouring passion in his mature days. 

What reading his mother taught him was out of the Lives of the Saints; those wise people who put the things of God first in their hearts and so made a huge success of their lives.

Early also this good mother taught - Marcellin that he was to take what she put before him on the table and he was to -3-

await his turn to be served. It is recorded that a neighbor, noting this domestic discipline, expressed surprise. Madame 

Champagnat remarked: "I have raised too many children not to see that if they are given free rein to follow their fancy, they will quickly acquire habits of greed. Marcellin is no different from other children."

This was the pious small boy who helped on the farm and ran errands connected with the flour mill that his father owned.

He was an obedient son, healthy, and he had a liking for work.

Both of them would combine their funds and raise lambs to be sold in the market place.

FTER a year of necessary elementary studies at the home of an uncle, who, fortunately, was a school teacher, Marcellin entered the Verrières Seminary in the Fall of 1805. He was close to seventeen now; the tallest and the most backward boy in the freshman class.

A an Backward he may have been in studies, but not in good disposition and manly piety. At this time he wrote these revealing resolutions: 'O my Lord and my God, I promise never more to offend Thee; to make Acts of Faith, Hope and 

Charity often; ... to give good example, so as to lead others to the practice of virtue as far as I am able...... to teach Catechism to the poor ... Grant, Divine Savior, that 

I may faithfully keep these Resolutions faithfully.' 

Seven happy, fruitful years Marcellin

Champagnat spent at this Little Seminary and in the October of 1812, he was trans ferred to the Diocesan Seminary at Lyons. 

Now in truth, he was a real Seminarian, entered on the direct studies for the cher ished priesthood. -8-

HE E had now begun to practice in earnest that science of the saints which teaches one to be harder on himself than on his neighbor. Many were the resolutions for personal mortification that he put into effect. One day, when he was out of doors, he happened to pass a cherry tree. 

The fruit looked tempting and he plucked off a berry. But as he was eating it, he remembered a recent resolution not to eat between meals. Out was spit the cherry and Marcellin exclaimed with the exaggeration of humility: "I am almost become a slave to the demon of gluttony!"

The vacations from the seminary were spent in Marlhes. The tall young seminarian gathered the children and instructed them in their catechism. He must have had an interesting way of teaching, for the children came for more. Like Saint John

Bosco, the Founder of the Salesians, who as a student, home from the seminary, taught catechism effectively, Marcellin = Champagnat interspersed the solid with the light. He called on his retentive memory for stories of the saints that entertained as well as enlightened and through the tale he taught the moral.

His Eminence, Cardinal Fesch, appointed Father Champagnat a curate at Lavalla. This was a village situated on the slope of Mount Pila and his parishioners were the mountain people; some two thousand of them. Like the Curé of Ars at a later date, Father Champagnat found his people irreligious from a lack of religious instruction. Living as they did in the scattered valleys and passes, the greater part of them were strangers to the interior of the village church and the spiritual neglect of the children was appalling. 

Always one to regulate his life by resolution, we find this written at the start of his work at Lavalla: 'I will try in a special manner to acquire the virtue of meekness, and in order to gain souls to God more easily, I will treat everybody with great kindness.'

So the young priest became a mountain climber to become familiar with his scattered flock. Winter and summer, night and day, he sought out the lost and strayed sheep. Many are the tales of his hazardous sick calls, made on foot; many are the tales of his success at these deathbeds. -12-

ATURALLY he gathered as many children as he could and began to instruct them for their First Confession and First Holy Communion. This opened 

Father Champagnat's eyes to the neglected state of these lambs of Lavalla's flock.

These children were eager to learn of the 

Guardian Angel they possessed as their very own; of the glorious Mother who queened it over Heaven, that fair land which the Good God had prepared for them when Life's school was out. 

But Father Champagnat felt his limitations. At most he was an inexperienced curate and these neglected children-- the innocent victims of The French Revolution's aftermath, were too many. 

The thought of enlisting the aid of young men who would be willing to give themselves and their futures to the care of such little ones had persisted for years in the mind of the young curate. It had been born back in the calm seminary days. Now this thought took possession of Father Champagnat.

HE 

ERE was a crying need for other helpers; young men who felt called, not to be priests, but to be religious brothers. -13-

It was a vocation distinct from the priesthood. Some were called to be His Ambassadors well and good! -- But as the Good 

God had 'many mansions' in His Heavenly

City, so he had many offices here below and He had need for other servants, whose duties were not to administer the Sacraments, but to prepare the souls of children for their proper reception. Both classes of divine servants would live out their lives on His Majesty's Service; both classes would receive their reward exceeding great. 

The Divine Vocationmaster would give to those whom He chose to be His nonpriestly servants the special gifts of body and mind and soul that would fit them for this important apostolate and make it easier for them to toil in the classroom rather than in the sanctuary. Those whom 

He invites to be His Brothers, spending their days practicing His Works of Mercy, have their own lifework cut out for them. 

Their talents may be five, the same as in other walks of life, or their talent may be one. It does not matter to Him in the least. Provided One Talent labors with greater love than Five Talents, he will please His Lord and Master the more. And to please God is no other than to follow -14-

out more perfectly Man's destiny in this world. For it is another way of saying the more familiar first answer in the Catechism, 'I was created to love, reverence and serve 

God in this world.' 

If the Good God is calling a young man to be one of His Brothers, and he heeds. this kindly invitation, he of a surety will be of the company of the blessed.

THAT God wished this new work started came strikingly to Father Champagnat's attention. He was summoned on a sick call. When he arrived at the poor farm house he found a twelve year old boy seriously ill. What shocked Father Champagnat was to discover that this boy, so soon to face God was so ignorant of his Faith that he did not even know of the existence of the Good God!

Father instructed as well as he could with the limited time at his disposal. He told the dying boy that he had a soul that could never die, and he told him of Almighty God and His Son, Jesus Christ, Who came to good children under the veil of the White Host to be their Light and their Guide into the fair lands that men called Heaven. Then the priest forgave the lad his sins and he gave him his First Holy 

Communion that was his Viaticum. Father departed on another sick call, leaving behind a silent child, who was wide-eyed -16-

'To have no opinion of ourselves and to think always well and commendable of others is great perfection,' is what Kempis says. It fits in neatly as this holy priest's epitaph. THUS
