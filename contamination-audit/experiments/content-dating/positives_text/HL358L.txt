Report to the President by the Atomic Energy Labor-Management Relations Panel 

July 1, 1953, to June 30, 1954

Origin of Panel.-On March 24, 1953, the President requested the 

Director of the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service to estab lish an Atomic Energy Labor-Management Relations Panel to handle disputes threatening interference with an essential part of the United States atomic energy program. 

The President, in directing this to be done, said that "uninterrupted functioning of our atomic energy program, without strikes or lockouts due to labor-management disputes, is essential to the national security and every practicable step should be taken to prevent such disputes from threatening construction, operations or services vital to this program. At the same time, the national policy is to place primary reliance upon the parties to any labor-management dispute to make every effort through negotiation and collective bargaining and the full use of mediation and conciliation to effect a settlement." 

The Panel was directed by the President to exercise substantially the same jurisdiction as was exercised by the previous panel¹ with respect to Government-owned, privately operated, atomic energy installations to assist the parties to arrive at peaceful adjustments of their disputes through voluntary methods. Accordingly, the Panel was instructed to take jurisdiction of such labor-management disputes in which the normal processes of collective bargaining and mediation and conciliation have, in the opinion of the Director of the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, been fully utilized, and which, in the opinion of the United States Atomic Energy Commission, threaten to interfere with an essential part of the atomic energy program.

The Panel was placed in the Federal Mediation and Conciliation 

Service, for housekeeping purposes only.

Function of Panel.—The Panel operates in accordance with policies and procedures which were drafted jointly by the Panel and the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, in consultation with the 

United States Atomic Energy Commission. 

These procedures give the Director of the Service exclusive authority to determine whether a labor-management dispute in the atomic

energy industry shall be referred to the Panel. In making this determination, the Director "will consult with the Atomic Energy Commission and other appropriate persons and organizations; and will take into account whether agreement-making procedures available to the parties have been utilized, and other relevant criteria." Disputes are referred to the Panel only after the Service is convinced that collective bargaining has been exhausted and further mediation would be fruitless, and the Atomic Energy Commission has concluded that the dispute threatens to interfere with an essential part of the atomic energy program. Upon certification by the Service, the Panel, in its discretion, takes jurisdiction of the dispute.

The procedures give the Panel full discretion to use any method of assisting the parties to arrive at a peaceful adjustment of their dispute through voluntary methods. This might include returning the dispute to the parties for further collective bargaining and mediation, or for settlement under an existing procedure mutually agreed to by the parties for the adjustment of disputes. The Panel may, in its discretion, recommend to the parties a basis for settlement, if agreement cannot be arrived at in any other way. The Panel expects the parties to continue work without interruption while a dispute is pending before the Panel, and if the Panel recommends a basis for settlement, it is expected that no action will be taken to impede production or services for a period of thirty days from the issuance of the recommendation.

The Panel's recommendations are not binding on the parties, except where they agree in advance to be bound, nor is there any compulsion on them to meet Panel expectations on continuity of operations during the Panel processes. Nevertheless, the Panel has had excellent cooperation from labor and management in this voluntary procedure, due in part to their recognition of the responsibilities inherent in participating in the atomic energy program, and the need for continuity of production at vital AEC installations.

Status of Panel.-As the situation has developed in the atomic energy program, through dealing practically with disputes as they have arisen, it has become increasingly clear that the status of the Panel needs to be kept under continual study. Status assumes importance because of its effect upon the attitudes of labor, management, the governmental agencies concerned, and the public towards the work of the Panel. Discussions have been had concerning clarification of the Panel's status, and it is hoped that these may lead to a proper solution in the near future.

Summary of Cases.-Fourteen disputes were referred to the Panel by the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service during the period July 1953 through June 1954. Thirteen of these were settled in accordance with Panel recommendations. In the remaining case, the parties reached agreement the day before the Panel was to begin hearings.

Nine of the fourteen disputes referred to the Panel involved production facilities at atomic energy installations, whereas the remaining five cases involved construction activities at such installations. 

Seven of these disputes arose out of negotiations under contract reopening provisions; four developed during negotiations for renewal of the contracts; two occurred when the parties were bargaining over the terms of initial contracts; and one dispute consisted of grievances of long standing.

Of the 13 cases settled pursant to a Panel recommendations, in 4 the workers were on strike when the disputes were referred to the Panel, but acceded to the Panel's request that they return to work pending disposition of the disputes by the Panel. After the initial 

Panel hearing in two cases, the parties conducted further negotiations at the request of the Panel, and succeeded in reducing the number of issues for Panel consideration. In one case, an issue in dispute was referred back to the parties for disposition, in accordance with the contract grievance procedure, and another issue was found to be nonnegotiable during the tenure of the existing contract. A summary of these cases is set forth below.

Cases No. 1-2: Oak Ridge, Tenn., AEC Operations 

These were disputes involving the Atomic Trades and Labor Council, AFL, representing production and maintenance workers, and the Carbide and Carbon Chemicals Co., operating the X-10 plant (Oak 

Ridge National Laboratory), and the Y-12 plant at Oak Ridge, Tenn. 

The disputes originated during wage reopening negotiations for an co

"across-the-board change in wage rates," effective July 15, 1953. After that date, pursuant to the contracts, both parties were released from any obligations pertaining to wage schedules and continuity of operations. The parties bargained extensively with full use of mediation, and on the deadline date, the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service obtained an extension of operations. On July 24, the cases were referred to the Panel, whose appointment was simultaneously announced by the President.

The Panel held hearings in Oak Ridge on August 5 and 6, 1953, and issued its recommendations on August 13, for an across-the-board increase of 7 cents per hour and an additional paid holiday. The parties had consented that the holiday question was appropriately included in the dispute. The Panel also recommended the restoration of service credits to certain pipefitters, who lost the credits as the result of an unauthorized strike. The parties reached agreement on the basis of these recommendations.

Case No. 3: Los Alamos, N. Mex., AEC Operations 

The parties to this dispute were the Sheet Metal Workers International Association, AFL, Local Union No. 49, and the Zia Company, AEC contractor for maintenance of the technical plant and for operation of Los Alamos town, New Mexico. 

The dispute originated during negotiations for a new contract, after a change in jurisdiction of association locals. The old contract expired July 8, 1953, at which time the sheet-metal workers stopped work. The stoppage became critical on July 27, when a picket line was established, which was honored by other crafts. On July 28, the parties jointly agreed to resume work, to submit the dispute to the Panel, and to be bound by Panel recommendations. The Federal 

Mediation and Conciliation Service, after extensive mediation, referred the dispute to the Panel on August 5. 

The Panel heard the parties in Washington, D. C., on August 17.

At the suggestion of the Panel, the parties resumed negotiations on August 17 and 18, and succeeded in reducing the number of issues in dispute to six. On August 19, the Panel issued its recommendations that (1) the hourly wage rate for sheet-metal workers be increased to $2.85; (2) the parties give further study to a pension plan; (3) the union withdraw the four remaining proposals in dispute, and the contract be completed with the wage increase recommended, and such other changes as the parties mutually agreed upon. 

Case No. 4: Portsmouth, Ohio, AEC Construction Project 

The parties involved in this dispute were the Office Employees International Union, AFL, Local No. 308, and the Peter Kiewit Sons' 

Co., prime construction contractor at this project.

This dispute arose out of negotiations for an initial contract. The parties had reached agreement on all issues except wages and effective date of the contract. During negotiations, the company offered a wage increase averaging about 172 cents per hour above the existing wage rates. Extensive bargaining with the assistance of the 

Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service failed to resolve the two issues, and the Service referred the dispute to the Panel on December 

14, 1953.

The Panel met with the parties in Washington, D. C., on December 

17. After full discussion of the two issues remaining in the dispute, the Panel recommended a wage increase of 2212 cents per hour (which included the amount offered by the company during negotiations), plus an additional 5 cents per hour in job grades 6 to 15. The Panel recommended that the agreement be retroactive to September 15, 1953. The parties accepted these recommendations in a memorandum of agreement the same day, and later incorporated them into the contract. 

Case No. 5: Hanford AEC Works, Richland, Wash. 

This dispute was between the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners of America, AFL, locals 1699 and 1849, and the Hanford

Contractors Negotiating Committee, representing the contractors engaged in construction at the Hanford AEC Works. 

This dispute arose during negotiations under a reopening clause for changes in the Schedule "A," which covered wages and working conditions for the carpenters and millwrights. The Schedules “A” for each construction craft are addenda to the project-wide Hanford Works Agreement, and are negotiated separately by each union and the contractors' committee. The Hanford Works Agreement, how ever, is the subject of negotiation between the Pasco-Kennewick Building and Construction Trades Council, AFL, representing the project construction workers (including the carpenters and millwrights), and the contractors' committee. 

Members of the two carpenters' locals went on strike January 4, 1954, impeding all major project construction. The Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, which had been trying to help the parties resolve their dispute, referred the case to the Panel on January 15. The Panel wired the parties to resume work pending disposition of the case under Panel procedures. The carpenters returned to work January 18, and the Panel heard the case in San Francisco, on

February 3-5. 

The Panel issued recommendations on February 20. These related to such matters as burned, charred, or creosoted material; lost or damaged tools; stewards; lunch periods; foremen; and termination

clause. With respect to the wage issue, the Panel recommended that the parties adopt the current settlement reached between the union and the Spokane Chapter of the Associated General Contractors. 

As to the union demand for an increase in isolation pay, the Panel found that this was not an appropriate matter for negotiations involving these local unions alone. Isolation pay is covered by a provision in the project-wide agreement, which was at that time the subject of negotiation between the Pasco-Kennewick Building Trades Council, AFL (of which these two locals are members), and the 

Hanford Contractors Negotiating Committee. The parties used these recommendations as the basis for settlement of their dispute.

Case No. 6: Oak Ridge AEC Operations 

This case consisted of three grievances of long standing, involving the Atomic Trades and Labor Council, AFL, representing the overhead cranemen and the pipefitters, and the Carbide and Carbon 

Chemicals Co., operations contractor for the Oak Ridge AEC project.

The Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service referred these disputes to the Panel on February 3, 1954. In one case, the dispute centered around the establishment of a classification for overhead cranemen at a rate different than the rate paid journeymen electricians. The other matter involved an alleged wage inequity between pipefitters and certain other crafts, and a matter of work assignments to craftsmen other than pipefitters. The Panel discussed these issues with representatives of the parties at meetings held in Washington, D. C., and in Oak Ridge, Tenn.

On May 17, 1954, the Panel concluded that the grievance and arbitration procedure of the current contract provided the appropriate method for settling the cranemen wage rate issue, and the proper course for the parties to pursue was to resume discussions on this issue, at the earliest possible date, at Step 4 in the grievance machinery and to arrive at a settlement, or carry the matter to arbitration. The dispute was resolved by the parties accordingly. With respect to the wage rate for the pipefitters, the Panel concluded that the present rate was established by the current contract, and was subject to its provisions for the duration of the contract, unless modified by the mutual consent of the parties. The issue of pipefitters' work assignments was withdrawn by the union, during Panel discussion of these matters.

Case No. 7: Oak Ridge AEC Construction Project 

This dispute was between the Sheet Metal Workers International 

Association, AFL, and Kaighan and Hughes Co., the AEC mechanical subcontractor at the Oak Ridge Construction project.

The dispute had its origin in a request by the union, about July 1953, for the payment of travel time from Knoxville to the project, on the basis that it was the prevailing area practice. In the ensuing months, disagreement over what constituted the prevailing area practice continued to persist, and several stoppages occurred even after lengthy mediation efforts, and after a tri-partite fact-finding panel, appointed by the parties, reported the facts on this issue. On March 

29, 1954, the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service referred the dispute to the Panel. The sheet-metal workers had stopped work on the project. The Panel urged the parties to resume work pending disposition of the dispute by the Panel, pointing out that it was fundamental to Panel procedures for work to be continued while a dispute was pending before the Panel. Work was resumed, and the 

Panel held a hearing in Washington, D. C., April 7. On April 

13, the Panel recommended the payment of $2.10 to sheet-metal workers for each day worked, retroactive to July 16, 1953. The parties placed this recommendation into effect.

In making this recommendation, the Panel took cognizance, at the request of both parties, of the tri-partite fact-finding board majority conclusion that area practice was to pay sheet-metal workers 7 cents a mile travel time, and that existing contracts did not limit basing points to specified cities. The contractors took the position that if travel time was to be paid, Oak Ridge should be the basis for computation, whereas the union took the position that Knoxville was the proper basing point. The Panel also took account of an agreed statement at the hearing that approximately one-third of the sheet-metal workers came from Knoxville, one-third from other places, and onethird from Oak Ridge. The Panel concluded that since the Oak 

Ridge community was still in a stage of development, requiring large numbers of sheet-metal workers to be brought in from the outside, it would seem that travel time based on some area wider than Oak 

Ridge was justified.

The Panel's recommendation was based on the belief that the settlement of this dispute called for a compromise of the positions taken by the parties, and that compensation for travel should be in conformity with the equities of the current travel situation. The Panel used as the effective date that which was specified in the stipulation by the parties when the dispute was referred to the tri-partite factfinding body.

Cases No. 8-11: Oak Ridge and Paducah AEC Operations 

These disputes involved the production and maintenance workers at three Oak Ridge, Tenn., plants and one Paducah, Ky., plant, all of which are operated by the Carbide and Carbon Chemicals Co., un-

der contract with the Atomic Energy Commission. The unions involved were the Atomic Trades and Labor Council, AFL, representing the employees at X-10 (Oak Ridge National Laboratory), and the Y-12 plant, at Oak Ridge; and the United Gas, Coke, and 

Chemical Workers of America, CIO, Local 288, at the K-25 plant in Oak Ridge, and Local 550, at the Paducah plant.

The parties had a separate contract at each of the four plants, each of which contained a provision that the wage schedule could be renegotiated at specified 9-month intervals to permit an across-theboard change in wage rates to be made. No other question could be opened. Wage renegotiations were instituted, pursuant to contract, prior to April 15, 1954, the reopening date specified in each of the four contracts. The parties were unable to arrive at a settlement of the issue, and comprehensive mediation efforts were unsuccessful. On 

April 16, the four disputes were referred to the Panel by the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service. Hearings were held in Washington, D. C., on May 5, for the CIO cases; and in Knoxville, Tenn., on May 12, for the AFL cases. The Panel issued its recommendations on June 14, for an across-the-board increase in wage rates of 

6 cents per hour, effective April 15, at all four plants.

The company and the AFL unions accepted the Panel recommendations.

The CIO rejected the recommendations, and the local officers were authorized to call a strike. The workers went out on strike July 7, at the K-25 and Paducah plants. In the meantime, on July 6, the 

President invoked the national emergency provisions of the Taft- Hartley law, and the board of inquiry met with CIO union officials and company representatives on July 8. On July 10, the workers returned to work under an agreement reached under the auspices of the Secretary of Labor. Subsequently, however, a strike of CIO workers was scheduled to begin August 12. On the preceding day, a federal district court issued an order enjoining the strike. The employees, pursuant to this order, refrained from striking, and production at the K-25 plant and the Paducah plant continued. 

On November 7, 1954, the United Gas, Coke, and Chemical Workers,

CIO, and the Carbide and Carbon Chemicals Co. negotiated an agreement affecting the K-25 and Paducah plants, with the assistance of the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, as follows: 

1. An across-the-board wage increase of six cents per hour, effective April 15, 1954, in complete settlement of the April 15, 1954, reopening; 

2. An across-the-board wage increase of four cents per hour, effective January 15, 1955, in complete settlement of the January 

15, 1955, reopening; 

3. Holidays falling on Saturday to be observed on Friday.

The Atomic Trades and Labor Council, AFL, simultaneously negotiated a similar agreement with Carbide and Carbon Chemicals Company, for the employees at the X-10 and Y-12 plants. 

Case No. 12: Portsmouth, Ohio, AEC Construction Project 

This dispute involved the United Brotherhood of Carpenters and 

Joiners of America, AFL, Local No. 437, and the Peter Kiewit Sons'

Co., Portsmouth, Ohio, the prime construction contractor at this project.

The dispute in this case involved wages, and arose during negotiations for renewal of the contract, which expired June 1, 1954. Collective bargaining and mediation failed to resolve the wage issue, and the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service referred the matter to the Panel on June 14. At that time, a work stoppage was in progress, and the Panel wired the parties to resume work at the earliest possible moment, pending disposition of the dispute by the Panel under the procedures established for the settlement of disputes in the atomic energy program. Work was resumed on June 18 and the 

Panel scheduled a hearing for June 24. The parties notified the Panel the day before the hearing was to begin, however, that they had reached agreement on wages, and the hearing was then cancelled. 

Case No. 13: Portsmouth, Ohio, AEC Construction Project 

In this case, the parties were the Operative Plasterers and Cement 

Masons International Association of the United States and Canada, AFL, Local 232, and the Peter Kiewit Sons' Co., Portsmouth, Ohio, prime contractor of this project.

Along with most of the other construction crafts, the contract for this union expired June 1, 1954. The basic issue during negotiations was wages, and when no agreement was reached by June 10, the cement finishers stopped work. The Federal Mediation and Conciliation 

Service referred the dispute to the Panel on June 14, after further collective bargaining and mediation had been fruitless. As in the carpenters' situation (Case No. 12), the Panel urged that work be resumed, and on June 18, the men began to man the job. 

The Panel held a hearing in Washington, D. C., on June 25. The issues presented were wages, the Saturday overtime rate, the effective date of the contract, and the rate for time worked outside of "bulletin hours." After a full discussion of these issues, the Panel suggested that the parties make an additional effort to resolve the dispute through the process of collective bargaining, in the light of agreements which had been reached currently in Portsmouth, Ohio, area. The parties were unable to settle their dispute by further negotiations; however, they did agree that the effective date for the new contract should be June 1, and that the recommendations of the Panel on the remaining issues would be accepted as binding. 9*

On July 9, the Panel recommended that the wage rates for journeymen plasterers and cement finishers be increased ten cents per hour; that overtime work performed on Saturday in excess of four hours should be compensated for at double the straight time rate, and that the effective date of these recommendations be June 1. The Panel returned to the parties the problem of the rate to be paid for work outside bulletin hours, with the recommendation that further efforts be made to settle this issue, and the parties did subsequently reach agreement.

Case No. 14: ACF Industries, Albuquerque, N. Mex., AEC Operations 

This dispute involved the International Association of Machinists, AFL, Local 794, representing the production and maintenance employees, and the ACF Industries, Inc., Albuquerque, N. Mex., operating under a production contract with AEC.

This dispute arose during negotiations of an initial contract. The union was certified in April 1954, after an NLRB election, and the parties bargained extensively with the assistance of the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, until an impasse was reached on six remaining issues. The Service referred the dispute to the Panel on 

June 14 and the parties were heard in Albuquerque on July 16 and 17.

During the course of the hearings, the parties stipulated that they would abide by the Panel recommendations.

The Panel issued initial recommendations on July 29, 1954, and supplemental recommendations on September 22. The recommendations were (1) the number of paid holidays to be seven; (2) welders to have the same position in the wage structure as machinists; (3) no provision in the agreement for a break period; (4) no provision for payment of full wages during absence for job-incurred injuries or illnesses; (5) a paid vacation clause providing for a vacation of 1 week after 1 year of service, and 2 weeks after 2 years of service, with administrative features spelled out; and (6) a sick leave plan under which employees would be credited with 8 hours' sick leave for each month of employment, cumulative to 24 days, with provisions for eligibility and payment.
