PREFACE 

Ski Lure does not claim to be a literary masterpiece. Nor does the author pretend to be an authority on skiing. Rather, this book is a selection of articles, stories and pictures written and compiled in newspaper style for those who wish to acquire a bird's eye view of skiing and skiers in the 

White Mountains of New Hampshire. 

The material for Ski Lure was gathered and written by the author from first hand observation, interviews with skiing authorities, and through the courtesy of members of the Eastern Slope Regional Association who have so generously contributed in various ways to the book. 

In writing this book your author has endeavored to approach his subject from the point of view of any reporter covering any newspaper story.

FUN IN THE SNOW Courtesy Boston & Maine R. R.

THE 

TYROL 

OF AMERICA 

New England is blessed with more vacational features than any other section of the United States could hope to possess. For, here may be found such a variety of ways to while away pleasant hours that the holiday seeker often has to stop and ponder just where he can get the most out of his free week-ends or his weeks of playtime activity. 

Of all the places in the East offering year 'round vacation features, the Eastern Slope Region of New Hampshire is paramount in both the summer and winter resort field. Here, amid mountains and valleys which change their color from the green of summer to the multi-colors of autumn and finally to the best of all, the clean snowy whiteness of winter, those in search of adventure, excitement and outdoor life can find the nearest thing to vacation Paradise in this changing, troubled world. 

The Eastern Slope Region is composed of the ski villages of Bartlett, Conway, Intervale, Jackson, North Conway and Kearsarge. Each village and a large percentage of the people, contribute annually to the Region Association in order to carry on the work of familiarizing the general public with the area.

The stupendous strides made in skiing in this particular section of New Hampshire during the past five years may be attributed to many and varied factors.

Less than a decade ago, skiers and winter sports enthusiasts were a novelty to the natives of this section, who practically hibernated during the long, cold winter months.

It was the hardy groups of young collegiate skiers who journeyed up to the Eastern Slope to take advantage of the fine snow conditions in Tuckerman's Ravine that first made the section skiing-conscious. These youths soon found that there were many other slopes suitable for skiing within the region, without enduring the long, arduous climb into the famous snow bowl.

After a while, tourist homes which usually closed and nailed up in October, began receiving calls during the winter, from skiers for lodging facilities and hearty home-cooked meals. 

Natural slopes were enlarged, new trails were cut down the wooded hills, and mechanically operated ski-tows of many designs sprang up to haul the ski fans heavenward so they would have more time to enjoy their beloved diversion. Large hotels started catering to the winter visitors and the otherwise deserted summer resorts took on an air comparable to that of the famed European winter playgrounds.

The most imposing of the ski lifts is the $200,000 development on 

Cranmore Mountain in North Conway, where native son, Harvey D.

Gibson, now a New York banker, financed the construction of the now famed Skimobile, invented by George Morton of Jackson. This unique ski lift has grown in popularity during the past few years until now it caters to as many as 6,000 skiers on a single Sunday. Snow trains, another important factor in the growth of this winter business, account for the arrival here of thousands of snow sport lovers from all parts of New England during the snow season.

The Eastern Slope Region attracts all types of skiers, many of whom are ardent devotees of racing. During the season, the races sponsored by the Eastern Slope Ski Club and sanctioned by the United States Eastern Amateur Ski Association have proven themselves to be the equal of any races held in the country and boast an imposing list of entries which often include champions in many classes.

One of the most famous races held in the Region is the thrilling and death-defying Inferno, run from the summit of mile-high Mt. Washington down over the head wall of Tuckerman's Ravine and then through 21½ miles of tricky wooded trail for a 2500 foot vertical drop to the highway in Pinkham Notch. The record for this run is held by the National Downhill and 

Combined Open Champion, Toni Matt, Eastern Slope Ski School instructor, who made the 3.8 mile run in the astounding time of 6 minutes 29 2/10 seconds, which halved the previous record of 13 minutes set by Dick 

Durrance in 1934. 

Skiing, although it predominates the winter program, is far from the only sport enjoyed. Nearly every village has its popular ice-skating rink. 

Several of these are lighted for night use and a surprising number of skiers, after long days on the slopes and trails, can be found at the rinks, cutting figure eights far into the night.

Snowshoeing is another favorite with hundreds who delight in exploring old logging roads and pastures on their Indian-fashioned web feet. 

Skiers are a sociable group, they enjoy their evenings before the fireplaces in the many ski inns. The Skitillions, dances staged especially for skiers by the Jackson Ski and Outing Club, are gaining in popularity each winter,

Catering to the comfort of visitors, both summer and winter, although the largest industry of the region, is not the only one of importance. 

Conway, with its busy shopping area, is a center of trade for the tourists, campers and farmers. A heel mill, turning out millions of wooden heels, employs a large group of local people. Birchcraft Industries, manufacturing unique wooden novelties for gift shops all over the country, furnishes employment for many men and women. The Kennett Company has an extensive lumber business and operates a number of portable and stationary sawmills on their vast timberland holdings. 

Bartlett, at the entrance to picturesque Crawford Notch, at one time a large rail center, is now a favorite with many tourists. The Government maintains a large experimental station there. One of the few wooden peg mills left in the country now carries on at Bartlett, bringing employment to many locals.

Jackson, famous for years past as a summer resort, has a number of fine large hotels and inns, many of which now stay open for a winter as well as a summer season. During the past decade a number of well-to-do people have come to this little village in the shadow of Mount Washington to buy land or old farm properties which they have converted into fine vacation residences.

Intervale, which lies partly within the towns of Conway and Bartlett, is without a doubt one of the beauty spots of the Granite State. A number of popular ski Inns and fine residences are located here, all adjacent to the 

Intervale Slope and ski tow.

Kearsage, with all the quaintness of a Tyrolean village, has been converted from a one-time summer resort to a year-around playground for thousands and a twelve-month residential area for hundreds of city people. 

Nature has bestowed upon this territory unsurpassed resources for all winter sports, and more especially, skiing. New England's highest mountains,

many of them cleared for pasturage, furnish unexcelled opportunities for thrilling ski runs. Through the splendid cooperation of the National and 

State Forestry Departments, the Eastern Slope Ski Club, local communities, and private individuals, these natural resources have been developed and nationally-famous trails and open slopes have been constructed. 

This Region is readily accessible from all points in the East. The 

State and Town Highway Departments take great pride in maintaining safe and comfortable driving conditions. Roads are well plowed and sanded at all times. The Maine Central operates a train daily, except Sunday, from 

Portland, Maine. The Boston and Maine runs two regular trains daily, except Sunday, with a week-end ski special on Saturday. The New York, New Haven and Hartford serves the Region by connection daily with these two centers, and also operates a special Eastern Slope Express (Skimeister) every week-end, leaving New York on Friday P. M., returning from North 

Conway, Sunday P. M. The most modern traveling conveniences_and skiing services are afforded on this train, so that the major cities in the East, including Philadelphia, Baltimore, and Washington are all brought within overnight distance.

The scores of ski fans who have enjoyed their visits to the Eastern Slope Region during the winter months are now counted among the hundreds of thousands who consider this section of New England as their favorite summer playground.

All the advantages of a well-rounded summer vacation may be found here. There are numerous places where swimmers and bathers may enjoy splashes in the invigorating water of mountain streams and pools and in wood-fringed ponds and lakes that sport sandy beaches. 

Mountain climbers, whether they favor short treks to easily accessible hills or good stiff jaunts to New England's highest peaks, find all they could ask for in this region.

Horseback riding addicts state that the fine mounts maintained at the various riding stables here are, without a doubt, the equal of any in the East. 

The bridle paths offer the saddle fans a variety of rides over beautiful wooded trails and across picturesque intervales, the like of which they have never before experienced.

Every section of the region has a fine golf course where visitors can enjoy their favorite sport. These courses offer the metropolitan visitors something new and interesting for few of the hazards are artificial. Tennis courts are maintained by several of the towns and nearly every one of the Inns. Summer tournaments are held for the two above mentioned sports which attract not only sectional but national champions to this area. 

Roads have been opened to the summits of several mountains so that those who wish to ride in their cars may enjoy the beautiful vistas unfolded before them. The streams and lakes have been stocked with game fish and opened to canoeing and boating.

Countless other diversions, such as first run movies, Broadway hits. offered by summer stock companies and dances to the music of famed bands in fine ballrooms, are only a few of the highlights of the summer evenings here in the Eastern Slope Region. 

Referred to as "The Poor Man's Ski Heaven," by national magazines during the winter months, it would not be amiss to say that this section of New Hampshire in the summer offers enjoyable vacations to people with limited incomes, while it counts among its visitors many millionaires and other notables.

The summer resort business of the Eastern Slope Region dates back

for decades but it has been only ten years since the natives of the Region opened their eyes to the fact that they had in their own backyard the makings of one of the finest winter resorts in the country a miniature Austrian Tyrol - which would eventually lure some of the foremost winter sports enthusiasts in the country. 

Not long after the winter sports pioneers of the Region got busy developing their new business, sports-minded folks from all parts of New England and New York began to discover the advantages and pleasure in skiing, tobogganing, skating, and snowshoeing. From a sleepy little town which was buried for months by winter snows, North Conway, the hub of Eastern Slope winter activity, grew by leaps and bounds until local banks built up assets from a few hundred thousand dollars to millions. 

It took the lean years of the depression and new ideas in winter resort merchandising to bring winter sports into reach of moderately well-to-do folk whereas previously it had been the pastime of the idle rich. These new ideas were that there were more one dollar bills to be found floating around than there were five dollar bills . . . and so by making vacations inexpensive enough for the average office worker the Region soon became the mecca of thousands of winter sports lovers who had hitherto confined their activities to the local playground skating rink or the little snow covered hills in their own backyards.

To-day, anyone who can scrape together as little as two dollars per day can enjoy himself in any one of the countless ski centers at Jackson, North Conway, Intervale, Pinkham Notch, Kearsage, Conway or Bartlett.

Of course, there are a number of fine winter resort hotels which cater to the upper income brackets who wish to fill themselves with the Tyrolian atmosphere of the surrounding countryside, but to get acquainted with the real skiers and to get into the real spirit of the thing, the fraternal atmosphere of the ski barracks is the place to go. Here in rooms or dormitories lined with double decker bunks one can become a "regular". At these ski lodges one must carry his own luggage, make up his own bunk, turn out with the rest of the crew at the ungodly hour of 6:30 A. M., wax his own skis; and eat rough victuals with the rest of the camp's crew at the long board tables. But here is good comradeship which could not be bought for love nor money in any stuffy city. If you eat with the gang at one of these ski lodges, your neighbor may be a retired bank president, a college professor, or a bookkeeper. The woolen encased young lady across the table from you in the dining room may be a Park Avenue deb or a shop girl from Boston. 

If you go up to Pinkham Notch Camps during the season from January to June you can rub shoulders with the daredevils of the ski world who trek over the treacherous Head Wall, over which the snows pile a hundred feet deep into Tuckerman Ravine, high up on the side of Mt. Washington. Most of these veterans of the ski trails have little use for the ski tow and open slope and delight in dangerous treks over forbidden mountain terrain and twisting downward trails that only champions dare to run. No sissies are these "sons of the snow", for if they find themselves suddenly caught on the trail by nightfall, they bed down in one of the National Forestry Service cabins which are located high on the upper trails throughout the White Mountains. The 

Forestry Service, aided by the C C C, have made these mountains a skier's heaven by the erection of the cabins, the use of which doesn't cost a cent to anyone who is in need of shelter.

The High Country Cabins generally have two rooms. The walls are lined with bunks, and the cabins are equipped with blankets, cooking stove, heating stove, and kitchen wares for cooking purposes. In keeping with the tradition of the out-of-doors from the Klondike to the Rockies and from Hudson's Bay to the White Mountains, these High Country Cabins are always open to provide shelter to any hiker, hunter, trapper or skier who 1

may need their friendly protection. Many rugged outdoor enthusiasts and skiers unload their car, park it at the foot of the trail, tote their grub up the trail to one of these cabins and, making it their base of operations, ski in the surrounding country as long as they desire. It is not uncommon for two dozen or more skiers to spend a week-end in one of these cabins and if there are not enough bunks to go around, some members of the party sleep in "open face" shelters outside with a great blazing fire to keep out the cold. 

The most inexpensive way to enjoy a skiing holiday is to cook your own food at one of these cabins, because food is your only expense as there is no rent to pay.

However, there are countless Ski Lodges, Inns and private homes in the Eastern Slope Region, providing skiers with good food, cooked in New Hampshire style. Among these places are Whitney's at Jackson, Spruce 

Mountain Lodge, The Overlook, The Hawthorne, all at Jackson; The 

Clarendon, Kerry Patch Cabins, The New England Inn and Ski Barn, The Pequawket Inn, all in Intervale; Glenwood on the Saco at Glen; Russell's at 

Kearsage; The Eastern Slope Inn and Kearsage Hall at North Conway, and last, but certainly, not least, Joe Dodge's Pinkham Notch Camps at 

Pinkham Notch.

There have been many pioneers in the world of skiing in the Eastern 

Slope Region, including "Auntie Mae" Slade of Spruce Mountain Lodge, Joe Dodge of Pinkham Notch Camps and many others. However, the one who stands out among them all is probably Carroll Reed who pioneered the idea of setting up a ski school at Jackson to teach skiers the fundamentals of safe skiing. It was while convalescing from a skiing accident that Carroll got the ski school idea which was destined to bring the art of good skiing to thousands who loved the sport but who did not know just how to go about perfecting themselves in it.

When Carroll did finally get back on his feet, he organized the Eastern Slope Ski School and brought to this country Benno Rybizka, tops in European ski instruction, from the Hannes Schneider Ski School in St. Anton. 

During the first winter Benno trained a group of local boys in the Arlberg technique and in the spring, with the help of business men in Jackson and North Conway, and with the revenue brought in from that year's operations. four of the local ski teachers were sent back to St. Anton with Benno for five weeks of intensive training. The success of the Eastern Slope Ski School was attributed to large classes and low prices and during the first year 6000 lessons were given, and in the second year, 10,000 skiers flocked to the school. 

The following summer the Ski School was purchased by Harvey D. 

Gibson, the North Conway boy who went to the big city and made good as New York's youngest bank president, and who also became the brains behind the World's Fair of 1940. He then brought to this country Hannes Schneider, who is considered the outstanding ski teacher of Europe, and under his management the school has grown to the point where in excess of 24,000 lessons were given last year and it is rated as the largest in the world. The school, of which Benno Rybizka is Vice-President, employs 28 teachers and the Jackson branch is under the leadership of Franz Koessler. This tremendous increase in folks yearning to learn skiing is due to the fact that even an office boy can afford to take a course in skiing in the ski school. Twelve two-hour lessons cost only $12 and a graduate can navigate quite safely on most any slope. 

To-day, after a movement started under the direction and planning of native-born Chester ("Chuck") Emerson, former Dartmouth football, baseball, and skiing star who teamed up with Hannes Schneider and Noel Wellman, New Jersey industrialist, who came to Kearsarge to mend his shattered health, every boy and girl in the North Conway Region has become ski minded. Through this movement, countless boys and girls have become proficient in skiing and many of the boys have, after graduation from

the school, become fulltime and well paid ski instructors in ski classes throughout the Eastern Slope Region. Many of the local youngsters who could not afford to buy ski outfits have received them free of charge through the generosity of seven citizens of the Eastern Slope Region who have been equipping the youngsters at the rate of 100 per year until there are few young folks who are without the necessary accessories needed for the sport. 

Nearly every Eastern Slope town has become a ski village; ski boots and snow trousers are now the fashion for men, women and children in these towns. And how the kids love Hannes Schneider, who has taken the junior ski program to his heart and strives to teach the children all he knows. 

There is a story behind Hannes Schneider's last visit to his homeland that might easily read like a thrilling piece of fiction. The skimeister was visiting in his native land when Hitler's gray hordes marched into Austria and Hannes was caught along with millions of his countrymen in what seemed a hopeless trap. However, Hannes' close friend, Harvey Gibson, got busy and at the expense of a lot of money and much political wire-pulling got the skimeister out of Austria. What a story Schneider could tell of those historic, yet fearful days, when Hitler began his conquest of Europe!

Schneider's return to North Conway was also quite dramatic. On the clear cold winter night that Hannes arrived in North Conway hundreds of rosy-cheeked boys and girls from all the towns on the Eastern Slope were lined up in double rank to greet their friend and teacher. Their upraised skis formed a double line to greet Hannes, forming an archway for him to walk under all the way from the station, up the main street and to the town's largest hotel, where the older folks were gathered to greet him. What a spectacle that was, and what a thrill it must have been for the ski instructor, who was at the same time assured that his junior program was a complete success. 

A GROUP OF SKI ENTHUSIASTS

SKI HEIL! 

By Red Hughes 

Skiing as a sport knows few, if any bounds, and as a pure recreation for the winter months it cannot be excelled. Not a fad destined for a few years of life, but a permanent part of America, is what this exhilarating pastime has turned out to be. 

All that skiers require is the cooperation of the weatherman in furnishing them with their much needed friend, a good snow surface. "Schussnuts", as they are wont to call themselves, along with many other endearing names, do not meet the restrictions they do in other sports. Skiing offers a wide and endless variety of pursuits.

The large majority of ski fans have gone in for just plain slope skiing, making use of nearly every open slope in New England which offers a semblance of snow cover. Here they try "shusses" (straight runs), "sitzmarks," (falls), "snow-plow turns," (all the name implies), Christy, Telemark and Tempo turns, and, on the whole, thoroughly enjoy themselves. 

It is usually on the open slopes that one can find the greatest variety of skiers. Just for example, we will point out a few types of skiers, using their more popular ski names. The "flounders" are always with us - they are the skiers who spend more time in the snow than they do on their skis. 

Then there is the "Heel leaner," the skier who leans 'way back and gives one the impression that he is going tobogganing rather than skiing. Also the "pole skier," who labors under the belief that his poles were made to ride on.

Every slope has its "ski-babe," the very attractive young woman who dresses the part, but who wouldn't think of donning a pair of skis for fear that her hair wave might get mussed. The male counterpart of the "ski-babe" is the "ski-lush," who is the best dressed skier at the club, but who does most of his skiing along the bar rail.

The horror of every ski slope is the "ski-bunny," usually feminine, who delights in playing in the snow and throwing snowballs at every moving object. The most harmless member of the ski family is undoubtedly the "wax nut," who spends three quarters of his time waxing his skis and the other quarter telling other skiers the right kind of wax to use.

Next to open slope skiing, trail or down mountain skiing is without question the most popular. Unlike open slope skiing, trail skiing is not the best thing in the world for the novices, although many decide each year that the hurricane did not do enough damage in Eastern Slope Area and take it upon themselves to see how many trees can be bowled over. 

With sufficient practice any capable slope skier can get along all right on some of the easier trails. All beginners are warned by the old-timers to stay off the trails designated as "expert", until they have mastered the art of "control". As one well known instructor recently stated, "Trees will stand alongside of a trail for hundreds of years and then without warning jump right out in front of a beginner who thinks he is doing a fine job of breaking a trail record."

Downhill ski racing in the past decade has changed from one of the most hazardous sports to a well organized one; entrants have developed such a high degree of skill that the many accidents of early days have been practically eliminated. Whereas, in the past, a field of eight or ten racers would be considered large, it is not uncommon now to have an entry list of over 100 from more than a score of clubs throughout New England.

Cross-country skiing has never met with the popularity here that it has enjoyed in Europe, but there are many skiers now taking up the sport and experiencing the thrill of a good long trek on skis. The cross-country fan usually packs sufficient food to last him a couple of days, depending on his trusty skis to bring him to a suitable farmhouse or ski lodge for the night. 

Then, there are the more hardy brethren who prefer to put up in leanto

shelters situated along most trails in the National Forests. In these shelters before a roaring wood fire and tucked snugly in a warm sleeping bag, the boys picture themselves competing on a scale with members of Admiral 

Byrd's Antarctic Expeditions. 

Cross-country ski racing, although not a spectator sport, is one of the favorites with confirmed skiers, who believe that the race is its own reward. 

Courses for these races are usually laid anew for each race, through the woods, over hills, without any so-called marked trail. only colored streamers tied to tree branches for the races to follow. Some remarkable times are turned in on these races, although no one ever breaks a record, for every course and every race is different. Racers have been known to go 50 kilometers or 31 miles in a little over three and a half hours.

Far from the least exciting of the ski sports is jumping, when skiers buckle on their "barrel staves" and soar over two hundred feet through the air after getting a speedy start down a prepared chute. Although it was the first ski sport to become popular in this country, some of the glory of ski jumping has been stolen away by the other phases of the sport. 

Ski jumping requires poise, quick thinking, alertness, coordination of mind and muscle and plenty of courage. Jumping is not as dangerous as some people would have you believe, providing the jumper has had sufficient training. One expert points out that more people are hurt in bathtubs than on ski jumps, but it is also true that more people use bathtubs than ski jumps. 

Just as skiing has innumerable phases, so is it adapted to countless types of individuals. Skiers of all ages and walks of life are to be found on nearly every Eastern Ski Slope and it is one sport that an entire family can take up and enjoy together. Two-year-old youngsters can get a big kick out of the sport, although they do skip the technicalities, and oldsters, well over the three quarter century, mark have often been seen making their marks in the snow slopes and cross-country trails.

Skiing has something to offer us all or it would never have grown to a $35,000,000 business in such a short span of years. New ski tows are being built every day and new slopes are being cleared, so tighten up your riggins and "ski heil to all."

The Snow Train Goes Right into the "Snow Country" 

In winter, when Northern New England rings with happy laughter, while devotees of ski, snowshoes, skates, toboggan and even the bob-sled, make merry in this the perfect all-year-round vacationland, "The Snow Train" offers the best means of reaching and returning from the ideal winter sports places. 

Twelve years ago the Boston and Maine Railroad originated "The Snow Train". In these past eleven seasons those who have become ski-wise and devotees of the fun provided by snow on Northern New England's hills, have learned something more than how to ski and how to find new health, zest and pleasure in winter sports.

Experienced winter sports fans have learned that it spoils winter-time fun to be caught in sudden storms of snow and sleet: to stand and shiver while an expensive garage tow car pulls an automobile from where it has skidded off an icy road; that it is silly to wear nerves to the jitter-point, driving long hours on dark, wintry roads after a day of sport; and that the folks who have the most fun really get the most out of winter sports, going by "Snow Train".

Every Sunday from January 4 to March 8 inclusive, Boston and 

Maine "Snow Trains" run from Boston to North Conway and Intervale, bringing thousands of experienced and novice skiers alike from all parts of New England to enjoy the advantages of the skier's paradise - the Eastern 

Slope Region of New Hampshire. 

The Boston and Maine also operates week-end Snow Trains from Boston to the Eastern Slope Region every Friday and Saturday during the skiing season. These week-end trains return to Boston Sunday nights.

CARROLL REED'S SKI SHOPS 

CARROLL REED RENTALS SKI SHOPS REPAIRS 

A high spot of White Mountain travel is a visit to a Carroll Reed Ski Shop. Paneled in pine, heated by old wood burning box stoves and colorfully decorated with hand-painted peasant designs, these shops exude hospitality, while at the same time they offer browsers and purchasers the finest in quality ski and after-ski clothes, equipment and accessories. 

Carroll Reed, one of the first exponents of skiing as a diversion in this country, early realized that skiing was a specialized sport and called for specialized clothing. When founding the Eastern Slope Ski School in 1936, he also established a Ski Shop in Jackson and the fame of this store has spread with the growth of the region and the Ski School. Shops are now located in North Conway, Franconia and Jackson. In the fall, exhibitions of the latest in ski wear and equipment are held at many of the better known colleges in the East. Mr. Reed, who is also President of the Eastern Slope 

Region, Inc., goes along on these trips not only to sell his popular wares but to impart information about the Eastern Slope Region and to give talks on skiing while he shows moving pictures of his favorite sport. 

While skiing has been converted from a game in which youngsters slid down snowy hills on barrel staves to a recognized international sport, ski clothing has likewise been revolutionized. It is a far cry from blanket coats and heavy, clumsy, woodsman's clothes to the sleek and chic tailored togs of to-day. No longer must a ski suit be of backbreaking weight to keep out the cold, for lightweight and windproof gabardines have proven themselves equal to the rigors of the winter elements. 

The Carroll Reed shops do not find it necessary to go out of the country for the wares they have popularized. The work of local craftsmen, such as novelties, ski riggings, ski racks and numerous other gadgets can be found in abundance there. Among the original and interesting articles are hand-knit sweaters of intricate design, produced by the women of the Eastern Slope Region, in their homes. These garments, made of 100 percent unscoured virgin wool, with all the natural oils retained, are very popular with ski fans.

Many well traveled skiers claim these shops to be the most complete all-out ski shops in the East, and this fact is attested to by the large mail order business the firm enjoys.

At Cranmore Mountain, just a few minutes walk from the center of North Conway's business section, is the first and only mountain climbing tramway of its kind, anywhere in the world. Invented in 1937 by George 

W. Morton, a skilled motor mechanic of Bartlett, N. H., the Skimobile consists of two sections. The lower section starts at the Base Station at the foot of Cranmore Mountain and runs 3000 feet to the Halfway Station with a vertical rise of 760 feet and a maximum grade of 20%. The upper section of the Skimobile runs from the Halfway to the summit, a distance of 2000 feet, with a vertical rise of 620 feet and a maximum grade of 38%. 

One hundred and twenty individual cars, fastened to an endless 6000foot pulling cable, roll smoothly on rubber tires, up the wide wooden trestle of the lower unit. Each car resembles a miniature automobile and seats one person. Cars operate at variable speeds and are in constant motion, except when slowed down by an attendant for the convenience of passengers. 

Sixty cars are operated on the upper unit. Capacity of the Skimobile is 600 passengers per hour and about twelve minutes are required to travel from Base to Summit (2052 ft.). Motors, cables and cars are equipped with every modern safety device and on each tramway there is a magnetic brake which is held open by electric current and in case of current failure, the brake is automatically applied, thus stopping the tramway. There is also a safety device which will stop all cars in a distance of nine feet in case of cable failures.

Harvey D. Gibson, a native son of North Conway and now a national figure in the banking business, purchased the land for the Skimobile on 

Cranmore Mountain and sponsored the Skimobile. He has given his unstinted support to every effort promoting the scenic and vacational advantages of North Conway. One hundred and seventy-five thousand rides were sold on the Skimobile in 1940. For winter sports or pure scenic pleasure, the public have put their stamp of approval on this mountain climbing skimobile.

During the skiing season no amount of summer activity can compare with the ceaseless enthusiasm of the skiers. From right after breakfast until sunset, and even after that on the floodlighted slopes of Cranmore, the climbing and downhill running continue.

The Skimobile is the delight of both the novice and the expert skier. 

It makes possible so much more skiing per hour and on any type of slope desired. The skier gets off the skimobile at any one of the seven way stations and can run the very wide cleared slopes on either side of the tramway or take any of the seven well-laid-out trails leading from the Summit to the Base Station. These slopes and trails are grassed in summer, and so well graded that with as little as four inches of snow, good skiing is possible.

One of the finest ski patrol units in the East has been trained at Cranmore. The ten ski patrol men are all skilled in first aid, and are constantly running the slopes and trails enforcing rules of skiing courtesy and keeping slopes clear for runners. There are eight first aid control stations with telephone connections where toboggans and blankets are kept. Each man on the patrol wears a first aid belt and carries a signal flag on his right ski pole. Every possible safeguard is provided at Cranmore and accidents are at a minimum. During the winter of 1942 new slopes will be opened for the exclusive use of the Hannes Schneider Ski School.

All during the winter the trestle of the Skimobile is kept clear of snow with scraper plows which run continuously in all storms day and night. A huge snow roller and "a magic carpet" powder snow device are unique Cranmore inventions for keeping skiing conditions at their best. 

Designed and built to accommodate the skier, the Skimobile has attracted thousands of tourists, winter and summer, who ride up and down the mountain to enjoy the magnificent views and thrill to the sight of one of the finest panoramas available from any mountain top in New England.

RUSSELLS AT KEARSARGE 

This air view of Russells shows the Mt. Cranmore Skimobile in the background 

One of the most ideal spots for all winter sports activities in the Eastern Slope Region is Russells, located in Kearsarge three quarters of a mile from the Mt. Cranmore Skimobile.

Accommodating over one hundred guests, Russells makes available to winter sports enthusiasts a complete program of skiing on excellent slopes, skating, tobogganing, facilities of the ski school, recreation rooms, excellent food, a ski room and warm comfortable steamheated outside guest rooms. 

For many years and up until 1929, Russells remained open for a few days only, during the winter months, for the convenience of students and members of the Appalachian Mountain Club. However, in those days, skiing had not_come into popularity and it was not until 1937, after the formation of the Eastern Slope Ski Club during the previous winter, that Russells opened for its first winter season. Soon after this venture into the winter sports field the Cranmore development, now so well known to all who know the Eastern Slope and North Conway, got under way and the success of Russells as a winter sports rendezvous was assured. 

Since the opening of Russells for winter sports in 1937, Mr. Norman.

A. Barnes, manager, has added a toboggan slide, lighted ski slopes for night skiing, a 200 foot skating rink which is also lighted and which has a heated cabin for use of skaters in changing their skates. The cabin also has a music amplifier which entertains skaters in their leisure moments. Also, included in recent additions to Russells is a rumpus room for ping pong and dancing and also a ski and wax room for the convenience of guest skiers.

During the winter season the dining room at Russells is decorated with Swedish designs. Food which is especially designed for the appetite of winter sports fans when they return from the slopes or the skating rink includes afternoon tea. At night, dancing, bridge, and other entertainment is provided to aid guests in completing a day packed full of thrills and excitement.

A brief story of the history of Russells as a resort hotel in the Eastern Slope Region dates back to 1863 when the Russell cottages first opened their doors to the public when a Hartford banker and his family spent the summer season there. In 1890, "the boarding house", after enlargement and improvement, became a hotel. At that time the stable contained twenty-five fine saddle horses and driving horses. Golf and automobiles were then unknown, and bath tubs and swimming pools had not yet reached the mountains. Tennis and mountain climbing in those days were the popular sports for men, and the ladies were content with croquet and other games. 

In 1902 Russells acquired a golf links and in 1910 automobiles came into popularity, thus bringing even more guests to enjoy the beauty of the surrounding mountain country. And so, through the years since 1863, Russells has continually made improvements for the convenience and pleasure of guests until to-day, winter or summer, the hotel has become one of the most complete and most popular year 'round holiday centers in New Hampshire, not only because of its fine guest accommodations, but because it is also the nearest hotel of any size to the Cranmore Mountain Skimobile which is in operation winter and summer.

NEW ENGLAND LOOKS AT SKI TEACHING By Ford K. Sayre 

The Average New England Skier, which means you and me, is beginning to wonder what the ski teaching business is all about. We have heard a lot of talk and we have read, or at least seen, more books about different systems than we believed there were teachers. We have had to listen to a lot of foreign names and have been dragged into innumerable discussions about the Austrian, Swiss and a half dozen other techniques, most of which claim to be not only the original but the only way to learn to ski. The one point that seems to be definite about the whole subject is that the "one pole toe-strap era" is gone and that there is no one advocating its return. 

Perhaps it is not fair to credit the advance in American skiing to any one man, but if any credit is to go to anyone it should go to Otto Schniebs. 

We bring in Otto's name as he started us in the proper method of skiing and kept hammering at us until we listened. He took the stiffness out of us and made us crouch so low that we could almost ski through a barrel. To-day, few of us ski in that deep crouch that Otto first taught — but it is still a good way to ski. Eddie Wells, one of the ranking downhill and slalom men in the east to-day, skis so low he almost drags on the ground and on occasion, Dick Durrance gets down there too. When skiing really got started in this country there was an immediate demand for instructors that at first was awfully hard to fill. The call reached 

Europe and there began an influx of teachers, both good and bad but for the most part, good. Others followed and soon several of the excellent 

European schools had branches or representatives in this country. These men are especially well-trained and we are fortunate to have their services. 

However, as soon as the field began to get all crowded, arguments involving the various European instructors and their techniques became noticeable and these, fanned by national interests and national pride, assumed alarming proportions. Who started it does not matter, but skiing and skiing instruction in this country is suffering and it is time that it is stopped. 

The Swiss, the Austrians, the Germans, the Norwegians, and even the Americans have all got to get together and mend fences. The ski teachers of all these nations must realize that skiers in this country, no matter what their private views, care nothing about European politics, or what nation had done most for skiing when they are out on a slope taking a lesson. It is not necessary or possible, as yet, in this country to make all teachers teach exactly the same system. That may come, but it isn't important at the present time. 

What is important is that the various instructors recognize that it is possible that someone else's system has merits, so that the first lesson isn't wasted in telling the skier how badly he was taught at another school and that everything he has learned must go in the ash can. 

What makes the situation even more ridiculous is that when the methods of the warring factions are analyzed the differences in their systems are so insignificant as to be laughable. In the net result, frankly, it isn't important whether you stem the downhill or uphill ski first in stem turns, nor is it a fatal error if you lift your ski while doing a stem christie. Those things you do, or do not do, as the occasion demands and they are certainly not worth fighting about.

JUNIOR SKIING By Noel T. Wellman SCENE I 

On a winter's afternoon snow blankets the land and the mountain ski trails and open slopes are thronged with enthusiastic skiers. 

On that same afternoon the headquarters of the Eastern Slope Ski Club is thronged with youngsters, an eager expectancy on their faces. As each name is called, they come forward to receive tall bulky packages and soon the floor is literally knee-deep in the wrappings which are quickly torn from shiny new skis and equipment. It is not Christmas time, but one happyfaced boy exclaims, "Gee, this is just like Santa Claus had come." 

With hearty thanks, accompanied by broad grins and smiles, the happy youngsters hurry away to the nearest hillside slopes.

This is the first scene of the Junior Ski Plan which was started by the Eastern Slope Ski Club last winter when nearly one hundred boys and girls in the Region, between the ages of twelve and fifteen, were furnished, at a nominal cost of two dollars, a complete ski outfit consisting of skis, poles, bindings and boots. This was made possible through the generosity of private citizens, summer residents and the North Conway Rotary Club, as well as the splendid cooperation of the Dartmouth Cooperative Society which furnished the Club with this equipment at very low cost. 

Again this winter, sufficient funds have been contributed or pledged to equip another hundred children and again the Dartmouth Cooperative Society has promised its cooperation. 

SCENE II 

On another winter's afternoon the ski slopes are thronged with old and young enthusiasts. One part of the slope has been reserved for junior skiers and a class is in progress. Down the slope comes a ruddy faced boy, eagerly trying to twist his legs into a "snowplow." "Bend the knees," calls the instructor, "and keep the points of the skis together.' Next, follows a limber little girl, proudly showing the boys how it is done. "Bravo," calls the instructor. One after another they try snowplows, snowplow turns, schusses (straight runs) and "sitz-marks" (falls). The frosty air rings with the happy laughter and yet there is a certain earnestness about the youngsters who are being taught to ski safely. 

The lesson is over and the young pupils trudge home with a promise to show up at the class next week.

This is the second scene of the Junior Ski Plan in the Eastern Slope Region. Having furnished the juniors with proper equipment, the Club has completed plans for this winter to give the youngsters free ski instruction, so that they may learn to ski safely.

The Eastern Slope Ski School has arranged to supply competent instructors who will hold classes one afternoon a week in each of the principal villages of the region. The public school authorities have agreed that those pupils who are up in their studies and are eligible for ski lessons, may be released from school early one afternoon each week to enable them to join the junior ski class.

This plan is recognized as an important part of recreational training of the junior boys and girls.

SCENE III 

It is a clear, crisp day, and the sun shines with a sparkle on the new fallen snow and skiing conditions are perfect.

Thirty or more youngsters, who have been diligently attending the junior ski school and have qualified for their first race, are lined up at the top of the open slope. Below them is marked out a slalom course with bright red and blue flags fluttering in the breeze.

With the racers stands the starter with pistol in hand while at the finish line the ski school instructor awaits with a stop watch. 

There is the sound of the pistol shot and the race is on! The first boy starts with a push on his poles and then twisting, turning, sliding, scrabbling and sometimes falling, he makes his timed run. One after another the excited boys and girls follow. There is much shouting of encouragement and sometimes a burst of high glee as some runner takes a spill and knocks down a flag or two.

After the race, they crowd around their instructor to learn who won and who was next. Then the proud winners are called to receive their prizes and the others, not in the least crestfallen, at not having won, look forward to the next race day when they will have another chance. 

This is the third scene of the Junior Ski Plan, but not the last. It describes the healthy competition provided under the competent supervision of their ski school instructors for those boys and girls who are learning to ski safely.

The last scene of the Junior Ski Plan may be written in the years to come. Who can foretell how far-reaching this plan may be, or who can forsee its influence on the lives of our youngsters? Organized, competent instruction and healthy sportsman-like competition builds character and helps to make our children grow to be better citizens.

CROSS-COUNTRY SKIING By H. I. Baldwin 

One of the Greatest Attractions of skiing is that it knows no bounds, is not restricted to a rink as skating usually is, but offers an endless variety; it is at once a means of travel and a pastime. On a cross-country ski tour, successive hills, fields and wood roads unfold as to an explorer. 

In the Alps, cross-country touring is served by innumerable small ski huts and inns where meals and lodging can be secured. In New Hampshire many such services are available and it only remains to plan the trip. Some hardy explorers prefer to bivouac on the trail. There are leanto shelters on the National and State forests which are suitable for winter visits with plenty of bedding and a roaring fire in front, and make admirable objectives for winter picnics. 44 Cross-country racing has been rudely neglected since the appearance of organized downhill skiing in New England. Cross-country does not draw a crowd of spectators as jumping does, and in the past, competitors have been 'exploited" at times by being forced to follow a course laid along roads and streets so that they might be viewed. It seems as if we must always try to capture any sport which appeals to us and cage it in some sort of amphitheatre where it can be made a gladiatorial contest for the amusement of the majority who do not engage in the sport. Cross-country skiing refuses to be so caged. 

To the true cross-country lover, the race is its own regard. 

Courses for cross-country racing are usually laid out anew for each race, and follow no predetermined route; they are often laid through woods with no trail other than the colored streamers tied to tree branches. Competitors are not allowed to go over the track before the race, and have as many surprises as the roving ski-tourist. They are, however, often shown a map of the course, with the location of up and down grades, so that they may plan their pace.

A course should be laid so that the first mile or so does not have steep climbing until the racers have warmed up; neither should the finish be uphill. An attempt is usually made to divide the course into one third uphill, one third downhill, and one third level skiing, with the finish at the starting point. Several laps are sometimes made around a relatively short course, or the track may wind over hills and valleys for 31 miles (50 km.) in the case of championship races.

Extraordinary times are sometimes made over these long distances. 

The 18 km. course is often covered in very little, 1 hr.; 30 km. in less than 3 hrs. and the 50 km. was done by Vestad in 3 hrs. and 38 min. in 1933. 

Hedlund did the 90 km. Vasalopp in 52 hrs. in 1928. Among miscellaneous records may be mentioned the Lap Lars Tuorda, who in 1884, covered 206 km. in 21 hrs., 22 min., of which 12 hrs. was time out for rest. Actually, there can be no records in cross-country skiing in the sense that track records are made. No one ever "breaks a record" in cross-country, because no two courses are the same, nor are weather and snow conditions comparable. 

They change from hour to hour.

ADVENTURES IN MOUNTAIN CLIMBING 

For inexpensive and sheer joy of achievement, no other sport can compare with climbing. In the great fraternity of trail enthusiasts there are men and women, boys and girls, representing nearly every class of human society. In this mechanical age, they have found soul-filling satisfaction in scaling unspoiled heights and penetrating remote wilderness strongholds. 

Established in 1876, the Appalachian Mountain Club is the chief representative in the East of this group of people. It pioneered the development of much of the trail system in the White Mountains and spent large sums upon the development of huts and other facilities in the vast area which has now become the White Mountain National Forest, all of which are available for use by the general public.

The eight A. M. C. huts operated for the public during the summer are described herein.

The huts are planned to be a comfortable day's tramp apart, and through the use of their facilities you may "travel light" without loss of comfort. For those who care to pack the necessary bedding, food and cooking

equipment, numerous shelters are maintained by the Club, the White Mountain National Forest and other organizations. 

In winter, Pinkham Notch Camp becomes the mecca of skiers and alpinists throughout the East.

The huts provide bunks, mattresses, pillows and blankets in dormitories for men and women. Meals are served at 7 A. M., and 6 P. M. (Daylight Saving Time) in family style by trained hutmasters who are your hosts and the Club's official representatives. Information on local attractions, routes and probable accommodations at other huts will be supplied by the hutmen. 

Pinkham Notch Camp is open throughout the entire year. The other huts are open from about the middle of June to late September. Early season conditions are frequently much more favorable than during the rush period of August. After Labor Day, the huts again are less crowded, with the weather ideal for climbing.

It is desirable that parties of more than two persons make reservations, if possible. Such requests may be made to Joseph B. Dodge, Manager, Gor ham, N. H.

Even in summer, the weather is changeable and severe in the high mountains. Climbers should take care to carry warm, windproof clothing; toilet articles and towel, gloves, flashlight, compass, knife, matches, and firstaid kit. Guide books and maps may be purchased at the huts and at other points in the White Mountain area.

Special attention should be paid to footwear. Heavy, well-fitting walking boots, properly broken-in, are usually chosen by experienced climbers. 

Heavy, high sneakers often give satisfaction. For maximum foot comfort, woolen socks are essential.

Permits are required to build any fires in the National Forest. They may be obtained from any district ranger or forest guard; at Pinkham Notch

SNOW AND ICE FASHIONS By Edna Somers, Director of Fashion Center, Jordan Marsh Company 

With the snow beginning to fly, thoughts fly toward hours on sunny slopes and new ski runs, toward good conversation before a roaring fire. And inevitably, that sometimes, unwelcome thought, "What will I wear," intrudes upon the pleasant visions.

Just a few years ago when ski enthusiasts were few and far between, wondering what to wear culminated, usually, in a motley collection of heavy, warm snowcloth jackets and baggy trousers, and an extremely varied collection of rather busy-looking accessories. The result usually dampened the ardor of the novice, and hastened the acceptance of classic ski clothes by nearly everyone. 

To-day, ski clothes reach a new peak in functional simplicity. Designed for freedom of movement, classically simple, they are, nevertheless, keyed to the season's fashions. Streamlined trousers, whether they are called Springerhösen, downhill trousers, or jumping pants are first choice.

Sleek and classic, they have that professional look much to be desired. Done in navy blue, ice blue, lovely color against a white slope or forest green, they take to sweaters, or parkas, weskits or jackets with the greatest facility. 

Newest, are those done in black gabardine, elasticized of course, for comfort as well as chic. 

For those who preserve a steadfast loyalty to the complete ski suit. or for those whose skiing technique limits them to inevitable on-looking, there are new ski-suits of wool gabardine cut with infinite pains and precision. 

The fitted, hip-length jacket, single or double-breasted tops, beautifully tapered trousers, and has, on everyone, a remarkably professional look. 

Classic as these suits are they make a gesture toward current fashions, by taking extraordinarily gay colors for jacket linings, and interesting details in pockets, revers and buttons.

To further the professional look there are knitted caps, hair-concealing bandannas, huge goggles and ski shells. And of course, waterproofed canvas knapsacks that strap snugly around the waist. 

If the most hazardous trail, or most strenuous slalom is an old story there is nothing smarter than the above-the-knee-length gabardine skirt worn with hip-length ribbed stockings, topped by a weather-proofed cotton twill windbreaker this is a costume that will start conversation. Over it, a warm-up coat of wool-lined poplin, or a great coat of Alpaca pile in snowy white, or, it goes without saying, a sports fur coat. 

For the skater who enjoys ensembling a costume, or for the one who scorns "special" clothes skating skirts with flannel shirts or bulky, simulated handknit turtlekneck sweaters in kelley green, red, royal blue or yellow, are the most popular. On outdoor rinks where warmth is a necessity, the sweater or shirt can be topped by a jacket. The skirts are best when worn knee-cap length but for the really professional skater the 18-inch skirt in tweed, wool, velveteen or cotton gabardine is workmanlike and eye-catching. And of course, worn under the short skating skirt, wool panties or long tights in bright colors. 

or The after-ski fashion story is an easy, casual one, with slacks and tucked-in sweaters and cardigans. No matter how tailored the slacks and sweaters, color used with a lavish hand contributes a festive look. With gray flannel slacks - a royal blue sweater and magenta scarf with natural gabardine slacks, a cardigan in bright orange with a kelley green scarf newest of all, black velveteen slacks with a fuchsia pullover. For the very feminine, or for those who take their winter week-ending in the grand manner, there are "dress for dinner" dirndl skirts in longer calf-length, and fireside dresses in the new mid-calf length. These are best in bright wool and rayon mixtures, with rather a peasantish feeling in applique and embroidered motifs. They add a gala note to a wardrobe assembled purely for fun on snow and ice, and they create a restful mood at the end of an out-of-doors day.

MOST DEBATABLE SUBJECT OF SKIING IS WAXING 

If ever a civil war should split the ranks of skiers into different camps its cause would most likely be the friction of a wax discussion. The purposes of waxes are threefold: (1) to protect the wood of the ski from undue wear; (2) to provide a smooth sliding surface; (3) and to permit a reasonable grip for climbing. (People forced to live with skiers may admit these three, but add a fourth; to smell up the house.)

The first step in preparing new skis for service is to remove all varnish from the running surface with a steel scraper and fine sandpaper or steel wool. 

If a base wax is to be used, it should be rubbed over the wood evenly and melted in with a hot iron. Some skiers prefer to use one of the numerous lacquers now on the market.

After the average skier gets past the point where he derides all forms of wax because "he goes too fast already," he begins to realize that a tremendous amount of energy is wasted in climbing. He is now ripe for a demonstration of the fact that a wax can climb and slide, if applied correctly for the type of snow available. It does seem hard to believe, and becomes even worse when explained away by lofty "dynamic friction," but nevertheless, it is perfectly true that it is possible to select such a wax for most snow conditions.

The simplest to use, easiest to remember system is to stick to one type of wax, and apply it differently for the various snow conditions. "Blue

Label" is a widely used variety with this system of waxing and is a good starting point. For dry powder snow, a thin layer, well rubbed in with a cork, will serve. The wetter the snow, the heavier the layer of wax. If it is planned to climb for a half-hour or so, then slide down, it is well to apply the wax in fairly thick patches, several inches long. This will climb well, and may be smoothed out at the top of the hill for the trip down. 

If the snow becomes too sticky for comfort, the skis may be speeded up by a coat of paraffin or graphited paraffin, rubbed on smoothly. While this will slide in damp snow, it is distinctly an emergency measure, for it will not climb nor will it stay on for long. 

Real, honest-to-goodness wet snow, the dripping granular stuff that occurs only in late spring calls for Klister. Klister is worthless on anything except real spring snow and need not be carried except at that time of the year. This variety of snow is formed when deep snow fields melt at day and freeze at night, resulting in a homogeneous mass of wet granules of ice. With all its mess, Klister materially aids in prolonging the sport, so we recommend making its acquaintance next spring.

So far, we've advised a good base, plus varying amounts of "Blue Label" for nearly all ordinary uses, while paraffin and Klister have been mentioned for special purposes. The skiers who have been through several seasons will no doubt wish to include a dozen other waxes, such as "Mix," "Green Label," "Orange Label," various "Universal" waxes, "gold Klister," "Pastel Pink" and essence of dried shark's liver. But, let the newer skier learn to walk before he runs.

RACING By Alexander H. Bright 

Oh, for those good old days of downhill racing when, of a night before a Moosilauke Down-Mountain Race, the whole existent downhill ski racing fraternity would sardine into the sitting room at the Hillside Inn and talk things over. The tail-wag, the Arlberg Crouch were exhaustively explored. 

We were all novices in experience, yet the ego that goes with downhill racing was fast making us experts in the patter and prattle of skiing. Only a letter from abroad or the voice of our oracle, Otto Schniebs, carried any weight in deciding our endless problems.

Our best downhill racing course was the Old Carriage Road on Mount Moosilauke. This narrow, sharply switch-backed, old tote road was an inadequate and dangerous makeshift for a race course; yet when the conversation shifted to this subject, as it usually would during an evening, the racer's courageous spirit for more and more speed boiled thoughts down to grumblings over the flatness of the straight stretches and the speed-reducing sharpness of the corners. One evening Otto could bear it no longer, and said, “I dink vat you boys iss want iss a trail so shteep for to race dat vun of you, he come down mit a broken arm, vun of you mit de broken leg, and vun of you he doan come down at all."

Under the conditions, Otto was right, yet, two or three years later, with the skiing technique greatly improved, Otto laid out the famous Hell's Highway Racing Trail on Mount Moosilauke which, until the hurricane strewed it with trees, was the most outstanding devil-be-damned course ever built. 7 - 

The early era of catch-as-catch-can downhill racing roughly from 1930 to 1934 carries particularly happy memories of the Mount Moosilauke Carriage Road, Peckett's Hill, the big hill at East Hebron, Gunstock Mountain, and the Fire Trail on Mount Washington. The Taft Trail, undertaken by the initiative of Miss Katherine Peckett, was the forerunner in providing better conditions for downhill racing and set a splendid standard of what was needed.

The winter sports gathering organized by the New Hampshire Development Commission in May, 1933, energetically obtained help from the newly created Government's C. C. C. The Taft Trail was smoothed up and many new ski trails built among them, the John Sherburne Ski Trail, the

Wildcat, and the Bear Mountain Trails, all of which, have become very popular and are frequently used for downhill races. 

For every downhill racer of seven years ago there are one hundred or so to-day. As general skiing has advanced, so has the technique and detailed skill of preparation for and running in a downhill race. The nervous anticipation on the night before is still there, and the excitement and thrill of the race itself are as real and potent factors in the enjoyment of the sport as ever. Where in the old days the winner took more than five minutes to run two thirds of the present Taft Trail and many seconds separated the times of the different runners, the present-day competition is so keen that frequently only split seconds separate the leaders, and the full Taft Racing Trail has been done in less than two minutes and a half. 

Racing has come to be so well organized and the racers have developed such a high degree of skill that the many accidents of the early days have been pretty well eliminated to such an extent that in the past year or two there have been no accidents to competitors or spectators in the major races. 

This keenest of solo competitive sports, which was practically unknown in the United States before 1930, has had an amazing birth and childhood growth on New Hampshire trails, and promises a sturdy, long life of active interest to a large growing following of skiers. -Courtesy "TROUBADOUR"

SKI JUMPING By Carl E. Shumway 

Most persons, who see a ski jumper catapult himself from the lip of a ski jump and soar 100 to 200 feet or more through the air, say "It's not for me.' For this reason, ski jumping during the early years, perhaps, held back the broad development of American skiing, as to the general public, ski jumping was the only form of skiing they knew. Now the tables have been turned and for the last few years ski jumping has been handicapped by the huge general interest in downhill skiing. But ski jumping is coming back.

It never has slipped among many skiers. It simply has recently been overshadowed by downhill skiing, whether on a golf course or a mountainside, which the public could learn much more easily and have far more opportunities to enjoy. 

New Hampshire has pioneered in both ski jumping and downhill running. At Berlin, one of the first ski clubs in America was started and for years Berlin has turned out some of the finest ski jumpers. Dartmouth 

College, in turn, was the first American College to have a ski jumping contest back in 1910 while in 1912 and 1913 Dartmouth men were the first to ascend Mt. Moosilauke and Mt. Washington on skis. In both of these communities and many others ski jumping still rates high because it is the most thrilling and spectacular sport that a man does on his own legs. It requires poise, quick thinking, alertness, coordination of mind and muscle, stamina and some courage. Danger? No more so than any other thrilling sport. As one

American Olympic skier points out, there are more persons injured in bathtubs than on skis; while another says all speed skiing is dangerous. The latter, although an expert, skis at speeds beyond his ability or age, or else under hazardous conditions. For persons in their thirties or more, ski jumping would usually be dangerous if they were not previously trained skiers. 

A good all-round skier should know how to ski jump. No matter if he prefers downhill and slalom he will be better in them if he knows how to handle himself in the air and at the speeds consistent with large jumps. This has been plainly shown in scores of cases of good ski jumpers becoming expert. downhill men in a comparatively short time. SNOW AND WEATHER REPORTS By Joe Dodge 

It has been alleged that your correspondent sits up in Pinkham Notch and "mixes brew" that is the pleasure and displeasure to the skiing fraternity. 

When the skiing is good everything is "hunky dory" but when Jupe Pluvius sends us rain it is thumbs down on the "master brewer." Once in awhile, over the past few years, enough skiers have repeated Otto's prayer to St. 

Peter and we have enjoyed the glorious white that makes the waxed boards. fly, but all too often, it has been otherwise. However, we are all looking forward this year to the Paul Bunyan winter of the "Great Blue Snow" when even the tall fir trees will be buried and skiers can tumble off the tops of the highest peaks and only land in a heap of deep, fluffy, powdery snow. That will be the day when we all can ski over the tree covered hills and another Bunyan can repeat to our great-great-grandchildren about his skiing days, back when the snow was deep.

A NEW HAMPSHIRE CAMP IN WINTER By Adelbert M. Jakeman 

A Summer Camp on the shores of a New Hampshire lake is not without its attractions even in the dead of winter. When the snow lies deep and white on the rough woods trail that winds its silent way from the smooth state road and the nearby village, over the fields and past the whispering pines to the snug cabin by the water's icy edge, Nature displays fully as much beauty as in midsummer. True, her offerings are vastly different from those she presents in the warmer months. However, they are still both exhilarating and inspiring.

A camp, of course, must not be too far away from the main highway, and should be easily accessible from the owner's permanent home. With this for the ideal setting, many a pleasant excursion can be undertaken on a Saturday afternoon or Sunday. Experiences that may well be compared to the pleasures of boating and fishing when the elements are more temperate. 

Breaking a path, with snowshoes and skis, through the whiteness and stillness of a pastoral scene is certainly invigorating. The brisk breeze brings a tingle to the blood and a ruddiness to the cheeks as the outdoor enthusiasts stride along with light packs strapped to their backs, and with arms swinging free. Stately trees send down a plaintive sigh from above. Here and there, new-fallen pine spills sprinkle the snow with green and brown. A wild rabbit jumps into the brush ahead. Criss-crossing one another, and leading off over the gentle undulations of the rolling countryside, are dainty tracks of graceful deer who have escaped the hazards of the hunting season. 

There are also tell-tale signs of many other four-footed inhabitants of the vicinity. Every few steps one encounters the burrowings of hungry little animals in search of sustenance. And lastly, the sweet perfumery of the forest penetrates the nostrils in sharp contrast to the smells of the busy city and fills one with a sense of well-being. 

Since a camera is really an indispensable part of the equipment for this expedition, snapshots are taken which rival in interest those secured when the foliage was in full bloom. Next summer, around the logwood fire on a long rainy evening, the photograph album will be produced and this happy holiday outing in the heart of winter will be lived and laughed over once again.

Finally, the camp is reached. A key is inserted into the cold lock, and the party is let into the chillness of a room that has been closed for many months. Soon a fire is roaring, built with wood stacked against the wall last 

September in anticipation of just such an occasion as this. 

While the brilliant blaze is raising the temperature of the cozy interior some of the group may take a tramp out onto the frozen, snow-covered surface of the lake, while the rest remain within to prepare hot coffee and other palatable edibles suitable to the season. The meal is eagerly devoured, interspersed with an exchange of numerous reminiscences of days at camp. when dinner was served on the broad front porch, or near the field-stone fireplace, rather than close to the friendly stove inside. 

After that, with a song or two and a few appropriate games, the glowing embers are allowed to die as the sun drops down toward the crest of the distant hill. The camp door is shut from the outside; there is a last nostalgic backward glance over the cold expanse of frozen lake, and the slow homeward way to the city is unfolded in the early iridescent twilight of a crisp winter's day - a day that has lent new life to people confined in steam-heated apartments. DOG SLEDDING IN NEW HAMPSHIRE By Eve Seeley 

Dog Sledding in New Hampshire has now become one of the recognized winter sports. Until recent years, sledge dogs were considered almost exclusively as the "Beasts of Burden" of the north and used by the traveler as a means of transportation. 

Soon after the "Gold Rush" in Alaska, men became interested to prove the worth of their dog teams and arranged a competitive race which started from Nome and covered a distance of over a hundred miles. This event attracted the Sports World and spread to Canada and the States, the first sled dog race being held at Berlin, New Hampshire, and called the Interna tional Dog Derby. 

Several persons living in New England decided to stimulate this unique type of sport and formed the New England Sled Dog Club, the purpose of which, however, was to improve the breeding, care, training and driving of these arctic dogs. As a result, these so-called "wolf dogs" are now friendly animals and the Club schedules races every week. These events are held in various towns about New England, the New Hampshire Outing Clubs sponsoring most of the races. Thirty members, men and women, drive their dog teams in this amateur event, the distance not over twenty-five miles a day, but spectators may witness one of the most colorful and romantic of sports. Over a hundred dogs meet and seem to enjoy this competition more than the drivers. People on the side lines make friends and select their "favorites" and follow the races each week. The drivers themselves enjoy this fascinating sport, which does not demand professional training. 

Recreational sledding is perhaps the latest wrinkle in this sport and those who wish to have the thrill of riding behind these huskies may engage dog teams and experienced drivers to take them over the snow-covered trails, where scenic beauty is unsurpassed, and denied those who travel the main highways. Still another phase of this sport is a Dog Driving School. Here one may learn the mysteries of mastering the dogs and participate in "riding the runners" after a few instructions are imparted to the pupil. All ages of people, who never before have attempted "animal training," have become experienced drivers and delight at the obedience and willingness of these four-footed animals, and obtain vistas of pleasure that do not enter any other winter sports. Ski-joring with dogs, driving "one-dog sled team" by children, and seeing these canines living in "A Village of Huskies" has given 

New Hampshire the distinction of being the center for dog sledding.

Weather conditions make it possible for a long season and sledding is enjoyed from December to April.

NEW HAMPSHIRE'S By William P. House ALPS 

Among those who come to enjoy New Hampshire's mountains are a few not content to take the recognized trails but who seek to try their skill and experience against the difficulties of rock cliffs and ice gullies. They may often be seen as dots joined by thin threads of rope high up on the cliffs of Owl's Head, Cannon and Willard Mountains, and Cathedral and White Horse Ledges. Less seen are those who eschew the popular ski trails and journey into Huntington Ravine, there with ice axe and crampon to try to force a way up the steep ribbons of ice which furrow the headwall. It is mountaineering in the real sense for although the scale and distance are much less than in the Alps or in our great western mountains the technical difficulties, plus the very real hazard of weather, make ice climbing on the slopes of Mt. Washington in the winter serious undertaking. 

Armed with ice axe, crampons, and rope, with ample sized boots and warm clothing our party stands at the foot of one of these gullies. They plan to try the Central Cully, now, in January, a slip, but if that should happen it is the leader's job to stop him; he, therefore, watches with an eagle eye ready to tighten up the rope and prepare for a shock at the first indication of a slip. Now they are together and the responsibility of the second man commences. He must place himself in an even more secure position for the leader is climbing above him and should he fall there would be no mild pull on the rope but a body-tearing jerk which may drag him from his steps and hurl him, with the leader, to death on the bottom of the ravine. If the slope is very steep and footsteps alone fail to give sufficient security he will drive in long iron pegs through which the rope is looped. So they climb, foot by foot, taking turns at the arduous and exacting task of step cutting, holding themselves under rigid control, physical and mental, for the risks are in inverse proportion to the care they exercise. 

Finally tired, but elated, they reach the top, having taken perhaps five hours to go five hundred feet. Their feet and fingers are numbed but at last they can move freely and rapidly to restore circulation. They have overcome an obstacle which has called for great judgment, ability and nerve, and though they have neither score card nor trophy to prove it they have accomplished something which will send them back to their jobs refreshed and more confident of their ability to overcome other kinds of obstacles.

Reliable equipment is one of the paramount requirements for protection and enjoyment in any kind of sports activity. 

For over one hundred years The Iver Johnson Sporting Goods Co., located at Adams Square (formerly Dock Square), Boston, has been serving New England sportsmen with the finest sporting goods obtainable anywhere in the world. Álmost one million sporting goods items are to-day offered to 

New Englanders who have a yen to ski, snowshoe, skate, hunt, fish, or follow any one of the scores of sports which have made New England the Playland of America.

The Iver Johnson Company was founded in 1840 by John P. Lovell. 

In those days the business was taken up entirely with the manufacture and sale of firearms, the parts for which were being made in England and shipped to the company in Boston where they were assembled and offered for sale to Boston sportsmen who liked to spend their spare time hunting various kinds of game in all parts of New England. In those days a good gun sold for from $135 to $145 and a story told by one of the officials of the Iver Johnson Company relates that the sale of two of these more expensive guns to Maine and New Hampshire farms once saved the company from bankruptcy, in that their sale helped the founder, Mr. Lovell, to meet a $200 note which was due and which he did not have money enough to meet until he happened to make the sales both at one time. 

In 1900, Lovell failed in business and the company was taken over by the Iver Johnson Company which has conducted the business until the present day. Before Lovell failed he had been acting as agent for the Iver Johnson 

Company, selling the Iver Johnson guns which were manufactured at their factory in Fitchburg. It was about 1870 that guns were first made by machinery and the old fashioned gunsmith who made his guns by hand was thrown out of employment by mass production methods. When machine methods came into effect, Lovell, who had been making his guns by hand, became agent for the Iver Johnson Company. About this time, Lovell started adding other items of sporting goods to his line of guns, including fishing tackle and athletic goods.

In the Iver Johnson Company, after the failure of Lovell, took over the business and it is recorded that the company started to sell such items as toboggans, Norwegian skiis, skates, sleds and many other present day popular sporting equipment.

The winter sports fad came into being about 1900, right after the bicycle craze of '98. About this time New Englanders were spending their leisure winter hours in sliding on the hills of their own community or skating on nearby ponds. In those days skiis were a novelty and were not too much in demand and it was not until about 1920 that skiing really came into popularity. However, since 1920 skiing as a sport has continued with rapid momentum until it is to-day one of the ranking resort industries of the East. 

According to Mr. D. R. Harvey, of Iver Johnsons, ski togs really got their start about 1930 when skiing became more popular than ever. At first ski togs consisted of knickers and wool shirts and it was not until six or seven years ago that the European style ski pants came into popularity. 

Men's and women's ski togs are almost identical and there seems to be little change in the styles of ski togs from year to year. 

Iver Johnsons, which is the largest sporting goods house in America, carries a complete line of skis, and clothing for the well dressed skier or winter sports enthusiast. In their ski department, Johnsons features the Lund 

Ski, made by the Northland Ski Company, and all in all, there are five different makes of skis to choose from, including Lund, Northland, Flexible Flier skis and two imported brands, the Jensen and Gresvig skis. Eight different ski waxes as well as ski poles, Bass boots, ski bindings and dozens of other items are also in stock at Johnsons' ski department.
