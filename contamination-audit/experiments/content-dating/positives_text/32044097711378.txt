COMMENTARY Introduction Two provisions of the Social Security Amendments of 1960 

(Public Law 86-778) were of special interest to the South Dakota Legislature. One provided federal funds to the states for medical services to aged persons (65 years or older) receiving

Old Age Assistance. The 1961 South Dakota Legislative Session appropriated funds for medical services and administration for the 1961-63 biennium to be used for this purpose (OAA Medical Care).

The second provided federal funds to the states for medical assistance to aged persons not receiving Old Age Assistance. Action on this matter in the 1961 Legislature was postponed pending further investigation of such a program. 

To determine better what course of action South Dakota should follow, the Legislature adopted House Concurrent 

Resolution No. 12 directing the Legislative Research Council to study the matter and report to the Thirty-eighth Legislative 

Session (Appendix I). 

General 

As the standard of living has risen, Americans have become increasingly conscious of health problems. We have come to expect, indeed often demand, more and better medical services, More abundant medical services of increasing quality have been available; at the same time, these services have become more expensive. The effectiveness and expense have had a very marked effect upon aged persons. Medical research has made tremendous strides during the past several decades. 

These advances in turn have contributed to longer life spans for the population. Progress in preventive and therapeutic medicine has also changed the demand for health service, with emphasis shifting from control and treatment of communicable diseases to chronic and degenerative diseases. 

Emphasis is concentrated now on problems like heart disease and cancer--diseases characteristic of aged persons. In

The portion of our population 65 years or older is steadily increasing, and probably will continue to do so. 

South Dakota, this trend is amply demonstrated by the changes from 1950 to 1960:

There is also increasing pressure for retirement at age 65 with moves toward even earlier retirement. The longer life span and earlier retirement will inevitably result in a larger group of Americans living on a fixed income. 

Because the need for medical services increases with age, and because of a longer life span, health costs for the aged have become an area of major concern. A person reaching 65 today is faced with the prospect of a longer life span, a rising level of medical costs at the period when they are most needed, and a steady decline in real income due to general price increases.

Cost of Health 

Americans today are spending about $25 billion annually on health and medical care--roughly five percent of the gross national product. According to "Trends in the Supply and Demand of Medical 

Care,' " (U. S. Congress, 1959), the average family spends about five percent of its income on medical care, or a family with a yearly income of $5,000 spends about $250 for health. The per- centage is not even, however--families with incomes of less than $2,000 devote nearly twelve percent, and families with incomes of $7,500 or over devote about three percent to health. 

This substantial outlay for health has resulted in attempts to equalize the burden--particularly for persons on a wage or salary. The result is most apparent in the rapid growth of medical insurance. Today more than seventy percent of the population is covered by medical insurance, and this insurance pays about one-quarter of Americans' private medical care bills.

Public spending (including federal, state and local governments) also accounts for about one-quarter of the total amount spent on medical care--a substantial portion of this amount being spent on aged persons. This is an area of major concern to state legislators.

Aged Persons and Public Welfare 

Traditionally, the welfare of aged persons has been the responsibility of the family or private concerns. There has been a trend for this responsibility to move toward higher levels of

government, however, and this trend continues. From the family, responsibilities for the aged moved to counties and municipalities, the state and federal governments. 

During the 1930's the federal government assumed some responsibility by passage of the Reconstruction Finance Act in 

1932, the Federal Emergency Relief Administration Act in 1933, and finally the Social Security Act of 1935. The Social Security 

Act provided federal funds for state-wide programs on a permanent basis, and federal and state governments committed themselves to a share of the responsibility for the welfare of aged persons. 

South Dakota created a Department of Public Welfare (called Department of Social Security until 1957) to administer state and federal funds for the program. Medical benefits were minor, but acceptance of responsibility to care for aged persons implies help for health purposes.

The Department of Public Welfare operates four categorical aid programs:

Old Age Assistance (OAA), Aid to Dependent Children, Aid to the Blind, Aid to the Disabled, and also the Division of Child Welfare. Of these programs, OAA is of primary import to the subject of this report.

History of Medical Care in South Dakota 

Medical aid to aged persons in South Dakota falls into four distinct periods, with a possible fifth period under consideration. The first period is prior to 1936 when medical care was the responsibility of families, municipalities, and counties. The second period covers 1936-39 when the State assumed responsibility for rather extensive medical payments included in money grants to OAA recipients. The third period (1940-61) saw the State withdraw and substitute only limited medical assistance payments to persons receiving OAA. In 

August of 1961 this aid was changed in nature and its amount increased, representing a fourth period. A program which would extend medical aid to needy persons not receiving OAA would be a fifth distinct period.

Very little need be said of the three periods prior to 1961. 

During the first period (up to 1936) medical care was no of state government. During the second period (1936-39) the state did assume some responsibility, but quickly withdrew to make small payments to public assistant recipients during the third period (1940-61). During the third period the state did, however, recognize the heavy burden being borne by county governments in this area, and a Committee of the Legislative Research Council made a comprehensive study of medical care for aged persons during the 1957-59 biennium. Changes in federal statutes in 1956 had made increased federal funds available for public welfare. Although the South Dakota Legislature amended the law in 1957 to

take advantage of such funds, no money was appropriated, and the ability to accept increased federal funds was thus virtually negated.

The Legislative Research Council Subcommittee on Medical 

Care in 1958 recommended to the Legislature: a. b. C. 

That a medical care program for public assistance recipients be adopted, subject to financial and program limitations, to be supported by federal, state and county funds and that state funds for such a program be supplied through special appropriation;

That by support through a minimum state appropriation in addition to county participation and federal grant funds, the state-administered program thus financed relieve and aid the counties in their provision of medical care for the indigent; and 

That county participation in the state-administered program be based on the average number of categorical aid recipients within each county.

These recommendations were not accepted by the 1959 Legislature, and medical care remained chiefly a county function although limited funds continued to be provided to OAA recipients by the Public Welfare Department. The OAA program prior to 

June 30, 1961 provided glasses and denture allowances to a specified maximum; medicine for persons with diabetes, heart disease, and anemia; occasionally nursing home care; home care if necessary which could include a registered nurse; and miscellaneous services. During 1960 this assistance accounted for about 9.5 percent of total OAA funds expended. In absolute terms, medical care administered by the State Department of Public Welfare to OAA recipients cost more than $620,000 in 1960--about $171,000 were from state appropriations and the remainder from federal matching funds.

Amendments during 1960 to the Social Security Act (Public 

Law 86-778) modified nearly all sections of the Act. This legislation encouraged expansion of medical care under the OAA program, and in 1961 the Legislature appropriated $740,000 for the 1961-63 biennium for medical care to OAA recipients. state thus passes from the third (1940-61) period of limited implied responsibility, to a fourth period (since August 1, 1961) of explicit responsibility for limited services.

Under the OAA medical care program, services to recipients have been expanded over those extended during 1940-61. In addition to the services previously supplied, the Public Welfare Department now provides dental care and glasses (previously only

an allowance for these items), eye examinations, and orthopedic aid. The Department does not include payments for drugs. 

Health of Aged Persons 

No one doubts that aged persons' medical needs exceed those of younger persons. A one-day survey of hospital patients made by the American Medical Association revealed that twenty percent of all hospitalized persons were over 65 years of age--even though the aged represented only eight percent of the total population. Today aged persons represent 8.8 percent of the total population and undoubtedly occupy more than twenty percent of the available hospital space. Persons over 65 years old spend an average of 1.78 days per year in hospitals, or almost three times the average 0.66 days for persons less than 65 years old.

Not only do older persons spend more time in hospitals, but they also visit the doctor more often than persons less than 65 years old. 

The increase is gradual--males 65-74 years of age averaging almost seven visits per year and females of this age group averaging about seven visits. For persons more than 75 years old visits average almost seven for males and nearly eight for females. This rate would probably be higher if all aged persons received as much medical care as they needed. In addition, aged persons require a number of other health services including nursing, health education, restorative services, home care, social guidance, and physical theraphy. 

Because many older persons live on fixed incomes, the cost of medical service is a pressing problem. The rise in medical cost in recent years further complicates the matter. Fees and charges for medical care increased about 47 percent during 1950-60--the largest increase being in hospital charges which almost doubled. These hospital services are, however, of much better quality today.

The need for additional medical care together with increased costs results in aged persons spending over twice as much per capit for medical services as persons less than 65 years old. In the cas of hospital care and drugs, they spend about 2.5 times as much. 

Although spending for various other goods and services may be curtailed with age, there seems little doubt that medical costs impose a heavy burden on aged persons. All data in this section were taken from "Health and Economic Conditions of the American Aged, a 1961 U. S. Senate publication.

Medical Aid to Aged Provisions of Public Law 86-778 The 1960 Amendments to the Social Security Act, which prompted South Dakota to engage in expanded medical services unde:

the OAA program, also contained provisions designed to encourage state governments to initiate or expand a Medical Aid to Aged (MAA) program for persons not recipients under the OAA program. 

The Congressional action left to the Legislature the determination of whether South Dakota should extend its responsibility to needy persons not covered by OAA, and, if such aid is to be extended, to what extent.

Federal law relating to MAA allows the states wide discretion in determining the scope and nature of a plan and also in determining eligibility, but all plans must be approved by the Secretary of 

Health, Education, and Welfare. 

The state may provide any or all of the following: in-patient hospital services, skilled nursing home services, physicians' services, out-patient hospital services, home health care, privateduty nurse, physical theraphy, dental services, laboratory and X-ray services, prescribed drugs, eye glasses, dentures and prosthetic devices, diagnostic screening and preventative services, and any other medical care or remedial care recognized by state law. 

A state may also provide for osteopaths, chiropractors, optometrists and remedial services provided by Christian Science practitioners.

In short, it appears that almost any service the state desires can be accomodated under the MAA provisions. 

The plan can cover all medically needy persons 65 or over, including any under retirement plans--public or private. The state may disregard in whole or in part the income of recipients, but Congress apparently intended that reasonable outer limits be determined for the income of persons receiving this aid. 

Although this wide latitude is provided, the plans must comply with section 2 of the Social Security Act as amended. 

To obtain federal assistance, there are ten general requirements, including:

Prohibition of property liens on account of benefits during recipient's lifetime, and limitations on recovery after recipient's death.

Cost of an MAA Program in South Dakota 

According to final 1960 census figures, South Dakota's population was 680,514, an increase of 4.3 percent over 1950.

During this same period the number of aged persons increased from 56,000 to 72,000--an increase of over 29 percent. Or, to state it another way, in 1950 aged persons represented 8.5 percent of the population. By 1960 this percentage increased to 

10.5 percent and it is possible that the figure could increase to more than 12 percent by 1970. This group, This group, steadily growing, will become an increasingly influential political factor in South Dakota. 

In mid-1962 a total of about 8,100 persons received aid from the State Department of Public Welfare under OAA, representing 11 percent of the aged population in South Dakota. Subtracting these persons from the total aged in South Dakota, indicates about 64,000 possible recipients of MAA. The great majority of these would probably be excluded because of adequate means for providing their own medical care, but a broad MAA program could cover at least as many as the present number of OAA recipients. Estimates of costs are virtually impossible, of course, without knowledge of the actual number which might be eligible, and the possible "clients" of an MAA program is unknown. However, rough estimates suggest that there may be as few as 2,500 and as many as 10,000.

If these figures are used as the minimum and maximum, and a $15 monthly cost per person is assumed, the overall cost would therefore probably vary somewhere between $450,000 and $1,800,000 annually ($900,000 - $3,600,000 biennially) of which South Dakota's share under the matching provisions would be from $250,000 to $1,008,000 biennially.

MAA: Pro and Con 

The White House Conference on Aging held in Washington in January of 1961, presents the best single statement of aged persons' philosophy regarding medical care. The Conference determined that furnishing high quality health care for the aged was so complex that its solution will require a variety of approaches including individual resources, health insurance, industrial programs Social Security, public aid, and other programs. The new federal legislation (including MAA) was called desirable, and the Conference urged all states to take advantage of it. A minority report on financing health care noted that public assistance medical care programs have demonstrated the inability of states, even with federal assistance, to provide comprehensive programs of high quality. To achieve these ends the minority continued, only a plan for paying during the working lifetime for medical care after retirement will suffice. This philosophy was reflected in attempts to include additional medical insurance provisions under Social Security which were defeated in the 

2nd session of the 87th Congress (1962).

Opponents of expanded medical aid to the aged have stated that this responsibility properly rests with the individual. 

Present public aid at the county, state, and federal levels does an adequate job; and even if the program were expanded, a large number of aged persons might refuse it. An expanded program of free aid would increase the demand, drive medical costs up and lower the overall quality of service now rendered. Even if it is conceded that additional public aid is necessary, coordination of present programs might prove adequate without a new program that is made somewhat inflexible by acceptance of federal funds. 

Free medical care is an infringement upon personal liberty, and it will destroy the traditional personal relationship between physician and patient. The exact cost of expanding present programs is not known, but there is little doubt it will be expensive. 

Conclusion 

It is extremely difficult to be objective where the health and welfare of people is involved. For example, what medical service should you buy for a loved parent or child? The best available probably, most of us think. The sacrifice in terms of money cost loses much of its importance under trying circumstances.

Some of this same philosophy affects our thinking in regard to public aid to our senior citizens. Few people are crass enough to believe that the quality or abundance of medical care for the aged should be determined solely on the basis of cost, but there is definitely a question as to who should bear the cost. This is a very important consideration; and short of complete medical aid supplied by the state, the responsibilities of government and private citizens must be clearly defined.

The study of the MAA plan for South Dakota led the Committee to the determination of the basic question: Is such a program necessary and desirable? 

The answer, as submitted in its recommendations, is: Yes, it is necessary and desirable, but the Committee believes an experimental program must come first to define problems and to identify eligible applicants. It is the opinion of the Committee that South Dakota can ill afford to establish such a potentially expensive program without preliminary exploration of the pitfalls that may exist.

RECOMMENDATIONS 

The Committee recommends that the 1963 Legislature: a. enact legislation empowering the South Dakota State 

Welfare Commission to contract with non-profit groups to obtain pre-paid medical and hospital care for the medically indigent under a pilot program on a contractual basis to implement the 

Kerr-Mills proposal for medical care to the aged 

(MAA) (see Appendix III); 

b. c. appropriate necessary funds to implement the pilot program; and direct the Legislative Research Council to review at three month intervals the operations of the pilot program during the biennium. 

It is the belief of the Committee that medical welfare of senior citizens over sixty-five years of age is of prime importance to the state. It is also the belief of the Committee that because South Dakota lacks experience in the operations of an MAA program, the first steps to be taken in that direction should be of an experimental and exploratory nature. The pilot program proposed and advocated by the South Dakota Medical Association (the full text is reproduced as Appendix III) will answer the questions which the Legislature has sought for the past two biennia. Before a full MAA program is recommended, the 

Legislature must know how many applicants for MAA there are likely to be, what benefits will be needed to provide the medical assistance needed, what financial burdens the state must be ready to assume, and how best to provide for the administration of such an all-inclusive MAA program. In order, therefore, that the 1965 

Legislature may have these necessary data, the Committee recommends the implementation of the pilot program suggested by the South Dakota Medical Association, with the provision that the Legislative Research Council be directed to review the operations of the pilot program on a quarterly basis.

2. 

The Committee recommends that the Legislature create a $10,000.00 revolving fund for the MAA pilot program to provide an additional margin of safety in the program's operations and as a contingency fund for emergency medical assistance cases which may arise. 

As a part of its proposed pilot program, the South Dakota Medical Association suggested that such a $10,000.00 revolving fund would allow the program to continue insuring the medical and hosptial care after a participant had exhausted his benefits under the provisions of the contract entered into by the South Dakota 

Welfare Commission and the South Dakota Blue Shield and. Blue 

Cross corporations. It is the belief of the Committee that a reserve margin of safety is necessary to assure the continued participation, when needed, of those individuals initially selected as participants in the pilot program. The Committee therefore, recommends the creation of such a revolving emergency and contingency fund.

3. 

The Committee recommends that the State Welfare Commission consider modifying the present OAA medical plan implementation to employ a contract basis for providing hospital and surgical-medical services.

Effective January, 1962, the state of Texas instituted a program for provision of hospital and surgical-medical services for OAA medical care by means of a contract between the state and Blue Cross-Blue Shield. Considered by many a "model" implementation of the OAA medical care program, this program appears to be working extremely well in that state, providing necessary services efficiently and with a minimum of "red tape" and concommitant administrative costs.

The Committee has studied and was impressed with the Texas contract provisions. It is therefore recommended that the South Dakota State Welfare Commission consider taking the same approach to our state's OAA medical care program. 

The 1961 South Dakota legislation is broad enough to allow this type of approach without further amendment; therefore, only administrative action would be necessary to make the necessary modifications.

Facts about the Legislative Research Council The State Legislative Research Council is a permanent, joint committee composed of every member of the South Dakota Senate and House of Representatives. Its purpose is to consider legislative policies between sessions of the Legislature. To achieve this purpose, the 

Council, during the interim between sessions, gives advance study and consideration to problems expected to confront the next Legislature. 

The work of the Council is conducted by committees and subcommittees and by a research staff, under the direction of an Executive Board, The Council staff serves the Executive Board and committees and subcommittees in development of a program for consideration at the next legislative session, and serves every legislator in an impartial and objective fact-finding capacity. 

In its work, the Executive Board: 1) acts as a clearing house for ideas on current legislative problems through receipt of proposals for study; 2) determines and directs the study and research necessary for proper consideration of all topics accepted; 3) disseminates advance information on these problems to legislators and the general public by means of reports and newsletters and by discussion at public meetings; and 4) reports directly to the Legislature in advance of regular sessions, making recommendations in the form of bills or otherwise, and summarizes the material prepared for use of the Legislature in considering the Council program. 

Since 1951 the State Legislative Research Council has provided an open forum to which legislators, administrators, citizens, and organizations have submitted their proposals for consideration. 

As a result of Council activities, problems of South Dakota state and local government receive continuous attention and study by members of the Legislature.
