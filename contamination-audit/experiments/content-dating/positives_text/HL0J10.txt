REVIEWING FISCAL 1970 

Civil Aeronautics Board regulatory action touched virtually every area of the agency's interest in fiscal 1970. 

The public convenience and necessity is best served by an economically sound industry and the air transport industry, while basically sound, has been assailed of late by a number of problems, including spiraling labor costs, disparity between capacity and demand, demands on capital resources, the high cost of money, inadequacies of the air traffic system, problems of environment and crimes against aircraft.

The CAB took on rate and route problems as they evolved in fiscal 1970 and also took several major actions that can be expected to help guide the air transport industry soundly into the '70's. 

In January 1970, the CAB launched the Domestic Passenger Fare 

Investigation (DPFI), the first such broad, searching look at passenger fares in more than a decade. 

Hope to Reduce Time

The DPFI is in nine phases-three rulemakings and six formal hearings-covering every major area of fare concern. Because of the protracted nature of large-scale rate proceedings, the conditions they set out to deal with have often altered by the time the cases have concluded. 

By the use of multiple proceedings in the DPFI, and using every possible expedition without sacrificing care, the Board hopes to reduce substantially the time required to conclude this far-reaching investigation. 

The Board also acted on several International Air Transport Association (IATA) agreements affecting international fares and rates. It approved, after expedited formal investigations, an IATA North Atlantic passenger-fare agreement which eliminated the round-trip discount on normal fares and established low contract bulk inclusive-tour fares. It also approved, March 1, 1970 through March 31, 1971, an agreement which made available certain excursion fares and substantially reduced fares for large affinity groups. 

One IATA agreement readopting basic passenger fares in the Western 

Hemisphere through March 31, 1971, and another closing passenger fares in parts of the Australasian area, were also adopted. 1

Worldwide Cargo Pact 

The Board also approved an IATA worldwide cargo rate agreement through September 30, 1971.

During the year certain service mail rates were reduced and military cargo rates were lowered as a result of a cost review. 

In route matters, changes and additions wrought in fiscal 1970 will convenience more air travelers, not only as a result of improved or expanded service to many of the nation's large metropolitan areas, but also by giving them new or closer air service ties with a number of smaller markets and lesser-known points.

Board action in a number of local service air carrier route cases strengthened routes and improved service in several regional markets while helping to lessen the carriers' dependency on Federal subsidy. 

Route awards to trunkline carriers opened important new markets in several broad regions of the United States, or provided competition in several major existing markets.

Air Taxi Expansion 

Several Board actions during the year evidenced its awareness of and preparation for coming developments in the burgeoning air taxi segment of the industry.

Fifteen hundred air taxi operators registered with the CAB and maintained a level of liability insurance under a regulation that became effective with the start of fiscal 1970. Insurance coverage requirements also were modified during the year to require coverage among other things, for substitute aircraft not owned by the insured when it was necessary to use substitute aircraft. The Board also undertook an investigation to determine whether and, if so, how the air taxi limitation of 12,500 pounds maximum certificated takeoff weight should be modified. 

The first phase of an important proceeding bearing on air traffic growth was decided. An examiner found in the Northeast Corridor 

VTOL Investigation that service between and among major cities from New England to Washington, D.C., to be provided by vertical and short takeoff and landing aircraft using landing sites in city center and densely populated suburb areas, is technically and economically feasible and will fill a pressing need. Board action on the decision, pending at year's end, was to determine whether the case should advance to the second phase which will include choosing the carriers to provide the service. 

Air Congestion Study 

With regard to air traffic congestion, the Board and the Federal Aviation Administration are reviewing airline scheduling practices. This is being done with a view toward alleviating some of the present air traffic congestion during peak hours. A report of the findings, conclusions and recommendations to Congress is due in early 1971. 

Enforcement actions were taken on several fronts, ranging from commodity misclassification in airfreight forwarding, through tariff violations 2

and air taxi regulation and reporting violations, to unauthorized air transportation. Complaints from the public received a large share of attention. But charter violations demanded by far the greatest amount of the Enforcement Bureau's time and the results overshadowed the rest of the Bureau's work. 

During the fiscal year the Bureau concluded three of the most extensive investigations in its history, all dealing with charter operations on the East and West coasts. A number of these actions continue into the new fiscal year.

A View of Industry 

This was the look of the industry at fiscal year's close: 

Revenue passenger-miles for the certificated route air carriers totaled 

128.9 billion, up 7.6 percent from the 119.8 billion of fiscal 1969. Of the total figure, local service accounted for 6.9 billion revenue passengermiles, an increase of 16.1 percent over the earlier fiscal year. Domestic trunkline carriers reported a total of 93.4 billion revenue passenger-miles, up 5.3 percent from fiscal 1969.

The certificated route industry 12-month rate of return was 3.52 percent as of March, 1970, down from 5.29 percent in the comparable 1969 period and still down markedly from the 10.5 percent determined by the Board to be a fair rate of return for the domestic trunkline carriers in the General Passenger Fare Investigation of 1960. The trunkline rate of return for fiscal 1970 was 4.1 percent, as compared with the 1969 rate of 5.1 percent. The local service carriers' fiscal 1970 rate of return was

DOMESTIC AVIATION ROUTE IMPROVEMENTS TO AID PUBLIC 

Many cities received new and improved air service and the air traveler was eased past certain congestion centers as a result of awards to carriers in major route proceedings.

Minneapolis and Milwaukee received vastly expanded authority to major points throughout the country in two cases, the Twin Cities- 

California Service Investigation and the Twin Cities-Milwaukee Long- 

Haul Investigation. Emphasizing new or improved competion, the CAB awarded new authority between Minneapolis and Los Angeles/San Francisco/Seattle/Portland on the West Coast. On the East Coast, Boston, Baltimore and New York were linked directly to Minneapolis by more nonstop carriers. Boston, Baltimore, New York, Seattle and Portland also were given new nonstop links with Milwaukee, a move that is expected to reduce congestion at Chicago's O'Hare Airport by enabling more

Milwaukee passengers to move directly to their destinations, bypassing 

O'Hare. Western Air Lines and Northwest Airlines received the Min neapolis-Los Angeles/San Francisco authority and also participated with United Air Lines and North Central Airlines in the other east-west awards.

Improved East-West Service 

Cities across the southern portion of the United States were joined by air as the Board focused on improved east-west service needs of the major cities in the Southern Tier Competitive Nonstop Investigation. Basically, the awards permitted new and improved competitive service. Atlanta, Miami, Houston and Dallas received new service to Los Angeles and San Francisco; Dallas was tied directly to Miami and Tampa, Braniff, Continental, Delta, Eastern, National, Northeast and Northwest Airlines and Airlift International shared in these awards.

A number of cities in the South made or strengthened air route ties with four major points on the nation's periphery. The North Carolina cities of Charlotte, Raleigh-Durham, and Greensboro-High Point were given new and improved service to the Chicago, Miami and New York areas in the North Carolina Points Service Investigation. The Board

spread the awards between Delta and United. New nonstop, single-plane authority also joined Birmingham and Huntsville, Alabama and

Memphis, Tennessee, on the one hand, with Los Angeles, on the other hand, in the Memphis/Huntsville/Birmingham-Los Angeles Service Investigation. United received the authority.

Effective July 1, 1969, air taxi operators were required to register with the CAB and to maintain a minimum level of liability insurance. During the fiscal year, 1,500 air taxi operators registered. Of this number, 168 were commuter air carriers performing either mail air services or scheduled air services on an individually ticketed basis. Commuter carriers also began filing required traffic and schedule data. 

The CAB amended Part 298, the economic regulation governing air taxi operations, to require commuter air carriers, air taxis interlining traffic with scheduled carriers, and air taxis that carry passengers in foreign air transportation to raise the limits of passenger liability established by the Warsaw Convention from $8,290 per passenger to $75,000 per passenger. This is the same limit which applies to most air carriers, including foreign air carriers, engaged in international air transportation. 

In addition, the CAB modified the insurance requirements of Part 298 by alleviating some of the problems encountered by air taxi operators in attempting to comply with the regulation. The new rule modified the "open-end" coverage requirement by authorizing the limitation of insurance coverage to specific aircraft declared in the policy of insurance rather than all operations of air taxi operators in air transportation. The 

CAB is requiring that the insurer "cover" substitute aircraft not owned by the insured when it is necessary to use substitute aircraft. 

In another proceeding the CAB instituted an investigation to determine whether the usual air taxi limitation of 12,500 pounds maximum certificated takeoff weight should be modified and, if so, in what respect. 

During the year the CAB issued nine exemptions to air taxi operators to use large aircraft, such as the DC-3, in specific geographic markets or in unique and special services.

ALASKAN SERVICE 

Hearings were held at six Alaskan cities in the Alaska Service Investigation as part of the CAB's comprehensive review of major route patterns serving Alaska. Realignment of routes, possible route transfers, additional service, and suspensions are being considered in this major proceeding. 

In connection with this proceeding the CAB permitted Western to suspend service at Cordova and Yakutat until 60 days after a decision is made in the investigation.

Labor Provisions of the Federal Aviation Act 

During the fiscal year seven third-party complaints were filed against air taxi operators, alleging violations of the labor provisions of the Federal Aviation Act which, among other things, require each air carrier to maintain rates of compensation and maximum hours of all pilots and copilots so as to conform with a 1934 decision of the National Labor 

Relations Board. 

SUPPLEMENTAL AIR CARRIERS 

As of June 30, 1970, 14 U.S. carriers held certificates from the CAB to provide supplemental air services. Geographic areas of permitted operations have been variously defined for these carriers. 

At the close of Calendar 1969, the U.S. carriers had assets of $564.9 million. Operating revenues for Fiscal 1970 amounted to $343.1 million. 

One carrier, Standard Airways, was not operating and was involved in a bankruptcy proceeding. In line with this, the CAB instituted an investigation to determine whether Standard's certificate of public convenience and necessity should be modified, suspended, or revoked for lack of financial and operational fitness. Shortly after the fiscal year closed the Board temporarily suspended Standard's certificate pending the outcome of the bankruptcy proceeding. 

In the licensing area, the Board is processing an omnibus case involving requests by 10 supplemental carriers for renewed or new transatlantic charter authority (Transatlantic Supplemental Charter Authority Re newal Case, Docket 20569). In this proceeding the Board also will consider a number of the transatlantic supplemental carriers' proposals for liberalizing the regulations governing their authorities. Carriers presently holding transatlantic authority are American Flyers, Capitol, Overseas 

National, Saturn, Trans International and World Airways. 

(Shortly after the end of the fiscal year the Board adopted a rule change which allows supplemental carriers to transport any number of 10

tour groups on the same aircraft if the aircraft is chartered to a single tour operator and if each group consists of more than 40 persons.) 

The CAB also had tentatively determined to amend its regulations to require supplemental carriers to maintain a cash escrow for the protection of customers' deposits with the carriers. An escrow agreement between a bank and an air carrier would not be effective until approved by the Board.

INTERCORPORATE AGREEMENTS 

The CAB reviewed and acted on 1041 intercarrier agreements affecting air transportation during the fiscal year. As in past years, major emphasis was placed on those multilateral agreements constituting concerted action by members of various associations of air carriers and foreign air carriers, such as the Air Traffic Conference of America and the International Air Transport Association. Emphasis also was placed on certain other agreements involving important antitrust and other substantial public interest 

Generally, the agreements reviewed by the CAB involved such matters as administration of the aforementioned associations, matters dealing with airport congestion, technical assistance to foreign airlines; equipment interchanges; passenger baggage and cargo practices; reservations and ticketing; interline traffic procedures; traffic documentation; passenger and cargo agency matters; airport usage and services; and a myriad of other subjects affecting air transportation.

For example, the CAB approved an agreement establishing a joint ATC/IATA insurance program designed to cover increased exposure caused by higher individual settlements, increased passenger loads on high capacity jet and supersonic aircraft, and greatly increased hull values; and an agreement among several domestic carriers establishing mittee to set specifications and evaluate proposals for certain Boeing 747 support equipment. 1 ¹City Investing later in the fiscal year withdrew its application.

The CAB approved, for an indefinite period, renewal of the Airline Mutual Aid Agreement (Docket 9977) for an amended agreement entered into later by the 10 carrier parties to the agreement-American 

Airlines, Braniff Airways, Continental Air Lines, Eastern Air Lines, Northwest Airlines, Pan American World Airways, Trans World Air lines, United Air Lines, and Western Air Lines. The amended agreement increased the amount of supplemental payments by nonstruck carriers to a struck carrier from the original 25 percent to 50 percent of the struck carrier's normal operating expense during the first two weeks of the strike, 45 percent for the third week, 40 percent for the fourth week and 35 percent for any period thereafter.

The CAB suspended for investigation revisions in air express rates and charges proposed by REA Express and airlines participating in air express tariffs, and it deferred action on an agreement between REA and the airlines to extend air express service beyond July 31 since the agreement was tied to the suspended tariffs. Simultaneously, the Board instituted an "Express Service Investigation" to explore the future role of express of priority cargo air service.

The CAB approved an inter-airline agreement increasing the number of departures from Chicago's Midway Airport from 38 to 83. But the

CAB warned that if carriers serving Chicago did not soon unilaterally choose to increase their Midway operations, the Board would consider an investigation to determine an appropriate solution to Chicago's airport problems, including the possibility of certificate amendments that could decrease the flow of connecting traffic through Chicago. Daily departures from Chicago's O'Hare Airport were averaging about 820.

The agreement increasing the number of Midway departures resulted from carrier and civic party discussions authorized by the Board earlier in the fiscal year.

Also, earlier in the fiscal year, the CAB authorized the airlines to hold discussions and agree to reduced frequencies to meet problems arising from the reduced capacity of the Air Traffic Control System resulting from the air traffic controllers' “sick-out."

TRAVEL AGENCY MATTERS 

As reported in previous annual reports, the CAB has no authority to regulate directly the selection and/or retention of travel agents by the airlines, or the relationships between such persons. However, the domstic and international airlines through their trade associations have developed over the years comprehensive rules to govern such matters. 

Since these rules involve concerted action by the airlines affecting air transportation, they must be filed with the CAB, and are subject to approval or disapproval in the public interest. 13

Two important matters affecting travel agents were under CAB consideration following oral argument. One involved the CAB's tentative findings with respect to an Air Traffic Conference (ATC) agreement pertaining to travel agents' commissions and establishing provisions dealing with ATC's process for the selection and retention of travel agents. The Board had said in its tentative findings that it believed the commission rate for point-to-point sales should be somewhere between seven percent and eight percent irrespective of the value of the ticket sold by a travel agent, and that certain of the new selection and retention standards should be disapproved.

The second proceeding before the Board at the close of the year was an ATC agreement among 19 air carrier members dealing with a common computerized automated-seat reservation system for travel agent usage. The two-way communication system (ATARS) would enable an agent to book and cancel reservations on all or a substantial number of airlines. Among the questions the Board has to resolve is whether CAB approval of the agreement will give ATARS an exclusive right to deal and lock out other suppliers of similar systems.

The CAB is, through the hearing process, determining whether the authority of the specialized used household goods airfreight forwarders should be renewed and whether any additional applications for this type of authority should be granted.

With the conclusion of the Motor Carrier-Air Freight Forwarder In vestigation and the decision on remand upheld by the Courts, the CAB is reviewing other applications of long-haul motor carriers of general commodities who seek airfreight forwarder authority. Air travel, including scheduled and nonscheduled, continues to increase in importance in relation to the total movement of passengers between the United States and foreign countries. (See Exhibit 1.) During the period from fiscal 1960 through 1970, the total number of international passengers moving by sea and air increased from 5.7 to 18.6 million, or 224.6 percent (excluding Canada). The number by air, however, increased 293.1 percent, from 4.5 to 18 million passengers, raising the air share percentage of the air-sea from 79.9 percent to 96.7 percent. 

The number of passengers traveling by sea has declined steadily since fiscal 1961; in fiscal 1970, it was only a little more than half the number reported in 1960.

Freight Traffic 

In calendar year 1969, a total of 1.2 billion freight (including express) revenue ton-miles were produced in international and territorial operations of the major international U.S. carriers (Braniff, Flying Tiger, Northwest, Pan American, Seaboard and Trans World). This figure compares to 1 billion freight revenue ton-miles in the previous year. As a part of the total revenue ton-miles produced in all categories of movementpassenger, freight, express and mail-freight (including express) accounted for 27.4 percent of the total revenue ton-miles in 1968 and 29.7 percent in 1969. In the period, 1959-1969, freight has been slowly, and noticeably in recent years, accounting for a larger share of the total revenue ton-miles generated by U.S. carriers in international and territorial operations.

The U.S. carrier share of U.S. foreign airborne trade, based on shipping weight data compiled by the Census Bureau, declined slightly 17

from 45.percent in calendar year 1968 to 44.7 percent in 1969 (See

Exhibit 9). Since 1962, the first year for which Census Bureau data on shipping weight became available, the overall record has been one of an increasing U.S. carrier share. From 1964 to 1965, the percentage increase, in the shipping weights of U.S. foreign airborne trade, at 43 percent, was the highest recorded percentage increase registered in the period 1962-1969 (See Exhibit 10). 

Europe was, in 1969 as in other years, the leading continent for U.S. foreign airborne trade, accounting for slightly more than half of the shipping weight total (See Exhibits 11 and 12). The continent of Africa was conspicuously small in terms of freight movement to and from the United States based on shipping weights.

PRESIDENT'S POLICY STATEMENT The White House issued a Statement of International Air Transporta tion Policy approved by the President June 22, 1970. It was developed by an interagency committee, which included CAB representatives, appointed to review existing policies and recommended changes. The last review was in 1962-63 and resulted in a policy statement released by the White 

House April 24, 1963. 

The new policy statement continues the present system of exchanging air transport rights with other countries on the basis of bilateral agreements embodying the Bermuda principles. 18

"In general," the statement reads, “under Bermuda principles each airline is free to decide the number of flights it will operate on agreed routes, subject to ex post facto review of its operations to assure that they conform to certain general principles: (1) the airlines have a fair and equal opportunity to compete; (2) the airlines of one country do not operate so as to affect unduly the operations of the airlines of the other country; (3) the air services bears a close relationship to public requirements; and (4) the primary objective of air services is to carry traffic to and from an airline's homeland."

The policy statement notes that both the United States and international air transportation have been well served by the liberal economic environment fostered by these principles. It calls for vigorous resistance to arbitrary restrictions imposed by other countries against United States carriers, and for the application by the United States of appropriate measures against the carriers of such countries. 

Charter Services and Market Development 

Charter services by scheduled and supplemental air carriers are considered as important means of developing the bulk market. The policy statement expresses the view that charter services by both classes of carriers have been useful in holding down fares and rate levels and in expanding passenger and freight markets. At the same time, the policy statement voices concern regarding substantial impairment of scheduled services. It sets out the need to take the necessary steps to prevent such impairment “. . . where necessary to avoid prejudice to the public interest." The policy statement calls further, in the matter of charter operations, for the regularization of landing rights for charter services on the basis of intergovernmental agreements. 

On the subject of fares and rates, the United States is called on in the policy statement to ". . . work for the broadest range of potentially profitable services designed to appeal to the broadest consumer market and based on the lowest cost of operating an efficient air transport system." Acceptance of the International Air Transport Association as the machinery for pricing scheduled services is continued, subject to the necessary safeguards and supplemented by increased informal direct exchange between governments. The policy statement also gives continued support of the objective of establishing IATA and non-IATA charter rates on a free competitive basis and recommends the grant of authority to the CAB to regulate international rates. 

The policy statement discusses at some length the subject of competition among and between U.S. carriers and foreign carriers. It views competition among air carriers, as in other areas of economic activity, as the means to improve the quality and variety of services to the public, to keep the level of prices down, and to enlarge the market for all carriers. 

It states that the United States should maintain a flexible policy on certificating competition among U.S. carriers on international routes, taking into account the public's need for additional or improved air services. The policy statement concurrently recognizes that the competi- 19

tion of foreign air carriers in the market-place provides significant benefits for the traveling public and the shipper.

Important Operating Capability 

The international all-cargo services of U.S.-flag carriers are viewed by the policy statement as an important national transportation capability serving commercial and military needs. It supports the present bilateral system of exchanging cargo rights as part of Air Transport Agreements with other countries, as opposed to a system of cargo-only bilaterals. It further states that cargo routing flexibility is a valuable operating right to both combination and cargo-only carriers and that the United States should anticipate the need for such right in negotiating new or amended agreement.

Elsewhere in the policy statement, the United States is called on (a) to insure certainty, speed, and sufficiency of recovery by the injured party in the matter of carrier liability, (b) to require foreign carriers of marginal financial resources to maintain minimum amounts of liability insurance, (c) to take all practical measures to facilitate international air movement, (d) to continue the existing and proposed U.S. policies on user charges, fees, and taxes, and (e) to carefully consider the potential effects of international air transportation on the balance of payments.

BILATERALS 

The United States has in force a total of 70 bilateral air transport agreements. (See Exhibit 13). These include 16 agreements based on an assumption of rights and obligations by former possessions of agreements in force between the United States and the respective mother countries. 

Bilateral air transport agreements serve as the basis for exchanging air rights with other countries, and provide the framework for managing the bulk of our commercial air relations with other countries, including the resolution of problems or disputes which may arise. 

In those circumstances where no agreement exists, as in the case of Argentina and the Philippines, air operations between the United States and the concerned countries are conducted on the basis of unilateral grants of operating authority.

Responsibility for bilateral relations between the United States and foreign countries is divided into four geographic areas: Area I, Canada, Mexico, the Caribbean, and Central and South America; Area II, comprising Greenland, Iceland, the United Kingdom and Ireland, Western and Northern Europe, and Eastern Europe, including the Soviet 

Union; Area III, Southern Europe, the Near and Middle East, and

Africa; and Area IV, the Far East and the Pacific Basin, including 

Australia, New Zealand, and Indonesia.

MOROCCO-An agreement with the Kingdom of Morocco was signed February 9, 1970. This is only the third formal Air Transport Agreement signed by the United States within Africa, the other agreements being in force by virtue of the assumption of ex-home country obligations.

INTERNATIONAL CIVIL AVIATION ORGANIZATION (ICAO) 17th Session of the Assembly 

On March 16, 1970, the Council of ICAO at the request of 10 contracting States (Austria, Belgium, Denmark, Federal Republic of Germany, The Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, Spain, Switzerland and the United Kingdom) decided to convene an extraordinary session of the Assembly to deal with the subject of security in international civil aviation. The 

17th Session (Extraordinary) held in Montreal, Canada commenced 

June 16, 1970 with 91 Member States, one noncontracting State (U.S.S.R.) and 12 international organizations in attendance. 

The CAB Chairman and other high-ranking Government officials attended this extraordinary session to demonstrate the importance which the United States Government attached to this meeting. A CAB staff member served on the U.S. delegation to the 17th Session of the ICAO 

Assembly. 

The 91 contracting States attending the Assembly passed numerous resolutions aimed at preventing or punishing unlawful attacks on international civil aviation. Some of the major actions of the Assembly were: a resolution condemning all acts of violence against civil aviation; a directive to the ICAO Council to convene the ICAO Legal Committee to prepare a new international convention on acts of violence other than hijacking; a request of Member States to adopt technical security measures covering passengers, baggage, mail and security of airport facilities and aircraft; a request of Council to obtain and analyze reports on hijacking, and to study amendment of the Chicago Convention to include a provision relating to hijacking. Other actions urged States to ratify the Tokyo Convention and enact national criminal laws making hijacking and sabotage a crime carrying severe penalties.

through June 3, 1970 at the ICAO headquarters in Montreal.

RATE REGULATION DOMESTIC PASSENGER FARES 

Airline fare matters wrote much of the air transport industry's fiscal 1970 journal and loomed as one of the important chapters in fiscal 1971. 

The CAB suspended proposed domestic fare increases, but permitted certain fare revisions, effective October 1, 1969, based on a cost-oriented fare structure formula. The formula, which produced industry-wide fare increases that raised revenues an average of 6.35 percent, was a first in the unpatterned history of air fares. 

Permission for the fare revisions was conditioned on the carriers' agreement by the end of January 1970, on the establishment of additional joint fares in markets where more than one carrier must be used, and on how to divide joint fare revenues equitably between long- and short-haul carriers.

Also in January, the CAB approved an interim agreement and instituted an investigation of joint fares and divisions which, later in the month, the Board consolidated into a general investigation of all elements of the domestic passenger-fare level and structure, the Domestic Passenger 

Fare Investigation, which will be decided in nine separate proceedingsthree rulemakings and six formal hearings-covering every major area of fare concern.

Speed Up Investigation The rulemaking proceedings deal with phase (1) Aircraft Deprecia tion, (2) Leased Aircraft, and (3) Deferred Federal Income Taxes.

Hearing procedures are under way in phases (4) Joint Fares, (5) Dis count Fares, (6) Load Factor and Seating Configurations, (7) Fare Level, (8) Rate of Return and (9) Fare Structure. The Board hopes to reduce substantially the time required to conclude the investigation through the use of these multiple proceedings. (Just after the close of fiscal 1970, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the 

District of Columbia determined that the order permitting the October 1969 fare increase amounted to ratemaking and was invalid and that tariffs filed on the basis of that order were unlawful. The Court's decision was in an appeal of the CAB's fare decision filed by Rep. John E. Moss and 31 other Members of Congress. The Court allowed the CAB to keep 39

the existing domestic fare structure in effect to October 15 while new lawful fares were being established. The Board emphasized it would allow full time for the public or any interested party to file complaints against the carrier tariff filings.)

In August 1969, the Board issued its opinion in the Standby Youth Fares "Young Adult" Fares Case, conducted pursuant to remand by the U.S. Court of Appeals. The CAB found that reduced standby and reservation fares for youths aged 12 through 21 were not unjustly discriminatory, but remanded the case to the examiner for further hearings to determine the reasonableness of the fares. 

Family Fares Included 

On remand by the U.S. Court of Appeals, the Board also instituted an investigation of family fares. This investigation was later consoliated with the standby Youth Fares “Young Adult” Fares Case. Both these investigations were later consolidated into the Discount Fares phase of the Domestic Passenger Fare Investigation, which also includes the carriers' Discover America excursion fares. All these promotional fares remain in effect pending completion of the investigation. 

The Board has under review the examiner's decision in the investiga tion of Group Inclusive Tour-Basing Fares to Hawaii. 

Formal hearing was completed with respect to intra-Hawaii fares, and the examiner's initial decision was issued at the close of the fiscal year. 

The examiner found that the increased fares which the carriers placed in effect on November 1, 1969, were proper. The Board is currently reviewing the examiner's decision.

The Board also instituted an investigation of time payment plans offered by the various air carriers.

A subject area audit program, conceived in fiscal 1967 primarily for auditing the large nonsubsidized carriers, has proven highly satisfactory. 

Audit findings have been of higher quality and the audits have required less time for each carrier.

An improved version of the subject area program is now being developed for application to all carrier classes. It will provide for an indepth review and evaluation of carriers' accounting system and procedures rather than verification of account balances and will be applied on a progressive basis in order to review the whole scope of a carrier's accounting and reporting over a two- to three-year cycle. As part of the examination, CAB auditors will review the public accountants' working papers and reports. The new approach makes audits more timely and ensures that most carriers get some coverage each year. 

Difficult Areas 

The progressive type of audit, however, still will not permit adequate monitoring of commuter air carriers and airfreight forwarders; nor will the present staff size allow sufficient auditing to evaluate airline diversification problems, now acquiring paramount importance. Maintaining the integrity of the air transport entity, in combination with various nontransport operations and conglomerate organizational entities, thus grows increasingly difficult.

COST-FINDING AND STATISTICAL SERVICES 

The CAB cost-finding program continued development work on a computer-based integrated costing system which will help meet the agency's specialized costing needs. Final development of the system was temporarily deferred in the latter part of the fiscal year when much of the staff was assigned to the extensive costing requirements of the Domestic Passenger Fare Investigation. Still, much of the research that went into developing an integrated costing system found immediate use in the refined and expanded costing methodologies advanced in the Domestic Passenger Fare Investigation. Primary costing effort was in the investigation's fare level and fare structure phases but the costing complexities touched practically all phases. 

As the U.S. certificated route carriers introduced wide-bodied jet aircraft in January 1970, the CAB staff initiated a quarterly report to monitor in detail the operating cost and performance characteristics of these new aircraft. Because of their widespread economic impact on the airline industry in the decade ahead, the cost and performance behavior of these new aircraft must be continually tracked as they emerge on the airline scene.

A new CAB quarterly statistical publication, Schedule Arrival Performance in the Top Markets by Carrier, produced during the fiscal year on a recurrent basis, shows on-time performance of the certificated route air carriers in selected markets.

The modernization of the passenger origin-destination survey of airline passenger traffic was completed, and data for the first six calendar quarters on the new survey basis have been issued. 

CARRIER ACCOUNTS 

The Board adopted a regulation calling for automation of the CAB statistical data collection and processing system. Through the use of magnetic tapes and punched cards, the system provides for faster, more efficient communication of traffic data between the industry and the 48

CAB. It also will significantly enlarge the scope of data available for regulatory purposes. The Board also issued standard practice letters and general clarification for use of the new system. 

At the same time, efforts are being directed at modernizing standards and structures of the financial accounting and reporting regulations and at realignment of financial reports to facilitate freight, mail and passenger cost findings.

New statistical reporting requirements for air taxi operators who carry mail and offer scheduled air transport service (commuter air carriers) became effective. The Board also issued an interpretation to the industry establishing uniform accounting and reporting by the certificated route carriers for transactions associated with substitute service provided by air taxi operators under CAB-approved agreements. 

Reflecting the changing profile of the industry's route pattern, the CAB on January 22, 1970 adopted a rule which broadened and clarified the agency's reporting definitions and designations for domestic, territorial and international operations. The term “domestic" now embraces the 

50 states. By screening out domestic results related to Alaska and Hawaii, the carriers' international reports are made more useful. 

Consistent With Industry 

The Board also promulgated two other rules to keep its accounting and reporting requirements uniform and consistent with industry practices. One of the amendments requires annual verification of the carriers' passenger revenue accounting practices and provides for a more consistent statement of traffic liability. The other amendment, which deals with ferry flight statistics, was formulated to achieve uniformity in the reporting of statistical data related to flights made to position aircraft for service.

To further maintain uniform standards and accounting practices and to keep abreast of changing conditions affecting the industry, the CAB issued several interpretation letters. One of importance dealt with the reporting of investment tax credits under both the immediate flowthrough and deferral methods of accounting. Another of these letters asked the carriers to submit selected monthly financial data for review and evaluation in view of the present economic conditions of the airlines, generally, and the critical liquidity situation of certain carriers. 

Consistent with the CAB's duties to protect the public interest (which includes stockholder and rate payer interests as well as the interests of the traveling and shipping public) the Board proposed a rule to assure its receipt of complete and accurate data from the air carriers relative to their stockholders and to implement the new reporting obligations of some stockholders, as required by Public Law 91-62.

The staff is formulating a comprehensive system of accounts and reports for airfreight forwarders which would enable the CAB to get meaningful information from this growing segment of the industry on the forwarders' role in the overall transport picture. 

Also under consideration, as a result of the trend toward increased 49

diversification of air carrier activities, is a proposed rule that prescribes specific accounting regulations on multiactivity transactions and provides for disclosure of associated matters of substance in the carriers' reports to the CAB.

Two questionnaires were circulated to the air carriers, one to obtain information concerning the significance of carrier credit practices to the total economic well-being of the industry, and the other to obtain information on the scope and magnitude of air carrier relationships to diversified business activities. 

DATA PROCESSING 

Automatic data processing (ADP) continued to grow in importance in expediting the work of the CAB. 

The resources of the CAB's ADP facility have become important operational elements in the Board's statistical information program which includes collection, verification, processing and dissemination of airline industry facts. As the amount of data and the expertise of the staff have grown, so has the facility's leverage grown in the scope of application. 

The ADP facility work programs now are mainly the continued acquisition and processing of new and previously available data banks of airline industry information, the recurrent and ad hoc accumulation of that information for dissemination, and the extension of ADP speed and economies into the Board's analytical processes. Emphasis continued on dissemination and servicing of statistical needs. 

Domestic and international charter traffic movement data was added during the year, passenger origin and destination survey production was improved and recurrent aircraft traffic statistics were made more timely. 

Additional Sources 

System planning and design and developmental programming efforts to acquire, improve or extend information sources were aimed toward (1) revision of the basic traffic data reporting to include both detailed segment and air carrier summary information; (2) standardization of data used in calculating all airport-to-airport distances, and (3) construction of data banks of domestic local passenger rate tariffs and operating authorities and conditions. When these additional sources become available, they will provide new and added detail for ADP-supported analyses. 

The addition of another information system and its use with earlier acquired systems expanded and expedited employment of the ADP facility. The use of punched cards was added to a terminal used by the 

CAB, which is connected to the U.S. Department of Agriculture computer, and allowed new computational work to be developed. Many timesharing programs now can be processed on the Board's central computer if needed.

During the year, the agency began to automate textual information, enabling it to prepare Board decisions on a magnetic tape typewriter and 50

have the Government Printing Office use the tape to set type automatically for the bound volumes of decisions. The tapes are retained to accumulate Board decisions for possible later information retrieval and ADP processing techniques. 

The Mark IV user system is operational and is being used by the analytical staff.

Work Transferred to NTSB

ECONOMIC ENFORCEMENT 

In all categories the workload of the Bureau of Enforcement increased substantially in fiscal 1970. In addition, the Bureau devoted a substantial part of its manpower to conducting the most extensive investigation in CAB enforcement history, involving chartering organizations, travel agents, domestic and foreign carriers engaged in charter operations over the North Atlantic from the West and East Coasts of the United States. 

Launched in April 1969, the investigations were followed by nonpublic hearings before CAB examiners and required almost the full time of the Bureau's Investigation Division and a major part of the time of its Legal Division. Over 50,000 documents were copied and about 10,000 questionnaires were mailed to charter participants. 

By fiscal year's end, the Bureau had filed complaints against 65 parties, alleging violations of the Board's charter regulations. On January 23, 1970, the Bureau filed complaints against three supplemental and two foreign charter air carriers, eight travel agencies, 14 chartering organizations and 18 individuals, all operating on the West Coast. The CAB also has issued cease and desist orders against three individuals and two travel agencies. On June 25, 1970, the Bureau filed similar complaints against two supplemental air carriers, two U.S. trunkline carriers and one foreign air carrier, seven travel agencies, two chartering organizations and six individuals, all operating on the East Coast. 

It is principally charged that the carriers, contrary to their tariffs and the limitations of their operating authority, carried members of the general public under the guise of operating pro rata charter flights. 

Other allegations include rebating, the violation of Board regulations, and engagement in unfair and deceptive practices. The chartering orga nizations and travel agents are alleged to have participated in the sale of seats on flights to the general public under the pretense of pro rata charter transportation, and in many cases to have acted as unauthorized indirect air carriers.

In a separate investigation, the CAB issued an order to cease and desist against a New York resident who had solicited individual students in various colleges for purported pro rata charter flights. 

The rest of the CAB enforcement program involved five other categories: unfair and deceptive practices, unauthorized air transporta- 54

COURT ACTIONS 

XE Litigation during the fiscal year for the most part consisted of direct statutory review of CAB orders in the U.S. Courts of Appeals. However, one suit was instituted in the District Court against the Board Members, and on two other occasions litigants who had been unsuccessful in appeals against the Board in the courts of appeals, field petitions for certiorari in the Supreme Court. In the latter two instances the Supreme Court refused to review the lower courts' decision in favor of the Board. 

Twenty-eight proceedings were initiated against the Board during the fiscal year. This compares with 11 in the preceding year. At the close of the fiscal year, 15 petitions for review were disposed of and 20 other petitions were pending. In the preceding year there were eight petitions disposed of and seven others pending.

Pending at the close of fiscal 1969 were two cases in the Court of 

Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit challenging CAB orders in the Washington-Baltimore Helicopter Service Investigation.

In Na tional Capital Airlines, Inc. v. C.A.B., 419 F. 2d 668 (C.A.D.C. 1969) 

the Court affirmed the award of a temporary certificate and exemption authority to Washington Airways, Inc., authorizing helicopter service in the Washington-Baltimore area. National Capital Airlines, an applicant for this authorization, unsuccessfully sought further review of this decision in the Supreme Court, 398 U.S. 908 (1970). In Palisades Citizens Association, et al. v. C.A.B., 420 F. 2d 188 (C.A.D.C. 1969) the Court affirmed the Board's order denying intervention as a matter of right to seven individuals and two citizens' associations in the same Helicopter Service Investigation. These "concerned citizens" alleged a substantial interest in the reduction of noise, the problems of air pollution and the possible safety hazard which might result incident to the institution of the contemplated area helicopter service. The Court held that the denial of intervention as a formal party amounted to a reduction of the petitioner's status in form only and that petitioner's interests had been adequately protected under Rule 14 (14 C.F.R. 302.14). The Court also found, significantly, that questions of environmental impact are proper “public interest" questions to be considered in the CAB's certification inquiry. 

For the second time the Second Circuit was presented with a challenge to the Board's order granting the applications of three long-haul truckers or their subsidiaries to act as airfreight forwarders for a five-year trial period. In the earlier case, ABC Air Freight Company v. C.A.B., 391 F. 65

2d (C.A. 2, 1968) the Court had vacated and remanded to the 

Board for resolution of the ambiguity whether the Board had established a policy of entry for all truckers who wanted to act as airfreight forwarders or merely granted the applications that were before it. In the second challenge to the Board's decision, ABC Air Freight Company v. C.A.B., 419 F. 2d 154 (C.A. 2, 1969) the Court held the ambiguity was resolved and that the Board might initiate this properly controlled experiment in the authorization of truckers as airfreight forwarders, with the limitation on numbers and the reporting requirements an experiment would be expected to entail. The petition for review was denied and the 

Supreme Court declined to review the decision further. 397 U.S. 1,066

LEGISLATIVE DEVELOPMENTS LEGISLATION ENACTED The Airport and Airway Development Act of 1970 (Public Law 91258, approved May 21, 1970) is a measure of great importance to the aviation industry. It provides, in substitution for the Federal Airport 

Act, large-scale Federal assistance for expansion and improvement of the nation's airport and airway system. To provide additional revenue for the financing of the Federal assistance, the Act imposes new and increased aviation user charges, including, among others, an eight-percent passenger-ticket tax, a five percent waybill tax, and a $3 charge on international passengers departing the United States. In order to insure that the aviation user charges are expended only for the expansion and improvement of the airport and airway system, an “Airport and Airway

Trust Fund" is established, into which such user charges are deposited. 

In light of the urgent need for improvement in the airport and airway system, the CAB urged the enactment of appropriate legislation, and testified in support of bills (H.R. 12374 and S. 2437) leading to enactment of the Act.

Another measure enacted at the beginning of the fiscal year which directly affects the functions and activities of the CAB is Public Law 91-62, approved August 20, 1969. The law requires CAB approval of the acquisition by any person of control of an air carrier as of August 5, 1969. The CAB is authorized to exempt any acquisition of control of a non-certificated air carrier from the approval requirement to the extent that such may be in the public interest. 

Unless the CAB finds otherwise, any person owning beneficially 10 percent or more of the voting securities or capital of an air carrier is presumed to be in control of the carrier. This law also requires any person owning, beneficially or as trustee, more than five percent of any class of the capital stock or capital of an air carrier to submit annually, and at such other times as the Board may require, a description of the stock or other interest owned and the amount. 

A third measure involving the Board is Public Law 91-190, approved January 1, 1970, designed to ensure that the Federal Government use "all practicable means" to maintain and improve the quality of the human environment. The measure directs all agencies to “include in every recommendation or report on proposals for legislation and other 76

major Federal actions significantly affecting the quality of the human environment" a detailed statement evaluating the proposal in terms of environmental impact. Agency statutory authority, regulations, policies and procedures also would be revised as necessary to conform to the purposes of the Act.

MATTERS PRESENTED TO THE CONGRESS 

The CAB presented testimony in regard to one aspect of the postal reform bills (H. R. 4, H. R. 13124 and H. R. 11750). The CAB opposed provisions which would allow the postal authorities to contract with certificated air carriers to transport mail at rates other than those fixed by the Board.

Other matters in which the CAB testified dealt with those relating to the Board's operations and appropriations; a proposal for the creation of a tariff-filing mechanism for establishing mail rates; the adequacy of air service to smaller communities; termination of the Washington- 

Baltimore Airport Investigation; the Board's rulemaking proceeding concerning overseas military personnel charters; the establishment of minimum Military Airlift Command (MAC) rates; certification of airfreight forwarders; and aviation problems in California.

Also, the CAB submitted approximately 57 reports on legislation to the Congress or to the Bureau of the Budget for coordination and subsequent submission to the Congress.

LEGISLATIVE PROPOSALS 

The CAB is required by the Federal Aviation Act to include in its annual report such recommendations for additional legislation as it believes to be necessary for discharging its regulatory functions. The 

CAB's present legislative proposals for 1971 are encompassed in the following program. The CAB also has under consideration various other legislative proposals. 

1. Depreciation Accounting 

One legislative proposal if enacted would erase the regulatory gap caused by court rulings that the CAB lacks authority to regulate the depreciation accounting practices of air carriers. 

At present, the carriers are free to select their own depreciation practices and employ methods which lack uniformity. Thus, some carriers have improved their reported profits by changing depreciation practices, while others depreciate their entire aircraft fleet or particular aircraft over periods different from those used by other airlines. The result is that the CAB cannot readily obtain reliable financial data needed to conduct its regulatory activities. Also, the public cannot rely on the carrier's reports portraying the carriers' true financial condition. The CAB believes that it should have the authority, possessed by comparable agencies, to regulate depreciation accounting (S. 1172, H.R. 8668). 77

2. Consolidation of Certain CAB Proceedings 

Legislation is needed which will strengthen and clarify the CAB's procedural powers with respect to the consolidation of proceedings pending before it. One of the most persistent problems the Board has encountered in route proceedings has been the contention of applicants, based on the doctrine of the Ashbacker case (Ashbacker Radio Corp. v. 

FCC, 326 U.S. 327 (1945)), that they are entitled as a matter of legal right to consolidation of certain applications with such proceedings. 

The CAB is not opposed to the principle that mutually exclusive applications must be disposed of on simultaneous consideration. However, the application of this doctrine has in many cases resulted in protracted delays by reason of interlocutory appeals from consolidation orders.

The CAB believes that its consolidation orders should not be subject to interlocutory appeal but should be subject to court review at the conclusion of the proceeding. The CAB recommends that legislation be enacted which will bar interlocutory appeals and clarify the guidelines for consolidation (S. 1174, H.R. 8667). 

3. Ineligibility of Air Carriers for Subsidy 

Another CAB legislative proposal would amend the Federal Aviation

Act to withdraw subsidy eligibility from carriers who have become selfsufficient. Only one of the trunklines has received subsidy during the last five years, and none receives it now. Other carriers have and will continue to pass the developmental stage where they need Federal financial aid to operate. Nonetheless, those carriers remain eligible under the statute to apply for subsidy.

Although the CAB might decline to grant such assistance, the carriers' continued eligibility may place the Federal treasury under a major contingent liability. 

To alert the industry that it is expected to rely on its own resources, the CAB supports legislatior withdrawing subsidy eligibility from trunklines and most other carriers not now receiving subsidy, and making operations conducted by presently subsidized carriers ineligible for such assistance after a five-year subsidy-free period (S. 1244, H.R. 8669) . 

4. Repayment of Charges in Excess of Tariff Rates 

Legislation is needed which would permit the CAB to issue appropriate remedial orders in enforcement proceedings directing the repayment of charges in excess of those in lawfully filed tariffs. While the CAB may entertain complaints against overcharges, it is not authorized, in specific terms, to require repayment. Passengers desiring to collect the overcharges must resort to the courts. In most instances, and particularly in the case of group travel, this is not practicable because of the slight amounts involved for each passenger. The aggregate amount involved for each group, however, could represent a sizeable sum which the CAB should be empowered to require a carrier to repay to passengers (S. 1241). 78

5. Regulation of Rates and Practices in Foreign Air Transportation 

The CAB should be given comprehensive legislative authority to regulate rates and fares in foreign air transportation. The Federal Aviation Act does not authorize the CAB to regulate directly the charges for international air transportation except for a limited power to prevent discrimination. The CAB's only ability to influence international rate and fare levels is through its power to withhold approval of rate-fixing agreements to which U.S. air carriers may be parties. As a practical matter, the effective power to control rates for air services between the United States and foreign countries is in the hands of the foreign countries, with neither the U.S. Government nor U.S. carriers being in a position to bargain on equal terms in fixing rates. Legislation has been drafted. 

6. CAB Court Representation 

Legislation is needed which would grant the CAB the right to representation through its own counsel. The judicial review provisions of the 

Federal Aviation Act do not expressly provide that the CAB may participate in court proceedings through its own counsel as a matter of right. 

This right would be comparable to that exercised by other independent regulatory agencies. Legislation will be drafted. 

7. Notice of Tariff Changes 

A more recent CAB legislative proposal would amend the Federal Aviation Act so as to require a carrier to give at least 60 days' notice of any proposed tariff change and to require the Board, in the event that it decides to suspend a tariff, to do so at least 15 days prior to the time when it would otherwise become effective. The present 30 days' notice period does not allow the public sufficient time to learn of tariff proposals and for time to file complaints. Often the Board is unable to resolve the question of suspension until a few days before the effective date of the proposed tariff. Consequently, the traveling public, shippers, and carriers can be, and have been, inconvenienced by suspensions occurring immediately before the effective date of a proposed tariff. Under the CAB proposal, the Board would have a 45-day period in which to consider complaints that request suspension. The public and carriers. would then be given at least 15 days' advance notice as to whether a tariff will become effective or not. Legislation has been drafted. 79
