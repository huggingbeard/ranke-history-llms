THE YALE UNIVERSITY LIBRARY GAZETTE VOLUME VIII JANUARY 1934 NUMBER 3 The Yale Robinson Crusoe. 

THE recent gift from a member of the Yale Library Associates adds to the treasures in the University Library the first edition, in three volumes, of Daniel Defoe's masterpiece, Robinson Crusoe, and further enriches an already great collection of 

Defoe's works and Defoeana. 

Over a span of years covering the period from 1689, the year which saw the publication of what is probably his first recognized tract, Reflections Upon the Late Great Revolution, Written by a Lay-Hand in the Country, For the Satisfaction of some Neighbours, until 1730, the year preceding his death, when he published An Effectual Scheme for the Immediate Preventing of Street Robberies and Suppressing the other Disorders of the Night, Defoe wrote, usually anonymously, nearly four hundred political and trade pamphlets, essays on the social and economic conditions of his time, moral treatises, poetical satires, and stories of adventure. He contributed to or edited nineteen periodicals appearing from time to time between 1704 and 1729, the most important of which was the Review. Defoe was its sole editor. In its early existence, which began on February 19, 1704, the Review was issued as a weekly newspaper, but when it ceased circulation on June 11, 1713, with Defoe's laconic "exit Review," it was appearing tri-weekly.

In addition, the extant letters from Defoe's pen number close to a hundred. They are addressed chiefly to the Whig and 

Tory leaders, Sidney, first Earl of Godolphin, who became Lord 

Treasurer for a third term in 1702, and Robert Harley, first

Earl of Oxford, at one time Chancellor of the Exchequer; to 

Charles Spencer, third Earl of Sunderland, who rivaled Harley

Up until 1864 when the attention of the late William Lee, one of the greatest of Defoe's biographers, was first directed to the Mercurius Politicus, Dormer's News Letter, and Nathaniel 

Mist's Weekly Journal by the discovery of the letters just mentioned from Daniel Defoe to Charles De La Faye, Defoe's earlier biographers, George Chalmers (1785), Walter Wilson (1830), William Hazlitt, the younger (1840), and John Forster (1855), generally supposed that in 1715 Defoe had written his last political essay. He had by that year suffered three imprisonments and two business failures involving enormous sums, his first imprisonment and his second bankruptcy being traced directly to the writing in 1702 of his best known pamphlet, The Shortest Way with the Dissenters, a masterpiece of irony. After 1715, no longer the political propagandist, Defoe was supposed to have turned from the arena of political and religious strife between Whigs and Tories, High Churchmen and Dissenters, to a leisure that permitted his active spirit, his wit, and his genius to create the immortal Robinson Crusoe, Roxana, Captain Singleton, and Moll Flanders.

Consequently, though the ninety-nine titles in Chalmers's list were augmented to the "183 certain, and 52 attributed works," of Hazlitt's list, the catalogues of Defoe's works appended to these and the intervening biographies failed to include a quantity of tracts laid at Defoe's door by his contemporaries, Abel Boyer and John Toland among others. With the discovery of the part Defoe played in the conduct of the newspapers just mentioned, William Lee, rejecting thirty titles from the list prefixed by Wilson and twelve from Hazlitt's list of those believed by him to be genuine, though retaining seven of those Hazlitt had rejected, published in 1869 a list containing

Through the patient investigations made by such men as James Crossley, the great Manchester book collector, and George A. Aitken, author and editor, of a past generation, and the more recent work of such scholars as Professor Emeritus 

William P. Trent, of Columbia University, Professor Charles 

E. Burch, of Howard University, Professor John Robert 

Moore, of Indiana University, and Professor Arthur W. Secord, of the University of Illinois, the list of Defoe's individual works, comprising a bibliography that even now probably fulfils Lee's prophecy, has reached that amazing number of nearly four hundred separate items.

While many of the titles included in this total are far rarer than a first edition of Robinson Crusoe, and while many of them exist in only a single known copy, yet for the universal literary interest attached to what has been called the first English novel, and for the bibliographical interest attached to all copies of the first edition, a set of the three parts of Robinson Crusoe must be ranked first in any Defoe collection. 

Written when its author was fifty-nine years old, The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe was published on April 25, 1719, in an edition of, perhaps, twelve or fifteen hundred copies. It bears the imprint of William Taylor at the Sign of the Ship in Paternoster Row, one of the best known and most highly respected publishing booksellers of the early eighteenth century. The truth of the contemporary story that Defoe hawked the manuscript of Robinson Crusoe about the streets of London before he could find a publisher is much to be doubted, for as William Lee remarks: "I think the statement very improbable, as the talents of the author were too well known and appreciated for anything from his pen to go begging." A year earlier Taylor had printed for Defoe A Continuation of Letters Written by a Turkish Spy at Paris, and one may conjecture that the association continued, for several of Defoe's

works of the years immediately following, especially Christian Conversation in Six Dialogues (1720) and Memoirs of a Cavalier (1720), were also published by William Taylor. 

The immediate popularity of Defoe's masterpiece, which according to tradition brought a fortune for its printer, is indicated by the fact that the eighth of August saw the publication of the second printing of a fourth edition. A fifth edition followed in 1720, and in 1722 William Taylor issued two printings of a sixth edition. There were innumerable piracies, as well as several abridgments, many of these issued in 1719. Robinson 

Crusoe also appeared in a newspaper of the time, The Original 

London Post, or Heathcote's Intelligence, between October 7, 

1719, and October 19, 1720, in all probability the first appearance of an English novel in serial form. National popularity soon became universal. In 1720 the work was translated into 

French, German, and Dutch; into Italian in 1731; and subsequently into most of the languages of the civilized world. 

On August 20, 1719, William Taylor published the Second Part, The Farther Adventures of Robinson Crusoe. Perhaps 

Defoe misconstrued the success of Part I, and "the charm that has bewitched the world," as Charles Lamb expresses it, "of that solitary island isolation," and thinking that the public wanted more adventures, took Crusoe back to his desert island and its growing colony, thence into China, and by way of Tartary and Siberia once again to England. Robinson, the boy who ran away from home, returns to London in 1705 an old man of seventy-two.

The Third Part, Serious Reflections during the Life and 

Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe, a group of moral essays with the addition of the hero's name to help its sale, was issued on August 6, 1720. The "Publisher's Introduction" to this volume, prefacing the text, contains Taylor's own confession to his profits: "Not a dog has wag'd his Tongue at the

Work itself, nor has a Word been said to lessen the Value of it, but which has been the visible Effect of Envy at the good Fortune of the Bookseller." The naïveté of this is at once apparent,

This linking of two great publishing houses was more than one of mere location. At William Taylor's death on May 5, 1724, William Innys and John Osborn, his intimate friends and business associates, were appointed his executors. On the fourth of August, 1724, they sold to Thomas Longman, later to become Osborn's son-in-law, "all the household goods and books, both bound and in sheets, according to valuation" belonging to Taylor; and a contemporary newspaper, the London Journal for 

January 16, 1725, contains this intimate news item: "on Tuesday last (14th) Mr. Innys, an eminent Bookseller in St. Paul's churchyard, was marry'd to the Relict of Mr. Taylor, late of 

Paternoster Row, and worth thirty thousand Pounds."

Less than a month later, on February 3, 1725, occurred the sale of Taylor's copyrights. Entry no. 35 in the Sale Catalogue, dividing the copyright into two parts, reads: "Robinson Crusoe, in 2 vol. 800 and 12mo, with cuts 10% to be paid [presumably to the author] for every 1000 of the first Part and 10£, 10s. more when every 1000 of the 2d Part is put to printing, and 5£ more when 500 of the 2d vol. are sold; just going to the Press." Entry no. 36 for Serious Reflections names "the whole," a marked copy of the catalogue which has survived informing us that one half of the copyright of the first two parts fetched 15£ 155., the other half 15, but that the copyright of the Third Part was not sold.

It is a pity that no records of the association between Defoe and Taylor have come down to us. One would like to examine the files of Taylor's correspondence and his account books; one would like to see his presses and type fonts, for the publisher of Robinson Crusoe was also a printer. Robinson Crusoe is not a well-printed book. It appeared in an age of book-pirating, inferior type, and careless composition, in a quarter century that had seen the London booksellers twice vainly applying to Parliament for a new Licensing Act to protect their rights. Its text is filled with many typographical errors, such as misprints, transposed type, and broken letters. Indeed, it is these very errors which make copies of the first edition of Robinson Crusoe so interesting bibliographically and assist the collector in detecting imperfect copies. We recall that before the middle of August, 1719, there were six separate printings of Part I. Numbered uniformly throughout, the pages of these editions are practically identical line for line and catchword for catchword. The texts of many rebound copies of the first edition contain leaves inserted from one of the later editions to make the copy complete. It is, therefore, not alone the extraordinary immediate popularity of Robinson Crusoe, resulting literally in its being read to pieces, which accounts for the rarity of the first edition. 

Not many copies of this world-book have survived in as perfect condition as that of the copy which comes to the Yale Library. Probably less than ten exist in libraries, or in private collections, bound in the panelled calf so typical of the period, with the frontispiece and maps of each volume unmarred, and with all the book advertisements intact.

As all the mistakes were quite probably corrected while the sheets were passing through the press, it would depend on how the sheets were collated by the binder to what category any special copy belongs. That the Yale copy has a semicolon after "London" on the title-page, the corrected "apply" for "apyly," but the uncorrected "Pilate" for "Pilot" is comparatively of little importance. The Yale copy is unique in that there is to be seen on the title-page of Volume I the signature of its first owner, "John Lord," and the date, "April, 1719," together with the price paid, five shillings. This inscription is conclusive proof that here is a copy of the first edition and one that passed over William Taylor's counter during the early days of sale before the publishing of the second edition on May 12. 

The gift of this scarce and interesting book from a member of the Yale Library Associates adds another rarity to an important special collection in the Yale Library. Of the four hundred separate items now more or less definitely ascribed to Defoe, the Library possesses more than three hundred. Of the nineteen periodicals to which Defoe contributed, the Library has complete runs of eight covering the months during which Defoe was associated with them. Most important among these are the files of Applebee's Weekly Journal, Fog's Weekly 

Journal, the Protestant Post-Boy, and the Whitehall Evening

Post. Of that rare periodical, the Review, the Library has five of the nine volumes and parts of another. Only one first edition of the famous romances is lacking. To comment on many of the scarcer trade and controversial pamphlets found separately and among the bound "College Pamphlets" would be to call attention to an ephemeral tract which exists only in the single copy, or to one of only two or three known copies. 

When one praises the scope that is indicated by these statistics, one should not forget that the completeness is due, at least in part, to the dispersal of the books assembled by some of the earlier scholars and biographers previously mentioned; to

1842, to William Lee, whose library was sold on July 30, 1883; and to James Crossley, whose books were dispersed in 

1884 and 1885. Sale catalogues still exist to bear witness to the extraordinary industry of a past generation of literary historians and antiquarians in gathering together the works of the first English pamphleteer and novelist, Daniel Defoe. 

Another possible source from which the Yale and other present-day collections may have been augmented was the forty-seven-page catalogue of Whitmore and Fenn (1829) attesting to "an extensive collection of the writings of Daniel Defoe." From Walter Wilson's library, too, the entire Defoe collection comprising "more than 154 volumes and 208 pieces" was sold in July, 1847, and found its way to America. A contemporary account of the sale tells us that the purchaser was "a Mr. Rich" (probably Obadiah Rich, the well-known bibliographer and one of the founders of the Boston Athenæum) and complains "many of our choicest old books are being transported across the Atlantic." Lastly should be mentioned the collection of Henry Labouchère, in seventy-seven large bound volumes, an important unit of the collection formed by Professor 

William P. Trent now in the Boston Public Library. 

To praise the assiduity of the collectors of a bygone day is not to say, however, that Chalmers, Wilson, Lee, and the others succeeded in housing in their own libraries all the works which they assigned to Defoe in their bibliographies; or that these lists, even as far as they went, were accurate. The collection of George 

Chalmers contained but sixty-seven of Defoe's works. William 

Lee's carefully annotated copy of Walter Wilson's threevolume work, Memoirs of the Life and Times of Daniel Defoe (1830), mentioned in Lee's Sale Catalogue (1883) and now in private hands, indicates that Wilson did not own seventy of the 210 items he listed, that of these seventy Lee had twenty-nine, and that of Wilson's entire list Lee possessed 144. Yale's collection of Defoe's works and Defoeana must take rank with the collections in the Bodleian Library and the British Museum, with the Wrenn Library collection, and with the Trent Collection. There is an association between the two 

New England collections, for Professor Trent did much of his work in the Old Library at Yale, and many of the scarcer items catalogued under Defoe's name bear Professor Trent's attribution. The generosity and the comradeship of scholarship need no better proof.

If walls could speak there would assuredly be many a report of conversations between Professor Trent, the late Professor Thomas R. Lounsbury, B.A. 1859, long an active member of the Library Committee, and Professor Addison Van Name, B.A. 1858, the scholarly and businesslike head of the Old Library for so many years. It was when the Library was under the direction of John Christopher Schwab, B.A. 1886, and when the present Librarian was Reference Librarian, that Professor

Trent did much of his work with Defoe's trade pamphlets; and the handwriting of the late Professor Franklin B. Dexter, B.A. 

1861, is too easily recognized not to assert that he, also, had a share in many of the ascriptions made. Through the important

Yet large as is the debt of the Yale Library and of the Yale special collections to the past, and large as it is to the present generation, it should be even larger to those who are now responsible for the future. As the Library has grown over nearly two centuries, so must it grow in the years to come if its service to the University and to scholarship is best to be rendered. The part played by the Yale Library Associates and by all friends of the Library in adding to its collections of books and manuscripts cannot be overestimated.
