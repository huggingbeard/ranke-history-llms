PART ONE OUR GENERAL OUTLOOK ON ARITHMETIC* Chapter 1. Getting a Broader View of the Subject WHAT IS NUMBER? 

In response to the above query, What is number? it is not enough to say, "Such things as 2, 3, 7 are numbers."

There is a demand upon us to deal a little more fundamentally with this question, to inquire more fully into the nature of number. Such an inquiry is commonly regarded as an excursion into "philosophy," but it seems best to avoid this term and refer to the inquiry as a procedure of analysis. 

IMPORTANCE OF A THEORY OF NUMBER 

The purpose of such an analysis is to formulate, if possible, an acceptable theory regarding the nature, genesis, and function of number in human experience. For those who think that an inquiry of this type bakes no bread in the general economy of human affairs, there is a ready answer. 

One's fundamental notions of things affect one's behavior towards them, one's action in regard to them. The fundamental notions of educational leaders about the general place of number in the experience of man furnish the platform from which they talk to us about the teaching of arithmetic. When one of these leaders suggests that the objective of arithmetic is the cultivation of skill, the mastery of tools, the development of intelligence, the acquisition

of generalizations, the accumulation of information, or success in business, watch him carefully, for he is indicating a range or an emphasis for the subject, that is probably determined by the aforesaid platform and may be questionable.

A CURRENT THEORY OF NUMBER 

The nature of number is still beclouded with much uncertainty. In the present state of knowledge we are dealing with assumptions, when a question arises in regard to the fundamental nature of number. And since a difference in assumptions is usually followed by a difference in consequences, as intimated above, the intelligent educator will canvass the possibilities in the way of assumptions, and proceed according to that assumption which seems most consistent with the body of belief to which he holds. 

It would be exciting, if not refreshing, to devote the remainder of this paper to an examination of a theory of number around which a 1937 book on the teaching of arithmetic has been written. A few statements from the Preface will give the reader an idea of the theory and the importance of weighing assumptions.

There appears to exist among teachers of arithmetic in the schools and among writers upon the teaching of arithmetic the belief that number is to be found in the world of things and of experiences and of practical affairs, and that all one needs to do to lead children to the discovery of number is to bring them into contact with objects and experiences and situations. The belief expresses itself in the prevailing methods of introducing the procedures of arithmetic to children. Thus, to introduce the activity of counting, children are surrounded with objects which must be attractive enough to gain attention; . . . The belief suggests that arithmetic is part of the material world in which children live, and that it may be extracted by them in proportion to their contacts with the material world.

One of the major purposes of this book is to combat such belief. Although number may be applied to everything in the world, it exists nowhere in the world. The world does not itself possess number; the world has been invaded by trained minds which have been equipped with number.

GENERAL OUTLOOK 

3 The second paragraph is indeed a very surprising statement. If the world does not possess number, what does?

The contention is, of course, that minds possess numberjust as if mentality were not a part of this world of ours. 

The thinking is in terms of the old mind-matter dualism and number is regarded as a mental construct that is in some way clapped by the mind on physical existents. The opponents of this view, on the contrary, find the source of all concepts, including the numerical, in the natural world. 

The content of concepts is first given, they believe, in sensory experience.

According to this view, the terms or symbols of the number system originally represent existent aspects of the objective world. In a word they stand for facts of order and magnitude, and their relations, among which equality and similarity are prominent. The defender of the latter view, accordingly, does not condemn, as the author of this book does, the educator who teaches that "fiveness" can be abstracted from a concrete situation. He supports such an educator, because the latter seems to be looking in the place where the content of all basic concepts is originally located. 

This does not mean that arithmetical conceptions are not applied to concrete situations. The contention is that they are first derived from such situations, then later applied to similar situations. They are inductively developed and deductively applied.

QUALITIES OF THINGS COME IN AMOUNT 

What is actually known about number is a matter of experience. From this point of view the objects of knowledge, the things we know, are, more definitely, complexes of experience. The psychologist speaks of complexes of sensations. An apple as we perceive it is such an object. 

It is analyzable into several different types of experience. 

It is red, smooth, cool, sweet, heavy. The adjectives used refer to qualities of the apple. Who has not learned that

an adjective is a word used to express quality? Indeed for us the apple is the sum of its qualities put together in a certain arrangement. Now, note the next point carefully: every quality comes in some amount, which is the same as saying, in impressive phraseology, that it has a quantitative aspect. We recognize this fact in everyday experience when we refer to some apples as redder than others, smoother or sweeter than others.

NUMBER AS MEASUREMENT OF AMOUNT 

Things, then, are complexes of qualities, and qualities always exist in the more-less dimension. Moreover, the demand or desire for the things of this world is closely associated with amounts of quality. The redder the apples, the more attractive, ordinarily; the greener, the less attractive. The word "attractive" is just another way of expressing desire or demand. In view of the close connection between quantity and demand, the need arises for more accurate knowledge of amount. To know that one orange is heavier than another is important for the housewife who serves orange juice for breakfast. To know that the Jones farm is larger than the Smith farm is important, if one is asked to pay more for it. But such ideas of amount have a limited helpfulness because of their crudity. They may be regarded as representing a gross kind of measurement, or expression of amount. Thus one understands the encouragement that has been given to the invention of instruments that provide more accurate measures of amountinstruments for determining the amount of weight (scales) or length (yardstick) or temperature (thermometer) of things.

It should be observed that all these instruments of measurement are made on the principle of expressing amount in number of units-weight in number of pounds, length in number of yards, and temperature in number of degrees, or in some fraction or multiple thereof. It may now be

GENERAL OUTLOOK 

5 plausibly argued that number in its first intent in concrete experience is fundamentally an expression of the frequency of units of some kind, represented by different strokes of attention in discriminating things. Even the primitive counting by sheep by tying knots or by dropping pebbles can easily be understood as a method of measuring, a method of determining the number of units in this case individuals in a flock.

DEVELOPMENT OF A NUMBER LANGUAGE 

The measuring just described could be conducted without number names for different amounts, without a name to express the total. One might simply point to the knots or the pebbles corresponding to the total. A name for the number, however, is obviously convenient. The development of a number language is no more mysterious than the development of language in any other area of life. It is demonstrably convenient to label or ticket an object of knowledge, to use a symbol for it, as "dog" for a certain kind of hairy quadruped, and "cat" for his immemorial enemy. In the same way "1" comes to be used for a single unit of anything, "2" for one more than a single unit, “3” for one more than that, and so on. According to this view, number symbols, the "figures" we write, are signs or terms which stand, in our language or thought, for realities in experience. A number name is a human invention; the amount for which it stands is an objective fact. 

The number system, with its arbitrary decimal arrangement, is in essence an abstraction from reality; it is an organization of concepts, symbolized as described, that had their origin in the realm of particular experience. One can get so involved in and fascinated by the abstract elements in this system and their interrelations that one forgets their earthly origin and comes to believe that they exist in an exalted realm divorced from everyday affairs. Abstractions do have a way of getting divorced from their

perceptual mates, but originally the two kinds of experiences appear in a perfect state of unity. All concepts, including number concepts, are derivatives of the perceptual world, or expressions of the relations of such derivatives, and are chiefly valuable for guidance as we dip further into the stream of life's particulars.

CONCRETE AND ABSTRACT WORK 

On the basis of such a general theory, let us now glance for a moment at some of the troublesome problems regarding the nature of our subject and the method of teaching it. 

If a child picks up two colored sticks, then two more, and reports that he has four sticks, the number work would be called concrete. If, however, the teacher places before the 2 child the combination, 2, and asks him to add, the example would be called abstract. It is abstract because each of the numbers is divorced from any particular content and represents a generalized value. Conservative schools tend to go to seed in the direction of the abstract, radical schools in the direction of the concrete. It is a mistake in any subject to proceed as if the generalizations of experience, the abstractions therefrom, can be taught successfully apart from the perceptual situations in which they originate. 

The instructional formula is percept to concept and back to percept again. It is procedure from a real problematic situation to a thoughtful conclusion and back to everyday life again for verification and use. This means induction followed by deduction. It means generalization from the facts and the later use of the generalization as a guide in behavior. Since, however, no one is mathematically skillful who cannot think numerical values abstractly and no one is mathematically trained who cannot perform the essential operations with abstract numbers, it is easy to see that neglect of either phase is to be deplored. The real problem is to determine the proper balance between the two aspects.

GENERAL OUTLOOK 

7 It seems certain today that abstract work is generally overdone, to the neglect of the concrete. 

COMPUTATION AND PROBLEM-SOLVING 

Not unrelated to the preceding problem is that concerning the relative importance of computation and problem-solving in arithmetic. The same problem is encountered on the psychological level when one asks whether habit or reasoning should predominate. The problem is again precipitated by one who belittles the formation of skills and exalts the importance of generalizations.

GETTING OUR CUE FROM LIFE 

If one again goes back to human experience for his bearings, it can be seen that all investigation, all research, all thinking originates in some kind of problem. One does not think without cause, without a stimulus or situation which is challenging, perplexing, and therefore thought-provocative. Such a situation is commonly referred to as a problematic situation, or merely as a problem. Problems come first, then attempts to solve them. Proper attempts at solution are acts of thinking, answers to the question, What is to be done to escape the perplexity? Several proposals, plans, ideas, hypotheses, are ordinarily considered by a proficient thinker before one is acted upon, and whether the accepted hypothesis is correct can usually be determined only by trying it out, as we say, in everyday life; or by experiment, to use the language of science. From this point of view, life is a series of problems and ensuing attempts at solution, while education is an organized means of developing ability in problem-solving.

TWO SEPARATE ABILITIES 

In the light of this hypothesis, let us revert to computation and reasoning in arithmetic. The word "computation" refers to the handling of arithmetical processes, to the correct manipulation of abstract numbers. This is ultimately

reducible to ability to do four things in arithmetic: add, subtract, multiply, and divide. If one can perform these four fundamental operations with integers, common fractions, decimal fractions, and denominate numbers, he is trained in the arithmetical computation of the schools. 

The reasoning problem arises in determining when to add, subtract, multiply, or divide. To solve a real problem in arithmetic a pupil must know, first, what to do, and second, how to do it. In connection with the first arises the great demand for reasoning, or thinking, or problemsolving ability; in connection with the second, the demand for ability to compute. Extensive correlation studies, though available, are unnecessary to convince one that a pupil may be able to compute satisfactorily without being competent in arithmetical reasoning, and vice versa―a fact which indicates that two general abilities, largely disparate, are involved.

In other words, the two kinds of ability under discussion are necessary for arithmetical competence, hence both should be trained. The point in dispute, therefore, again turns out to be one of relative value. The writer recalls, with no particular enthusiasm, an experience he had in a class of teachers-in-service which he was conducting a number of years ago. One day he propounded a question regarding the relative importance of the two abilities mentioned and requested an expression of opinion from the class. The teachers responded without hesitation, but they were so nearly unanimously in favor of giving computation the greater emphasis that the instructor, in spite of a different opinion, merely made an innocuous comment on the vote and then steered his bark into less turbulent waters. 

WHY THE EXTREME EMPHASIS ON COMPUTATION? 

The vote of the teachers referred to was consistent with a trend that still persists with great force, a trend that has been encouraged by the demands of the commercial world

GENERAL OUTLOOK 

9 for clerks who can carry the heavy burdens of routine computation, after most of the real problem-solving has been done; by the makers of standardized tests, who have given much more attention to the development of computation tests than to tests of arithmetical reasoning ability; and, indirectly, by writers on the teaching of arithmetic, who have had little to offer teachers on problem-solving. Ten books on the teaching of arithmetic were found, on examination, to devote only six per cent of their space to instruction on problem-solving.

From this it seems obvious that the marked emphasis of teachers on computation is largely due, on the one hand, to commercial pressure, and, on the other, to professional neglect. The latter is not entirely the fault of the makers of tests and the writers of professional books. The difficulty is traceable to the relative dearth of knowledge regarding arithmetical reasoning.

Teachers have been given little help by their leaders because little has been available to give. In recent years, however, the situation has begun to look more promising. Investigators in larger numbers are turning to this dark corner of arithmetic, and are reporting significant results. The indications are that we may soon have a considerable body of authenticated information for the guidance of instruction in the field of arithmetical reasoning. 

IS THE VALUE OF PROBLEM-SOLVING UNDERESTIMATED? 

It is not certain, however, that teachers would just now sufficiently emphasize problem-solving in arithmetic, even if they knew more clearly the methods to pursue. There is still a question regarding the relative value of computation and problem-solving. Perhaps there is a connection between the limited progress of experts and underestimation of the value of problem-solving. If so, it is time to reconsider the prevailing evaluation of this aspect of arithmetic.

THE PRESENT TREND TOWARD INTELLECTUAL 

TRAINING The present trend in education is in the direction of improving the school as a means of training intelligence. This movement has received impetus from many directions. 

The near collapse of our civilization since the World War has made it clearer than ever before, that our most precious ability and our chief hope of social salvation is intelligence. 

Our present bewilderment bears eloquent testimony to the scarcity of this much needed ability. Otherwise we should not stand so tragically helpless in the face of so many challenging social problems.

The contemporary social confusion, however, is not the only evidence of the need for better training of our people in ability to reason, to think, to solve the problems of life. 

The demand comes directly from many educational leaders regardless of difference of opinion on other issues. It can be truthfully said that nothing more deeply animates the group of educators led by Professor Dewey than a desire (it amounts to a passion) so to organize the schools that training in reflective thinking will become the central aim. 

Indeed the domination of this aim in the minds of some of this group is so marked that it throws the habitual and more or less mechanical elements of education into the shadow of neglect. 

Professor Judd's disparagement of "trivial skills" and his marked emphasis on the achievement of important generalizations is in the same general direction, for such generalizations are the product of the best intellectual efforts of the past and the only rational starting point for thinking in the present. In other words, things are conspiring generally to make the classroom a better training ground in reflective thinking. Since this is true for the school in general, it should be especially true in arithmetic, for this subject offers a superb opportunity for training in thinking.

THE INFLUENCE OF AN EARLIER PERIOD 

Neglect of this opportunity offered in arithmetic has been characteristic of the teaching of the subject since the latter half of the nineteenth century, when drill became the watchword and arithmetic settled down into a state of general stagnation. The situation at that time has been interestingly described by a writer near the end of the period. "Arithmetic," he remarked, "has thus become a science of difficult trifles and intricate fooleries peculiar to common schools, and remarkable chiefly for sterility and ill-adapte 1ness to any useful purpose." The arithmetics of that period represented a striking departure from earlier books such as Colburn's which contained little in the way of abstract examples and was practically filled with verbal problems. 

In recent years, however, the pendulum has been swinging back in the earlier direction. It is now being more insistently demanded that teachers seize the opportunity offered in arithmetic to improve thinking ability, without neglect of training in essential computational skills. These skills are important in life, but they are important only as means of solving problems. The deferred answer, then, to the teachers who voted so strongly against the writer is that training of intelligence should take precedence over training in computational skills.

MATHEMATICAL ABILITY AND SOCIAL INFORMATION 

In certain quarters it is now being suggested that the informational aim should be given greater recognition in arithmetic. The suggestion looks for support to a recent enterprise in the field of the social studies. A number of pamphlets has been prepared by a responsible organization, each designed to acquaint the child with an important social institution. Several of the institutions described, such as the use of Arabic numerals, ways of telling time,

and systems of weights and measures fall within the province of arithmetic.

CULTIVATING ACQUAINTANCE WITH SOCIAL INSTITUTIONS 

Few will question the view that each of the social usages mentioned is of fundamental importance and that a large part of the teacher's general job is the cultivation of acquaintance with social institutions. This is implied in the content aim in education. A difference of opinion arises, however, concerning the method of doing this. It must be borne in mind that the main interest of the committee in charge of this work was the development of understandings and attitudes in social science, and was only indirectly concerned with arithmetic. One almost infers, after reading a recent appeal for informational arithmetic, that we are about to embark on a program of making arithmetic an incident of the course in the social studies. 

Every subject of study can be conceived as a means of inducting the pupil into the ways, practices, institutions, of social life. In more stereotyped phrase, this objective is spoken of as preparation for effective participation in social life. Arithmetic as a subject has to do with the institution of number in social life, and the processes of arithmetic are an essential and technical part of this institution. In the past, as has been pointed out, teachers have been so intensely interested in developing skill in these processes that relatively too much attention has been given to the mechanical aspect of the subject, too little to the improvement of ability to think. Now what of the suggestion that another claimant for favor be admitted to a richer berth in the arithmetic course?

INFORMATIONAL POSSIBILITIES IN ARITHMETIC

If social science is a study of social institutions and history contributes to an understanding of these institutions, then obviously this material has a place in the social studies course. The question before us, however, related to the extent to which such historical material should be imported into arithmetic.

When children encounter the plus sign for the first time in this subject, should it be made a part of the course to find out where the sign came from? Now, admittedly, this sign is as interesting historically as the buttons on the sleeve of a man's coat. It goes back to the little Latin word, et, which means “and.” In handwriting, the word et became conventionalized in the form (&) and was further simplified into the mathematical sign (+) of addition. Let us take another symbol-the word "window." What is the origin of this word? Few educated people know, despite the fact that "window," either the word or the thing for which it stands, is an important social institution. 

Speaking more broadly, the question arises as to whether any considerable portion of the time allotted to arithmetic should be devoted to the connotation and genesis of unfamiliar political, economic, geographical, architectural, and other concepts. Indeed, the informationalist may go further and extenuate the use of difficult terms in statements of problems and explanations of processes as a means of vocabulary expansion. In other words, he proposes to use the arithmetic period directly for the teaching of English. 

At this point the theory seems to have reached its limit in a reductio ad absurdum and to run counter to one of the most conspicuous and defensible trends in modern arithmetic-that toward the elimination of all unnecessary linguistic difficulties.

DEFINING THE FIELD OF ARITHMETIC 

Opinions may differ as to whether children, urban children for example, should be given problems involving the

area of a gable, the contents of a silo, the size of a corral, or the construction of an adobe wall. Informationalists would use the arithmetic period as an occasion for the cultivation of ideas over a broad domain. The authors of the 

CHILD-LIFE ARITHMETICS, on the contrary, stress the value of concentration. Wherever possible, they make a concerted attempt to present the important arithmetical processes in social situations familiar to the child. Where they go beyond the range of the child for material, they do so to include an arithmetical process in common adult demand or an adult situation to which an arithmetical process is commonly applied. Even this may be regarded by many champions of the child-centered school as an undesirable concession to the preparation-for-life objective. 

The authors take the position that the most difficult subject in the curriculum, and one of the most important in life, requires concentration on quantitative material. It requires intensity of analysis, rather than extensity of experience. Moreover, since the quantitative aspects of a familiar situation are easier to grasp than those of an unfamiliar situation, a problem in such a setting is more likely to be understood. In other words, the curriculum problem is to bring important samples of social life under the arithmetical microscope in a manner to facilitate the development of ability to do quantitative thinking.

Everyone is in favor of enrichment and breadth of information, but this aim, worthy as it is in its place, must not be permitted to interfere with the fundamental objective, the development of mathematical intelligence and technique. The arithmetical enrichment of first importance in the elementary school is that which comes with the consciousness of number in a situation otherwise familiar, but in which number relations have not previously been sensed. 

The course built on the principle advocated introduces the child to number in thousands of important social situations and at the same time keeps first things first. The objective

If one wants to learn about a number of things let him travel around the world or take a course in the social studies. If he wants to learn about the number in things, let him first get down analytically to number in familiar events.¹

THE INFORMATIONAL AIM IN PERCENTAGE 

The informational aim is afforded its greatest opportunity in the field of percentage, for the objective in percentage is application of familiar arithmetical processes to situations not yet familiar. After the elements of percentage have been taught in familiar areas, that is, after the "percentum" idea has been acquired and the concepts of base and rate are understood, there is a long series of topics in which no new arithmetical content is encountered. The series includes profit and loss, commission, interest, discount, stocks and bonds, and taxes.

What is new is subject matter in economics rather than in arithmetic. The difficulty in these topics is not in understanding the calculation, but in understanding the social usage. Be it noted, however, that there is no importunate demand at the elementary school level for the history of the stock market or the banking business. The demand is for knowledge of these institutions as they function today. 

Incidental touches of history will prove an attraction; more than that, a distraction.

A BIFURCATION OF THE COURSE 

Beyond the sixth grade two kinds of courses are in operation in arithmetic. In one sense they are competitive; in another, complementary. The one course carries the pupil forward with less delay in mathematics; the other provides more extensive training in the social applications of arithmetic. The relative value of these courses cannot be determined apart from the vocational purposes of the pupils. 

There seems to be no good reason why both types of course should not be offered, even in the same high school. The same series of textbooks might branch off in two directions after the sixth grade, on the one hand in the direction of social applications and, on the other hand, in the direction of mathematical advancement. INVESTIGATIONS OF SOCIAL USAGE 

Until recently this principle was accepted quite generally among educators as the final arbiter in the selection of curricular content. Today there is increasing doubt of its adequacy as the sole determiner of this content. The situation in the field of fractions will serve as an illustration. 

The fractions found by investigators of social usage are next to the fundamental operations with integers in frequency, but they are so small and the operations so simple as to suggest a much reduced emphasis on fractions in the elementary schools. No outstanding textbook maker, however, has had the temerity to limit the work in fractions to the dimensions suggested by these samplings of social usage.

Indeed, there is now under way a distinct tendency to depart somewhat from these meager cross-sections of social life, first, because the validity of the investigations is at various points impugned, and, second, because social usage alone is not regarded as a sufficient criterion for the selection of subject matter. When two corporations out of several hundred whose stock is listed on the New York Stock Exchange have nearly a million stockholders, it is evident that millions of people² in the United States buy stocks and hence should know the arithmetic pertaining to their purchase and sale, yet at least two social-usage investigations have reported no problems relating to either stocks or bonds. This is an example of an invalid finding, which evidently results from the fact that the methods of investigation were not of a character to obtain a truly representative sampling of arithmetical usage.

CONSERVATIVE NATURE OF THE PRINCIPLE 

The second criticism amounts to an attack on a fundamental assumption on which the research has been based. 

The critics are not content to accept the practices of everyday life as the only guide in formulating their objectives. 

They believe that the schools have a duty not only to prepare for life as it is, but also to raise the level of present practice. Schools exist to improve as well as to preserve our institutions. If one were commissioned to prepare a course of study in morals, should he be content to accept the practices of present adult life as the standard for pupil performance? The answer is obviously negative. In a word, liberal educators call attention to the fact that the principle of social utility as commonly applied is a very conservative guide, that it is in reality a new name for the old doctrine that education consists in the transmission of a social inheritance from one generation to the next. Regardless of the name, it is urged that the principle tends too exclusively toward the preservation of the status quo, toward worship of the past, and not sufficiently toward improvement upon the past. The critics look forward to a school that will do more than merely follow in the wake of society; they favor a school that will be instrumental in advancing the welfare of society.

VALUE OF THE SOCIAL-UTILITY PRINCIPLE 

The foregoing argument does not mean that the results of social-usage studies should be ignored. They should still

On the positive side they have great suggestive value for the selection of the topics to be emphasized, the size of numbers and kinds of examples to be used, and the types of situations in connection with which the necessary calculation may most profitably be performed. 

Even here, however, caution is necessary, since a practice in computing found to be most frequently used in social life may be found by experiment to be less effective than a practice not so frequently used, in which case the latter has ordinarily a better claim for inclusion in the curriculum.

On the negative side the principle of social utility has probably made its greatest contribution. It has led schools away from things they have done that they ought not to have done. It has brought about the elimination of many topics, on the ground that the social demand for them is not sufficiently general to warrant their retention. Thus is explained the disappearance, from many forward-looking courses, of such subjects as true discount, foreign exchange, partnership, troy weight, apothecary weight, compound proportion, surveyor's long measure, surveyor's square measure, government land measure, progressions, cord measure, customs and duties, shingling, longitude and time, and other topics. The reader should note, however, that a satisfactory course of study cannot be constructed by the method of elimination alone, for this would imply the untenable assumption that what remained would constitute a reliable and an adequate body of activities.

WHAT RESEARCH STUDIES SHOW 

The real purpose to be kept in mind in grades 1 and 2 is made clearer by consideration of the findings of research in this subject. Children entering the first grade have already gained some number knowledge. They have had a variety of number experiences in child-life activities. Some of them know the easiest number combinations in addition and companion facts in subtraction. Most of this knowledge, however, is haphazard, and in the case of some pupils it is very slight indeed.

One of the investigations of this subject, based on the reactions of more than 1,200 children, shows that 90 per cent of the children could count to 10, 60 per cent could count to 20, and one-half of all the children or 50 per cent could count by 10's to 40. More than half of the children showed the ability to count 20 exposed objects and to tell the number counted. Some of the children even knew the sums of easy combinations in addition.

WHICH IS MORE, 8 OR 5? 

On page 26 the text takes up consideration of the relative size of numbers. The practice in noticing that one number is more than another or less than another began on page 14. Initial practice is given on that page, but the systematic study of this form of arithmetical relation is carried from point to point in the material, and the use of it on pages 26 and 27 is a good example of the manner in which this is done.

Notice that in no instance in this part of the book is the child asked to tell how much larger one number is than another, but only the questions, "Which is more?" and "Which is larger?" and the inverse, "Which is less?" and "Which is smaller?" The material should be handled in that way. The tendency is to begin definite subtraction too soon. If any pupil in the class voluntarily calls attention to the arithmetical difference between two of these numbers, he should be commended with a smile of approval, but the matter should not be pursued in the activity of the class as a whole. At this point in his study of number the pupil needs a large amount of experience in determining which of two numbers is the larger or the smaller. This should come in the form of applied practice in the situations that are recognized by the pupils in their daily life. It is presented in that way in the text of the CHILD-LIFE

NUMBER BOOK. From place to place the question arises in regard to the amount of money each child has or the position of a page in the textbook in relation to some other page that is given by number, but in all these instances the question is one of making the child conscious of the order in which these numbers come. It would be very premature to try to make him able to determine the exact numerical difference between the two numbers. Textbooks for primary grades have not provided sufficient material of this type in the past. The children have been permitted, and even encouraged, to proceed as soon as possible to finding the exact difference between two numbers. The child should learn to recognize the counting activity as the basis upon which he is able at first to judge which of two numbers is greater than the other.

SOCIALIZATION OF MATERIAL 

What is socialization of arithmetical material for children? This question appears to have been interpreted in a variety of ways, if one may judge by the textbooks that have been prepared for use in primary grades. Socialization is very conspicuously shown in the connected narrative composed of the five stories relating to the circus. 

Other texts on number for primary grades contain circus material and in some of them the socialization of this material is good. If we may judge by these textbooks, the children's experience in connection with the circus and with playing circus marks the high point that the authors of number books have been able to reach in socialization of material. In some of the textbooks, socialization, or the claim of socialization, becomes a mere screen to put up in front of the skeleton of abstract arithmetic. 

The authors of the CHILD-LIFE NUMBER BOOK con ceive of socialization of curricular material in arithmetic as requiring a number of factors, among which is common usage in everyday life, a type of activity in which the pupils can readily participate, and one to which they make a favorable emotional response. In the CHILD-LIFE NUM- 

BER BOOK socialization is not found simply at certain points where it is conspicuous, but it prevails throughout the book. It is found in all units preceding that contained in the five stories about the circus, and it runs on consistently throughout the other material in the text. It is interrupted in only a few places where single pages of arithmetical exercises furnish a good opportunity to give special practice needed by the pupils in some activity involving number. Pages like 29 and 114 are illustrative of this type of material. Even such material has an indirect relation to the social life of the pupil, for its purpose is preparation for certain kinds of activity which a properly socialized individual must be able to perform. The function of the socialized

curriculum is, of course, the socialization of the learner, a function so inclusive that it is commonly regarded as the fundamental aim of education.

SUMMARY OF PART ONE This completes Part One of the CHILD-LIFE NUMBER 

BOOK.

In Part One the child has had an opportunity to

form clear concepts of all the numbers up to 20 and to learn the number language relating thereto. He should know how to recognize the numbers in life situations and their relative size or value. He should be able to read the symbols of these numbers, whether written in figures or in words. 

He has learned something about the order in which numbers come. He not only knows how to count, but he knows that when we count 8 objects the last one of the 8 is the eighth. 

He has learned the distinction between eight objects and the eighth object in the group. This is vital and important to him in all his later study of number. He has also learned some of the social uses of number in his daily life, and he has learned much about the meaning of words that indicate relative position, size, direction, and so on. 

All of this material is suitable for use in the higher first grade, and the reading difficulty is regulated to conform to that standard. In schools that do not provide number instruction in the first grade, Part One will make good material for the use of beginners in the second grade.

COUNTING BY 10'S 

It is better to teach the child to count money by 10's than to depend simply on the child's ability to count by 10's in words. There is the same danger here that was found in learning to count by 1's. The pupil may be able to count by 10's to 100 without comprehending the meaning of the number 30 in the sense that it is three 10's. A parrot can be taught to "count" to 100 by 10's. Children sometimes count in that way in the game of hide-and-seek instead of

counting by 1's. There is justification for rote counting by 

10's when the motive is what we find in such a game. But when we are helping the pupil to develop a concept of a number such as 30 and to realize that it is made up of three 10's, we accomplish our purpose best by having him count real objects arranged in 10's. In the United States we are fortunate in having a money system which gives a definite concrete starting point for developing this concept. 

RELATION OF THEORY TO CLASSROOM ACTIVITIES 

Do not talk with the children about the theories of the system of 10's and 1's, but lead them in the activities and try to draw them out in conversation. Study their responses carefully and try to determine the degree to which the concept is developed from day to day in each child's mind. 

Use theories as a guide and the activities as a means. Incidentally, the child who possibly does not know that 100 cents and one dollar have the same value will have the opportunity to learn that practical fact. It is incidental, however. It is not our objective at this point to teach all about our number system or all about our money.

COUNTING BY 2'S AGAIN 

Page 87 recalls counting by 2's, but presents that activity in a slightly changed aspect. Counting by 2's was discussed in the Manual in connection with the story on page 75. On that page the immediate motive was to find how many persons were riding in the bus. Sue counted simply to get the total. The teacher saw there the first approach to multiplication. The pupils thought of it only as counting by a number larger than one. On page 87 the aspect of the activity is changed in such a way that Dick already knows that he has 10 pennies. When he counts by 2's, it is not to see how much money he has. It is to compare the number of 2's in cents with the number of little toy dogs taken one by one. 

He must now consider 2 cents as a distinct number and he must match 2 cents and 1 toy dog until he has matched all his pennies, two by two, with all the small toy dogs one by one. In this way he finds out that he has enough money. 

The teacher will recognize this thinking process as the underlying concept of division, but nothing should be said to the pupils about division. They should simply count by 

2's as Dick did and keep the process in concrete form. Any

attempt to make a quick transfer here to digits and a premature jump into the abstract process of division will be detrimental. What the pupils need is a number of motivated activities with toy money and other objects involving counting by 2's. The central interest, let us say, is to buy 

5 toy dogs. This interest spreads to or motivates any activity that helps buy the dogs. Counting by 2's helps, for it tells them whether they have enough money to make the purchase. Counting by 2's, therefore, becomes interesting, for it contributes as a means to a practical end.

The pupils should not speak loosely of the different parts of an object as halves if the object is divided into three parts, or four, or some other number.

She has discovered by experiment that one-half of 12 is 6 and one-half dozen is 6. The teacher will easily recognize this as an activity presenting the fundamental concept of another form of division, the form in which we divide a number into equal parts to find the size of one of those parts. No special mention of this point should be made to the pupils at this time.

Other activities should be organized on the basis of one dozen. These activities, however, should not be artificial.

Much has been said and written upon the question of the order in which these number combinations should be presented. All the leading authorities in the teaching of arithmetic are agreed that in general the easier number facts should be presented first. For guidance in this arrangement, investigations have been made to determine the relative difficulty of the 100 addition facts and the 100 subtraction facts, respectively. The most helpful study of this kind is the Knight-Behrens investigation. All of the investigators admit, however, that their results are to be interpreted only approximately. The results throw considerable light on the question of the order of difficulty, but they do not determine the exact order to be followed in the presentation.

NOT COMPLETE MASTERY BUT UNDERSTANDING 

No one knows exactly how many repetitions of one of these number facts will be necessary in the case of any individual pupil in learning. The important point is that the teacher should watch each individual pupil's progress in this respect and be guided by her diagnostic study of her own class from week to week or month to month. The teacher should remember at all times that the purpose of the CHILD-LIFE NUMBER BOOK in second-grade classes is not the mastery of the combinations but the understanding of them and the ability to recognize them in concrete situations. The CHILD-LIFE NUMBER BOOK sets up no standard of mastery for the second-grade pupils and recommends definitely that the material should be presented for the purpose of developing concepts and for the purpose of understanding the use of numbers and number combinations, and contemplates that the matter of mastering each number fact for future use should be postponed for later grades.

THE ORDER OF PRESENTATION 

On the general subject of the order in which the number

facts should be presented, Morton says, on pages 88-89 of 

"Teaching Arithmetic in the Elementary School," Volume I: 

"... the teacher may proceed systematically to making the pupils acquainted with the 25 easier combinations by taking up these combinations in any one of several orders. The order stated below is given as a suggestion." Morton then gives an arrangement of the easier addition facts and follows this table by saying: "The exact order is not important. But it is important that the teacher find out what the pupils already know and develop the program accordingly."

Morton also advocates a conduct of the teaching in such a manner as to allow considerable time to elapse between the presentation of the first and easiest number fact and the presentation of the last of these combinations to be added. 

He also recommends the teaching of each number fact and its reverse fact in connection. He would have the child become conscious of 2 and 3 are 5, and 3 and 2 are 5, at the same time, or in very close connection. He also advocates the teaching of the inverse subtraction fact in such a manner that the child recognizes its relation to its companion addition fact, but he calls attention to the need of first teaching a few addition facts to establish the addition concept before taking up this relation between addition and subtraction facts.

LOGICAL GROUPING OF NUMBER FACTS 

The teaching of addition facts and subtraction facts in relation contemplates that the pupil should finally become conscious not only of the relation of an addition fact to its reverse addition fact, as 2 and 3 are 5, 3 and 2 are 5, but also conscious of the relation of these two addition facts to the companion subtraction facts, 2 from 5 is 3, 3 from 5 is 2. This relationship of the four facts in such a group should be used by the teacher when she reaches the point of helping the pupil to master number facts for automatic

control in computing. This important question should be kept in mind in the later practice exercises where mastery of individual number facts for automatic response is the objective in view.

In the earliest presentations of the addition and subtraction combinations each combination should be presented separately in concrete form and the child should be led to discover the sum of two numbers or the difference between two numbers in number situations in his environment. He should gradually become conscious of the relationship of four such number facts, as mentioned above. However, any definite presentation of this grouping in abstract form for practice at this time would be premature. If a teacher is using this material in a school system in which the complete logical grouping of these number facts is called for in the second grade, she can easily show that relationship in her teaching. Otherwise we believe that this full use of grouping should be emphasized later.

HOW THE NUMBER FACTS ARE PRESENTED 

The arrangement of the number facts for teaching, in the CHILD-LIFE NUMBER BOOK, conforms to the rec ommendations of Morton and others on this question. Referring to the list of 12 addition facts and 10 subtraction facts given above, the reader will see that in this small group in the early teaching, each addition fact, such as 2 and 1 are 3, is promptly connected with its reverse fact, 1 and 2 are 3.

The reader will also notice that the 10 subtraction facts that are used in the early development of the subtraction concept are closely related to the addition facts. In the list of addition facts, for example, the text has 2 and 1 are 3, and in the subtraction facts it has 1 from 3 is 2. This plan is followed throughout the 10 subtraction facts that are selected for use.

SHOWING ADDITION OBJECTIVELY 

In trying to help the child understand what we mean

when we say "add," it is well to use a number fact in which the act of adding is plain enough to be recognized by the learner without explanation. On that account the CHILD- 

LIFE NUMBER BOOK begins addition on page 100 by having the child study the combinations, 2 and 3 are 5 and 3 and 2 are 5. If the book began with the material found on page 101, some children in the class would recognize the sum of 1 sled and 1 sled instantly, without making any conscious addition. The number 2 would be recognized because it is already known as a group measure. Some pupils might do this with the material found on page 100, but the probability is greatly reduced by using the numbers 3 and 2 and the sum 5 rather than smaller numbers as 1 and 1 are 2.

On this point the text is well supported by the illustration. If the kitties in the picture were all of one color, the probability of arriving at the number 5, without adding would be increased. The picture skillfully takes care of this pedagogical difficulty by having 2 white kitties and 3 black ones.

In accordance with the plan that has already been described, the concept of addition is developed in stories and exercises on pages 100 to 106. All of these stories and exercises should be taken up first as reading material, with free conversation about the content. During the conversation the teacher will discover how much each pupil in the group already knows about addition. After the first reading and discussion of the material on pages 100-106, it will be well to begin again at page 100 and talk about the arithmetical content of each page, providing supplementary exercises as needed.

MEANING FIRST 

Supplementary exercises should be of a spontaneous, natural character and should not in any case take up the use of the symbols arranged in the order of an example for addition. The thought of laying the foundation for later

study of the symbols, with understanding, should be uppermost in the teacher's mind. She should be chiefly concerned in ascertaining what progress each child makes in forming the correct concept of the meaning of adding two numbers and getting another number that has the same value as the two. This the children should show in many ways objectively. They should not be encouraged at this time to show the corresponding figures in an example. A premature jumping to the abstract figures has been characteristic of the teaching of arithmetic in the primary grades, and it is presented in that manner in some books. 

This premature effort to teach the child abstract forms has defeated the very purpose that the teacher has in establishing clear concepts.

TRANSITION FROM COUNTING TO ADDING 

It will be noted that after the first material is given on page 100, the other addition facts that follow consist of those in which 1 is added to some number and the reverse fact in which that same number is added to 1. In choosing these very easy number facts the book presents a phase of addition that shows how closely it is related to counting. 

The child already knows that the next number after 2, in counting, is 3. It is an easy transition for him now to use the word add and have the meaning modified sufficiently to grasp the signficance of addition. Immediately in connection with this act of thinking 2 and 1 more are 3, he finds in the picture the reverse of that fact, or 1 and 2 are 3. 

He is now started on the road to the generalization that the sum of an addition combination is the same when it is reversed.

The teacher should not attempt to explain this fact to the pupils, but she should bring the material to them in such a way that they discover the fact. The child will form the generalization gradually and any attempt on the teacher's part to hasten the process will do more harm than

good. It should be the result of a natural growth in the child's mind.

USING THE TAKE-AWAY IDEA 

The concept of subtraction that is presented here is based

upon the take-away idea, and the activities that the teacher organizes should correspond in character. We should be careful not to emphasize unduly in the child's early study, the different types of subtraction such as finding how much larger one number is than the other, or finding how much one number must be increased to equal another number. 

These distinctions must be brought out gradually and considerably later. The child should be allowed first to become familiar with the facts. Formal analysis and classification of the type mentioned can wait.

USING A DIAGNOSTIC PROGRESS CHART 

If the teacher has not already made a diagnostic chart of number abilities of her pupils, she should begin to do so at this time. She should be able to see on this chart exactly what number concepts each child gives evidence of having formed. She should be guided by what she finds on the chart each time the subject of number comes up in her program. The diagnostic progress chart will serve here the same purpose as the diagnostic chart used by the physician or the professional nurse. All this diagnostic activity on the part of the teacher should be conducted in such a way as to avoid formalizing the distinction between addition and subtraction situations.

LEARNING TO THINK ABOUT NUMBERS 

Pages 116 and 117 complete this brief round of treatment on more and less. If any pupil gives a wrong response on page 116, he should be set at work arranging numbers on paper in such a manner as to show the answer in the example he has. For instance, if a pupil had trouble with example 6 in the first column, he should be given an exercise in arranging all the numbers in the 30's and all the numbers in the 40's in two rows, one under the other. He should be able there to see clearly that any number in the 40's is more than the largest number in the 30's; that, for example, 46 is more than 38.

If a pupil gets a wrong response in an example in which the numbers are small, as in example 4 in the first column, he should be asked which of these numbers comes before the other when he counts. The teacher will then ask him which of the two numbers is more. He has already learned that the number that comes after the other is the greater. 

The exact form of the teacher's question will vary according to the circumstances.

On page 117 the same type of number knowledge is called for. The specific requirement here is more exacting, however, because the pupil must know definitely what number it is that comes between the two numbers on each side of a blank in the table. In this respect the exercise on page 117 is a generalized page calling for the use of the same ability that is required on page 115 in placing books on the library shelf.

LEARNING ABOUT ZERO 

Page 121 presents the first of the zero facts presented in the book. The study of the zero facts has been postponed until the pupils have made some progress in their study of the other easy addition facts. Most of the authorities on the teaching of arithmetic favor brief postponement

of the teaching of the zero facts. They say that several of the other number facts should be presented first, so that the child gets distinct concepts of addition and subtraction in which the result of the activity is a real change in the number with which one starts. They advocate that when this has been done, the zero facts may be presented in an easy, natural way, but that the treatment should be informal and the child should not be very conscious of taking up something that is considered quite different. This plan is followed in the CHILD-LIFE NUMBER BOOK. At the present point in the course the child makes use of 4 and 0. are 4, and on page 123 he finds some of the other zero facts in addition.

WHICH NUMBER TO SUBTRACT 

Pages 150-151.

The pupil has already had experience and considerable practice in recognizing a social situation which calls for subtraction. Pages 150 and 151 make use of some of the easiest subtraction facts for the purpose of focusing the pupil's attention on selecting the number that should be subtracted from the other number in such a situation.

problem 3 the number to be subtracted is plainly a number taken away. It is the same also in problem 4. 

The pupils should be given the opportunity to present easy original subtraction situations involving subtraction facts that they have been taught. In presenting these original subtraction situations or problems, the children should note carefully how they select the number that is to be subtracted from the other number. They should become aware of the fact that they do this by thinking of what the problem means.

EXPOSED ANSWERS PREVENT WRONG INITIAL RESPONSES 

The number combinations throughout this book are first presented with exposed answers. This is done for two reasons: (1) to prevent the child from getting a wrong first impression, which, evidence shows, tends to recur in the learning process involved in the mastery of the combinations; (2) to remove from the child the opportunity for counting in obtaining the correct response. Presenting the combinations with exposed answers tends to habituate the child to making immediate correct responses-the type of response toward which teaching is directed. While responding correctly to the combination presented without the answer is the end product of teaching, there is some evidence which indicates that repetition of the combination and sum from presentation with exposed answer is fully as effective in the process of learning, if not more effective, than repetition from presentation without exposed answers. 

The first type of presentation allows immediate association of combination and correct response; the latter type, even though representing recall rather than mere association, allows opportunity for wrong responses and for practice with unnecessary crutches in arriving at the correct response. The formula emphasized in this book is teaching through repetition with exposed answers, testing through presentation without answers, and then practice on combinations not mastered through more repetition with exposed answers and additional testing in combinations without answers.

THE DEVELOPMENT OF GENERALIZATIONS An assumption underlying the CHILD-LIFE Series is that one of the most important by-products of education is the power to make generalizations. It is assumed that arithmetic, if properly taught, contributes to the develop-

ment of this power. Thus, effort is made in the presentation of almost every process to introduce it in such fashion as to lead to the generalization, principle or rule governing the most effective method for dealing with the situation at hand.

On page 25 the pupil has his attention directed to the comparison of two numbers to see which is larger and how much larger it is. This is only a very small step from his previously acquired notion of subtraction. To the child, all subtraction may be considered in a broad sense as consisting of taking one number from another. When he compares two numbers, as on page 

25, to find the difference between them, his mental action in making a concrete subtraction, as in dealing with books or pencils, involves taking away from the larger number as many as the small number, in order to see how many are left.

The pupil is now moving along gradually in discovering the small differences that exist between subtraction situations in life. It is not necessary to carry this analysis of subtraction situations very far. Some books carry it to excess, trying to distinguish and formulate for the pupils all the differentiations that an adult mind can discover. 

Such minute differentiation in subtraction situations is unnecessary for young children. The most they need to know is the general resemblance of all these subordinate varieties in that they all fall properly under the process of subtracting one number from another number. Further progress in differentiating the types of subtraction situation should be limited to the major differentiations, and this plan is followed in the CHILD-LIFE ARITHMETICS.

alizations at the bottom of page 27 as their own conclusions in regard to zero. 

In teaching the zero facts, the teacher has a better opportunity to group facts in the pupil's mind with advantageous results than she has in teaching some of the other subtraction facts. The pupil should be taught to stop and think before he answers any question in regard to subtracting 0. Many of the zero errors made by pupils arise from thoughtlessness rather than ignorance.

Bring out this point in developing such material as this found on page 27. When the pupil starts to answer in such examples as those in exercise 1, the teacher should ascertain immediately whether he knows in concrete form what example 1A means. The pupil may be able to show this knowledge by giving a concrete instance. After having done this in several examples, he is prepared for the general inference that if you have 4 things or 5 things, or some other number, all at one time, and take away none, you still have the same number.

If the child can be led to stop and consider for a moment the meaning of subtraction in concrete situations, he will make very few errors in dealing with the zero situations. 

This is probably because zero combinations lend themselves readily to generalization, which in turn explains the fact that in the best study of the difficulty of subtraction combinations the zero combinations were found to have the least difficulty. We should, therefore, impress upon pupils the importance of thinking first instead of answering instantly in terms of the symbols represented.

LEARNING ADDITION FACTS BY ANALYSIS 

If some individual pupils are found to need further teaching in a few of the harder addition facts, they can be helped by a process of analysis of the addition that is required. 

For example, if a problem makes it necessary to add 7 and 9 and the pupil is uncertain in reference to the sum, he can be taught to start with 7 and add enough to 7 to make 10. He will then have used 3 of the 9 units that he must add to 7. He has 6 of those units still to be added. Starting with 10, he knows that 10 and 6 are 16. He has been taught that the meaning of the word sixteen is 10 and 6 more. His final sum in this problem, then, is found by him to be 16. In a similar manner the pupil may be taught to reason out any one of the harder addition facts and arrive at the correct sum. This device will prove especially helpful in assisting pupils to a mastery of the last few addition facts that they are studying.

Some teachers advocate using at the start, in all the harder addition facts, the method of separating the addition into two parts, the first of which brings the pupil up to 10 as a sum, the second bringing the pupil to the final sum by the method described above. It seems better, however, for pupils to make a direct approach to the addition facts in this group by studying the concrete application of each number fact as has been explained in the Manual. 

By the direct approach they gain a control over the number fact by which they can recall it for use without pausing to take any intermediate step. On this account, all pupils

taught by any method should ultimately gain a working control of each number fact sufficient to make it possible to recall the correct sum in one mental act.

ADDING BY ENDINGS 

The authors here present activities for use in teaching adding by endings. The pupils already know the addition facts that have sums not larger than 9. They have learned how to apply these combinations in the group of numbers known as teens, and they have acquired some degree of mastery of the 45 harder addition facts. They are now ready to apply all these addition facts above the group of teens and to learn how to use the facts in any of the groups of tens up to 100.

Adding by endings should be taught in two types of number situations. In the first type of situation the pupil should become conscious of the relationship of these elementary addition facts within the limit of each group of 

10, or decade, as we go up toward 100. The pupil knows that 2 and 5 are 7. He has found that 12 and 5 are 17. He now should become conscious of the fact that 22 and 5 are 

27, 32 and 5 are 37, and so on, up to 92 and 5 are 97. He should practice on other elementary addition facts that do

not produce sums larger than 9. He should still practice, for instance, on 3 and 5 are 8, 13 and 5 are 18, 23 and 5 are 28. He should use such combinations until he grasps clearly the idea that these lower addition combinations hold good in adding wherever he happens to find them.

In the second type of situation each addition results in a sum that is located in the next higher group of 10 above the group in which it starts. This is called bridging the 10's. 

The pupil knows that 8 and 6 are 14. He now learns that 

18 and 6 are 24, 28 and 6 are 34, and so on, up to 88 and 6 are 94. A sufficient amount of teaching by using additions that will bring a sum in the next higher group of 10 will develop in the pupils an understanding of this aspect of our number system. The teacher should regulate the character of the examples that she gives in such a way that the easier additions are called for first. This might be done in this second type of adding by endings by starting with 8 and adding 4, or even by starting with 9 and adding 2 all the way up the scale. Watch the reaction of each pupil and try to discover just when and how clearly it dawns on him that these sums are alike in a certain respect. 

This exercise of adding by endings should not be carried to extremes, but it should be used enough to establish an understanding of how it works. The growth of the ability of each individual pupil to do the adding will develop only very gradually. It cannot be hastened by any unusual pressure without danger. The activity is very helpful in adding long columns in which the sum of one column runs higher than 19. It is also very useful in multiplication.

The material in the textbook is arranged with this general thought in mind. It starts simply and becomes more difficult only very gradually. Chapter 5. Second Semester METHODS OF SUBTRACTION Pages 136-142. 

There are various methods of subtraction and various patterns of response in the process of making application of the various methods. The experimental evidence favors the take-away method coupled with the equal-additions pattern of handling changes in the columns. These subtraction ideas have been previously taught, but emphasis was always placed upon the from idea involved in the take-away method. Since evidence also shows that a majority of children, even when taught subtraction by the additive method, later change to the take-away method, it seems that forces in social life tend to favor that method of subtraction. If one eliminates making change in the buying transaction, which is really a process of counting and is quite unlike the mental process in addition or subtraction, it is reasonable to think that the take-away method is superior in situations employing real subtraction.

The ease of explaining the take-away method is one argument in favor of its use. It might be suggested that this same argument should also be applied to the equal-additions technique for altering the columns. As a matter of fact both the equal-additions method and its rival, the decomposition method, are rather difficult procedures to explain to children. The first rests on the mathematical principle that adding equals to both minuend and subtrahend does not affect the value of the difference, while the second is based on the principle that adding a given number to the minuend and subtracting an equal number from it does not affect the value of the difference. GUIDING PRINCIPLES IN TEACHING MULTIPLICATION 

Multiplication should be developed as a natural outgrowth of number experiences. In the previous exercises the child has had much experience in counting by 5's, 2's, 3's, and 4's. In the post office project on page 36 the children counted stamps and money-1 stamp, 2 cents; 2 stamps,

4 cents; 1 nickel, 5 cents; 2 nickels, 10 cents, etc. They had experience with such units of measure as pints, quarts, money, time, inches, feet and yards and often repeated simple operations of multiplication. In their games they counted by rote 5, 10, 15, 20; or 10, 20, 30, etc. These experiences will have furnished an excellent basis for teaching the times idea.

The following purposes prevail in the development of the work in multiplication: (1) to connect the new process with what the child has previously learned; (2) to present the work so that the times idea will have meaning; (3) to begin with 5's, because the child in all probability has had much experience with 5's in his counting and in his experiences with money; (4) to teach the operation objectively and in a variety of forms, including the form often thought of as division, but without mentioning the term division; (5) to teach the combinations in one order and then in the reverse order, to develop the generalization that, in multiplying, the product is the same no matter which number comes first; (6) to make application of the combinations in the solution of verbal problems as soon as possible; (7) to teach the combinations of one number rather thoroughly before teaching the combinations of another, and then to mix the operations of the two numbers-e.g., teach 5's thoroughly and then teach 2's; (8) to develop the multiplication facts in each "table" in a logical fashion but not to refer to the arrangement as a table; (9) to stress the different facts without relation to order, and finally, (10) to let the child see that there is an advantage in an orderly arrangement.

GENERAL PRINCIPLES IN TEACHING DIVISION IN GRADE THREE 

The chief objective in teaching division in grade three is the development of concepts as the basis for later study and practice in arithmetic. It is important for this to be

done before the pupil reaches a grade level where he becomes involved in the difficult procedures of the formal process of division. The CHILD-LIFE ARITHMETICS 

Grade Three presents a very simple preparatory course in division and makes a close connection with what the pupil has previously learned in multiplication and in counting objects by 2's, 3's, etc. The simple problems involving what is ordinarily called division are usually solved in life through the use of counting, multiplication, or addition.

The following aims are taken into consideration in the problems and exercises in this book. (1) to tie up division with multiplication, but to begin the teaching of division as a distinct process; (2) to think of division as the process of finding how many numbers of a given size are in a larger number and as dividing a larger number into a number of equal parts; (3) to express division in a variety of ways; (4) to teach division facts in combination with multiplication facts and to show their relationship; (5) to teach half, third, fourth, and fifth when teaching division of a number by 2, 3, 4, and 5; (6) to teach division of longer numbers, so as to show the real need for the technique of division; (7) to confine the work of this grade to simple exercises, involving only even divisions by 2, 3, 4, and 5, and the corresponding reverse facts.

FUNCTIONAL ARITHMETIC One of the outstanding features of the CHILD-LIFE 

Series is its emphasis on functional arithmetic. This characteristic is manifested first in the type of the situations

employed as a setting for the problems. However, there are other evidences: 

1. The statements of problems contain only the number facts which normally appear in the situations faced. 

2. The number facts which do not appear naturally in the problem situations are placed in abstract exercises and drills. 

3. Each new element of skill is presented in a problematic situation creating a need for mastery, number situations are provided for the mastery of the needed skill, and more problem situations are then offered in which the new achievement may be applied. 

4. Those phases of arithmetic are taught which are needed for meeting real problems, and children are enabled to solve these problems as they are solved in real life. 

5. Many problems are grouped about a central arithmetical idea and lead to a conclusion based upon the results of the related problems.

TREATMENT OF DIVISION 

In the CHILD-LIFE Series every effort has been made to minimize the difficulty of the division process. Various things have been done to accomplish this. Among them are the following: 

1. Teaching the so-called division facts thoroughly. 

2. Connecting the process of division closely with that of multiplication, e. g., speaking of five 6's as equal to 30 and of 30 as being five 6's united. 

3. Stressing division as finding the number of times one number is in another. 

4. Using different ways for expressing division. 

5. Teaching by using the long division form before short division. 

6. Employing only even division in the early exercises. 

7. Demonstrating long division with simple exercises involving even divisions to acquaint the child with the various steps involved. 

8. Using the long division pattern in all exercises involving uneven divisions. 

9. Introducing the process of long division with uneven division in very simple exercises and adding new difficulties very slowly and one at a time. 

10. Teaching the pupil to check the correctness of his work from the time of the introduction of the process. 

11. Reviewing repeatedly combinations in addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. 

12. Teaching short division as a short cut of long division. 

13. Using diagnostic tests repeatedly and employing remedial instruction adjusted to the pupil's specific needs.

Before doing any teaching in division the fourth grade teacher should examine carefully all that the textbook

presents on pages 99-128. This part of the fourth grade. course recalls and utilizes all that the pupil learned about division in Grade Three, increases slightly the degree of difficulty to correspond to the fourth grade level, introduces new terms to be taught and used, and presents for learning and use all of the 90 division facts not previously taught. This important body of material should not be hurried over as a mere review. It constitutes the basis for the fundamental preparation the pupil should have before undertaking the more difficult procedures in long division of whole numbers.

THINKING IN ARITHMETIC 

No other subject in the curriculum provides so great an opportunity for clear and exact thinking as arithmetic. 

The occasions for thinking may be confined to the problem per se or they may involve the general social situations through which the problem arose. Unfortunately, in teaching arithmetic so much stress has been placed on drill in abstract numbers, that pupils lose sight of the fact that arithmetic is really a means of handling the numerical aspects of problems that arise in the lives of men, and that the problems in daily life are not always clearly formulated, for vital elements may be omitted, and irrelevant elements may be involved in the data. 

The authors of the CHILD-LIFE Series have presented a great variety of problematic situations as they appear in the daily life of the pupils. They try to set up problem situations which will appeal to the child as real problems. 

They train the pupil to consider what is wanted, the facts given, and the process needed in the solution. The final statement in the pattern solution always answers the question that is asked. Pupils are asked to complete statements of problems. They are also asked to contribute problems from their own experience.

WHAT GRADE SIX DOES Grade Six of the CHILD-LIFE ARITHMETICS fol lows the general plan that is found in the books of this series. It begins by giving a thorough recall of what the pupils have learned in earlier grades and particularly in the next lower grade, in this instance the fifth grade. The authors then reteach addition and subtraction of fractions and proceed to give a detailed treatment of multiplication and division of fractions. Following this, the authors present a complete treatment and teaching of decimals. The subject of decimals is given careful attention from the point of introduction, in which the concept of the decimal fraction is taught, to the end of the treatment of division of a decimal by a decimal.

Grade Six also recalls the pupil's previously acquired knowledge of measures and expands the teaching of measures to include the four fundamental operations of computing measures. The material is thoroughly modernized to eliminate from the course the traditional treatment and bring the subject of measurements down to present practices in daily life. The book also contains other interesting and helpful shorter treatments of well known topics. The pupil is taught how to transact some of the simplest items in business and how to keep personal cash accounts. By the use of graphs pupils are given some training in understanding the meaning of large numbers by thinking of such numbers quantitatively, as shown in a graph. 

This course presents a thorough preparation for the work in mathematics in the grades above the sixth. It confines the problematical situations to such experiences as the pupils themselves have had or have become familiar with by association with adult members of their families. The entire subject of percentage, in which the pupil must become familiar with problematical situations that are foreign

The course in division of decimals takes up the division of a decimal by a decimal on page 196. Using a Familiar Principle for a New Purpose 

In connection with teaching this device in the division of decimals, call attention to the principle that the authors are using. Show that in division of whole numbers both divisor and dividend may be multiplied by the same number without affecting the quotient. In the demonstration examples that are given in the text the divisor is a decimal number having tenths. On that account the authors multiply both divisor and dividend by 10. The result is a simple example in division by a whole number. Some special variations of this device are shown on page 197 and practiced on page 198. These variations enable the pupil to make use of the device in special arithmetical situations and to give practice on them on pages that follow. In the course

of your teaching all of this material develop the meaning of the boxed generalizations and instructions that are found on pages 197-199.

MORE HELP IN UNDERSTANDING OUR NUMBER SYSTEM 

Before this unit closes it teaches the pupil how to divide a decimal number by 10 or 100 or 1000 simply by moving the decimal point. In teaching the class how to make use of this device be sure to bring out the general principle that the pupils have already studied, that of the changing value of a digit in accordance with the number of spaces it stands to the right or the left of the decimal point.

MEASURES IN EVERYDAY LIFE Pages 217-245. 

Make a careful examination of the teaching material on measurements as found in the pages within these limits. 

This should be done in advance of actual teaching of any part of this material or the techniques of procedure for which it calls. This preparatory examination of material by the teacher is important because the authors of the CHILD- 

LIFE ARITHMETICS have modernized the teaching of measures. The older and more traditional textbooks in arithmetic included in their pages a considerable amount of teaching material that is now obsolete. The handling of that material has sometimes been extremely mechanical.

The authors of the CHILD-LIFE ARITHMETICS have made a complete departure from this traditional position. 

They teach measures by presenting such social situations as call for changing from one measure to another, adding measured quantities of things, subtracting measured quantities, multiplying, and dividing, in activities that are familiar to pupils of the sixth grade age. This point of view should be kept in mind by the teacher. It will simplify the teaching and will make the results more definite and permanent.

After making such a preliminary examination of the material in the textbook, the teacher will be guided by the development shown in the text. Notice with what care and clearness the authors develop the method of finding the area of a rectangle as shown on page 235, and the method of finding the volume of rectangular solids as shown on pages 238-239. The pupil moves over by easy steps from a very simple use of measures to the more complicated forms in which measures appear in computing. Watch the development of each pupil throughout the entire section of the course in which the pupils are studying measures.

KEEPING UP IN WHAT HAS BEEN TAUGHT IN GRADE SIX 

The final pages of the course are devoted to a general review of the four fundamental processes, as the pupil has encountered the need of them in dealing with whole numbers, fractions, decimals, and compound measures. This is a running review in which the hidden weak spots in the ability of each pupil may be revealed. The material should be used for that purpose. As weak spots are revealed from day to day, they should be met by careful remedial teaching fitted exactly to the type of error that each pupil makes in his work.

The purpose is to make each pupil successful. Instill this purpose in the pupils. As it increases in power, it will largely determine their success.
