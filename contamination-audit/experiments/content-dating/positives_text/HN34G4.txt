PREFACE 

The Strathmore Plan is an educational program based on the use of the formula test-teach-practice-test-reteach. It will supplement any course of study in English or Arithmetic which you are using. Grade levels are not indicated on any of the materials, so that a seventh-grade or an eighth-grade pupil who has failed to master any of the skills which are taught in the fifth or sixth grades, for instance, can work on them without feeling that he may be doing work on a lower grade level. 

The Teacher's Manuals of the Strathmore Plan contain a copy of each test, exercise, and practice sheet which the pupil will use for the testing, learning, and practice of English and Arithmetic skills. The areas of subject matter in the Teacher's 

Manual are divided into units of work, each of which is preceded by an inventory test to determine what skills in English or Arithmetic the pupil has not mastered. 

The pupil's record on each of these inventory tests reveals the skill on which he needs further study and practice. The pupil's work sheets which provide this necessary practice are indicated on the inventory test, and may also be found by consulting the Table of Contents or the Suggested Grade Placement Sheets in the Teacher's Manuals. 

Since the practice exercises are keyed to the inventory test, the pupil studies and practices only those skills on which he needs help. Following the practice exercises, there are reviews and a self-test to enable the pupil to determine his progress and his readiness for the mastery test. The mastery test completes the final check of that unit for both the pupil and the teacher. The program is thus flexible and timesaving. It meets the needs of all pupils on any grade level, and pupils with exceptional ability do not spend time on what they already know. 

How to Use the Strathmore Plan 

There are eight procedures suggested for each unit of material: (1) Give the inventory or pre-test (2) Teach to obtain understanding (3) Use appropriate practice material (4) Use review exercise (5) Use additional practice sheets if necessary (6) Use the self-test (7) Use additional practice sheets if necessary (8) Give the mastery test

Each teacher will find the best way of presenting the material to her group. It iii

Reviews are given after pupils have completed the teaching and practice exercises. These are followed by Self-Test No. 1, Parts I and II, and the Mastery Test No. I, Parts I 

and II. Each page in the Teacher's Manual for Good Usage has a page designation in the upper right corner. The same page number is used on the File Guide for each page so that there is no waste of time in looking for materials. Either John or the teacher can find them without difficulty.

Reviews are usually given before the self-test, after the pupils have completed the necessary teaching and practice exercises. In order to maintain the skills he has already acquired, each pupil should do the reviews, self-tests, and mastery tests. 

The reviews are also keyed to the exercises so that the pupil may know what help

TO THE TEACHER: 

The teaching of correct usage has as its aims: 

1. To be able to express ourselves adequately to others by making our speech and writing intelligible, forceful, and acceptable to others 

2. To be able to understand what others express to us 3. 

To provide practice for good usage until the forms become natural to use and to hear

These aims may be accomplished by habit formation. There is little good in knowing "correct form" unless it is applied, through practice and usage, to every-day writing and speaking.

Since to change language habits is a hard task, the authors suggest that reading aloud or saying the correct forms suggested in "Good Usage" is essential in good habit formation.

The teacher may wish to read with the pupils the directions for each inventory test, exercise, review, self-test, and mastery test before work is begun. 

This will provide an opportunity to teach new words and to ascertain that the directions are understood by the pupil.

Since our associations with other people demand a written form of communication, it is well to have a uniform style for such communications. 

A uniform style of writing should have as its objectives the following: 1. 

To lead the pupil to adopt an easy and natural style 

2. To overcome a dread of writing 

3. To lead the pupil to use only those forms familiar to others

The authors do not recommend that "Written Forms" be taught as a separate unit. For instance, the lessons on letter writing in this unit should go hand in hand with the lessons on letter writing in "Capitalization," in "Punctuation," and in "Sentences and Paragraphs."

EXERCISE 1 What a Margin Is 

Do you remember how a printed page looks? The printing never reaches any edge of the paper. The clear spaces at the top, at the bottom, and on the sides are called margins.

The margins on a printed page vary in size. The margin at the top of the page is not always the same as the margin at the bottom of the page. The margin at the right is not always the same as the margin at the left. 

A written page should have margins, too. Margins make it easier to read a page. 

They also give space for the reader to write comments about the written matter. 

Perhaps your teacher has told you how wide to leave the margins on your written work. If she has, follow her directions. Otherwise use the rules given below. 

1. The margin at the top of the page should be about two inches. 

2. The margin at the left of the page should be about one inch. 

3. The margin at the right of the page should be about one inch. 

4. The margins at the left and right should be kept as straight as possible. 

5. The margin at the bottom of the page should be about one inch. 

Find some of the old papers that you have written. Compare the margins with the above rules. 

Think of letters that you have written. Did you leave margins?

Because the mailman must deliver a letter to the address on the envelope, it is important that the address be correctly and clearly written. 

Below are some rules for addressing an envelope that will help to have your letters delivered promptly. These rules were suggested by post-office workers.

Written Forms, page 14 EXERCISE 14 How to Make an Outline from a Report 

Miss Bright's class chose a report on safety from which to write an outline. Read the report.

Safety on a Bicycle 

Boys and girls who ride a bicycle these days must be very careful if they want to save lives and avoid broken bodies. They must know almost as much about traffic laws as their fathers who drive cars. On city streets a bicycle rider who goes through a red light might ride into the path of a motorist. He may do the same dangerous thing if he rides through a stop sign without stopping and looking. Another rule that a bicycle rider must follow is to hold out his hand for a turn. If he doesn't, he might turn into the path of an automobile whose driver can't tell where the bicycle is going.

Bicycle riders also must watch themselves carefully when they are riding onto a highway. They must "Stop and look!" They must be sure that the "coast is clear."

It is not easy to judge the speed of an approaching car. Traffic rules tell bicycle riders to ride on the right side of the road. Because a bicycle moves more slowly than a car, the rider should keep to the extreme right of the highway. He must ride in as straight a path as possible. This gives the driver of a car a chance to pass the bicycle safely.

Often a bicycle rider crosses vacant lots or rides at the side of a road. That is the time to watch for chuck holes. Such holes may cause nasty spills or they may cause a rider to go head first over the handlebars. On unpaved roads the rider must watch out for ruts. They're hard to get out of without a spill. Thick dust and loose gravel also will throw a bicycle rider if he turns fast.

Below is an outline that the class wrote from the report "Safety on a Bicycle." 

Can you see how each paragraph gave a topic and sub-topics? 

Safety on a Bicycle 

I. On city streets 

A. Watching traffic lights and stop signs 

B. Holding out hand to show turns

II. On highways 

A. Stopping before going onto a highway 

B. Riding on the right side of the pavement

III. Off the regular roads 

A. Crossing vacant lots or fields 

B. Riding on unpaved roads

Find a report or story that you like and write an outline of the first three paragraphs.

Name Written Forms, page 19 EXERCISE 19 Form for Writing a Play 

Perhaps you may want to write your favorite story in the form of a play. Below are some suggestions for the correct form to use. 1. 

Write the title at the top of the first page. (Remember margins.) 

2. Write the list of characters, or the cast, below the title. Each character should be called the name that he has in the play. Tell something about each character.

Example: 

Frank: a poor boy 

Don: his best friend 

3. Write the name of the character that is speaking before you write the conversation or dialogue. 

4. Write any stage direction. Stage directions tell the actors what to do in the play. They are written with the dialogue, but they are separated from the dialogue by brackets. Stage directions begin with a capital, but usually such directions are not complete sentences so they do not end with periods. 

Example: 

Frank: (Crosses the stage, puts down his basket of groceries, takes a handkerchief from his pocket and wipes his face) My, but this basket is heavy!

The unit "Spelling" consists of exercises to aid the pupil in the spelling and in the correct use of tricky words which he repeatedly uses in his written 

The authors do not attempt to supply a speller nor a program for either weekly or daily spelling lessons for individual grades. However, word lists are distributed appropriately throughout the exercises. Such lists are accompanied by hints on "How to Study Spelling." 

The emphasis as a basis for study is placed on seeing the word, pronouncing the word, hearing the word, saying the letters, watching for tricky letters, writing the word, and recalling the appearance of the word in the print and in the pupil's own handwriting.
