THE INN OF EXISTENCE By WM. CABELL BRUCE, Author of Benjamin Franklin Self-Revealed, 

A Biography of John Randolph of Roanoke, and Below the James, etc.

FOREWORD 

I am leaving the inn, known far and wide as the Inn of Human Existence, and issuing into the night that lasts longer than twelve hours. What kind of a place did you find it? Were its beds soft or hard? Were its bread and wine sweet or bitter?

Were your fellow-guests a surly, selfish set, or a brave, goodly company such as befits a spacious and a well-ordered and wellgarnished inn? If you were travelling a second time, by the same road, from the same far country to the same far country, would you willingly put up at the Inn of Human Existence once more? All of these questions I am being asked as I go; and it occurs to me, therefore, that it might not be amiss for me to jot down in the following pages some of the impressions made upon me by my brief sojourn in that inn. I am the more solicitous to do this, because, as the reader knows, whatever those impressions may be, I shall not be passing this way again. 1. 

Oh Inn! that stands beside the road, that leads from night to night,

I'm leaving now thy spacious halls and chambers full of light, Thy ruddy hearth, thy jolly wine, thy gracious company, And naught see I but darkness, As from thy door I hie.

2. 

Oh Inn! that stands beside the road, that leads from Birth to Death, To bewail my parting from thee, would be but squandered breath.

It is but justly due to thee that I should haste to say 

How bravely thou didst serve me,

While my strength sufficed to pay. 

Whither am I going? Ah! that no darkling wight can tell,

Mayhap, to fields of azure, mayhap of asphodel, Or, best of all, perchance, to the Jesus, pure and meek, Of Whom, in my sweet youth, I heard my saintly Mother speak. 

I must off! that is all that it concerns me now to know,

With staff and scrip, and fumbling feet, and head declining low.

Few calmer guests, in going, the Inn of Life has seen, And none who less lamented that he its guest had been.

BIRTH. 

More than once in English Literature recurs the thought that, if we could only foresee Birth, we should anticipate its approach with even more fear than we do that of Death. Thus, in 

Fletcher's Philaster, Bellario says of Death: "'Tis less than to be born; a lasting sleep; 

A quiet resting from all jealousy; 

A thing we all pursue; I know besides 

It is but giving over of a game 

That must be lost.”

The reflection is not a just one; for it presupposes a degree of pre-natal consciousness that would enable us to forecast the entire course of human life, and to bring under a single coup d'oeil all its disappointments and misfortunes; including the supreme catastrophe of Death itself. Manifestly, under such circumstances, the inevitability of Birth would exercise the same soothing influence over our apprehensions that Bellario ascribes to the certainty of Death.

And as for the other reassuring suggestions that he derives from our mortality, it is obvious, too, that, as Death surely follows Birth in the end, they would tend to reconcile us to the latter as well as to the former. If it be said that the prospect of Birth might well include things even more appalling than 

Death, such as racking, unremitting disease, or disgrace, it may be replied that self-inflicted Death, if Death is such a benison to us, as Bellario would make us believe, is always at hand to provide us with an escape from such intolerable woe; for, as a greater dramatist than Fletcher tells us: "Every bondman in his own hand bears

The power to cancel his captivity." 

The truth is that, if we could look forward to Birth through the lens of human maturity, our dread would be not of its coming but of its failure to come. The same motives that make us shrink from the transition from Light and Being to Darkness and Nonentity, which we term Death, would reversely make us eager for the transition from Darkness and Nonentity to Light and Being which we call Birth. 

It is only after the actual trials of post-natal existence that any sentient human creature would be likely to wish, in the words of Jeremiah, that his mother might have been his grave. 

Now that Theology is so lukewarm about hell-fire, a wise man should be philosopher enough to accept both Birth and Death as inseparable features of a plan of creation which he had no hand in fashioning, but which bestows on him decidedly more happiness than unhappiness. I will not say with Benjamin Franklin that, compared with the joyous tenor of life, on the whole, its occasional calamities are but as the prickings of a pin, but I do

say that the very tenacity with which we cling to life, except under circumstances of extreme mental or physical distress, is proof enough in itself that it is usually happy. 

II. 

THE CUP OF LIFE. 

Life's cup is nectar at the brink, Midway a palatable drink, And wormwood at the bottom. 

Jas. Smith, Author of Rejected

Addresses. 

That the bubbling, sparkling drops which mantle the Cup of Life have a truly nectarean quality, no one, of course, can deny. 

Except under conditions distinctly abnormal, perfect health is always joyous, and it is in youth that our bodily vigor is at its best, and our corporeal senses are most acutely alive to everything that ministers to their delight. At this stage of human existence, our vital activities are, as a rule, flawlessly automatic, and our minds are as unconscious of our good health, until actually deranged by some unexpected cause, as they are of some monotonous flow of sound, until cut short by abrupt cessation. To youth belong the lithe, supple limbs, the deep, free respiration and the fresh, dewy outlook which make the mere play of the lungs and heart, in the springtide of existence, a sensual luxury. Every physical craving is then set to its sharpest edge. We eat as though food and drink were heavenly ambrosia, or milk of our Blessed Lady; we sleep as though the oblivion of Sleep were as dateless as that of Death, and Love bewitches our vision as though we had drunk of the juice which, "On sleeping eyelids laid, Will make or man or woman madly dote 

Upon the next live creature that it sees."

The auroral flush of the morning has not yet faded into the light of common day; men and women have not yet entirely ceased to be the bright apparitions that they are to the marvelling eye of childhood; and repetition, familiarity, and the dreary recurrence of the same events, feelings and thoughts, day after day,

have not yet imparted a drab aspect to the face of the Universe. 

But I do not admit that Life's Cup is midway a palatable drink only; in other words, at most, merely a satisfactory home-brew substitute for the exhilarating champagne of youth. Middle Age is usually free from the heady impulses and the urgent desires that so often work the utter ruin of a young man. It has learnt by experience the supreme lesson of Epicurism that excess of pleasure is the death of pleasure. What it has lost in feverish excitement and self-indulgence, it more than gains in "that severe content", which, Keats finely tells us, "comes of thought and musing". 

Its happiness is none the less real because it is not a leaping, crackling flame but a steady, equable glow. If our physical sensations and appetites are not so lively or so eager in the meridian of life as in its forenoon, something more than mere palatable compensations for that fact is to be found in the enhanced selfcontrol, bred by long observation of human conduct, and its consequences, and the increased discipline and strength, fostered by the habitual exercise of the intellectual faculties, which are generally the concomitants of Middle Age. 

Nor do I admit that Life's Cup is wormwood at the bottom; though it is true enough that the human body sometimes limps or crawls on after it has ceased to be anything but a grievous burden to the Human Spirit. Certainly, such an idea is repugnant to the creed which assures the Christian that all his sorrows, including the infirmities of Old Age, as well as his joys, are ordered by a benignant Providence, and admonishes him that without the Cross, there can be no Crown. Neither is such an idea reconcilable with any rational system of human philosophy which surveys life, as a whole, from the cradle to the grave, balances all its profits and losses, and reaches the conclusion that the general average of human happiness from Infancy to Death is high enough to "absorb", to use a commercial phrase, the physical pains and penalties of living too long. 

III. 

THE CUP OF LIFE-(Continued). 

I have read somewhere that Washington, when asked whether he would be willing to live life over, replied that nothing could induce him to do so, but that Daniel Morgan, when the same

question was put to him, exclaimed: "Yes! even though it were only as a galley slave". Franklin, in his inimitable way, declared that he was ready to live his life all over, if he were but allowed the privilege of a printer of correcting in the second edition the errata of the first. Personally, I should be unwilling to live a second time, whether I were granted the opportunity to repeat my own life, or to live that of some other person, however prosperous or distinguished; and I say this despite the fact that, the usual measure of earthly disappointment and sorrow aside, my life so far has been wanting in none of the essential elements of human felicity. How could anyone contentedly live the physical and spiritual life of some other person? Were my existence to undergo such a metempsychosis, I should no longer be I, and my mind rejects the idea that anyone, who had not basely abused the gift of living, could be willing to merge his own. identity, body and soul, completely, into that of another. might not, by any means, be as fine a thing; but, as John Randolph of Roanoke once sententiously affirmed, every man is of some importance to himself. Or I might illustrate my point by recalling what Touchstone said of Audrey: "A poor Virgin, Sir, an illfavoured thing, Sir, but mine own; a poor humour of mine, Sir, to take that that no man else will". Indeed, it is hard to see how anyone could be correctly said to be living life over, if living it entirely through the agency of a totally different individuality from his own. Under such circumstances, he, himself, would, for all practical purposes, be as dead as if he had actually died in the ordinary way. It

And how could I relish the idea of commencing again at the same beginning point, and planting my feet, step after step, along the way of life, in the same footprints as before. Even when I take an afternoon walk there is a certain degree of staleness to me in the thought of returning home by the self-same path that led me away from it. The element of surprise, that is the prosperity of wit, must mark the incidents of human life, if they are to have any true zest, and they cannot have this quality, if they are merely chewed over like the cud of a ruminating animal. 

How dull would life necessarily be, even on its happier side, if we had nothing pleasant to look forward to except the reproduction, in a second existence, of the agreeable events that had 10

accompanied our first; and how unspeakably tragic would it be, if we could forsee, as in a glass, all the misfortune, including the misery inflicted by Death on Love, with which the second existence was to be strewn. No! annihilate me, doom me to be forever with those that are at rest, rather than devote me to a duplicate term of existence without a solitary sensation or transaction that would not be clearly forecast when it began. 

In expressing these feelings, however, I am, of course, assuming that such a state of existence would be attended by foreknowledge, in every respect, of what it had in store for me at every stage of its progress. Could I, by means of some opiate, taken in its beginning, be made wholly oblivious to the history of my first life, I should gladly be willing to live again that life, with all its checkered vicissitudes of good and bad fortune. But manifestly, some one else, who was born under a less friendly star than I was, might be very differently minded with respect to his life.

IV. 

MARRIAGE. 

In the Virginia community into which I was born, in 1860, divorce was almost unknown. "Once a wife, always a wife”, was the despotic rule which the old Virginia Society imposed upon its members. It deemed divorce such a disgraceful thing per se that the relative responsibility of the husband and wife for it received only lukewarm consideration. And what I have said of divorce in Virginia at the time of my birth, was doubtless true of it in other parts of the United States at that time. But, within the last half-century, the ratio of divorce to marriage, in our country, has increased to such a startling extent that, in the year 1925, it was, for the whole United States, not less than 1 to 6.73. In the State of Washington, it was as high as 1 to 

4.34. How appalling, how utterly subversive of all that has, in the past been supposed to constitute the strength of the Home and the State, how destructive of everything, that has, in a happier age, invested the commerce of sex with dignity and beauty, is the social revolution that these damning figures reveal. 

It is hardly necessary to point out the vast volume of acrid dis- 11

cord and the wide-spread and scandalous neglect of duty, that they indicate; nor the pernicious example of conjugal faithlessness that they present to the children of divorced parents. Marriage is all that there is of Love worth the name, and Duty is two thirds of all true married love. The marital tie and the scrupulous performance of the social and moral obligations that it creates are the only things capable of spiritualizing and dignifying sexual coition, and protracting its vividness and delight to the very verge of life. The love that lyrical poetry has endued with such ineffable charm, that Religion has sanctified with its holy offices, that parental and filial affection have done so much to refine and exalt, and that the commonwealth has been so eager to legitimize and safeguard would be reduced to an ignoble estate, indeed, if it were deprived of its ethical responsibilities, and became nothing but the sport of the wayward and fugitive passion that, unless blended with some element of moral permanence, is condemned by the very law of its perishable nature, sooner or later, to sink into "sad satiety". Human existence furnishes no more gracious or inspiring spectacle than that of a long, and happily, wedded husband and wife, who have, through all its accidents and vicissitudes, found their supreme joy in their intimate union, and their profoundest justification for self-approval in the conscientious discharge of the parental and other duties which spring from such an union. Nor does human existence furnish many spectacles more painful and forbidding than that of two persons who, under the stimulus of a brief erotic impulse, have assumed the grave responsibilities of matrimony without any real intention of complying with them. The present laxity of the nuptial bond, in the United States, is due partly to the general decline of the dogma which insisted that marriage was a religious sacrament, partly to the morbid hypertrophy of the senses, produced, by an age of luxury and widely diffused self-indulgence, and partly to the increasing economic independence of women. Will the current tendency keep on until the present ratio of divorce to marriage, 1 to 6.73, shall become as high as 1 to 2, or even 1 to 1, and the institution of marriage, as it long existed in our country, shall be displaced by a system of temporary connections, dissoluble at the will of either party, as caprice may dictate? An individual opinion on this subject 12

has little value; but, should things become much worse, certainly some kind of old-age pension will have to be devised by the Legislature for the maintenance of thousands of superannuated women who shall have become too timeworn to hope for even a transitory husband; and, as to the children of parents, who might be separated from each other, at any moment, there would seem to be nothing that could be done for them except to place them under the exclusive care of the State, whose servants, however, be it remembered, would also have been denied the invaluable training of the home, as the home had once been organized.

All this sounds very speculative; but it really is not. In the province of social life, at any rate, there is much profound truth in the observation of Hamlet that "there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so"; and, as a rule, an effort to resist any persistent social change is likely to be as futile as would be an effort to arrest a rise or fall in the temperature of the atmosphere. It is proverbial that revolutions seldom retrace their steps; and, unless the evils, begotten by the present instability of the marriage relation, shall call into being some such stirring evangel of reform as has more than once, in the history of the human race, awakened it to a bitter sense of its own profligacy, it is more probable that the present movement towards practically unrestrained divorce will culminate in the negation of all morality and social decency, as now understood, commonly known as free love, than in a penitent return to the honorable and duteous state of matrimony, about which the purest and noblest ideals of humanity have clustered for so many centuries. 

V. 

WOMAN. 

In my time, woman, in the United States, has drifted away from the status that she formerly occupied. As far as the ineffaceable differentiations of sex would permit, she has become distinctly more like man. She now looks to justice rather than to chivalrous tenderness for protection, and, as a matter of course, in acquiring the rights, enjoyed by the other sex, she has forfeited many of the special privileges that were once accorded to 13

her. It can no longer be cynically said of man and wife that they are one, but that the man is the one. As a rule, a woman, married or unmarried, holds her property by as absolute and indefeasible a title as does a man; and, as a general thing, her power to contract, married or unmarried, is as plenary as his. Her graver civil disabilities, of every kind, have been brought to an end; and, above all, she possesses the right that is claimed to be preservative of all rights, that is the right to vote. Nor is she shut out from employment in any field of human energy where the superior brute force of the male is not indispensable. It was not in the power of even such radical changes as these to make a woman a man, but they have made women not a little mannish; as witness the masculinity that her dress has taken on in some respects, and the extent to which she has contracted the male habits of drinking cocktails and smoking cigarettes. More significant still is the extent to which the artifices and blandishments of innocent coquetry which she once practiced with such skill and effect, have been succeeded by a matter-of-fact forthrightness in her intercourse with men that to a conservative like myself is by no means so ingratiating a thing. It has been said that God did not take woman from man's head because that would have been making her his master, nor from his feet because that would have been making her his slave, but from his side because that was making her his equal. The legal equal of man, in every way, is woman in the United States tending to become. That this ideally speaking, is but the just status for her under the conditions of modern civilization, must be admitted. But it may well be asked whether, with the loss of her dependence on man, she has not also lost some of the charm upon which her weakness formerly had to rely for its ascendancy. And there can be little doubt that her latter day equality with men, economically and politically, has made her much quicker than she was in the past to seek in the Divorce Court relief from a distasteful marriage. 

But "male and female created he them"; and it is curious to note how essentially unaltered the feminist movement has left the character of women in the United States. The ballot, the cocktail, and the cigarette have come in, but the rouge pot, the lip stick and the powder puff have not gone out. they were never so much in evidence as now. 

On the contrary, Man and woman 14

are still drawn to each other by natural affinities that can never cease to operate except with the destruction of the human race itself. She is the High Priestess of Eros; she is the mighty mother of the tramping generations of men. "Man", as Hudibras tells us, "Brings nothing but the stuff

She frames the wond'rous fabric of." 

No matter how much time she may give to Minerva, to Themis, to Aesculapius, or to other patrons of callings, which were once the callings exclusively of men, she will always have to reserve a large part of her time for Lucina, the divinity of child-birth, and the Lares and Penates of the domestic household. It is no longer true as it was in Byron's time that "Man's love is of man's life a thing apart; 'Tis woman's whole existence."

But no revolution of circumstances is conceivable, far-reaching enough to make the primary aims and interests of Woman anything except what they have always been. The only question is whether, with the growing laxity of the marriage tie, and the disastrous violence that it has worked to the ideals and standards by which her past has been moulded, she may not be a materially different thing, in the future, from the being which Christianity did so much to exalt, and the chivalrous feelings of later ages so much to soften and beautify, and which is so winningly pictured in the contrast of Paradise Lost: "For contemplation he and valor formed, For softness she and sweet attractive grace."

VI 

SIGHS FROM THE TREE IGDRASIL. 

(Mem.: At one time or another, all of us have Death brought home to us through those of whom it deprives us. The following paper was written many years ago by a bereaved father, whose name I withhold, immediately after the death of his infant son; and it reflects so faithfully the hard laws of human existence that I feel that, after such a long lapse of time, there is no reason why it should not be published.) 15

It was not until I was about twelve or thirteen years of age that I had a vivid sense of human mortality brought home to me. Of course, I had previously realized in the vague, illdefined manner of a child that our hold upon existence is not perpetual, and that, for every individual, there is a dread hour when life ceases, and its warm habitation of flesh is no longer tenanted by urgent needs and keen sensations. Once, my father had returned from the funeral of a friend with a face so full of distant sadness that, young as I was, I could not bear to scan it. 

More than once, I had seen a concourse of negro mourners gather in the little graveyard on his plantation, where the dead of the race were usually buried, and attest in those plaintive, measured cadences of theirs how lowly is the estate of death even to the lowliest. Swiftly, however, as a shadow from the wing of a passing bird, these incidents had flitted over the sunny surfaces of my life, and left no traces of their presence behind them. 

It was reserved for a later day to develop the personal experience, to which I began by alluding. 

I was at school in Tidewater, Virginia, and, among my schoolmates there, was a boy a few years older than myself, named John Barton. Like everything that we see in the fresh, charmed atmosphere of the early morning of our lives, his image is, at this moment, reflected with startling fidelity in my memory.

He was a fair-haired fellow, with a carmine flush in his cheeks that a pale girl might well have envied. But, in the whole round of my schoolmates, no one was further removed from effeminacy than he. He had all the hardy vigor derived from constant companionship with the open air. The pointer did not more dearly love the stubble-field, nor the thrush the greenwood. Any limits less expanded than the walls of the terrestrial horizon chafed him. 

Indeed, even they seemed to inspire his restless spirit with a feeling of constraint; for I remember that he lopped off the branches of a tall, solitary poplar, far away from any human abode, and frequently indulged the habit of ascending to its summit, and intently surveying its tributary prospect. Every interval that he could extort from the studies that claimed his reluctant attention, found him wandering, gun in hand, over the wide domain, attached to the school. He knew the range of every covey of partridges, the daily routine of every hawk, the 16

secret dens of all sorts of climbing and burrowing creatureseven of the shyest. To out-wit the wild geese, that never settled down upon the tender wheat fields except with broad stretches of level ground between them and the nearest cover; to paddle noiselessly around the sylvan bends of a tortuous creek upon a flock of feeding ducks; to listen in the hushed, ruddy woods at sunset for the pattering twig, that heralded a coming squirrelthese were some of the pastimes which, pursued by him with passionate ardor as they were, apparently marked him out as a veritable child of nature, destined to rove long and far upon her bosom, ere he sank into it. 

Vain presage! One Saturday morning, he and a cousin of mine, about as old as he, and I shouldered our axes, and started off for a primeval forest, on the banks of the Pamunkey, in quest of raccoons. This forest covered a great area of flat, depressed ground, too low and wet to be profitably cultivated. 

Scattered everywhere through it were stagnant pools of water, accumulated by the rains, or left by the high tides, that had backed up from the river along a sluggish bayou, and had thence overflowed into the thousand lateral channels prescribed by their predecessors. Scattered everywhere through it, too, were the rotting trunks of prostrate trees, which had germinated, flourished, outlived the narrow term allotted to man by the Psalmist, and then yielded to the tireless force of gravitation, and fallen. Rising above these dead stocks, through the encumbering underbrush, were the later generations of ash and black gum, sprung from their loins, which had shot up in many cases to extraordinary altitudes from gnarled roots, worn bare by the action of water, and had woven with their interlocked branches a dark crown of shade. Mantled with glossy verdure in spring and summer; kindled into blazing splendor by the fiery fingers of the frost in autumn; traced in lines of almost feathery delicacy against the steel-blue sky in winter; blended with the occurrence that I am about to relate, that forest is often in my thoughts. 

Descending from the plateau on its east side, we penetrated a considerable distance into it, narrowly scrutinizing the boles of the trees as we went. Finally we observed a tree which promised good results. A long streak of abraded bark, extending from its base to a round hole far up its stem, indicated to our 17

practiced vision, that one of the creatures sought by us was probably dozing in its hollow interior. Immediately, my two friends took off their coats, and began to cut it down. I wan- dered off a little space from them. As their task approached completion, they shouted to me to hurry back. I reached them just as the tree was beginning to totter, and to snap with its leaning weight, in quick succession, the few fibres, that still connected it with its stump. Unconsciously, we ranged ourselves in a line, so close to each other that our shoulders touched, he in the middle, I on his left, and my cousin on his right. Our eyes were fastened with such eagerness upon the butt that it did not occur to us to look for danger in the upper boughs. Suddenly, a long, lithe tapering limb, torn from a neighboring tree by them, in their descent, gleamed for one incalculable moment in my sight, as it madly shot down upon us from above like the projected body of a mighty anaconda, and the next smote him with its heavier extremity just over the head. He did not stagger. 

He did not emit a sound. The blow was too deadly for that. He fell right back at full length speechless and senseless upon his spine, and lay as straight as though his mother had composed his limbs. ror. 

For a few seconds, my cousin and I were palsied with hor- Rallying from this state, we at once addressed ourselves to the emergency. There were some laborers at work in a field about a mile away, and they had an ox cart with them. My cousin hastened to them as rapidly as the impeding conditions of the forest would permit; leaving me alone to look after our wounded companion, until he returned with help. How grave the injury inflicted upon the latter was, I did not grasp at the time. I was in no frame of mind accurately to estimate the force of the stroke that felled him. Nor would I have been under less agitating circumstances; for the sudden cessation of a life, familiar to me in all its vital relationships, was something that I had never known, and was, therefore, something that I was not quick to assume. My first act was to run off to one of the pools, that I have mentioned, and to fill my hat with water. Returning, I poured a portion of its contents upon the upturned face. The only response was a series of sighs so deep, so dolorous, so sug- 18

gestive of severance from beloved associations that I could hardly command enough resolution to remain within ear-shot of them. 

After a protracted interval of suspense, my cousin came back. With him was the plantation overseer, a muscular, powerful man. 

They had sent the cart around to a side of the forest much nearer to the scene of the casualty than the one by which we had entered. Taking the poor lad up in his arms, and pillowing his head as gently as he could against his shoulder, the overseer bore him to the rude vehicle, and placed him in it. 

Leaving the overseer in charge of him, my cousin and myself then set out, ahead of the creeping steers, for the school, to break the news, and procure a physician as soon as possible. 

When we arrived, our faces were so eloquent that there was no need for our lips to speak. My cousin's mother, the wife of our principal, uttered a sharp cry of anguish as soon as her eyes caught sight of our ominous countenances, and then observed the absence of the adventurous boy, who had left the house with us in the morning.

A messenger was straightway dispatched for a doctor, the cart arrived, John was lifted out of it, still breathing, and laid upon a lounge. When the doctor came, he pronounced his hurt mortal, and declared that he could live only a short while. Then, for the first time, my cousin and I apprehended the full meaning of what we had witnessed. We could not remain in that house. 

The season was winter, but, indifferent to its inclemency, we drifted away to a sequestered slope on a hillside near by, and threw ourselves down upon the ground, and pressed our faces against it, and sobbed and sobbed as if our hearts would break. 

It was like fire from the heavens; so sudden, mysterious and uncontrollable was it all. A few hours before, we were three jocund boys, light of heart as a leaping brook, and now there were, or soon would be, but two of us, and those two abject as the earth under the visitation of the new, strange all-imperious principle that had sped into our presence without a warning, snatched a comrade from us, and stirred the very hair of our own heads as it passed. After remaining in this condition, where we were, until grief had exhausted itself with its own transports, we were drawn back to the house by a faint hope that the judgment of the doctor might have been hasty. We approached it tremblingly; scarcely daring to ask the question that involved such a 19

fateful answer.

When we did, it was to have our worst fears verified. The doctor was right. He was dead. 

His body was taken to Baltimore, where his parents resided. 

His mother was passionately devoted to him; and, when the first frantic outurst of her distress had subsided, she became possessed with the same impulse that subsequently led Eugenia to cross the sea, and to seek the precise spot in the tall grass of South Africa upon which the last hope of the Bonapartists had yielded up his life to naked savages. One day, she arrived at the school, and, the next, my cousin and I conducted her to the scene of the tragedy. We seated her upon the trunk of a lying tree; perhaps the trunk of the one itself that had been the occasion of her affliction. She made us recount all the circumstances preceding and accompanying the lethal blow. She made us point out exactly where her son stood when conscious life deserted him. She even cast a shuddering glance at the murderous limb, that still lay as it had finally rested after rebounding from his temples. Not until all this had been done, and she had sat for a long time, silent and motionless, and betraying only in her eyes the overflowing abundance of her heart, did she obtain any inward peace.

The fleet years fled by. I left this school, and passed to one of higher grade. I studied law, and began to practice it; and later, I married. Some sixteen years had elapsed since John expired. I had formed many friendships, more or less intimate; my family connections were uncommonly numerous; and I had lived to see all the eight children of my father and mother, who were still hale, attain manhood and womanhood; yet, not once, during this time, had any cherished tie, that bound me to a fellow-creature, been sundered. Then came the death of my dear boyhood friend, James Harrison. We had been roommates at the school of higher grade just referred to. In a fit of despondency, he took his life with his own hand, repining, it was said, in a moment of recurring consciousness, just before he died, at his rash act. A few days ago, I was glancing over a grotesquely illustrated edition of Shakespeare, which my father had at college more than forty years ago, and that I would not part with even for a newly discovered play of the author. Strangely enough, although, perhaps, not so strangely, in view of the many defacing 20

additions, that the book had received, in the course of time, I then first noticed that, when we were at school together, James had underscored, and also endorsed, with his initials those tremendous lines:

There is a tide in the affairs of men, Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune,

Omitted, all the voyage of their life 

Is bound in shallows and in miseries.

Formerly, when I read these lines, the leading image presented to my mind by them was that of the resistless seas, rising up from main to main at the call of the moon, to follow her shining disk.

Now, when I read them, I see little but a lonely strand, forsaken by the ebbing waters, a white face and a form as limp as a spray of cast-up sea-weed. 

One night, after the death of James, I was sitting in my library at a late hour, toiling over work which rarely lays aside its whip and spur. It was a calm, clear starry night, bland as befitted the heavenly spell of weather, that tempered Christmastide that year. Silence and sleep were weaving their fleecy folds about the great city in which I lived, and stilling the last pulses of waking existence in its darkened palaces and tenements. No voices ascended to my ear from the street except, at long intervals, such as but serve to accentuate midnight in crowded centres of population; the rumble of a belated wagon, attended by the tremor that reminds us how perishable is even the earth itself; the hurrying footfall sounding as distinctly as though the pavements were vaulted; the short, fretful expirations of a locomotive starting out upon its nocturnal journey. Unexpectedly, there was a ring at the door-bell, and a few minutes afterwards, a telegram was laid in my hands, announcing that my sister, Dora, had just died suddenly. 

Dead! Oh! Most High! How could that be? 

Had not my wife and myself visited her only a week or so before at her home in Richmond, Virginia? When did her dark brown eye ever glisten with a tenderer or brighter light? When did her step ever leave a more fugitive impress? When did her cheek ever bring deeper confusion upon the rose? The warm currents of kindred blood, quickened by her affectionate greeting, were still 21

circulating through my heart. I still felt the glow of her farewell kiss upon my lips. So youthful, so animated, so intelligent, so suggestive of love and all his fluttering train, so useful, so benevolent, the adored of father, mother, husband, brothers, sister, friends, the youngest of all the eight, how could it be, how could it be that she had died?

These were the first sensations of blind grief; for thoughts, of course, they were not. They were succeeded, as the hard truth forced its way into my mind, by a serener and wider range of retrospection. Ah! How clearly did I remember the day of her birth; the happy stir from chamber to kitchen, which signalized the event; the additional joy which her sex created in that household of boys; the confused impressions excited in me, a child. And then how clearly did I remember the later stages of her beauteous existence; how she increased in stature until she grew like a tall and graceful flower, swaying in the wind; how insensibly her girlhood glided into womanhood; how admired she was of every drawing room and assembly, where form and soul and intellect claimed their high prerogatives; and how potent was the spell of her presence to bring chivalrous devotion from all sides to her feet until she wedded a writer of romances, who found his heroines in her, and made her brief married life an idyl. And then alas! there came over me the recollection of my own shortcomings. How feebly, during her lifetime, had I let her know how much she was to me! I had allowed the absorbing uncertainties of early manhood to foster a habit of inattention to all the nicer inflections by which feeling is communicated. 

There was no reason why the physical distance between us should have separated us as it did. I should have wrested myself from business, and its purely selfish interests, oftener than I had done to visit her. Even if this had not been practicable, I should at least have more frequently marked my enforced absence by sending her a book, a picture, or some other trifle that tends to freshen family affection with a still sprightlier green. Bitterest thought of all, I had not only been guilty of neglect, but of a neglect that I would never again have an opportunity to repair. No, never more; no matter how free from cares I might become; or how long the summer days might be; or how eagerly I might yearn to make full atonement. Were such an apportunity to return-I 22

started at the very reflection-with what tremulous gratitude would I embrace it! 

Morn broke fair and radiant. Before its gleam, I had sunk into a short and fitful sleep. For a second, as my opening eyes filled with cheerful sunshine, my mind did not revert to its sorrow. Then, with a gush of refluent force, the latter swept over me again, causing me to recoil from the cloudless day, as if it mocked me. Rising, my wife and myself made rapid preparations for our journey. These were so engrossing that I was once more lulled into a state of comparative forgetfulness, only once more to feel a flooding sense of loss, when my wife came into my presence, clad from head to foot in black.

A little later, we started for the station. How vainly did the shadowy throngs, urging their eager steps towards the heart of the city, seem to be disquieted! Like dull, pictured figures they lingered but a moment upon my wearied retina. A few years more, and they too, in the language of John Tyndall, would melt away like streaks of morning cloud into the infinite azure of the past. I avoided them; I knew that questions would be asked; and that answers would have to be given, and that even what is meant for balm inflames new wounds; however healing it may be subsequently, when administered by sympathetic hands. 

On the train, we met another friend, whom it was impossible to avoid. From time to time, his high talk beguiled me almost into the delusion that I had been dreaming, and I hugged the cheat, so poignant, in pauses of the conversation, was the reflection that every revolution of the car wheels brought me nearer to incontestable proof of the cruel truth.

We arrived at the house in Richmond, where she resided. 

When we were last there, her happy smile diffused light and warmth throughout it. Tranquil, melodious speech, born of intimate intercourse, music, laughter, all the characteristics of fond hospitality conspired to engrave it upon our memories as a House Beautiful. Now, the Valley of the Shadow of Death encompassed it. Closed shutters excluded every ray of the Sun. Sobs alone broke the silence of the cheerless rooms. In the passages, friends flitted noiselessly to and fro, performing the little offices that impart such increased pangs of bereavement, when performed by the bereaved. In her chamber, she lay oblivious to all. 23

The funeral took place. It was not so painful to see the vast assemblage of young and old, collected in the church, where she worshipped, to testify with moist eyes to what she had been in that community. It was not so painful to hear the solemn and impressive service of the Episcopal Church read by the venerable clergyman who knew her well. Its sublime words rebuke even human grief as vain, and hold out comforting pledges of a time when what is sown in dishonor and weakness is raised in glory and power.

But my heart flowed like a stream from the cloven rock when, from the choir behind me, arose a single voice so deep, so flexible, so mellow that the words of promise that it syllabled were drowned in the tide of harmony that bore them along. Now it sank to the lowest depths of grief; stirring the profoundest modulations of the latter into life; now it pleaded like the voice of an interceding angel; now, swelling with ecstasy, it ascended until it was scarcely able to sustain its burden of sound; and then, again, it died away by degrees until it seemed to be lost in an element more refined than the circumambient air. I have never seen the features of the singer, and, to this day, I feel as if it were no human voice but the spirit of my own desolated being that sung that hymn. 

The same day she was laid in Hollywood. As the earth received her into its arms, the sweet star of darkling eve shone softly above her like a visible token of divine assurance. At the foot of the open bluff, upon which her grave is situated, is the storied river so dear to the commonwealth of her fathers. In its long leap to the tide level below, it foams and surges about the islets and granite ledges, which obstruct its path, and forever murmurs a plain akin to that of humanity, but, at the end of its descent, it expands into a surface as restful and placid as the face of the alluvial meadows that fringe it.

The fleet years fled by. A son was born unto us. That natal cry in our childless home, who can measure the happiness that it begat? For us it opened up a whole realm of new interests and pleasures. The event had been duly anticipated. Awaiting the little sybarite, was every sort of warm, fleecy garment or covering that his tender infancy could call for. To these, as time progressed, were added, from one source or another, so many trinkets, gewgaws and toys that I sometimes wondered whether any adult 24

ever did have as many friends as a baby. For several weeks, he lay almost lifeless to an observer in his crib, wrapt up in his snug cocoon of wool, and of all that solicits the desire of manhood asking only sleep and drink. But the boundaries of his existence soon widened. First, a playful smile bespoke the dawn of

Intelligence and Human Sympathy. Then, he commenced to look about him, and to take cognizance of the sounds and apparitions of the miraculous universe around him; pointing his tiny finger earnestly towards objects to show that he wished to handle as well as eye the brave new world, that he was to inhabit. 

Especially did he affect the gaudy flowers of the scarlet geranium. 

Flaming candles and freshly lighted gas jets also came in for a share of his attention. One night, I took him in my arms and bore him to a window from which he could see the lamplit city below glittering like a fallen firmament. He gazed at it fixedly and soberly as if he felt that he would have to begin all his investigations over again. Brilliant sunshine, too, he never tired of basking in. But genuine boy that he was, he was never so elated as when the sight of a cat, a dog, or a horse aroused him to an eager pitch of excitement. 

No child was ever more idolized; rarely has there been one so full of vital energy. So vivacious was he that stratagem was usually employed to put him to sleep during the day or at evening. Again and again, he would awake in the night, not to fret or cry but solely to play. With what mixed feelings would tired nature hear the initiatory gurgle with which he would begin on these occasions! In the morning, he was up betimes, and the first thing that reached my ear would be the sound of his blithe clamor. As for crossing him, nobody ever contemplated it. 

What if the piano had been tuned only last week? There might be worse music than that of his artless palm smiting its keys. What if he did sit at meat with us, drumming his chair with a silver spoon until it echoed like a dead tree under the bill of a logcock, or, when he wearied of that, letting the spoon fall to the floor as fast as it was picked up for no purpose in the world but to luxuriate in its clangor as it fell? And what if he did form all kinds of capricious and irrational prepossessions, and press them with the entire force of his limbs and lungs, when not gratified; such as his predilection for a hanging thermometer or the mosquito net- 25

hook in our chamber ceiling that there was no ladder in the house long enough to extend to. No one minded; he was a chartered libertine. 

He lived nearly two years. He learned to lisp my name, and to listen for my step upon the staircase. He was learning to walk and to repay with caresses the boundless devotion of which he was the object when he was stricken with scarlet fever, and, after two weeks of suffering, passed away like a violet from the vale. He lies beneath some fine sycamores, and my footsteps, which he hears no longer, often wend thither, so short in that peaceful spot seems the distance between him, who is the Resurrection and the Life, and the little children that He suffers to come unto Him; and so near I hope is the prospect of my reunion with him.

On his headstone, are inscribed these words, uttered by another despoiled father, Ralph Waldo Emerson, in his anguish:

The gracious boy, who did adorn, The world whereinto he was born, And by his countenance repay 

The favor of the loving Day.

Here ends this history. Reader! The mournful sighs that breathe through its pages, what are they but the whispers of thy own sad soul?

VII. 

THE TRUE PHILOSOPHY OF THE HUMAN BODY. 

The sinfulness of our carnal promptings has been decidedly too much accentuated. For the idea that the human body is a "darksome house of mortal clay" (to use Milton's image) contaminated by original sin, that can be kept subdued only by the austerest pains and penalties, physical or legal, Christianity, much as the world is indebted to it in other respects, is principally responsible. That idea was alien to Greek thought; for to the 

Greek the human body was not "a darksome house of mortal clay" but a lustrous tenement of polished marble. It began with the extreme mortifications of the flesh, to which not a few of the early Christian pietests subjected themselves; it, later, 26

asserted itself in many other ascetic or puritanical forms; and, today, even in our own land, it survives, among other things, in the evangelical belief, that the human desire to drink alcoholic beverages calls for absolute prohibition by the State. The idea is a totally false one. It is quite true that, at times, we form certain cravings such as that for some sort of narcotic drug which are alogether morbid, and, if yielded to, almost invariably lead to the ruin of human health and character. Such cravings are vigorously proscribed by law, and the laws proscribing them are effectively enforced, because these laws are sternly upheld by Public Opinion. But, as a rule, our lawful corporeal appetites are entirely normal and legitimate, and, if properly safeguarded, do not necessarily work injury either to our health or morals. The abuse of them is altogether in the misuse of them. They all tend, in one degree or another, to excess, and, in excess, to loss of physical vigor and moral stamina; and it is our duty ever to maintain a vigilant watch over them; but none of them are of such an imperious description that they cannot be held safely in check, and be made to minister merely to the innocent pleasure of agreeable and rational living. Why, then, should they not all be moderately indulged?

So long as we do not harm ourselves or others, there is no more reason why we should not gratify these physical appetites than our moral or intellectual wants. Rightly considered, they are as much a part of us as the latter; nor should it be forgotten that the total measure of happiness, derived by human beings from the satisfaction of their physical propensities, is far greater than that derived by them from the gratification of their moral sentiments or their mental predilections; if, for no other reason, because the need for the nourishment of our moral or intellectual being is by no means so general or importunate as the need for the hospitable reception of the throng of "soft and delicate desires" by which our bodies are almost unremittingly solicited, and which are shared by the moralist and the man of genius as well as the humblest member of society. The arts of distillation and fermentation are among the simplest of all industrial arts. Man has been drinking alcoholic potations as long, perhaps, as he has been drinking milk from his mother's breasts. Our Lord thought so highly of wine that he used his 27

miraculous gift at Cana to convert six jars of water into it. 

For nineteen centuries his blood has been symbolized in the wine, drunk as a part of the holy sacraments of the Christian Church. 

Just how a clerical prohibitionist of our day can reconcile the continuance of this symbol with his fanatical conception of strong drink as a criminal outlaw is one of those problems that might well tax even the theological cauistry of the Middle Ages. 

Until quite recently, the only regions of the world, by which the use of alcoholic drinks was unconditionally banned, were the Mohammedan; and they are notoriously regions conspicuous for fanaticism and general deficiency in the chief attributes of modern civilization. One by one, all the Christian countries, except the 

United States and two of the smaller Canadian provinces (Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island) and Finland, which have embarked upon the experiment of prohibition in our time, have abandoned it because of the social evils and the flagrant disregard of all law that it never fails to beget; and there is good reason to believe that the United States, the two Canadian provinces just mentioned, and Finland, are growing more and more restive under the restraints of prohibition. Like all extreme forms of Puritanism, it simply breeds back to vice and lawlessness. 

It is a violation of human nature; it is an affront to human reason; it is a gross invasion of human liberty; it is an odious and tyrannical incubus upon human happiness. It is, therefore, destitute of all real moral sanction and incapable of real enforcement. 

In the United States, it is the conception of a group of provincial and narrow-minded men, so blinded by the undeniable abuses and scandals of the old saloon, to which no one wishes to return, as to be unable to set off against them the extent to which the drunkenness of a relatively small number of weak individuals is counterbalanced by the harmless enjoyment, found by millions of ordinary human beings in the reasonable use of alcoholic beverages. In other words, prohibition is an ineffectual effort absolutely to interdict by law a social habit which a proper system of legal regulation, backed by the salutary influences of the Home and the Church, and the code of personal proprieties and decencies, which governs the intercourse of respectable men and women, is far better qualified to take care of than prohibition. It utterly loses sight of the fact that man is a creature who can be 28

made better, but cannot be made over, by human agencies. So far from betokening a society, blessed with an advanced civilization, it only evidences one which contains a large number of members, too lacking in the elements of educated self-restraint to drink except to excess. In France, the use of wine is almost as general as the use of water in the United States. Even the carpenter, taking his midday meal, in front of the door of a cafe', has his glass of wine beside his plate. And yet how very rarely does any one see a Frenchman, whatever his position in life, under the influence of drink? And, if a Frenchman can, with perfect sobriety, make a daily practice of drinking wine, why could not the American People, if one-half of the energy that was expended in imposing Prohibition upon them, was expended in teaching them to be temperate, be brought, more and more, to consume wine and other mild liquors in the place of ardent spirits, and, more and more, to acquire the sense of decency and the capacity for self-control, which make wine to a Frenchman nothing but the good, cheerful creature that it truly is, when not perverted by human license? American prohibition should be so modified as to authorize Congress to establish a system of liquor control, akin to that of the Province of Quebec, compounded partly of Government supervision and partly of local option. Under such a system, there would be no occasion for thousands of Americans to go abroad for the purpose of satisfying a perfectly natural appetite, without fear of bootleg frauds or puritanical molestation; the scandals, abuses, and official corruption, that now attend the practical workings of prohibition, would come to an end; the revenues of the Government would again be swollen by one of the most eligible of all forms of taxation; and the world, that has so often been harried by mad misconception of what man is, or is capable of being, would once more have occasion to echo the sublime declaration of Edmund Burke: "Never, no never, did Nature say one thing and Wisdom say another". And all this is asserted by one who abhors drunkenness even more than he does prohibition.

VIII. 

THE BEST WAY TO BECOME RICH. 

The modes, honest or dishonest, in which riches are sought, are almost innumerable. They range all the way from the slow, 29

self-denying methods of accumulation practiced by the petty huckster or tradesman, to the big scale operations of the "moneymaggot", to use a phrase of Fletcher, the dramatist, of the industrial world, or the reckless ventures of the stockmarket “plunger".

Sir Robert Walpole said that he made it a rule to talk bawdy, at his table, because that was common ground on which his guests could all meet. Money might well, for the same reason, be selected, as a topic for general discussion by a host, at the present time; for now, as in Theognis' ancient day the mass of men know but one virtue; namely, to be rich. How to coax wealth from its secret retreats, or to capture it by a bold coup de main; how to make a breeding sow of it, as poor Richard would have said; how to expend it; how to pass it on to its next gilded beneficiary; these are the things that unceasingly occupy the thoughts, and agitate the tongues, of the great majority of such men as are equal to the successful pursuit of lucre at all. There are few, indeed, in our day, prepared to say, to gold and silver, in the words of Bassanio, in the Merchant of Venice: "Thou gaudy gold, Hard food for Midas, I will none of thee; 

Nor none of thee, thou pale and common drudge 'Tween man and man".

The wonder to me is that, while Mammon is coming in for so much idolatry, the best of all ways of making one's self truly rich should be so pitiably neglected. I am speaking of the selfenrichment that results from the adoption of a wise philosophy of life, which renders us wealthy by reducing the number and the urgency of our material desires. In the eye of a true conception of human existence, money assumes a position of secondary importance; in the life of even the busiest and most fortunate merchant it should be regarded but as a by-product in comparison with the best fruits of our being. From any point of view, its value, of course, is very great. There is no satisfaction to be found in indigence by anybody and but little to be extracted from narrow, straightened circumstances by any one who is not an eccentric recluse or a stoic philosopher. To be rationally happy, one must have at least a sufficient measure of pecuniary independence to exempt him from the pinch of privation, as privation is defined 30

by thoroughly sensible men, and to place within his reach the means of gratifying, in moderation, his reasonable personal needs. 

But given this degree of pecuniary competence, and how wealthy a man is does not depend so much upon his worth in money as upon the extent to which he finds in intellectual pursuits, in public service, in the habitual practice of doing good and in sane healthy avocations and pastimes the pleasure that less exalted and refined natures find only in sensual or luxurious living, or in the thousand and one ways of "blowing in", to use the slang phrase, inordinate wealth. Every mental or moral craving of ours that tends, in a natural manner, to wean the human spirit away from the superfluous demands of the human body, and to fix it upon things beyond the pale of mere animal existence, or sensuous enjoyment, or heedless extravagance, is a pecuniary as well as a spiritual gain, and makes a man, even in dollars and cents, just that much more affluent; for even, in terms of our federal currency, there is no practical difference, in effect, between adding to one's income by acquiring more wealth, and adding to it by curtailing the objects for which more wealth is usually sought. In other words, wealth is to be computed not so much by what a man has as by what he is, or ought to be, satisfied with. If I have never known an hour of ennui, in my life, because of my love for good books; if I have felt the profound satisfaction that results from the faithful performance of public duty; if I have given in personal service all that I could give to advance the general welfare of my fellowcreatures; if I have experienced all the wholesome joy that flows from communion with the varied face of nature, and from simple, manly sports and recreations, or from the cultivation of those social charities and sympathies, without which there can be no such thing as complete happiness, and if, at the same time, my pecuniary estate has been such as to supply me with a substantial degree of material comfort and ease, why should I not account myself as having been one of the wealthiest of men? 

Under such circumstances, unless I wished a great fortune merely for the purpose of promoting the general welfare of my fellowcreatures, what real good would such a fortune have done me?

It would have entailed upon me cares and anxieties, to which I have been a stranger; it would have absorbed time which I have devoted to things much more profitable than money-getting or 31

money-coddling, and it would have subjected me to enervating, demoralizing or, perhaps, even corrupting, influences, to which my character has never been exposed. Ordered as my life has been, I have been as wealthy as Vanderbilt or Astor, and I without the slightest insincerity or affectation, utter the prayer of the Scriptures: "Give me neither poverty nor riches".

IX. 

RELIGION. 

can,

Since I was a boy, Religion in the United States, that is to say, among Protestants, at any rate, of whom I am one, has undergone a great change. There are still a vast number of Protestant Churches and pastors; and they are maintained with no little zeal and usefulness; though, when one observes to what a preponderant extent this is done by women, he cannot but ask himself how far, as times goes on, women are likely to adopt, along with such male habits as drinking and smoking, the male habit of shirking church-attendance. Some time ago, when participating in the services of an Episcopal Church, with which I am very familiar, I observed that, of the nine carved angels in the reredos, above its altar, no less than eight were female angels, and that the proportions, in which my fellow-worshippers were men and women, were very much the same. It is manifest that the present drift is towards a woman as nearly identical with man as the original bar of sex will allow. Why, then, as women become more and more epicene, should they be any more devout than men? And should they become no more so, to what extent would the prosperity of the Church be prejudiced thereby? 

Religion is still a vegete and flourishing thing in the life of such of the people of the United States as are church communicants; but it is manifestly, so far as the Protestant sects are concerned, becoming more and more limited, in its outlook, to the terrestrial horizon. Steadily, it is withdrawing its gaze from "the world to come" (to use the old orthodox phrase) to the world that is; or, to express the idea differently, from the invisible world, that is evidenced by faith alone, to the visible world that is evidenced by "the sensible and true avouch" of our own eyes. 

In my boyhood, the church was not so much a human instru- 32

ment of social amelioration and charitable beneficence as it was a sacred institution, ordained of God for the purpose of conveying to men the divine message that, if they did not forsake their sins, they would be doomed to a life of eternal damnation beyond the grave, but that, if they did, they would enjoy, after death, a life of everlasting bliss. Nor was it an uncommon thing then for a preacher to paint, in glaring colors, Hell, the abode of the damned, as the scene of the acutest and most agonizing physical torments. On the other hand, Heaven, the abode of the redeemed, was pictured by the same preacher as a land of the purest spiritual delight, where the angelic hosts, by which it was peopled, experienced in reunion with those, from whom they had been separated by human mortality, and in melodious adoration of the Divine Perfections a measure of happiness far transcending anything that they had known on our sin, and sorrow, stained earth. The direful hell-fire, which was one of the features of this creed, has sunk into bleached ashes; and the celestial glory, with which it was so violently contrasted, appears to be gradually fading away as the vivid flush of a sunset sky does when eve is drawing over it the "gradual dusky veil", of Collins' "Ode to Evening". To be sure, thousands of human beings, within and without the Protestant Churches, still harbor, in one vague form or another, the belief that Death is not the end of all, and that its dark portals open upon amaranthine fields of re-knit human ties and sanctified human love. But these things are no longer believed, it goes without saying, as they were when Love and Terror laid the tiles of medieval cathedrals or, even as they were, when, sustained by the assurances of a later evangel, Tudor Randolph, the youthful nephew of John Randolph of Roanoke, gave up the ghost, saying, "Don't grieve for me; for I die happy," and his mother, Judith Randolph, sank into her grave, exclaiming: "Christ is my only hope". As a rule, if they are believed at all, it is only as we listlessly put our trust in something that we have accepted mechanically as a matter of mere conformity. By the majority of men they are either derisively rejected, agnostically waived aside, or unreflectively ignored.

Religious communicants constituted in 1916 only forty-one per cent of our entire population. Everything indicates that, in the future, church activity will be inspired less and less by the 33

old religious scheme of penalties and rewards here or hereafter, and increasingly by the injunction: "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself." The ecclesiastical functions of the Church will grow less important, and its ethical and eleemosynary functions more important. Its aims and ideals will be shaped not so much by 

God, seated in his heavenly pavilion, as by God, working in the hearts of his creatures on earth. Moral reforms, charitable projects, the benevolent relief of the ignorant, the erring, and the distressed will largely take the place of dogmatic theology and perhaps altogether that of specific conceptions of post-mortem damnation and salvation. In fine, the religious watchword of the future will be Humanity rather than Divinity. But whatever may be thought of the probability of a material Hell, it is deeply to be regretted that the Paradise of the Christian should not have the hold upon his credence that it once had; and should no longer, as it once did, irradiate his dying moments with its promise of endless and cloudless beatitude in a future state of existence, and soften the grief of those, whom he leaves behind him, with its hope of renewed intercourse between them and him in a far better and happier sphere of communion. No reasoned philosophy of the human intellect, no resigned fatalism of the human spirit has the power to exalt the human soul as it is exalted when, transported by his foretaste of the delights of a sinless and everduring Heaven, the Christian exclaims: "Oh! Death where is thy sting 

Oh! Grave where is thy victory?" 

Personally, no matter what other men may believe or disbelieve,

I shall always believe that an indestructible crown, and the rapture of reestablished companionship with relations and friends, who have preceded us, await in the Holy City, on the Hills beyond the River that we never cross but once, and recross never, all of us who, in this life, have been true to those stirrings of immortality, of which surely every human being, even those fashioned out of the coarsest clay, must, at some time or other, have been conscious. That fair vision first revealed to me through the teachings of my mother and of the Presbyterian and Episcopalian divines, under whose influence I passed in my early life, and afterwards invested with additional beauty for 34

me by the lives and deaths of godly men and women of my acquaintance, who cherished it as their most precious possession, can never be disspelled by any cold and mechanical processes of human logic whatever. It is the offspring of intuitions, emotions and precepts far more powerful and compelling for me than they. 

If fallacious, what harm will have been done me? If real, what ecstasy will be mine?

That religious feeling, whether detached from post-mortuary considerations or not, will never die out in the hearts of men, I do not doubt. Reverence for something higher than ourselves is as necessary to us as the love of something on a level with ourselves, and human nature at times expresses its feelings in religious cadences, if for no other reason, because no other language is profound or musical enough to voice its loftiest sentiments and aspirations. Even were the origin of the World and the Destiny of Man relegated wholly to the province of scientific hypothesis, what speculation would be so plausible as that which declares that the evidences of design, at once grave and exquisite, which the Universe discloses, convincingly demonstrate its entire subordination to the oversight and will of an all-wise and all-powerful Deity. Overhearing the infidel talk of a group of his officers, as he paced the deck of his ship, on a clear night, on his return from Egypt, Napoleon paused in his tracks, and, pointing up to the Moon and the Stars overhead, asked: "Who made them 

Messieurs?" The question was not answered then, and to this day it has remained unanswered by the infidel.

X. 

MAN. 

Man, as an object of scrutiny and appraisement, is not unlike a lady's fan. He can be spread out into large, or he can be shut up into small, dimensions. It all depends on the point of view, from which he is regarded, and the rod by which his pretensions are measured. He has even been contemptuously termed a forked radish. Pope, unable to discern any real unity of character in him, pronounced him "the glory, jest and riddle of the world". To David and Shakespeare, he was little less than a divinity. How he towers in Psalm 8, until his sublime head almost strikes the stars! 35

"When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained; 

What is man that thou art mindful of him? and the son of man, that thou visitest him?

For thou has made him a little lower than the angels, and hast crowned him with glory and honour.

Thou madest him to have dominion over the works of thy hands; thou hast put all things under his feet;

All sheep and oxen, yea, and the beasts of the field; 

The fowl of the air, and the fish of the sea, and whatsoever passeth through the paths of the seas". 

Equally lyrical are the words of Hamlet: 

"What a piece of work is a man! How noble in reason!

How infinite in faculty! in form and moving, how express and admirable! in action, how like an angel! in apprehension, how like a god! the beauty of the world! the paragon of animals!" “The paragon of animals!" That is the point of view from which the dignity and importance of man in the scale of creation must be approached. Contrasted with the Omniscience and 

Omnipotence of God, he is but a feeble understudy of the Deity. 

Compared with the unselfishness, the purity, and the loving-kindness of Christ, he might well have hesitated secretly to touch the hem of the latter's garment. Indeed, his imagination can readily envisage some supernatural being more gifted, powerful and benignant than himself. But who can deny that he is "the paragon of animals"? Not of gods or demi-gods, or of other beings, who partake of their transcendental character, but of animals. This he must have been even at the earliest stage at which he became anthropo-morphous. In the beginning, his superiority over the lower animals was not so great as it is now, but this could be said of it. It was great enough to bestow upon him an original fund of intelligence, sufficient in amount and quality to be capable of almost indefinite enlargement, with his growth in observation and experience; whereas the original fund of intelligence, with which the humbler members of the Animal Kingdom were endowed, was too limited to open up the way to subsequent accretions. Primeval man was not so swift or strong as some of the ferocious beasts around him, but he had the will with which to conceive devices, and to fashion weapons, 36

which made him more than their match; and, in process of time, to frame rude systems of civil polity and morals which led gradually to all the triumphs, material and spiritual, of modern civilization. There is no evidence to show that the monkey, the dog and the horse, which are accounted as being among the most bright-witted of the lower animals, had any less intelligence in prehistoric ages than they have today. They simply did not have enough then ever to acquire any more. But what a different being is man today from primeval Man with the Sun and the Stars about him? The profusion with which Nature has supplied him with the materials, that he has employed in achieving his brilliant exploits in the fields of discovery and invention, furnish the most persuasive of proofs that God made all things with a view to putting them under his feet, as his terrestrial vice-gerent. 

And yet the achievement of Man in those fields, remarkable as they have been, are not so admirable as the progress that he has made, since his career began, as a moral being. It is a long ascent from the cave-dweller to Homer, Dante, Shakespeare, Raphael, Mansfield, Marshall, Chatham, Napoleon, Jefferson, Gladstone, Watt, Fulton, Morse, Marconi and Wright. But it was a still longer ascent from serpent and bull, worship, gladiatorial butchery, witchcraft and human slavery to the philanthrophy and social welfare work of our day. The last step in this ascent will never be reached until all the civilized powers of the earth, including our own, shall have made of the present League of Nations a central agency of peace equal to the task of keeping international warfare-that last and vilest lair of human savagery-in check.

To me the most interesting thing about men is the wide range of intellect and character that they exhibit. It is said that no two leaves on a tree are exactly alike, and an effective system of police detection has been built up on the theory that no two human thumbs make exactly the same impression. Closely as men resemble each other in the gross, they differ almost infinitely from each other in detail. Here is a man, who has barely enough brains to count his own fingers, and there is another who can rapidly cast up three long columns of figures almost simultaneously. Here is an unselfish citizen who finds his chief happiness in promoting the welfare of his fellow-creatures, and 37

there is a gun-man as quick to lap human blood as a cat is to lap milk. The human intellect ranges all the way from Napoleon to Simple Simon; the human character all the way from Washington to the type of individual who is set down in some parts of the United States as an "onery cuss". And between these limits of variation the human mind and the human character are marked by diversities not only too many to be enumerated but often fully as subtle and refined as those which distinguish one leaf or thumb-mark from another. But whatever the dissimilarity between human individuals in other respects, in one respect they are the same. In the breast of each one of them, practically speaking, there is forever going on a struggle between the Principle of Good and the Principle of Evil. In some breasts, the one or the other of these combatants is strong enough to maintain a constant supremacy, but in others the strife reminds us of the stage direction in Hamlet: 

Laertes wounds Hamlet; then, in 

Scuffling, they change rapiers, and Hamlet wounds Laertes.

It was at one of those moments, when conscience and weakness are contending in our bosoms, that John Randolph of Roanoke gave himself a blow over his heart with his fist; crying out as he did it: "This rebel is ever in revolt." Sometimes the balance in the dubious fight, as we have said, is firmly preserved by virtue from first to last; sometimes it is lost and regained; and sometimes, alas! it is too hopelessly lost ever to be redressed; but, except in this last event, the stark battle never wholly ends. 

Ultimately, the puerilities of lingering fundamentalism will melt away in the waxing light of the merciless rationalism, which, from generation to generation, dispels human superstition and error, as silently and irresistibly as the dawn displaces the night. Then the myths and fables, which have gathered about the Origin and Destiny of Man, will pass away, and to the popular as well as to the scientific eye it will become apparent that it is not so much God that has made Man in his image as Man that has made God in his image, and that, anatomically and physiologically, Man, lofty as are the present levels of his intelligence and power, is but a mammal, connected by indubitable links of kinship with 38

lowlier members of the natural order to which he belongs. But, at the same time, in action so like an angel, and in apprehension so like a God (to revert to Hamlet's phrases) is he that there will be no just reason whatever for doubting that he is a creature worthy of the dignity of being specially designed and created by the Supreme Being, who can, as it suits his pleasure, work out his results either by an abrupt fiat or through long ages of evolutionary change.

XI. 

THE BEST GIFT BUT ONE. 

Once, when I was sleeping, in my chamber, at the Inn of 

Existence, I dreamt this dream: I was awakened from slumber by a sudden flush of radiance in my room; and behold! there stood at the foot of my bed an angel with a smile of heavenly benignity upon her face, and a brow such as belongs only to the serene beauty of contemplation. "Friend!" she said, in a voice sweeter and richer than any to which I had ever hearkened, "Thy conduct, through long years of probation, has been such as to win the approval of Him who is thy Master and mine; and, as a just recompense to thee, He has sent me to say that any wholesome, earthly gift that thou mayest ask of Him, in addition to the crown of eternal life, which awaits thee beyond the grave, if thou shalt but be faithful to the end, will be gladly granted to thee by Him through me, His messenger". Abashed, as soon as I could recall to life the senses benumbed by the sudden illumination of my chamber, and those supernal accents, I replied: "Oh, thou celestial visitant! who fillest this room with the glory of thy face and the harmony of thy lips, grateful though I be for the joyful tidings that thou bringest to me from my Master, I shrink from the task of saying which one I prefer of all those good and perfect gifts that make up His overflowing bounty. Often, things that I have eagerly sought have, when secured, proved to be wholly deceitful and empty; and, often, things that have come to me unsought have been among my rarest blessings. The burden of selection which has been imposed upon me is greater than I can bear. Choose thou for me, and I will gladly abide by thy choice." 39

Then, satisfying herself by a glance as searching as the spear of Ithuriel that I meant in truth what I had said, the Angel rejoined: "Mortal! I will respect thy scruples, and willingly exercise thy option for thee. As thy gift, I will elect a joy which will attend thy footsteps, and be a source of the purest and richest happiness to thee until thy very last day. It will never cloy. It will never inflict upon thee any of the physical and moral penalties which follow so much that is called pleasure. It will never bring to thee any sense of personal degradation. It will never implant in thee any sting of conscience. It will never grow old. On the contrary, as the circle of thy other enjoyments narrows, with advancing years, and augmenting infirmities, it will afford thee more instruction and recreation than ever before. It will be thy delight in health, thy stay in sickness, thy solace in distress. It will open up new worlds of imagination and speculation to thee; it will give thee an insight into the most secret arcana of nature; it will bring under thy retrospective eye the long caravan of humanity moving over the sands of the ages; it will bring to thy door the unfamiliar, strange aspects of Man and Nature in other lands than thy own; it will make thee familiar with the noblest thoughts, the loftiest sentiments and the most musical utterances of the human mind and soul. It will ennoble and elevate thy whole being; and bring thee nearer than anything else, except human love, in its highest manifestations, can do, to the attributes of the Divine Nature." "Bright Angel!" I exclaimed, my face aglow with rapt expectation, "What can this precious gift which you extol so eloquently be?" "Henceforth", she replied, "Thou shalt be possessed by a deep, eager and insatiable love of reading, such as unfortunately thou hast never hitherto known." And with that, as a symbol of the priceless gift that she had conferred upon me, she plucked from her bosom and handed me a book, in which, among other things, were printed in letters as bright as sunlight the 8th verse of the 4th Chapter of I John, which tells us that "He that loveth not, knoweth not God; for God is love," 

the Hamlet of William Shakespeare, and the Comus of John 

Milton. 40

XII. 

CHILDREN. 

The complaint is very common that children are no longer as obedient or respectful to their parents as they once were. 

Of course, in part this idea originates in the general disposition of human beings to contrast the Present unfavorably with the Past. In most instances, that disposition is inspired merely by the fact that the Present is different from the Past. It would be easy to cite respects in which the former is distinctly inferior to the latter; but, on the whole, its superiority to the latter is so unquestionable that no one who sees beyond his nose denies it. To a certain degree, children have always been undutiful in their relations to their parents. I recall the fact that in my youth an old negro complained bitterly to my father that none of his thirteen children would help to maintain him. "Old man!" my father replied in his kindly way, "do you not realize that it is easier for one father to take care of thirteen children than it is for thirteen children to take care of one father?"

And even in particulars less exacting than the obligation to expend money in the support of an aged parent, whom Time has put out of commission, it is no new thing for a child to be slack in the discharge of his filial obligations. Comparatively few parents are dependent upon their children for pecuniary assistance—the balance in that ledger is overwhelmingly the other way—but almost all parents, rich or poor, are pathetically dependent upon their children for those little attentions which mean so much to the love of a father or mother and so little to the time and comfort of a child. In his Old Man's Musing, Frank 

L. Stanton, the Georgia poet, moans: "But mother an' children-where are they? 

The mother went home long ago, To the place where the light 

Is eternally bright, And there's never no winter an' snow.

And the children-they're fur from the home place, An' mostly fergettin to write, An' that's why I'm feelin' so lonesome 

In the snow that is fallin' tonight!" 41

"Fergettin' to write", forgetting to pay an occasional visit to the old homestead, to send the little trifle of a present, to give the kiss or the caress, to do the small thing, whatever it may be, that helps to feed the flame which never dies, however poorly fed, these are the omissions that cause so much pain to parents. They make them feel that they give so much and receive so little; that their share in the commerce of affection between parent and child is so small; that they have outlived their day; that they are superfluous, and that they might as well be disposed of in some such manner as a burdensome old member of the tribe is disposed of by certain savages, of whom I have read, who place him in a tree, and dance around it, shaking it, as they dance, and crying out: "The fruit is ripe! 

The fruit is ripe! 'Tis time for it to fall!" No one will ever adequately depict the misery which parents suffer from filial neglect. It is for the most part a silent suffering, silent partly from the reticence of self-respect, but partly also from love too deep even to indulge in reproaches by reason of the lack of what is justly due it. It may be well questioned, however, whether the total amount of distress inflicted upon parents by such minor derelictions of filial duty is not greater than that inflicted upon them by all the graver misfortunes of their lives. 

I am speaking especially of mothers, I hardly need say; for sometimes children find that if they cannot reciprocate affection their fathers can reciprocate neglect.

No one expects a child to bestow upon a parent the same over-flowing measure of tenderness that a parent bestows upon a child. That would be asking as much water of the river as of the sea. 

Parental and filial love are incommensurable quantities.

A man has but to have a wife and child to realize that what has been said by poets and orators of the boundless opulence of a good mother's unselfish devotion is but the barest commonplace. 

I know a mother who has all that womanly timidity which it has always been one of the proudest privileges of manhood to safeguard and protect. Any unusual sound in the night, any visible evidence of danger, by day or night, is enough to arouse her fears; but I know as well as I know anything, of which my mind has ever taken cognizance, that, if she were to find herself in a situation in which she could preserve the life of one of her 42

children by the sacrifice of her own, she would devote her own to destruction as unhesitatingly as though it were but a soiled ribbon. The ocean has a bottom, but that love has none. 

By no means, are all children neglectful of their parents; and least of all daughters; and it is only just to admit that such neglect is often referable to mere thoughtlessness. Every one of us can recall many a duteous son or daughter whose relations to his or her parents are marked by everything that a father or mother can crave. What more proper object for respect can there be than a son, who has left the parental roof, and yet, despite the demands that his own wife and children, and his business preoccupations make upon him, never fails each day, in addition to other evidences of solicitous attachment, to seek that roof, even though it be for a few moments, because of the assurance that such visits give of undiminished gratitude and undying affection?

On the whole, it is my belief, too, that the bearing of the child toward the parent is not as exemplary as it once was. 

What has become of that filial love so deep that it even took on the nature of reverence? I know many children who love their parents but I know none now who revere them. "Honor", one of my father's overseers, once said to him after the Civil War, "went out with the nigger". So, apparently, did reverence for parents. It went out with the habit that the old-time wife had of looking up to her husband, when he was a truly worthy man, as if he were a being just a little superior to herself, and of calling him "Mr." instead of "John" or "George". Manners change with the times, and the reader may say that there is simply less formality, but not less real love, in the intercourse of children with their parents now than formerly; or, in other words, that child and parent are merely on a more intimate and familiar footing now; and that what has been lost in reverence has been gained in increased intimacy and attachment. I deny it. "I always knew that it was impossible to disobey my father," declares Robert E. Lee, Junior, the son of the great Commander, in his recollections of the latter. But because of this fact did anyone of the General's children love him the less? No! Everyone who knew the Lees knew that. I have numbered among my friends more than one contemporary of Robert E. Lee, Junior, 43

whose character and manners were moulded by the same social and moral influence as his, who could thank God that, though guilty of many sins and shortcomings, he could truly declare that he had never once uttered a word to either of his parents that was flavored with the slightest degree of disrespect. It is notorious that the unhappiest children are the children who are permitted to go undisciplined by the foolish fondness or weakness of their parents, and that there is no such filial love as that excited by a father or mother whose attitude towards his or her children is such as to command dutifulness as well as love. 

Whatever else may have helped to make the part of children towards their parents less respectful and obedient than of yore, it cannot be doubted that the main cause has been the extent to which, in these evil latter years, husband and wife have been ignoring their duty to each other. Divorce is the worm that has blighted the bud of filial love as well as the full-blown flower of connubial love. To no small extent the former is the sequel of the latter. How can love in any form be expected to bloom in the shadow of that home of which Shakespeare says: "War is no strife 

To the dark house and the detested wife."

How can children be expected to entertain the same feelings of affection for a father and mother who have set them an example of squalid domestic quarrels, and violated duty, day after day, or of actual separation, instead of the exemplary wedded constancy and harmony, which, in its noblest forms, is the finest thing in human life? The highest function of the matrimonial state is to impress upon the minds of children the purest and loftiest principles and ideals of duty and good conduct. This function becomes but a mockery when a child hardly knows at what moment the animosities of his parents may end in the scandalous atmosphere of the Divorce Court either because of infidelity, cruelty or some other flagrant offense, or because of disregard of the minor decencies which go along with mutual consideration and forbearance. A child deprived of both of his parents by death is a distressing object, but a child deprived of only one of his parents by divorce, when divorce is attributable to mere caprice or failure to comply with the ordinary behests of duty, is 44

to my mind an object hardly less distressing. When such a child finds himself released by divorce from the joint care of his parents, the divorce may have taken place under circumstances utterly destructive of every salutary inspiration or impression that wedlock is capable of communicating to the mind of the offspring.

In the end, lax divorce will not only work a lasting abatement in the love of children for their parents but in the love of parents for their children. Parental care and filial love belong to the same root stock. Children are often the tendrils by which the two portions of the matrimonial structure, that would otherwise fall apart, are kept together. One of the best assurances of filial love is nuptial love; and one of the best assurances of nuptial love is filial love. Even if filial love survives the divorce court, as respects one parent, how rarely, if ever, is it equal to the task of maintaining itself, as respects both parents. Where a marriage is a truly happy one, the husband loves his child not only because it is his but because it is his wife's also; and the converse of the statement is equally sound. 

Under such circumstances, the child is the precious pledge of the mutual endearments of the two parties to the union, and a tie of extraordinary strength in preserving it; but, after divorce, the fact that the child was partly the offspring of the other hated partner can not fail to have at least some effect in diminishing its value in the eyes of the remaining partner. 

If marriage is to become a mere temporary relation, by all means let the child be surrendered by the parties to it to the State, as a foster mother. Better one artificial parent than two erring, though natural, ones. The proverb tells us that good wombs have borne bad sons, but no proverb, so far as I know, tells us that bad wombs have borne good sons; and the worst of wombs is a dishonored marriage. 

XIII. 

PUBLIC LIFE. 

Public Life furnishes a field for some of the loftiest motives and aims by which human nature can be swayed; but it is also discredited, at times, by human infirmity, extending all the way 45

from despicable timidity and tergiversation to downright depravity. "Public office is a public trust." This saying is attributed to 

Grover Cleveland; and certainly no one, unless it be George 

Washington, ever lived up to it more faithfully than he. 

It is, however, at least as old as the early part of the Eighteenth Century. But, no matter when, or where, it originated, it expresses a simple truth that should never be forgotten or lost sight of. 

Few persons merit censure and contempt more richly than a private trustee who converts the trust fund, in his care, to his own use, outright, or makes a profit out of it, in any manner, however remotely, for himself. Such a man, in common parlance, betrays his trust. How much more glaringly perfidious is the trustee who betrays the confidence, not of one, or a dozen, individuals, but of an entire people! If a private defaulter should be chastised with whips, a public defaulter should be chastised with scorpions. One of the redeeming facts about human nature is the fact that its ability to rise to the level of its responsibility usually increases with the increase of its responsibility. A larger measure of courage can be expected of the uniformed policeman or soldier than of the ordinary individual, in citizen's dress. It is the business, it is the duty of the policeman or the soldier to be unflinchingly brave. A larger measure of right-living can be expected of the Priest than of the ordinary layman. It is the business, it is the duty of the Priest to be impeccable. A larger measure of impartiality and self-restraint can be expected of the Judge than of the most scrupulous man off the bench. It is the business, it is the duty of the Judge to steel himself against bias and passion: "Unless above himselfe he can 

Erect himselfe how poore a thing is man!"

Brass buttons do not always make a policeman or soldier, nor a cowl a monk (cucullus non facit monachum); nor a black robe a Judge, but, as a general rule, they do, because they help the wearer of the uniform, or of the cowl, or of the robe, to erect himself above himself.

In a greater or lesser degree, the tenure of every public office, whether the office be important or insignificant, should produce 46

man. the same elevation of spirit. "Ich dien" (I serve) is the motto that both the President of the United States and the humblest messenger in one of the Departments at Washington should bear about with him in his heart from day to day. Every public officer takes a special vow of service, which pledges him to a quantum of service more exacting than that of the common He is, as he is so often termed, a public servant; not a servant to be abused or neglected by the citizen, as household servants are sometimes abused or neglected by ill-bred masters or mistresses; but a servant charged with the duty of being governed always not only by irreproachable principles of faithful and honorable conduct, but by an even more marked degree of courtesy and unselfish consideration than is usually exhibited by the household servant. For just as the eyesight and foot of the latter is quickened by the sense that he serves no mean master, but some strong and gracious one, so the zeal of the public servant should be stimulated by the thought that his allegiance is to the most august and powerful of all masters, the Commonwealth. That thought should ever accompany his footsteps, whether he be some Statesman, "with gifts and graces eminently adorned", elected by God to some great work, his glory and people's safety, or some routine placeholder whose duties are almost as purely mechanical as the movements of a watch. To be sure, it would be laughable to declare that such high standards of conduct are universal in the public service. 

The Royal Court has its pimps, wantons, favorites, pensioners and parasites; and Democracy, superior as it is in the long reckoning to all other forms of Government, breeds many office-holders that are but the superserviceable tools, or the dependents, of powerful politicians, in or out of office, who, at times, exert an influence over them quite inconsistent with the public welfare. 

This is the office-holder who, to use the words of Cowper, fishes up "His dirty and dependent bread 

From pools and ditches of the Commonwealth."

And then there is a great body of subordinate office-holders who perform their tasks with one eye on the folio before them and the other on the clock. In such a country as the United 

States, the administration of public business, so far as it is 47

committed to such hands, can never be conducted with the same energy and painstaking attention as a private enterprise, directed by the spirit of individual initiative, animated by the desire for pecuniary gain, and holding out its tools, at wages far in excess of public standards of compensation, to those that can best handle them (to recall Napoleon's phrase).

Nor are the higher placeholders of public life free from infirmities, peculiar to public life. It was a saying of Franklin that all old women are good women; but the only incumbent of a public office, so far as I am aware, of whom that adjective can be used almost unqualifiedly, is the President of the United States, during his second term, when there is but little to excite his selfish hopes or fears. How often is even a Governor or a 

Mayor diverted from the strict line of his duty by personal ambition or political pressure; though my observation is that, on the whole, when the pinch comes, Governors and Mayors only rarely fail to do their duty as it presents itself to their best judgment; and God knows that, if there is a despicable spectacle on earth, it is that which is so often seen in the legislator, who is in a hopeless quandary, as to whether he is the actual possessor of his own mind or soul, or not, and is even more desirous of securing reelection than of doing his duty, that is to say, in the legislator who is nothing more than a well-oiled weather-wane, which reverses its revolutions with every change of the wind, except that, unlike a weather-vane, in his timid vacillations of purpose he sometimes turns one way when the wind is really blowing towards another. Such a man, to quote one of the most Shakespearian of all Shakespeare's similes, "Like a vagabond flag upon the stream, Goes to and back, lackeying the varying tide."

On the whole, however, I do not hesitate to say that men in public life, whether they are holders of minor or major offices, perform their duties pretty well, so long as they are required to perform only governmental duties, and are not asked to perform duties that properly appertain only to the domain of private business enterprise. In a free country, naturally enough, the delinquencies of individuals, in public place, are not a little exaggerated, if, for no other reason, because there so many 48

persons outside of the pale of office who are eager to demonstrate that they could fill the offices within it much better than the existing incumbents. Even the political bosses who, in the past, were regarded as such abandoned creatures, were nothing like as bad as they were supposed to be. They are often spoken of as if they were mere thugs or assassins. They were nothing of the sort. It so happens that I never knew one of them who was not a good husband, a good father, a good friend. 

Just as cancer has been said to be merely tissue out of place, so they can be set down as being merely merchants out of place. 

They undertook to huckster and traffic in something: viz., public office and legislation, that the public could not afford to let anyone trade in. As to the mass of executive and legislative placeholders, it can be truly said that they are alive to the obligations of their positions, and render to their master, the People, a satisfactory amount of service. In the early part of my political career, political rings and bosses exerted far more influence over legislators than they had any right to do, but I never knew a time when the members of a legislature did not scurry to cover, if the public really knit its brows, and looked black, and made a definite specific demand upon them; and of the great majority of legislators it is also true that, hedge and dodge as they may, and as much disposed to pursue a safe pathway as long as they can they are, they act, after all, from no principle, worse than that of the martyr who said, “To the stake, if I must, but not to the stake, if I can help it." And it is only fair to say that there is a class of public officers to whom, (whether from a natural taste for public life, or from that ambition which finds its highest gratifications in the ability to command the applause of listening Senates, or in the achievements of constructive statesmanship, or merely in the honest, simple desire to serve) public service is nothing less than an altar, lit up by a pure and steady flame. To understand what this kind of zeal means to the welfare of a people, one has but to read the life of the elder Pitt, or of one of our greater American statesmen, or of some comparatively obscure functionary in one of our Federal or State Departments, who brought to his work much the same self-consecration that is so often displayed in the supreme achievements of human character in the field of private 49

unselfishness. To induct such men into the public service, and to keep them there until their influence shall have become a torch and an inspiration to all around them, is the real art of Government. Many such men have I known in the course of my public life, which has now extended over a long tract of time, and I never see one of them that I do not feel that my nature is just a little exalted, and that I have in them a new fount of strength and enthusiasm, to which repairing, I can fill my urn with golden light, to call to my aid the beautiful image of Milton.

In the social movements of my time, I discern, or think that I discern, much to fill me with misgiving, if not, fear; but, in the political movements of my time, I can see nothing of that sort. 

In my humble judgment, since the Hayes-Tilden scandal, political progress in the United States has been arrested only as the progress of the tide is arrested by its brief refluxes, when it is climbing up higher and higher on the strand with its every fresh onset. The political boss or the political machine is nothing like so powerful as he or it once was. A purifying and enlightening influence of enormous value has been found in both the province of the Federal Government, and in the respective provinces of our State and Municipal Governments, in the merit system of appointment. Legislators are no longer so amenable to outside dictation as they once were, except when the Anti-Saloon League applies its prod. Elections are far freer from fraud, trickery and violence. The votes are far more independent. Public opinion is far more exacting and public men are far more sensitive to its conclusions. It is true that in some respects crime is more rampant than it is in other countries, but that is only because the free spirit that has come down to us from our simple beginnings has not yet reconciled itself, as it will inevitably later do, to the preventive, as well as the punitive, methods of dealing with crime, which have been adopted by the foreign lands, from whom we have already wisely borrowed so much. It is true that some bad blunders have marked the stages of this progress, such as the mad attempt to substitute Prohibition for Temperance, and certain extremer forms of interference with natural economic laws; but it is only fair to the American people to admit that even these blunders bespeak either a spirit that only reluctantly admits that there is any obstacle to the perfectibility of human beings, or 50

that there are any hardships, begotten by the workings of natural forces, that cannot be ended, or at least mitigated, by legislative benevolence. So far, some benignant star has guided our national pathway through every dark defile, and over every frowning steep, and as yet there is no reason to believe that its light has failed. 51
