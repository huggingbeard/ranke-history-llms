PREFACE 

Miss Zeligs has once more made an important contribution to the literature of teaching Jewish history. Her Child's 

History of Jewish Life fills a very urgent need in the Jewish school and fills it admirably.

Leading Jewish educators have long urged that the teaching of Jewish history for children must extend beyond the Biblical period. There is no logical reason for assuming that Biblical history is simpler and therefore more suitable for children than the Post-Biblical epoch or the Middle Ages. Simplicity is not a matter of the period which is studied, but is achieved by the method of presentation and the careful selection of material. Heretofore, writers of children's histories have merely simplified the traditional Graetz and have rewritten texts which, like his, are almost entirely concerned with politics, persecutions, and literature, or with religion and rabbinics. The only difference between works for children and those for adults was that, like father's made-over coat, they were shorter and smaller. The difference between children's history and a mature treatment should be in the type of experience described. Elementary history should deal with concrete experiences and should be descriptive of the life that was lived at different periods. These factors are equally important for adult study, but in addition, the mature treatment must include more abstract phases, such as class conflicts, the formation of parties and sects, movements and social forces.

Miss Zeligs is the first writer of history for Jewish children who has succeeded beautifully in portraying the concrete life of a very difficult period. Life in Babylonia was always hidden within the pages of the Talmud, and the Middle Ages were a hopeless vale of tears studded with occasional literary monuments, hardly the story of ordinary human beings at all, but mainly a tale of martyrs and scholars. Miss Zeligs has ventured into that period and by means of painstaking research has made of the story a fascinating human document. vii

Through the clever device of relating the experiences of a child, the author achieves genuine childlike interest. The story actually lives, like a contemporary document. We are shown a rich panorama of real life in Babylonia, Palestine, Spain, and the Central European Ghetto. 

With the aid of this text-book, teachers need no longer be fearful of adventuring into those important but muchneglected periods of Jewish history-the Post-Biblical and the Middle Ages.

A child studying this history will live with his people and through it will share its joys, and not merely the traditional sorrows. The tragic part of our history, the story of the 

Crusades, forcible conversions, and the Black Deaths, have been wisely subordinated to the more normal aspects, though they have by no means been omitted; for life even in the darkest periods is never completely tragic. The brighter hues always set the predominant tone. 

The plan of selecting the outstanding characteristics of the period and introducing the continuity incidentally is extremely well suited for the type of treatment required in a children's text. 

Teachers of intermediate grades will receive this volume with gratitude and will ask for more.

INTRODUCTION A Child's History of Jewish Life follows chronologically A Child's History of the Hebrew People. It covers the first sixteen centuries of the Common Era. 

The volume is planned to be used as a text book for children of about eleven to thirteen years of age. This placement is suggestive only, and due regard should be given to the individual differences of children. As a book for general reading, it should be of interest to older children also.

Description of Contents 

The first two units of the book deal with life in Palestine and Babylonia respectively during the development of the Talmud. Although this period has had a profound influence on Jewish life, it has been a more or less forgotten one in the religious schools. The almost invariable method of treating the Talmudic Age has been to tell the stories of the rabbis. In A Child's History of Jewish 

Life the writer has tried to recreate the life and times of the Talmudic period and to give some reality and substance to an epoch so vague in the minds of children. 

The stories of the rabbis also become more meaningful when placed in their proper historic setting. 

The Spanish period is treated in the third unit. Here, too, the author has attempted to capture the spirit of the times by presenting many of the details of social living. 

The story of leading personalities and political events are correlated with this social setting.

The fourth unit is a study of the ghetto and gives a ix

characteristic picture of Jewish life in the Middle Ages as it existed in most of the countries of Europe. 

Underlying Principles 

The same plan followed in the first volume of presenting history as a study of group life has been continued here. The teaching of history as a civilization rather than merely as a sequence of political events or a story of great personalities has come to be the accepted viewpoint of progressive educators.

Another principle which the writer has tried to embody in this book is the orientation of Jewish history with the history of other peoples. Her purpose has been to present 

Jewish life in its world setting, showing the influences of the nations with which it came in contact and the Jewish contribution to other civilizations. In this way it is hoped that children will avoid the difficulty which they so often experience in associating Jewish history with the contemporary events of other nations. 

Throughout the book the development of present life from the past is emphasized so that Jewish life of the present day is seen as an outgrowth of its history. 

The material has been carefully selected with a view to child interest and comprehension. Certain movements and influences, like that of the Karaite sect, for example, have been omitted because they are too abstract and difficult for children of this age. Although of importance in 

Jewish life, their study can well be postponed to a later age and grade.

Method of Presentation 

As is evident from the previous discussion, the book is organized into four large units. This procedure aids in creating a clear and unified impression of each epoch. It

Instead of a mass of unrelated facts, the units present concrete and unified pictures to the minds of children. The unit plan is also helpful in carrying out an activity program or in following other types of teaching procedure. 

Each unit opens with the author directly addressing the young reader. The journey into the past is begun from the present so that the child may identify himself more readily with the history of the people he is studying. In this approach the author sketches in broad outlines the important generalizations of the period. A more detailed study follows in the form of a story. Through the significant experiences of imaginary children or historic characters, the civilization of a period is unfolded. 

The young reader is able to identify himself with the children in the story and thus relives imaginatively the historic experiences related. A large part of the material is thus in story form, a method which should appeal to the interests of children.

UNIT I PALESTINE IN THE DAYS OF THE RABBIS From the Very Beginning 

HAVE you ever wondered why You are You? Why aren't you a Chinese peasant boy eating rice with chopsticks, or a woolly-haired negro in an African jungle, or a Hindu lad in a crowded village of India? If you were any of these three, how different you would be and what different things you'd be doing! But You are You, and being a Jewish child, you are going to be Bar Mitzvah or confirmed at thirteen, and you have a Seder at Passover every year, and you are in a Hanukkah play at 

Sabbath-school. You celebrate many festivals like Succoth and Rosh Hashonah and Purim. If you belong to an orthodox Jewish family you don't eat certain kinds of foods and you observe the Sabbath in certain special ways.

Now the question is, how did You get to be that kind of a You? It's really very simple. You are You because your parents are They. And They are They because their parents were So and So. And their parents were So and 

So because their parents were Such and Such. Maybe it's not so simple after all, for you could go Back and Back and Back, until you got dizzy and still you wouldn't be back far enough. But suppose you kept right on going, because you do want to know why You are You. Where would you land after a while? 

Imagine that you are Alice-in-Wonderland falling down the hole, as she did. Do you remember that she passed some shelves on the way? Suppose each shel 1

stood for a hundred years. After you passed four shelves, which would mean four hundred years, you would come to the place where Columbus discovered America. But don't climb out on that shelf. You have to go down much farther to find out why You are You. Keep right on falling down the Hole of Time. It's getting to be sort of fun. You've passed the fifth shelf. Now fall gently past fourteen more. That makes nineteen, doesn't it? Nineteen hundred years! And still you're not sure why You are You. Sit on that nineteenth shelf a little while and swing your legs. What was the date nineteen hundred years ago? It's an easy problem. Just subtract nineteen hundred from the year you are living in now. What would the date be? That's rather close to the Year One, isn't it? Perhaps you know why that year is called the

Year One. It wasn't the beginning of the world! In order to get There you'd have to keep on falling for so long, you'd get very, very tired. The Year One is the time when Jesus was born. Since he was the founder of the Christian religion, the Christians counted the year of his birth as the Year One. Everything that happened before that time, they marked B.C. which stands for "Before Christ." Everything that happened after that year they called A.D. which stand for the Latin words, "Anno Domino," and they mean, "in the year of the

Lord." So any date that is marked A.D. means since the birth of Jesus. What year A.D. is it at the present time?

Now that you know what B.C. and A.D. mean, there are two more sets of letters that you should understand.

In many books on Jewish subjects you will find that the letters B.C. and A.D. are not used. The period of time since the birth of Jesus is known as the "Common Era." 

The letters B.C.E. stand for "Before the Common Era" and mean any time before the birth of Jesus, just as the

letters B.C. do. The letters C.E., which mean "Common 

Era," are used with any date after the birth of Jesus, just as A.D. is. Now you have four sets of letters to remember. B.C. and A.D. are generally used. B.C.E. and C.E. are used in Jewish books and writings. The Jewish people have a calendar of their own, but since it would get people all mixed up if they went according to different calendars, everyone goes according to the Christian calendar most of the time. For the Jewish holy days, however, the Jews use their own calendar. 

Are you ready to fall some more now? Remember, you've passed nineteen shelves. There you go! Before coming to the twentieth shelf you pass the Year One. 

Now you know what that means. But don't stop yet. 

You'll have to go much farther down to get a really good idea of why You are You. You've passed twenty shelves already. Now fall past twenty more. That makes forty altogether. Forty hundred years! If you like, you can think of it as four thousand. 

It's really time to get out. Climb on the shelf. Get hold of my hand and I'll give you a pull. Whew! Is your head going 'round from that fall? We're at 2000 B.C.E. now. Let's look around and see if we can find out something about what you want to know. Why, there's someone you recognize. It's Abraham, the first Hebrew, sitting outside his tent. But you know him so well, we really don't have to stop here very long. If you want to visit with him and find out how he lived, you can read A 

Child's History of the Hebrew People or some other book on the same subject. If you have done so, you also know about his son Isaac, and about Jacob, and Joseph, and Moses who led the Children of Israel out of Egypt and gave us the Ten Commandments. You've already found out some reasons why You are You. If you believe

in One God instead of idols, it is because these ancient Hebrews taught the world that idea. If you follow the 

Ten Commandments, then it is because Moses and the Children of Israel whom he led, left them for you. But there are many other reasons why You are You, so let's go on. 

Now you may start climbing up the Hole of Time.

Abraham is at about 2000 B.C.E. Use the shelves as a ladder and climb up about a hundred years at a step. 

When you've taken about eight steps, you will come to the year 1200 B.C.E. Moses lived about that time. Now two more big steps and you'll be at 1000 B.C.E. which was when Solomon built the First Temple in Jerusalem and ruled in all his glory. Up you go, past the time when the Northern Kingdom was conquered by the Assyrians. 

Then the Kingdom of Judah in the South of Canaan was overcome by the Babylonians. Do you remember that many Jews were carried away as captives to Babylonia? 

Later, when that country was conquered by the Persians, Cyrus, their king, permitted the Jews to return and rebuild the Temple. If you climb up a few more steps you will get to that time. There you are about 500 B.C.E. 

Then came Nehemiah and Ezra. They helped the Jews rebuild the land and the Temple and made it possible for them to live according to Jewish laws. Take a few more steps and stop at 200 B.C.E. Just above it is the time. when Antiochus, the Syrian king, tried to make the Jews give up their religion and live as the Greeks did. But 

Judah Maccabee and his army defeated him in the year 165 B.C.E. and rededicated the Temple which the Syrians had defiled. That is why you are in a Hanukkah play at 

Sabbath School. Now go up past 165 B.C.E. to the shelf marked 100 B.C.E. and then take the next step to the year One. Just above that is the year 70 C.E. From this

The Romans had been carrying on a bitter war with Palestine. They finally captured Jerusalem in 70 C.E. and burned the beautiful Temple to the ground. Palestine was now in the hands of the Romans. With their capital city destroyed, and their Temple gone, what happened to the Jewish people? Many were scattered in different lands. Some were brought as captives to Rome and Spain. Others went to Egypt, Babylonia, Arabia, and other countries.

Most of the people, however, remained in Palestine. It was to this land that the Jews of other countries still turned for help. How were they to lead Jewish lives in strange, far-away lands? What would keep the Jewish people together? Would they just get lost among other peoples and never be heard of again, like the Ten Lost Tribes of Northern Israel? It is true that even while the

But as long as they knew that their own land of Palestine held its place among the nations of the world, the Jews in other countries felt that they too had a land of their own. How was Jewish life to go on? It was just as if a person had suddenly lost his home, his family, and his wealth. In the same way, the people of Israel were left-without a home, with many of their possessions gone, with their religious life broken.

A Nation and a School 

There was one thing, however, which the Romans could not take away. The Jews still had their Bible and other Jewish laws. They had all the wisdom of Jewish life which the prophets and religious leaders of the past had given them. If the Torah could be kept alive in the minds and hearts of the Jewish people, then they could always remember that they were Jews, no matter where they lived.

Even while Jerusalem was still standing, some of the rabbis of the Great Sanhedrin were deeply troubled about the future of their people. They knew that the Roman army which was camped outside the city walls would win the victory. What would then happen to Jews and Jewish life? One of the rabbis, whose name was Jochanan ben 

Zaccai, decided upon a plan. He would leave Jerusalem and start a school somewhere else so that Jewish learning could go on. It was very hard to escape from the city at that time as the gates were carefully guarded by Jewish soldiers who would let no one in or out. There is a story that Jochanan ben Zaccai got into a coffin and his friends carried him out of the gates, pretending that he was dead and about to be buried. Since Jerusalem was a holy city, no one was allowed to be buried within the city. Jochanan went to the commander of the Roman army and asked for permission to start a little school in Jabneh, a small village near Jaffa. Since this rabbi had always stood for peace, the general could see no harm in granting the request. So this wise and courageous rabbi started his school. Many scholars came to study with him, for he was known as a great teacher. In the difficult years, after the Jewish people had lost its capital and its Temple, it turned to Jabneh for help and encouragement. And from

this little town, the rabbis and teachers of Israel taught the people how to live Jewish lives no matter in what country they dwelt. 

Bar Cochba and Rabbi Akiba

Sixty-two years after Jerusalem was taken, the Jews made another great effort to free themselves from the hateful Roman government. The Emperor Hadrian had passed some very harsh laws against the Jews. He refused to let them practice their religion. He made the people pay heavy taxes. The Jews were always most deeply aroused when their religion was in danger. They rebelled against the injustice of Rome. A leader arose, an able general whom the people called Bar Cochba, which means "Son of a Star." He was regarded as the 

Messiah, the leader who would re-establish the Jewish kingdom. Bar Cochba led the Jews in a fierce struggle against Rome. Hopes rose high that they might reconquer the land and rebuild the Temple. Rabbi Akiba, who was living at that time, urged the young general onward and inspired the people to follow him. But the great force of Rome steadily defeated one fortress after another which the Jews had gained, until the entire land was again in Roman hands. Bar Cochba was killed in battle.

The laws were enforced more cruelly than ever. The rabbis were ordered to close their schools and those who dared to disobey were put to death.

The brave Rabbi Akiba continued to gather his pupils about him and teach them the Torah, in spite of the law against it. He died as a martyr to his religion. Akiba was arrested by the Romans and put in prison in the city of Caesarea. Even while in prison he summoned his pupils and taught them. There is a story that as Akiba was suffering the agonies of death, he remained calm,

praising God, expressing his love and devotion for Him and pronouncing the Shema, "Hear, O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One."

When things seemed very dark for the Jewish people, Emperor Hadrian died and a kinder ruler came to the throne of Rome. A number of Hadrian's harsh laws were put aside. Soon the Jews gathered their rabbis together in another Sanhedrin and opened the schools of the country. Palestine continued to be the center of Jewish life and learning.

The Age of Great Rabbis 

The period of Jewish history from the time Jerusalem was destroyed to about the year 500 C.E. might be called the Age of Great Rabbis. Some of the best known and beloved rabbis of history lived during this time. Among them were Rabbi Akiba and Rabbi Meir. There are many delightful stories about these two which you will hear later on. There was Hanina, who like Akiba, died for his religion at the hands of the Romans. There was 

Simon, son of Gamaliel, whose kindness and justice were known throughout the land. During this period lived 

Judah, son of Ilai, who was very fond of arguing with 

Rabbi Meir on questions of Jewish law. About three thousand wise sayings of Rabbi Judah have come down to us. There was the other Rabbi Simon, son of Johai, who together with his boy, Eleazar, had to flee from the anger of the Romans and remain hidden in a cave for thirteen years. You shall hear some charming stories later about these rabbis and other Jewish leaders.

A Story of Two Children in the Days of the Rabbis 

Let us see what life in Palestine was like in the days of the rabbis. We shall visit the land in the time of

On the Sea of Galilee 

A gentle breeze was rippling the waters of the Sea of Galilee. Saul and Daniel drifted along in a small rowboat, enjoying the pleasant warmth of the October sun. 

They looked up at the clearest of blue skies and then gazed questioningly at each other. The gentle breeze grew a bit stronger. "The old fisherman at the wharf told me that a storm was brewing," Saul said to his companion, "but I don't see even a wisp of a cloud." "You know how suddenly storms arise on this lake," replied Daniel. "It will be wiser not to get too far away." "The water is so calm and peaceful here most of the time, I always find it hard to believe that an angry storm can blow up quickly." Saul looked out over the pleasant grey-blue waters which reflected the sunlight and mirrored the surrounding hills. Boats of different kinds and sizes dotted this inland sea. Most of them were small fishing vessels with graceful sails that looked like the wings of huge white birds. The sails were widest at the bottom and tapered off to a point at the peak, so the top offered little resistance to the wind. Several larger boats with gaily decorated sails and painted prows glided by. They belonged to the wealthy and pleasure-loving

Romans who lived in Tiberias, on the western shore of Galilee, from which the boys had just come. 

The boys were watching a fisherman in a small sailing boat close by. With a skilful swing of the arms, he cast a circular net into the water. Jewish Holy Days 

The next few weeks were busy and important ones for Rabbi Eliezer and his family. First came the solemn festivals of Rosh Hashonah and Yom Kippur. Rosh

Hashonah, the New Year, was a time for serious but happy thoughts. The beginning of another year meant an opportunity to do better and to express renewed faith in God. The second holiday, the Day of Atonement, was especially impressive to Daniel. All day the synagogue was filled with the white-robed figures of Jews who wept and prayed. They asked to be forgiven for their sins and they prayed God to rebuild Jerusalem and the 

Temple.

Then came the building of the Succah. The children loved to help with this task. They decorated the booth with sheaves of wheat and many kinds of fruit. The festival itself was a happy one. Not so long ago, in the days when Trajan and Hadrian had been emperors they had passed harsh laws against the Jews and had made life very difficult for them. Even now the Jews suffered under heavy taxes which they had to pay Rome, but some of the cruel laws which forbade them to practice their religion had been taken away by a kinder ruler.

Another important event was happening in the family.

But now he was actually to begin the study of the Bible. He would learn to read the words of the sacred text. There was a special family celebration in honor of the happy day, for it was considered a great privilege and joy to begin the study of the Torah. Rabbi

In the Market Place 

Since Naomi did not go to school, she had a chance to do a number of other things. She liked to go to the market-place with her father or mother and watch the busy scenes that went on there. On Mondays and Thursdays the farmers would come in from the districts outside of Tiberias, and display their wares in the wide, open space near the gates of the city. In large sacks, in baskets of many shapes and sizes, and in jars, they brought vegetables and fruits, olive oil and honey, grains, wool and flax.

Figs, dates, grapes, and pomegranates made their appearance in due season. Fresh fish from the Sea of Galilee was seen in abundance. Much of it was pickled and packed in large casks to be exported to other countries. 

There were numerous cities along the seacoast of Palestine which carried on trade with other lands, and then brought their imported products to the markets of inland cities. For the wealthy Romans of Tiberias and for the 

Jews who could afford them, merchants from these coast cities displayed their wares-apples from Crete, lentils, beans, and gourds from Egypt and Greece, plates from Babylonia, baskets from Egypt, dresses from India, and veils from Arabia. Traders from the coast cities of Sidon and Tyre in Phoenicia came with their costly purple linens and fine glassware. Damascus in Syria was con-

nected with Palestine by fine Roman roads, and sent her beautifully woven carpets and finely tempered steel blades. 

In return, Palestine sent to other lands her products of wheat, oil, balsam, honey, and figs.

People and Costumes 

Naomi never wearied of watching the crowds in the market-place. There were the Romans in their fine linen cloaks, followed by slaves who carried their purchases. 

There were many Syrians in the crowds, for Syria was just north of Galilee. Poor and rich mingled together. 

Jews, Romans, and Christians touched elbows in friendly fashion. They all wore at least two garments which were alike in style if not in quality. One was the tunic, a narrow sack-like shirt. It had sleeves and came down to the feet. The tunic was worn either next to the skin or over another garment of linen. The outer dress was the cloak or mantle. It was a loose, square-shaped garment, fastened around the middle with a girdle. The rabbis, and others who observed the Jewish law carefully, wore fringes upon the corners of these garments like the fringes on the prayer-shawls. This was in obedience to a command in the Bible, which said, "Thou shalt make thee fringes upon the four corners of thy cloak with which thou coverest thyself, that thou mayest look upon it and remember all the commandments of the Lord and do them." 

The women dressed very much the same as the men except that their garments were larger and fuller. 

For the feet, sandals made of hard leather and fastened with straps were commonly used. Some of the wealthier people wore shoes of soft leather. Those which the women wore were often adorned with little bells or metal plates.

Both men and women wore turbans on their heads when out-of-doors. This protection was necessary in the hot climate of Palestine. The turban was a thick headdress wound several times around the head.

Bright colors were much liked, and so the market-place was gay with blue, violet, and crimson robes. These colors were sometimes blended to make many-colored tunics and mantles.

Naomi especially liked to watch some of the fine ladies who came tripping by adorned with much finery. They wore bracelets around their elbows and wrists, anklets, ear-rings, necklaces, and rings. They liked to use paint and often darkened their eyebrows and eyelashes. Perfume was also much used, and many ladies carried a small bottle of scent with them.

Beggars in their ragged cloaks and Bedouins from the desert added a darker and dustier note to the scene.

The public well, which was also near the gates of the city, was always a lively and interesting place. People who had no cisterns of their own came here to draw water. This was considered a task for the women, and their gossiping voices could be heard as they awaited their turn. When their jars were filled, they placed them upon their heads or shoulders and walked gracefully away. Not far off stood a large building with a domed roof. It was the public bath house and had an important place in the life of the town. 

Naomi often passed the soup kitchen where, every day, those who had nothing to eat could get food. The town tried hard to take care of its poor people. The Jewish law required this. On Fridays, needy ones were given grants of money. Orphans and widows received special care. Every Jew gave a portion of his earnings to the

synagogue, and part of this money was used to take care of the poor.

It was not unusual for the busy market scene to be interrupted by a funeral procession on its way to a tomb outside the city walls. It was against the Jewish law to bury anyone within the city. The body was carried on a sort of stretcher by friends. The relatives followed, expressing their grief by weeping and' wailing. Hired mourners singing sad melodies were a part of the procession. The tomb was either a natural cave or a small chamber cut out of the rock. The opening was low and narrow, being about three feet in height. In the walls of this chamber, shelves were hollowed out of the rock, each one big enough to hold a body. The grave was covered with a stone slab and the entire cave was closed by a huge stone.

Rabbis Working at Trades 

On some days, Naomi loved to sit beside her father outside his tiny workshop in one of the busy market streets. While he sewed on his sandals she would watch the shoppers pass and listen to the polite bargaining that went on.

It was not unusual for rabbis to work at a trade. Many of them had to earn their living in this way since they received no money for their work as rabbis. To accept pay for studying and teaching the Jewish Law seemed wrong to them.

The rabbis believed, too, that it was good for people to do some kind of work with their hands. Labor was regarded with respect. They thought that the ideal life was one which combined work with study. It was considered a father's duty to teach his son a trade. The rabbis themselves set the example. Hillel had been a

wood-cutter, and Shammai, a carpenter. Rabbi Hanina earned his living as a shoemaker, and Rabbi Meir as a scribe. Other well-known rabbis worked as tailors, bakers, needle-makers, and in many other trades.

Naomi did not think it at all strange for her learned father to sit outside his little shop and stitch sandals.

Many people worked out-of-doors where it was cooler and pleasanter than inside. The shops were small and had no windows. The only opening was the door. And so the goldsmiths and the silversmiths, the weavers and the potters, sat outside their shops and carried on their work, pausing only when a customer stopped to make a purchase.

Naomi wished that she could spend more of her time at the market-place but there were household tasks to be done at home. Then, too, her mother disapproved of a girl as old as Naomi being seen too frequently in public places. Women and girls were expected to remain quietly at home most of the time.

At the Home of Judah Ha-Nasi 

On the day of the party, Daniel and his father started out for the beautiful home of Judah, the Prince. This famous descendent of the family of Hillel did indeed live in princely fashion. His palace was surrounded by lovely gardens. He had many servants and slaves. 

As Daniel and his father approached the tall double gates which led into the gardens, a gate-keeper swung them wide open. The two walked up a pleasant pathway to the big stone mansion. They announced their arrival by tapping on the door with the knocker of wrought iron which hung there. A servant led them through a hallway into a large inner courtyard. Daniel looked around him in delight. A cool fountain played in the center, upon a marble basin. The floor of the courtyard was tiled with colored marble except for the places where a

few mulberry trees grew. An open porch supported by pillars led into the house on all four sides. 

The men of the Sanhedrin and the students of the school were gathered in this place. forward and greeted the newcomers. 

Rabbi Judah came 

He was dressed in a fine linen cloak tied with a girdle of many colors. The turban on his head was also of fine linen. 

The rabbis who gathered here were well-known among Jews all over the world. There were the brilliant young students from Babylonia, Samuel and Rab, who later went back to their homes and started schools in Babylonia which became great centers of Jewish learning. 

The guests were summoned to the banquet hall where a royal feast was served. The tables were arranged to form three sides of a hollow square. Along the outer side were soft couches of about the same height as the tables. On these couches the guests reclined while they ate. Servants came and went along the inner side of the tables bringing many kinds of well-cooked foods. 

Forks were not used. The guests ate with their fingers and wiped them frequently upon large embroidered napkins.

Daniel sat near his father on the soft couch and enjoyed the good food while he listened to the interesting talk that went on. The company entertained itself by relating delightful stories which brought out some point worth remembering. Words of wisdom were treasured by these learned men. Many of their wise sayings, together with those of great rabbis who lived before them, became a part of the Mishnah and were called "Sayings of the Fathers."

In the midst of the merriment, another visitor was announced. Rabbi Judah arose to greet him. The visitor bowed low. "To the honored Patriarch of the Jews,

Rabbi Judah, the Prince,-greetings! The Emperor of the Romans sends his good wishes on this happy occasion. 

As an expression of his friendship, he presents you with this gift." "My humble thanks to His Majesty, the Emperor," replied the rabbi. "May it serve as a sign of good-will between his people and mine." He led the messenger to a place at the table and saw that he was well-served. 

The talk, merry-making, and feasting went on until far into the night. Finally the guests began to depart. 

The narrow streets were unlighted and quite dark. Rabbi Judah sent a servant with each small group of guests. 

The servant walked in front swinging a metal lantern which contained an oil lamp. Daniel greatly enjoyed the walk home, for he was seldom out after dark, and the streets looked strange and interesting in the dim light of the lantern. For many days Daniel talked of the grand party at the home of Rabbi Judah.

A Wedding Celebration 

Naomi was dressing with special care. She was to be a bridesmaid at the wedding of Saul's sister and had been looking forward to this day for a long time. 

Just one year ago, the engagement or betrothal had taken place. That had been an important ceremony. 

The families of the bride and groom had met and drawn up an agreement in writing. This betrothal was just as binding as marriage. It could not be broken without a divorce. 

And now, after much preparation, the wedding day had come. When Naomi arrived she found the bride in an inner chamber, surrounded by a group of older women and young companions, all trying to help her get ready for the coming event. She was seated on a beauti-

fully carved bride's stool especially made for this occasion. The bride was looking into a small hand mirror made of polished metal while one of the women drew an ivory comb through her soft brown hair. The fine linen garments which they put on her were embroidered and delicately perfumed. She wore gold bracelets on her elbows and wrists, and silver anklets around her feet. 

Naomi thought she had never seen anyone so beautiful.

Finally, all was in readiness. At the hour of sunset, the bride was seated in a throne-like sedan chair which was covered with a canopy or "chuppah." She was carried by her relatives to her husband's home. Through the streets went the joyous procession. Her handmaidens walked beside her, spraying the air with perfume. They each carried a lamp which was made of wood and had a little vase at the end in which oil was burning. Some carried myrtle branches which they waved over the bride's head. A number of the friends and relatives went in front, scattering nuts and roasted grains of wheat to the children who lined the streets. Some played upon the drum and the lyre. Others danced and sang. As the procession went through the streets others joined in the lively and joyful parade.

The solemn moment came when the bride entered her husband's home. There, some of the older women gathered the maiden's flowing hair and arranged it under a thick veil. Never again would she have her head uncovered in public. A wedding blessing was pronounced by an official of the synagogue. Then came the wedding feast. It was considered a religious duty to be present at a wedding, and even the most serious and learned rabbis danced before the bridegroom to do him honor. 

Everyone sang praises to the bride, calling her "lovely, blooming, and gracious."

Naomi found it all very exciting and was sorry when it was time to leave. The celebrating and feasting would continue for seven days, with different guests coming and going. Naomi thought it was the grandest celebration she had ever seen.

"In other countries of the Roman empire the Jews are also well-treated," the speaker went on. "Let us hope that matters will get no worse and that the Roman emperors will regard us kindly." Now you have seen something of how the Jewish people lived in the days of the rabbis. Has it helped you find out why You are You? Many of the laws and customs of Jewish life which you observe today were kept in that far off time. If the rabbis had not taught the 

Jews how to live according to these laws, our ancestors might gradually have forgotten the Jewish religion, for the Temple was gone, and the Jews lived among strange peoples. You will now hear how they were scattered even more, after the days of Rabbi Judah.

The death of Judah Ha-Nasi brought great grief to the people. He became known simply by the name, "Rabbi." For about two hundred years after his death,

Palestine remained a center of Jewish life. The Mishnah was now the chief subject of study in the academies. 

Every sentence in it was discussed and explained by the rabbis and students. These discussions were finally arranged in a certain order and called the "Gemarah." The 

Mishnah and Gemarah together make up the Palestinian Talmud. This, together with the Babylonian Talmud of which you will hear later, has been studied and treasured by the Jewish people for hundreds of years. If you go into an Orthodox synagogue on almost any afternoon, you will find a group of men poring over the pages of this ancient treasury of Jewish knowledge. The Talmud fills many large volumes. It contains the beautiful stories of the rabbis and their wise sayings, as well as explanations of the Jewish laws.

Palestine Weakens as a Center of Jewish Life 

Somewhere between the years 313 and 325, the Roman Emperor, Constantine, the Great, accepted the Christian religion. He began to persecute the Jews in Palestine. 

They were not even permitted to study their laws in the synagogues. In the next hundred years, the Christian 

Roman emperors made life more and more difficult for the Jews. They were not allowed to build synagogues. 

Their religion was interfered with in many ways. When they rebelled against these injustices they were treated still more cruelly. Under these sad conditions, the academies of learning in Palestine grew smaller and smaller. 

Many Jews left the country and went to Babylonia where they would have more freedom to live as Jews.

Since so many Jews were living outside of Palestine, and many more were departing in these sad days of persecution, one of the Patriarchs, Hillel the Second, arranged a Hebrew calendar in writing so that all the Jews would know when to observe the holidays without waiting for messengers from the Sanhedrin. The leadership of Palestine in Jewish life grew weaker and weaker. In the year 425 C.E., when the Patriarch died, there was no one appointed to take his place as head of the Jewish people. But a new center of Jewish life and learning had arisen in the East, in the land of Babylonia. 

STORIES OF THE RABBIS A Parting Blessing 

JOCHANAN ben Zaccai was the rabbi who had started the Academy at Jabneh. He had reached an old age and was about to die. His sorrowing pupils gathered around him and asked for a parting blessing. Rabbi Jochanan replied, "May it be God's will that the fear of Heaven be as strong in you as the fear of man!"

"What," exclaimed his pupils, "should we not fear God more than man?" "If you fear Him as much," answered the Rabbi, “I shall be well content. If one is about to do wrong he often looks around him to see if anyone is looking. Let us show the same fear of God, who always sees, and we will not do wrong so easily." 

Rabbi Joshua ben Hananya

Rabbi Joshua was a well-known rabbi who lived about fifty years after the Temple had been destroyed. He earned his living as a maker of needles and as a charcoal burner. Rabbi Joshua was very clever, but he liked to relate the following stories which show how two children and a woman conquered him in wit on different occasions. "Once, on my travels," he said, “I came near a town where the road separated to the right and the left. I was uncertain which one to take and so I asked a little boy which one led to town." "Both," answered the child, "but that to the right is short and long, and that to the left is long and short." 

Then he ran away before I could ask him what he meant by that puzzling answer. I chose the road to the right, but had not gone very far when my way was blocked by hedges and gardens. On returning to the place where I had started, I found the boy again, and asked him how he could be so unkind as to misdirect a stranger. "I didn't misdirect you," answered the boy. "This road is shorter but it is longer because of the many things that are in the way. The other road is longer but it is really shorter, being a public road." I admired the lad's wit and went on my way. "I was walking through the streets of the city, one day," went on the rabbi, "when I met another little boy

carrying a covered dish. What have you in the dish, child?" I asked. "My mother would not have covered it, master, if she had wanted its contents to be known," he answered, showing unusual good sense. "I once made my home with a certain family. The woman prepared my dinner and set it before me. Since 

I was very hungry I did not leave a portion for the servant as was the custom. The next day I did the same thing. On the third day, my hostess determined to point out my wrong-doing. She put so much salt in the food that I could not eat it. After a few spoonfuls I stopped, and finished my meal on bread. "I understand why you are not eating," the woman said. "Yesterday and the day before you forgot about the poor servant, so you are making it up today by leaving most of the food." Then I was humbled and admitted my fault. 

Rabbi Joshua and the Princess 

Rabbi Joshua's mind was more beautiful than his body.

But although he was far from handsome, his wisdom and kindness won the love and respect of many people. 

He even won the favor of Emperor Hadrian at Rome. 

One day while he was at Court, the princess teased him about his looks. "How does it happen," she asked, "that such glorious wisdom is enclosed in such an ugly body."

The rabbi was not disturbed by her rudeness. "In what kind of jars does your father keep his wine?" he inquired. "In earthen jars, to be sure," she answered. "Ordinary people do that," the witty rabbi answered,

"but since your father is an emperor he should keep his wine in silver and gold jars."

The princess commanded her servants to follow this suggestion. In a short time the wine was spoiled. "That was very fine advice you gave me," she told the rabbi. "The wine has become sour and spoiled." It "Now you can understand," replied Rabbi Joshua, "that wine keeps best in plain and ordinary vessels. is the same with wisdom."

The Lion and the Crane 

When the people were getting restless under the rule of the Romans and wished to rebel against them, Rabbi Joshua advised that they remain at peace. "The weak cannot always demand their rights of the strong," he said, and told the following fable.

A lion once suffered from a bone which was lodged in his throat. After many useless efforts to remove it, he called together all the animals and offered a reward to the one who could relieve him. Most of them were afraid to try it, but finally a crane agreed to do so. He thrust his long narrow neck into the lion's mouth and removed the bone. "What is my reward?" he asked. "Reward, indeed!" answered the other. "Is it not enough for you that I allowed you to take your head out of my mouth and did not snap it off?"

A Fable of the Fox and the Fish

At the time when Akiba lived, the Roman government closed the schools and forbade rabbis to teach the Bible

or Torah. In spite of this, Akiba continued to do so. 

When one man asked him why he thus endangered his life, he answered in the form of a fable as he was fond of doing. "One day," he said, "a fox was wandering along the banks of a river. Looking in, he saw that the fish were swimming around in great excitement. "What is the trouble?" he asked them. "We are afraid of the nets which have been cast in the river to catch us," they replied. "Well then," the fox advised, "come on dry land. Here the nets cannot catch you." "And they say you are a sly fox," the fish answered him. "If we are in danger right here in the water where we are most at home, how much more will the danger be when we are in a place that is strange to us!" "The same is true of the Jews," explained Rabbi Akiba. "If we are in danger while living according to the Torah where we are most at home, how much greater will be our danger if we forsake the Torah!" 

Whatever God Does is for the Best

Rabbi Akiba was once compelled to leave his native land because of the persecutions by the Romans. His only possessions at this time were a lamp, a cock, a donkey, and a book of the Law. Akiba spent a number of days in wandering over deserts and waste lands. Once after traveling all day, he came to a small village on the edge of a forest. He rejoiced at the thought that food and shelter and human companionship was at hand. But much to his sorrow, one family after another turned him away. He had to go to the forest to sleep. Lying down under a tree, he lighted his small oil lamp and proceeded to study the Law. Suddenly a storm arose and put out

the light. "May I not even study the Torah in peace,” cried the rabbi, but added, "whatever God does is for the best." He closed his eyes and fell asleep. After a short time a wild animal came and killed the cock. "Who will now awaken me at dawn for the morning prayer," Rabbi 

Akiba said sorrowfully. "But whatever God does is for the best." Scarcely had he uttered these words when a lion came and killed the donkey. "Everything is gone now," moaned the lonely traveler. "But God is just. 

Everything he does is for the best."

Akiba did not sleep much that night, and in the morning he went back to the village. Much to his surprise he found very few people left alive. A band of robbers had entered the village during the night, killed the inhabitants, and robbed the houses. When the rabbi recovered from his surprise, he cried, "How little man can understand the ways of God! If these hard-hearted people had not turned me away from their doors, I would have been killed. If the robbers had seen the light of my lamp, or heard my cock crow, or my donkey bray, I would have shared the fate of these people. Truly all that God does is for the best."

Stories of Rabbi Meir 

Another outstanding man of this period was Rabbi Meir. He had been one of Akiba's pupils. Rabbi Meir earned his living by being a scribe. He copied the Bible by hand.

It is said that one Purim he was in a little village where no copy of the Book of Esther could be found. It had to be read at the synagogue service. Rabbi Meir wrote the whole book from memory. It was found afterwards that he had not made a mistake in a single word.

Rabbi Meir taught his pupils both wisdom and kind-

ness. He made up many beautiful stories and proverbs which are found in the Talmud. It seems that he taught them most by his own example, for he regularly gave a third of all the money he earned to the poor. His wife, Beruriah, was also well-known for her high education and fine character. The following story is told about her. Rabbi Meir and Beruriah had two splendid boys whom they loved very dearly. Both of them suddenly became ill of a sickness that swept through the city striking many. On the Sabbath morning, Rabbi Meir went to the synagogue, while Beruriah remained at home with her sick children. Both of the boys died while the rabbi was away. With a grief-stricken heart, Beruriah covered their bodies, closed the door of their room, and went to greet her husband who was just returning. She did not wish to disturb his Sabbath peace and so said nothing of what had happened. She put off his questions about the children until twilight had come, and the Sabbath day was over. Then she said to Rabbi Meir, "My husband, a number of years ago, some one came here and entrusted to my care two precious jewels. Now he has come and asked for them. Shall I return them to him?" "Surely, Beruriah, you must return cheerfully to the owner what was entrusted to your care." "Then come." She led him to the room where his children were lying. Rabbi Meir broke down and wept. 

Beruriah comforted him and said, "Remember what you told me.

The Lord has given them into our care for awhile, and now He has taken them back."

Men are Responsible for One Another

Rabbi Simeon once explained to his pupils how a person's conduct affects others as well as himself. He told the following story.

A boat filled with people was on the sea. Suddenly one of the passengers began to bore a hole through the bottom of the boat. "Stop, stop," his frightened companions cried. "Why are you doing that?" "I am only digging a hole underneath my own seat," the other answered. "That has nothing to do with you." "Indeed, it has," they shouted. "When the water comes in, it will drown us as well as you." "So you see, one person's bad actions often make many people suffer."

The Greater Gift 

Rabbi Judah once received a valuable pearl as a gift from the emperor. In return, he sent a Mezuzah, the small metal case containing a piece of parchment on which are inscribed some verses from the Bible. 

The emperor thought that he was not well treated. "The gift I sent you was much finer," he told the rabbi. "My present is more precious than your pearl," Rabbi 

Judah replied, "for I must guard the pearl while my gift will guard you.”

UNIT II BABYLONIA IN THE DAYS OF THE TALMUD AND THE GEONIM A Nation's Memory 

How would you feel if suddenly you couldn't remember what happened yesterday or last week-or last year? Suppose you couldn't remember anything at all, except your name and where you live. It would give you a very queer feeling-a very insecure and uncertain feeling.

Suppose the United States forgot what happened to it in the past. Suppose it forgot all about the American 

War for Independence in 1776, and how the Pilgrims came over in the Mayflower, and how Columbus discovered America! Then you couldn't celebrate the Fourth of July, or Thanksgiving, or Washington's and Lincoln's birthdays. There would hardly be any holidays from school. That wouldn't be so nice, would it? Everyone would have a sort of mixed-up feeling, too. We wouldn't know how we got to this country, or what happened to us here. A country's memory is really its history. 

The Jewish people have many, many memories which reach into the past. These memories are our Jewish history, and they help to tell you who you are, because the things that happened long ago explain why You are You today. 

What is the next important memory in Jewish life?

You have seen how the Jews lived in Palestine for several hundred years after 70 C.E. You know how Roman persecutions made the Jewish colleges grow smaller and smaller. When Palestine became too weak to be the 57

leader, Jewish knowledge did not die out. Another country had gradually been taking over the leadership. That country was Babylonia. 

See if you can find it on the map. Put your finger on

Palestine. Now move eastward, which is to the right. 

First you will come to a desert. A little farther east you reach a land where two great rivers flow, and then join into one. This land of the two rivers is Babylonia. 

I am sure you have heard of this country before. You must have studied about it in school, for Babylonia is one of the first countries in the world that we know anything about. The people there were among the earliest in the world to be civilized. That means, they learned how to read and write, how to irrigate the soil, how to build good homes and grand palaces, and how to make laws, long before most of the countries of the world knew these things.

It was this land of Babylonia which next became important in Jewish history. The story of the Jewish people really began there, because, according to the story in the Bible, Abraham was the first Hebrew, and he lived in Ur, a city in Babylonia. It was from Ur that Abraham, together with Sarah, his wife, and Lot, his nephew, and all his followers, set out into the desert to seek the Promised Land, which was Palestine. 

Many, many years later Babylonia became the home of a large group of Jews. It was at the time when that country was a mighty empire, and it conquered the little Kingdom of Judea in the south of Palestine. The king, Nebuchadnezzar, carried away a number of Jewish families as captives and brought them to Babylonia. They were not treated as captives, however. They were given land on which to settle, and were allowed to govern themselves.

But the greatness of Babylonia did not last much longer. A strong and war-like nation, which lay to the east, overcame her. Persia, under the leadership of King

Cyrus, conquered Babylonia and became its ruler. 

See if you can find Persia on the map. Put your finger on Babylonia. Move it eastward, to the right. After you cross the Tigris River you come to a land of plateaus and mountains. This land is Persia.

Cyrus was kind to the Jewish people. He allowed them to return to Judea and rebuild their beloved homeland. Most of the Jews, however, remained in Babylonia, where the Persians who were now the new rulers, treated them kindly. For a long, long time, Babylonia remained a part of Persia and the Jews lived under Persian laws. 

Let us see what kind of life the Jewish people were leading in this land of Babylonia, for now, Jews in other countries, even those in Palestine, were turning to her for leadership.

We were looking in at Palestine at about 200 C.E. 

Now take two big steps to 400 C.E. and then a little bit more, to 425 C.E. How long ago was that? Subtract 

425 from the year you are living in, and you will know.

Now let us pick out a little boy, Yohani, who lived at that time, and see what his life was like.

The Story of Yohani 

Yohani leaned idly against the trunk of a date palm and stared through its branches at the intensely blue sky above. The Prince of the Exile

The Exilarch was a very wealthy man.

Babylonia in 800 C.E.

You have been following the story of Yohani in the year 425 C.E. Climb up now to the year 800 C.E. That is a nice round number to remember. How many years did you climb up from 425 C.E.? Do you know how long ago the year 800 C.E. was? Subtract that number from the year you are living in and you will find out. 

Over eleven hundred years ago, wasn't it? How many shelves would that be in the Hole of Time where you are now climbing upward?

Babylonia in the year 800 C.E. was still the most important country in the world for the Jewish people. From the far off lands of Europe, Jews looked to Babylonia for help and guidance in the religious questions which were always coming up. It was natural that many questions should arise, for the Jewish religion had laws and customs for almost everything a person did. There were laws about daily prayers, about food, about cleanliness, about how the festivals should be observed, how slaves should be treated, how the poor should be taken care of, and countless other subjects about the daily lives of the people. These laws were contained in the Talmud, and people in other countries felt that questions about these laws could be answered best in the academies of Babylonia where the Talmud had developed. These academies of Sura and Pumbedita in the year 800 were still filled with interested scholars who spent much of their time in studying the Jewish laws. The name and memory of 

Rab Ashi was held in great respect by these scholars, for he had arranged a large part of the Gemara. The Mish- 85

The heads of the two academies were given the new title, "Gaon," which means "Excellency." They had a great deal of power in the land, and like the Exilarch, appointed judges and other important officers. 

The head of all the Jewish people in Babylonia and surrounding countries was still the Exilarch, or the Prince of the Exile. He lived almost like a king, and had a great palace and many slaves.

As before, many Jews earned their living by farming the rich soil which was kept fertile by the two rivers and the numerous canals. Others worked as weavers, tailors, dyers, silversmiths, tanners, potters, millers, and at other trades. Even many of the rabbis and students of the academies worked at these trades or farmed the land to earn a living. Some great rabbis earned their living as shoemakers and blacksmiths, for work was considered honorable.

The Fire-Worshipping Magi 

These years which you have just climbed, from 425 

C.E. to 800 C.E., were not all peaceful ones for the Jewish people in Babylonia. Shortly after Rab Ashi's death, a new king began to rule in Persia. He was a good friend of the Magi, the fire-worshipping priests of the 

Zoroastrian religion. These Magi, or priests, got the king on their side and he passed some unkind laws against both Jews and Christians. The priests believed that since fire was holy, there should not be any in Jewish homes. 

On Zoroastrian festivals these Magi would enter homes of the Jews and remove all lights and fires to their own temples. The burning coals were carried away in metal fire-pans. Fear of the priests made the Jews kindle the

Chanukah lights secretly, although before this they had been placed near the window. The Jews were even forbidden to observe the Sabbath and to study the Talmud. 

For about one hundred and fifty years, the Jews suffered from these laws. Then a kindly Persian ruler came to the throne, and life was peaceful again for the Jews. 

The Story of Mohammed 

You will find that Babylonia in 800 C.E. was somewhat different than it had been in 425 C.E. The country was no longer ruled by the Persians, under whose laws the Jews had lived for about a thousand years. A group of people with a new religion and new ways of living had grown up in Arabia. They had conquered Persia and 

These people were called Mohammedans in honor of the founder of their religion, Mohammed. They were also known as Moslems. many other countries.

You will want to find out something about these people, for the Mohammedans and Jews had a great deal to do with each other for many years.

First, find Arabia on the map. It is a very large peninsula between the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf. The northern part of the peninsula lies between Babylonia and Palestine. 

The people in Arabia used to worship idols and believed in good and evil spirits as other heathen people did. Those in the south of Arabia lived as desert Bedouin and spent much of their lives outdoors. They thought that each object in nature had a spirit which must be worshipped. A spring of water or a certain tree might possess one of these spirits.

During the days when the Jews in Palestine were persecuted by foreign conquerors, a number of Jewish families escaped into the desert of Arabia and lived like

Bedouin. From them the Arabs learned much about the 

Jewish religion. A number of Arabs became Jews. At one time a whole tribe of Arabs accepted Judaism, and a small Jewish kingdom grew up in a part of South Arabia known as Yemen. Later, the army of a neighboring country, Ethiopia, conquered the little kingdom. Jews from this part of Arabia are known as Yemenite Jews. 

In the past, Arabs and Jews generally got along well together. They both belong to a certain branch of the white race called Semites. The Arabs believed that they were all descended from Ismael, who belonged to the family of Abraham, and so they felt somewhat related to the Jews. Even today, the places in Palestine where the great ancestors of Israel lie buried, are holy to the 

Arabs too.

The Christian religion was also spreading through many countries and helped to do away with the belief in heathen gods. The Arabs were, therefore, prepared for the new religion which was founded by Mohammed, an Arab. He was born in the year 571. Not much is known about his childhood except that he was left an orphan at an early age and earned his living as a shepherd boy. Later on he married a rich woman for whom he had worked. He then had plenty of time to think about the ideas of God which had come to him while tending his sheep on the lonely hills. Mohammed spent much of his life among Jews and learned a great deal about the Jewish religion. A number of these ideas he put into his new religion, which he called Islam. 

The first principle of this religion was this "There is no God but Allah and Mohammed is His prophet.” "Allah" was their other name for God.

At first the new prophet had only a few followers, but later on many Arabs joined him and the new religion

grew rapidly. Mohammed even tried to make the Jews become his followers and was angry when they refused. 

The holy book of the Mohammedans is the Koran which Mohammed wrote. It is the Mohammedan Bible.

Their holy cities are Mecca and Medina in Arabia. 

Before Mohammed died, he told his followers that they should force people to accept the new religion, even at the point of the sword. The Arabs formed large armies and went out to surrounding countries, which they conquered. People were made to accept Mohammedanism or suffer punishment.

The chief ruler of the Arabs, after Mohammed, was called the Caliph. The first Caliphs were excellent generals and led their armies on a conquering march through the countries of the East and even into Europe. They conquered Palestine, Syria, Persia, Babylonia, the northern coast of Africa, and a large part of Spain. 

One of the Caliphs built the city of Bagdad on the Tigris River in Babylonia and made it his capital. As the Arabs grew more and more powerful and rich, they stopped being so war-like. They became interested in education and studied the arts and sciences. The leaders built beautiful palaces and learned to live grandly. 

At first, both the Jews and Christians in Palestine and Babylonia were badly treated by the Arabs because they would not join the new religion. Caliph Omar entered 

Jerusalem and laid the foundation for a mosque, which is a Mohammedan house of worship, on the place where the Temple once stood. It is called the Mosque of Omar. 

Close by it is a wall which is left from the Temple. It is called the Wailing Wall because Jews come there to mourn the destruction of the Temple. 

When the Arabs stopped their war-like methods, they allowed the Jews freedom to govern themselves. The

Exilarch in Babylonia made his headquarters in Bagdad and paid regular visits to the court of the Caliph, where he was received with honor.

And so, for the most part, life for Jews in Babylonia was peaceful and free. They had to pay taxes to the 

Arabs, but they had their own courts, and their chief rulers were still the Exilarch and the two Geonim.

The Story of a Lad in Pumbedita 

David was walking about in the lovely garden surrounding his home. He had just finished his studies for the day and was glad to be outdoors. Ahead of him lay the waters of the Nehar Isa Canal, for his home and gardens were on its banks. This canal stretched from the 

Euphrates to the city of Bagdad on the Tigris. David hoped very much to visit Bagdad some day and see the palace of the Caliph. His father, who was a merchant, made several trips a year there.

David watched a boat moving slowly down the canal, drawn by men who ran along the bank opposite him. He picked a tall reed which grew at the edge of the water and was idly twisting it around his finger when he saw a slave runhing toward him. "Master David, your father sent word from the Academy. A batch of letters has just arrived from Spain. 

He said he promised to inform you.” "Good," cried the lad. "I shall go at once and be present when they are read. Just think, it takes a whole year for letters to reach us from Spain, and yet the scholars of our Academy are always in touch with the scholars there."

David hurried back to the house to put on a better suit of clothes. His home was made of bricks covered with a bright glaze of blue and yellow which gleamed in the sunlight. He passed the large banqueting-room whose lofty domed ceiling was supported by columns made of palm trees. His bedroom was well furnished with a beautifully carved chest of drawers, a chair and table, and a large bed which was so high that there were several steps leading up to it. On the wall a fire-pan was hanging. During the two or three cool months of the year, hot coals were placed in this pan to warm the room. 

David slipped on a fine linen garment and fastened it around the waist with a girdle. Then he hastened through the well-kept gardens, out of the gate, and down the street to the Academy. Although his father was one of the wealthiest merchants in Pumbedita, he was a scholar as well, and spent many hours in study. The Gaon of the Academy and he were good friends.

David opened the door of the large room where the rabbis and students were meeting and, stepping softly down the aisle, sat down beside his father. The Gaon sat at the head of the room, facing the others. In the first row sat the most learned scholars. Here, riches did not count. Knowledge was respected above all else.

The Gaon was looking at a roll of parchments on the table before him. "We must consider carefully every question that is presented in these letters from our fellow Jews in Spain," he was saying. The letters were written in Hebrew. 

They contained questions about the meaning of certain parts of the Talmud. Two rabbis in Spain did not agree about the meaning of certain sentences and wanted the scholars of Pumbedita to settle the dispute for them. 

There was another question about observing the Sabbath

Day while on board a ship. It asked whether a Jew was allowed to disembark if the ship landed on the Sabbath. 

One rabbi wanted to know some point about the treatment of slaves, as explained in the Talmud. 

The Gaon gave each problem to a certain rabbi for special study. The questions would then be brought up in the Academy for a long discussion, and a decision would be made under the leadership of the Gaon. Then answers would be sent back to Spain in long letters called "responsa." 

As David listened to the letters that had just arrived, he wondered about Jews in other parts of the world. He felt proud that they should all regard Babylonia as the center of the Jewish world, and his own town of Pumbedita, as one of the capitals of Jewish law and learning. 

Even the Jews in Palestine turned to Babylonia for the final opinion on important matters of Jewish life. 

Many letters that came from other lands contained gold coins, which were contributions for the Geonim and the academies.

The meeting at the Academy that day was especially interesting for a batch of letters which was all ready to be sent out to different countries was read to the rabbis once more. Several of these were going to cities on the northern coast of Africa where there were large Jewish communities. These cities carried on trade in silks and precious metals. The letters were about how the Sabbath should be kept by Jews who were travelling in caravans for many weeks. Some caravans went all the way to

India and China and brought back ivory, spices, and other fine things of the East to the countries of Europe. A caravan could travel all the way from Spain to India and still be within the boundaries of the Mohammedan Empire.

Letters to the Jews in some of the lands of Europe contained words of comfort, for the Jews were not well treated everywhere. They did not all have the freedom and self-government which Babylonian Jews enjoyed. 

As the responsa were read David listened with interest. 

They were generally addressed to the chief rabbi of the congregation and were written in fine style. One which was sent to Barcelona, Spain, began in this way: "Amram ben Isaac, Gaon of the city of Pumbedita, to all scholars and their disciples and those of our brethren of the house of Israel who dwell in Barcelona, and who are dear, beloved, and revered unto us. May their prosperity increase and wax great! Receive ye greetings from us and from Rabbi Isaac and from the President of the Court of Justice, and from the heads of the Kallah, and from all other scholars and disciples of the college, all of whom ever pray for your health and that God in 

His great mercy may have compassion upon you. "The questions which ye have laid before us we have caused to be read unto us while all the sages and disciples sat before us. We have studied them and weighed all that is within them, and with divine help have given to them the following answers."

The answers then followed very fully written out, and the letter ended in this way: "May God reveal unto thee,

O friend and brother, and unto all the scholars and disciples of thy city, the Torah of wisdom and of understanding, and clothe you with a mantle of glory."

After the meeting in the Academy was over for the day, David walked home with his father. "I'd like to be a traveller some day," he said. "I want to go on a sailing vessel far across the sea, or perhaps join a caravan that winds its way across the country to India or China." "I can see that you really have listened to the responsa

this afternoon," smiled his father. "Now you are curious about your fellow Jews in other countries. Yes, our people may be scattered through many lands, but they are bound together by their love of the Torah and the Talmud, and their increasing desire for knowledge. 

These responsa which are sent to far off lands do much to bind the Jewish people together."

Bagdad! The very name was magic to David. The city had been especially built to serve as the capital for the Caliph, ruler of the great Moslem empire which reached from Spain to India. Often as David walked along the banks of the Nebar Isa Canal, he thought of the city at its other end and wondered if he would ever see it. Bagdad was not really so far away, for the

Spain Compared with the Rest of Europe

The period of the Middle Ages in Europe is often called the Dark Ages because it was a time of widespread ignorance. The people who could read and write were very few. Even kings and other people of wealth and importance seldom knew how to sign their names. What then could be expected of ordinary people! Only the priests who lived in the monasteries received an education. 

But Spain in the Middle Ages was different from the rest of Europe in a number of ways. A large part of this land had been conquered by certain tribes of Arabs from northern Africa. They were called Moors. The Moors were believers in the Mohammedan religion and so were also known as Moslems. They brought with them a love of knowledge and comfortable ways of living which they had learned in the East. The Moors were far more educated than the Christians of Europe in the Tenth Century. 

These Arab tribes established a great, independent kingdom in Spain with a Caliph, or king, at the head. It was called the Caliphate of Cordova after the name of the city which was its capital.

The Jews came to Spain in large numbers and settled there. They, too, brought with them from the East their old love of education and highly civilized ways of living. 

The Moors and Jews made Spain into a powerful and wealthy country where people lived in a much finer way than in the other lands of Europe.

The Story of the Travellers 

There is a story which tells how Spain and other European countries became great centers of Jewish learning, so that people no longer looked to the rabbis of Babylonia for leadership. It seems that four well-known rabbis started from Babylonia on a long voyage across the Mediterranean. Their purpose was to travel among the 

Jewish people in western lands and collect money to support the colleges in Babylonia which were about to close for lack of funds. The ship in which the rabbis were travelling was captured by an admiral who was in the service of the Spanish Caliph. The people on board the ship, the rabbis among them, were taken captive and sold as slaves. The rabbis were sold in four different countries of Europe. They were freed by fellow Jews and each one established a school of learning in the West. 

We do not know just how true this particular story is. 

We do know, however, that a number of learned Jews, Rabbi Moses among them, travelled from the East to the West and started colleges like the ones in Palestine and 

Babylonia. Let us imagine, then, that we are following the experiences of one of the four rabbis and his young son who travelled from Babylonia to Spain. Through their eyes we shall get a picture of what Spain was like in the days when Jews and Arabs were settled there.

Arabs invented the mariner's compass by which a ship is steered. "But it is in the noble field of medicine that our Jews chiefly excel. Kings and noblemen seek their services.

You will find many Jewish and Arab physicians in Spain." "But you still have not mentioned the art which Arabs love most," Rabbi Moses reminded him, "and that is poetry." "It is a fact too well-known to need mentioning," the other replied. "We have Jewish poets too as you shall see. They write in both Hebrew and Arabic. The Caliph loves a beautiful poem. He will reward a writer even more richly than a gallant soldier on the field of battle. 

Cordova is proud of having large libraries of valuable manuscripts written by hand on the finest of linen paper." "The Arabs brought the secret of paper-making here from the East," Rabbi Moses added. "I am content to be in this land where knowledge is pursued and loved. I am happy that our Jews have a good home here." 

Industries and Occupations in Cordova

Asher proved to be a helpful and kindly friend in those first lonely days. He and Rabbi Moses had a number of interesting conversations while Enoch was content to sit by his father's side and listen.

"How do our people earn a living?" the rabbi asked 

Asher one evening. "What business and industries do they carry on in this beautiful Cordova and throughout the fair land of Spain?" "Our trades and occupations are too numerous to mention," the other replied. "As in Palestine, our ancient homeland, the olive and grape grow well in the soil of southern Spain. Many Jews own vineyards and engage in the making of wine. Right outside this city are acres of olive trees from which large quantities of oil are made each year. Orange, lemon, and citron trees also thrive in our sunny climate." "It does indeed sound like Palestine," Rabbi Moses said with a sigh and a smile. "In Spain the East and West have met," Asher went on. "The Moors have brought many new plants into 

Europe from the East. Rice, sugar-cane, ginger, bananas, mulberry trees and palm trees are being grown in Europe for the first time. "An industry new to Europe has also been brought here with the mulberry tree. The Jews know how to care for the silkworm and to manufacture silk. Many countries of Europe and even lands in Africa and Asia now depend on the Jews and Moors of Spain for their finest silk. 

Woolen cloth is also made here in large quantities by our fellow Jews and exported to other lands. "Spain is noted for other manufactured articles," Asher went on. "Our own city of Cordova is well-known for the fine leather articles it produces. Cordovan leather is prized in many lands. Another famous product is made in Toledo, a city in central Spain. The fine steel swords and blades of Toledo compare well with the renowned blades of Damascus. The art of making beautiful glass-

ware and pottery has also been brought here from the East. "But let us go on to other occupations. Spain, as you know, is a land of mountains and hills. Within these, great treasures are hidden. Gold and silver, copper and lead, iron and quicksilver are brought forth from the mines. There are precious stones, too. Rubies, agates, garnets, and other lovely jewels are unearthed from the depths of the land. Rich pearls are gathered on the coast near Barcelona, the seaport of the Northeast. Skilful workers are needed to handle these fine materials. You will find Jews among the goldsmiths, silversmiths, and jewellers. "Spain has other treasure hidden in the hills. The fine marble palaces, stone synagogues, and beautiful mosques which you will see all over this fair city are built chiefly of the many-colored marble and jasper which the workmen quarry continuously from the mountains." "With all these industries Spain must carry on a large trade with other countries," Rabbi Moses suggested. "She does indeed," Asher agreed. “Our harbors are filled with busy vessels that sail up and down the Mediterranean and over to England. Moors and Jews own hundreds of merchant ships. Spanish caravans, too, travel from this land all the way to India and China.” "The East and West are beginning to meet each other," Rabbi Moses said thoughtfully. "The ancient civilizations of Babylonia, Persia, Syria, Palestine, and Egypt are travelling westward in the footsteps of Jew and Moor.

Let us hope that the light of knowledge will soon begin to brighten the ignorance which now darkens most of Europe." "Spain is the most civilized land in the West at the present time," the other said proudly. "None can com-

pare with her in knowledge, power and wealth. And who have brought this about but the Jews and Arabs! "Our people help to direct the affairs of the country," 

Asher continued with enthusiasm. "A number of them occupy high places in the government of the land. The 

Caliph trusts them and respects their ability. His chief adviser and friend is the Jew, Hasdai Ibn Shaprut, the minister of finance. He is known to Jews all over the world." "He is indeed a bright light in Jewish life as well as at the court of the Caliph," Rabbi Moses agreed.

Enoch had been listening to this conversation with great eagerness. But he still had not entirely recovered from his long journey and his head began to droop. "You must rest now," Jehuda ben Asher decided as he noticed a weary look on the boy's face. "There will be days enough for talk."

Rabbi Moses Becomes Known 

A new life began for the little Babylonian boy. He did not long feel lonely. It was good to be among his own people and to make friends with children of his age. 

During the services at the synagogue, he sat with the other children on the stone seats provided for them, at their fathers' feet. Every day he went to school. The classes were held in a building right next to the synagogue. In loud sing-song voices the children chanted the verses of the Bible and studied the Talmud. 

In a short time Rabbi Moses went about looking for work. One day he passed the Beth Hamidrash or Jewish College where many learned men were discussing the Talmud. Rabbi Moses entered and sat down near the door. The head of the college, Rabbi Nathan, was explaining a certain point. Rabbi Moses listened carefully.

Then he arose and asked permission to speak. "There is another explanation of this point you are discussing," he said. In his interest and eagerness he soon forgot that he was unknown and poorly clad. As he talked, the people listened with growing respect and amazement. Who was this shabby stranger from whose lips such wisdom and knowledge flowed! That he was a great scholar could not be doubted. When he had finished speaking, the Chief 

Rabbi Nathan arose. "I do not know who you are, my honored friend," he said, "but pray come forward. Your place is at the head of the table and not in back of the room." To the rest of the people he declared, “I can no longer be your teacher. Here is a man who is far more worthy of that position than I am. Let us elect him head of the college." All the people agreed gladly for they respected knowledge more than anything else. "And now tell us who you are," they pleaded. "I am Rabbi Moses ben Enoch of Babylonia," the newcomer finally told them. "And I thank you for receiving me so kindly into your homes and hearts." 

"Rabbi Moses! The great scholar! The famous Rabbi 

Moses!" The people could hardly realize their good fortune in finding so great a teacher among them. "This is indeed a happy day for Spain," they cried, crowding around him with respectful joy. The day was concluded with happy celebrations.

And so Rabbi Moses came into his rightful position and was one of the most honored men not only in Cordova but in all Spain. Under his leadership the College of Cordova became more and more famous. Many students from other lands flocked there to study with him. 

Spain became the most important center of Jewish learning of that time.

Exploring the Wonders of Cordova 

Enoch began to love the pleasant life he led in sunny Spain. He and his father now lived in a beautiful home of their own, with servants to attend their every need. It was a type of house which he had known and loved in the East, for the Arabs and Jews brought with them into Europe the ways of living which they had followed in their older homelands. As usual, the outside of the house was very plain, with few windows. A large gateway led into the paved courtyard, in the center of which a cool fountain played. Around the court ran a sort of open porch supported by slender pillars, with arches above them. This porch led into the various rooms of the house. 

The windows of the upper story opened on a balcony formed by the roof of the porch below. 

Enoch especially loved the inner garden where roses and violets bloomed. It was reached by a gateway which led from the main courtyard. In the center of the garden there was a pond in which tiny bright-colored fish darted. 

As Enoch sat at the edge and watched them, he could easily imagine that he was back in his old home in 

Babylonia. 

The most delightful days were those which he spent wandering about the city in the company of his new friends. Joseph, the black-eyed lad whose father was a goldsmith, had befriended Enoch in those first days at the Jews' Inn and remained a warm and loyal companion.

The other was Omar, an Arab boy who knew the city well. Omar attended a school connected with one of the mosques.

Joseph and Omar were eager to show the newcomer many interesting sights in Cordova. Enoch was filled with amazement as he beheld the splendid many-towered

palaces built of marble, the hundreds of mosques, the beautiful public baths where people often spent a whole day in exercise and entertainment as well as in bathing. 

One day the three walked beside the massive stone walls which encircled the city. Great towers forming part of the walls were built at regular distances apart. 

Soldiers could be seen walking in and out of these. "There are Arab, Christian, and Jewish soldiers," Omar remarked. "Although they pray in separate houses of worship they work together as good comrades." "Our noble Caliph Ab-ar-Rahman, the Third, treats all his people fairly," added Joseph. "He likes to have them work and study together." The boys stopped for a while at the main gate of the city. It was very large and was decorated with two huge stone columns on each side. "There are seven gates in the walls of Cordova," Omar told them. "Let's walk all around the walls," suggested Enoch. 

Joseph laughed. "It would take you a long time. The walls are miles and miles in length." 

They wandered for a while through the market-place outside the gates watching the mixed crowd of people from many lands and of different religions. Then they walked through one of the bazaars or business districts of the city. "Let's stop at my father's shop," suggested

Joseph, and so they turned into the Street of the Goldsmiths. Sitting on a stool outside his small shop, Joseph's father was engraving a gold ring. "Why aren't you busy with your studies, my son?" he inquired as Joseph came up. Then he caught sight of

Enoch and smiled. "To practice hospitality is even more important than study," he said. "I am glad Omar and 

Joseph are acquainting you with Cordova. How does our city appear to you?"

"It is truly a city of marvels," exclaimed Enoch. "I once visited Bagdad on the banks of the Tigris River in the East and I thought no city in the world could equal its splendor. But now I have found its equal in this other 

Mohammedan city of Cordova.” "Many say that Cordova even surpasses Bagdad," the goldsmith replied.

Omar looked at Enoch with great respect. “I did not think I would ever see anyone who had been as far as 

Bagdad," he said.

After a short visit the boys went on. Omar led the way through the Street of the Silversmiths, the Silkmarket, the Street of the Tanners, and other business thoroughfares where people worked at their trades and sold their wares. Enoch stopped admiringly before a shop where leather goods was displayed. Every article was decorated with carved designs which showed fine workmanship. "I must select a gift for my father," 

Enoch decided. "We'll help you find something," the others suggested.

The three finally selected a portfolio used for keeping important papers. It was made of the famous Cordovan leather and was interestingly carved. 

One day the three decided to visit the Great Mosque. “It is the largest and most beautiful house of worship in all of Europe," Omar assured Enoch earnestly. A short trip brought them there. "The outside of the building is quite simple," Enoch remarked. "It looks somewhat like a fortress with its towers and battlement and plain walls.” "That highest tower is the minaret," Omar explained. "The call to prayer is made from it five times a day."

The children entered one of the gates into a large courtyard. It was planted with sweet-smelling orange trees

and was called the Court of Oranges. Enoch counted six fountains and three reservoirs of clear and sparkling water which was brought through pipes from a neighboring mountain. "Every Moslem must wash his hands and feet before entering the mosque," Omar told his companions and proceeded to do so. The courtyard was a delightful place in which to walk. Arabs dressed in flowing garments and beautifully bound turbans were squatting in the sunshine or moving among the trees. Soldiers and beggars and children were enjoying the pleasant sunshine. 

Massive bronze doors led into the mosque. As the three entered Enoch drew a quick breath of surprise at the scene that suddenly burst upon him. There seemed to be endless rows of beautiful stone pillars of all colors. "Why it's like a great forest of stone trees," he cried. "Look, they are all of different colors." "And of every shade," Joseph marvelled. "Red and yellow, blue and green, black and brown. And here are some of many colors." "Look at the double arches above the pillars," Enoch exclaimed. "They are all colored black and red.” "If you were here during a festival," Omar told them, "you would see the whole mosque lighted up. There are two hundred and eighty chandeliers which give forth a blaze of light. There are thousands of lights in the building." "It must take barrels of oil to keep them all filled," said Joseph, whose duty it was to take care of the two oil lamps at home. "Come," Omar urged, "you haven't seen the most beautiful part yet." He led them down one of the aisles into a small room of octagonal shape. This was the most sacred part of the mosque. Enoch looked around with

delight. The ceiling was formed like a shell and was carved out of a single block of white marble. The walls were decorated with mosaics and with carved stone. "You must see this, too." Omar showed them a box which was covered with gold tissue and set with rubies and pearls. "Do you know what is kept inside?" he asked. "Tell us," the other two demanded. "One of the earliest copies of the Koran. That is the 

Mohammedan Bible, you know." "Here is the pulpit and right near it is the seat of the 

Caliph," their guide went on. "They are magnificent," cried Enoch. "The costly wood is almost hidden by the ivory and precious stones with which they are inlaid." "Even the nails are made of gold and silver," added 

Joseph. They gazed for a while in admiration. It was with a feeling of regret that Enoch remembered it was time to go home since the hour for his own evening prayers was at hand.

On other days Enoch and his friends liked to linger near the great University of Cordova and watch the hundreds of students as they walked to the lecture rooms to learn about law, religion, and science. Sometimes the boys would loiter along the banks of the Guadalquivir River which flowed through the city. Enoch liked to walk across the great bridge built by the Romans hundreds of years ago when they had ruled Spain. It was twelve hundred feet long and had seventeeen arches and one hundred and nineteen turrets.

The lad never wearied of the wonders of this great city which was now his home. At times he would wander off into the suburbs which had such lovely names as the 

Vale of Paradise, the Path of Roses, the Beautiful Valley. He loved best to pass by the Alcazar where the

Caliph lived. The Alcazar was both a palace and a fortress. When the Caliph wished to rest from the affairs of government he retired to one of the other numerous palaces which he possessed. One of the most splendid, was in the suburb of Az-zahra, about four miles from 

Cordova. Enoch heard almost unbelievable tales of its grandeur. Thousands of servants were occupied there. 

Its fountains were decorated with statues of gold set with precious gems. The marble Hall of the Caliphs had a roof of thin marble and gold. There were countless beautiful marble pillars and garden terraces of polished stone. There were luxurious pools for bathing built in the inner gardens. 

Truly, Cordova, which boasted of such splendors, was said to be the greatest city in Europe at this time. Even the other cities of Spain, Seville, Toledo, Granada, Barcelona and the rest, could not compare with Cordova, proudest of them all.

A Visit to Hasdai Ibn Shaprut 

One day when Enoch returned home from some of his wanderings he found his father dressed in his holiday garments. "Come, my son, put on your finest clothes,' he was told. "Where are we going?" asked the boy. 99 "We have been invited to visit that noble leader in 

Israel, Hasdai Ibn Shaprut."

A splendid carriage drawn by four horses was sent for the guests. As they rode grandly along, Rabbi Moses told the lad more of the famous person they were going to see. "At first Hasdai was the Caliph's physician. But he soon became more than that. He is a man of great ability as well as of fine character, so the Caliph turned to him more and more for advice about important matters

of government. Hasdai is a scholar, too. He knows 

Hebrew, Arabic, and Latin. Latin is the language in which nations carry on their affairs with each other. 

Hasdai Ibn Shaprut is a loyal Jew as well as a powerful statesman. He does much to help Jewish students and scholars. When the Jewish people have to appeal to the 

Caliph for anything, Hasdai is our spokesman.” "Look, we have arrived," interrupted Enoch excitedly.

The carriage stopped before a stately palace. They were received with many respectful greetings and bows, and ushered into a large room. The floor was of marble tiles. 

The lower half of the walls was inlaid with brightlycolored tiles which were arranged in interesting designs and patterns. The furniture was beautifully carved.

Candelabras of finely-wrought iron stood about the room. 

Their host met them with great friendliness. He was dressed in silk robes and wore a turban. He and Rabbi 

Moses ben Enoch talked about Jewish life in all parts of 

Europe and the East. "It is fortunate for Spain," Hasdai told his guest, "that a scholar like you, who has studied in the great schools of Babylonia, can be our leader here. 

The Jewish world now turns to Spain for help and leadership as they once did to the East." "It is you who are doing much for Jewish learning," the rabbi answered. "You spend your money freely in the cause of education. You keep in touch with Jews in all parts of the world and help many who are in need. 

And I know it was you, also, who helped to make me the permanent head of the college." “Our Caliph, Abd-ar-Rahman, the Third, was pleased to hear of you," Hasdai told him. "He loves men of learning. And what is more, he was not happy to see so much Spanish gold being sent out of the country to sup-

port the Jewish colleges in Babylonia. He would rather see Spain become a greater center of learning." "Truly," replied the rabbi, "we Jews have found a real home here in Spain. It is no wonder that our people have learned to love this country." "The Jews have been in Spain for many years," said Hasdai. "In the days before the Moors came, our people were not well treated here. But when the Arabs conquered the land in the Eighth Century, things became much better. It is true that our people pay heavy taxes to the Caliph but they are free and respected citizens." 

Thus the talk went on and Rabbi Moses and Enoch learned many things about life in Spain from the man who was the personal friend and adviser of the powerful Caliph. Hasdai told them of a trip he had taken to the northern kingdoms of Spain which were ruled by Christian kings. The Queen of Navarre had appealed to the 

Caliph of Cordova to send a special doctor for her grandson who had become uncommonly fat. Hasdai Ibn 

Shaprut had been sent and had brought about a more friendly feeling between the Moslem and Christian kingdoms. "The proud Queen and her kingly grandson came here to pay a visit to our Caliph and ask for his friendship," Hasdai related to his guests. "The Caliph was greatly pleased because I had arranged this matter." "Your high position with the Caliph has been of much help to the Jews," Rabbi Moses assured him. "The Jews are my people," Hasdai answered simply. "I am happy to do all that is in my power for them. "But I am forgetting my duties as host. We must have some refreshment now." A servant soon entered carrying a small low table about fifteen inches in height. 

It was made of a round silver tray set upon a stool of fine wood inlaid with mother-of-pearl. The servant spread

a piece of tapestry on the marble floor and placed the table upon it. Hasdai and his guests sat upon cushions around the table. Another servant brought a silver basin and a silver pitcher with a long spout. The three washed their hands and dried them on linen napkins, repeating the prayer, "Blessed art Thou, O Lord our God, who hast honored us by Thy commandments and hast commanded us to wash the hands." Other servants now brought cool drinks of water sweetened with fruit juices. They served fresh figs and grapes and sweet cookies. 

While eating, Hasdai talked kindly to Enoch. "My son," he said, "you must follow in the footsteps of your noble father. Perhaps some day you also will be Chief

Rabbi of Spain. Then it will be your task to be the leader of the College and to appoint rabbis and judges for other Jewish communities as your father is doing now." "I will do my best, Sire," answered Enoch shyly.

After further talk, the guests rose to depart. From the time of this visit, Hasdai and Rabbi Moses became good friends and saw each other frequently. 

In the Jewish Quarters of Cordova 

Much as Enoch loved to explore the marvels of the great city which was now his home, he always returned to the Jewish district, the Juderia, with a feeling of pleasure and satisfaction. He liked to wander down the winding paved streets which were lined with pleasant homes built of stone and covered with fresh whitewash. 

The walls looked somewhat blank for there were only a few windows facing the street. But Enoch knew that if he should step through one of the arched gateways he would probably find himself in a charming courtyard where most of the windows faced. A fountain would be

playing there, no doubt, and flowers growing. He had friends now in many of these homes and he knew how comfortable even the simple houses were with their cool brick floors, their white walls, and the little built-in niches and shelves decorated with colored tiles. 

Although most of the people in this district were Jews, Arabs and Christians also lived here and were friendly neighbors. 

Enoch's own lovely home was a gathering place for the wise and educated men of all three religions. Under the kindly rule of the Caliph, Jews, Arabs, and Christians often met on the most friendly terms. Christian students as well as Jews had gotten much from the Moors. They spoke the Arabic language and eagerly studied the Arabic books. They even adopted the dress and customs of the 

Arabs. In the pleasant courtyard of Rabbi Moses, poets and philosophers, doctors and scientists, students of both the Talmud and the Koran met and spent the hours in learned discussion.

Yes, life was happy in the Jewish quarters of Cordova. 

The holidays were especially enjoyable. Purim and Passover were celebrated with much joyousness in which the children had a large share of the fun. The Holy Days of the New Year and Yom Kippur were observed in a serious but happy spirit. 

Enoch especially looked forward to the Sabbath. It began late on Friday afternoon when an officer of the synagogue known as the beadle knocked on the doors of the houses announcing that all work must stop and that services were about to begin. His voice could be heard ringing through the streets as he went from door to door with his wooden mallet. Rabbi Moses and Enoch often entertained special guests for the Sabbath meals. One evening there was a new student who had just arrived

from a town in northern Africa to study at the College of Rabbi Moses. A number of other students from different cities and lands were also guests at the home of the rabbi for the Sabbath meal. The Jewish community was very kind to these students, many of whom were poor. In many other homes it was a custom to have a stranger as a guest for the Sabbath.

The meals on this day were always especially good. 

On this particular Friday evening there were well-roasted meats of several different kinds, many vegetables, and bread of wheat flour. Between the courses the guests sang Hebrew songs with gay melodies in which Enoch joined with a happy spirit. The older students always gave him much attention and he loved these Sabbath gatherings. The guests began a discussion of some point in the Talmud. Enoch listened with interest especially when some of the delightful stories of the rabbis were told. 

The new student then told about the life of the Jews in the cities through which he had passed. There was discussion about how Jews lived in other lands and everyone agreed that Spain offered the best home for the Jewish people. 

On the following afternoon, Rabbi Moses reviewed with his son the work he had covered in his studies during the week. This was the custom in many homes. Then there were pleasant walks and visits to friends until the stars announced that it was time for the Habdolah blessing. This was the ceremony marking the end of the 

Sabbath and the beginning of the working week. The father chanted a prayer over a goblet of wine and smelled of sweet spices in honor of the sweetness of the Sabbath.

The stately marble synagogue was always a lively place. People went there several times a day to worship. 

But it was more than a house of prayer. Between parts

of the service, men would wanaer in the pleasant courtyard and discuss the news of the day. Many came to study and meet their friends. All the important business of life in the Jewish community was carried on in the synagogue. The people paid their taxes there. When the elders made new laws for the Jewish people they were announced in the synagogue. When it was time to elect certain officers for the Juderia, assemblies of all the people were called. The Jews managed most of their own affairs. They had their own courts and judges. They had officers who kept good order in the district. 

To the synagogue the people also brought their contributions for the care of the poor. Certain officers divided the money among those who needed it. There was a special fund for clothing. All were made to contribute to these funds according to the amount they could afford.

In this new homeland, Enoch grew to manhood. He studied hard and became a great scholar. After the death of Rabbi Moses, Enoch became head of the College. He married the daughter of a wealthy family by the name of Peliag. And here we can leave him who had come to Spain as a poor and homeless boy. 

Wars between Moors and Christians

Many changes took place in the government of Spain during the six hundred years from the Eighth to the

Fourteenth Centuries that the Jews lived there. Some of these changes brought suffering and unhappiness to the 

Jewish people, for a time. 

The Christian kingdoms in the North were pressing downward on Moslem territory. Unfriendly Arabs from 

Africa attacked the Caliphate of Cordova from the South.

In the early part of the Eleventh Century, the city of

Cordova suffered greatly from civil war. A short time later the Caliphate broke up into a number of small kingdoms. The chief one had the city of Granada as its center. Many Jews fled from the unfriendly Arab rulers who were now in power at Cordova and settled in the new capital. Others found a home in Christian kingdoms under kindly rulers. These kingdoms were now making rapid progress in conquering the disunited Moslems. In the frequent warfare that went on, Jews fought bravely for the countries in which they lived, so there were Jews on both sides of the battlefield.

The Moslems feared that they would be completely overcome by the Christians and they sent an appeal for help to their fellow Arabs of North Africa. Tribes of wild, uncivilized warriors soon swept over the land. They conquered the Christians, it is true, but they also seized the government of Moslem Spain and became the ruling class. These African Arabs called themselves Almohades. 

They represented a certain branch of the Mohammedan religion. During their rule, both Jews and Christians who lived in Moslem lands suffered greatly at their hands. The 

Almohades demanded that everyone should accept the

Moslem religion. If not, they would be put to death. Many 

Jews fled to Christian kingdoms where they were received kindly. Others had to pretend to follow the Mohammedan religion but they secretly remained Jews. Not a few suffered the death of martyrs. 

Fortunately, the Almohades did not remain in power for a long time. The Christians pushed steadily southward until they controlled most of Spain. Only the kingdom of Granada in the Southeast remained Moslem. This was the stronghold of the Moors who were ever friendly to the Jews.

The Rest of Europe in the Tenth Century Now that you know how people lived in Spain in the Tenth Century, let us look at the rest of Europe and see what it was like in the middle of the Middle Ages. We 

I will wander about and see.

There are few towns and these are rather small. They have protecting walls around them. 

Perched high on hilltops or beside rushing streams or wide rivers, we find great stone castles. They are always built where they cannot be easily attacked in times of war. In the Tenth Century these castles are very simply made. As we approach one, we come first to a deep ditch or moat which surrounds the castle. During a war this moat is filled with water. We cross a bridge which can be raised and lowered and enter the courtyard through a heavy gate in the wall. This wall is made of sturdy tree trunks which have been driven into a groundwork of hard earth that had been taken from the moat, The main part of the castle is a large square tower built of huge blocks of stone. Around it there are a number of smaller buildings made chiefly of wood. Life in these castles is not very comfortable. The rooms are cold and dreary. There are few pieces of furniture and these are made in very simple styles. A castle is really a fortress and is built as a place of defense as well as for a home. 

Later on in the Middle Ages, the castles became much larger and were better made. There were great stone walls and many towers and large fine buildings containing great halls and many smaller rooms. It was from eastern lands that Europe learned how to build finer castles later in the Middle Ages. 

Who do you think lived in these castles? Not all the people you may be sure. They were the homes of power-

ful nobles or knights who owned a great deal of land. 

These nobles were like kings in a small way. They ruled over the peasants or common people who lived in little villages around the castle. The nobles received their land either from the king or from some other powerful noble who had more land than he could take care of himself. 

In return for the land, the person who received it would become the other man's vassal. The one who gave the land was his lord. A vassal owed his lord loyalty and service. This meant that he would help the lord during a war or in other times of need. Often he would be expected to pay a certain sum of money or offer some gift to his lord each year as a sign of his loyalty. 

Only people who belonged to noble families could receive land in this way. Generally a noble was a vassal to some more powerful knight and at the same time was a lord to nobles who had received smaller pieces of land from him. Warfare between great nobles was a common thing in these days, so it was important for each noble to have as many loyal followers as he could. The nobles spent their time in fighting each other, in hunting, in knightly tournaments or, if they were not too lazy, in managing their large estates. Not far from the castles we find small villages where the peasants lived. They were the vassals of the lords who owned the land. 

The lives of the peasants were quite different. Let us visit one of their huts and see what it is like. The little cottage is made of twigs and branches of trees plastered together with mud. The roof is thatched with grass or reeds. Inside, we find a single large room. The floor is of hard earth. There is no chimney. The smoke from the fireplace escapes through a small hole in the roof or through the door and window. This window is just an open space in the wall and in cold weather it has to be

stuffed with straw. Near the fireplace there is a large bed piled high with feather mattresses, where the entire family sleeps. We learn that many huts do not have a bed, and the people have to sleep on piles of straw thrown on the floor. A table and some benches complete the furnishings of this peasant's home. The farm animals run in and out of the cottage as freely as the children and we almost stumble over several chickens who are flapping around our legs.

The peasants are poorly dressed. Their clothes look worn and untidy. They are rough in their manners and their ways are unpleasant. They have no education, seldom leave their village, and spend their days in hard labor.

Each family has a small plot of land on which to grow food. In return for this, they owe their lord in the stone castle many services. They must plow, sow, and harvest the land that belongs to him. They must perform the work necessary in the castle. They must give him a portion of the food they raise on their little plot of land. 

Each family has certain other taxes to pay every year, such as a chicken or a dozen eggs, or a piece of homespun woolen cloth. The lord owns the village mill where the flour is ground, the public oven where the bread is baked, the winepress where the wine is made. Each time a peasant uses these things he must pay a portion of his goods to the lord as a fee. In times of war the peasants must fight beneath the banner of their lord.

In some countries of Europe, the peasants became almost like slaves. They could be sold with the land and were not permitted to move from one village to another. 

Such people were called serfs. Others had more freedom but not a great deal. In some lands the peasants were better off than in others.

People lived in this way all over Europe in the Tenth Century. There were lords and ladies in the castles, and poor, hard-working peasants in their little villages. Even those who lived in the towns generally had to have the protection of some lord.

The Place of the Jews in the Middle Ages Let us see where the Jews fitted into this life of the Middle Ages. In Spain, as you have seen, many of them lived in the cities, which were larger and far superior to those in the other countries of Europe. Others owned land and tilled the soil. There were even Jewish lords or knights who lived in castles like the Christian and Arab nobles.

In the other lands of Europe the Jews did not fare so well. Only rarely were they allowed to own land. They could not live as peasants on some noble's estate. Jews were too accustomed to leading an independent life of their own.

They generally wanted to live with large groups of fellow Jews. This was necessary in order to carry out the laws of the Jewish religion. The Jews in other countries of Europe, therefore, settled chiefly in the towns. They received the special protection of the king or some noble but paid for this privilege in heavy taxes. 

A number were engaged in handicrafts of different kinds, and many were merchants and traders.

PART TWO The Golden Age of Spain 

It will be pleasant to return to Spain after the rather gloomy view we have had of life in the rest of Europe. 

Except for short periods of unhappiness, Jews found a good home in Spain for about six hundred years. In return for the kind treatment they received, the Jewish

people added greatly to the glory, wealth, and power of their adopted land. Many Jews became great writers, poets, scientists, doctors, and leaders in government. 

Poets sang their songs in beautiful Hebrew, Talmudic scholars studied in the colleges, doctors and scientists added to the store of knowledge, and Jewish officers helped to direct the affairs of the state.

During the Eleventh and Twelfth Centuries particu larly, Jewish writers and poets produced work of such lasting value and inspiring beauty that this period is called the Golden Age of Hebrew Literature. The mod ern prayer-book used in synagogues and temples today is filled with the writings of these great men. Some of the most well-known and beloved characters of the Jewish past lived in this Golden Age. Let us meet a few of them and find out something about their lives. 

GREAT MEN WHO LIVED IN SPAIN 

Samuel Ibn Nagdela

The story of Samuel's life sounds almost like a fairy tale. He spent his youth in Cordova and was one of the students in the college of Rabbi Moses. Samuel wrote and spoke seven languages. He studied sciences and literature. His penmanship was unusually beautiful. 

When the Caliphate of Cordova broke up, Samuel settled in the kingdom of Granada. He earned his living by keeping a small spice shop. Close by was the palace in which lived the prime minister or vizier of the Caliph. 

Some of the servants in the palace asked Samuel to write letters for them to their master. The vizier received a number of these beautifully written letters and became interested in the writer. One day he stopped in the small shop and became acquainted with Samuel. "You are a young man of talent and education," the

vizier said to him. "This little shop is no place for you. 

You shall come to the palace and be my secretary.' 99 Samuel not only became the secretary to the vizier of Granada, but also his chief adviser on important matters of government. The vizier recognized Samuel's keen mind and trustworthy character. He depended more and more on the young man's judgment, and the kingdom of Granada was governed wisely and well. 

It happened that the vizier became very ill. Before dying, he said to his master, the king. "You have often praised my wisdom in handling the affairs of state. Know then, Sire, that one of my secretaries, Samuel Ibn Nagdela, has advised me in all matters. I commend him highly to Your Excellency."

When the vizier died, King Habbus appointed Samuel to take his place. And so it happened that this unknown young man rose from the humble position of shop-keeper to become the prime minister of a king.

For about thirty years Samuel filled this position with wisdom and honor.

Samuel was a noble Jew as well as a wise statesman. 

The high rank he occupied did not make him forget his own people. As Hasdai had done before him, Samuel aided the Jews whenever he could. He supported many poor students. He engaged scholars to copy the Talmud and then presented these to the students at the Jewish colleges. Poor scholars in other lands, too, received help! from this Prince in Israel. Indeed, the king officially bestowed upon him the title of Nagid, or Prince of all the Jews in the kingdom of Granada.

In the midst of his many duties, Samuel found time to take an active part in Jewish life. He lectured on the 

Talmud to the students of the college. He wrote scholarly

books in fine Hebrew, and was the author of poems as well.

Some of the Arabs envied Samuel and tried to do him harm. They did not like to see a Jew occupying so high a position. It was he, in fact, who ruled the country, for the king relied upon him in many matters. But this wise statesman was able to make even his enemies into friends. 

He never tried to hide his humble past, and in this way silenced his enemies. The land prospered under Samuel's rule and Granada became one of the strongest kingdoms in Spain. Many Jews lived there at this time. The capital of Granada was sometimes called the "Jews' City."

Samuel was greatly beloved by his people. They said that he possessed four crowns-the crown of the priesthood, for he was descended from the priestly family of 

Levites, the crown of learning, the crown of fame, and above all, the crown of good deeds.

Solomon Ibn Gabirol 

One of the great poets and thinkers in this Golden 

Ages was Solomon Ibn Gabirol. Some of the poems which he wrote at seventeen years of age are recited in the synagogues at the present time, on certain sabbaths of the year. At nineteen, he wrote a Hebrew grammar in verse. 

Gabirol's parents both died when he was young, and the boy had a sad and lonely childhood. He left his home and wandered about until he came to Saragossa, which is in the north of Spain. Here, a Jew who had a high position in the king's court, befriended him. The poet was happier for awhile. Then his friend died. Ibn

Gabirol wrote beautiful poems about him to express his grief. At last the poet found a new home with Samuel 

Ibn Nagdela, the vizier of Granada. Samuel's friend-

ship made Gabirol much happier and he could now work whole-heartedly at his art. He wrote a number of books expressing his ideas and feelings about life. One of these, The Fountain of Life, was written in Arabic. It was translated into Latin and was read by many Christian scholars. They did not know that the author of this beautiful Arabic writing was a Jew.

Moses Ibn Ezra 

In the very same year that Solomon Ibn Gabirol died, another great Hebrew poet was born. His name was

Moses Ibn Ezra. Moses lived at Granada, where his father was an officer at the royal court and served under 

Samuel Ibn Nagdela. There were four brothers in the family and they were all well-educated, talented in their studies, and wealthy. Moses was the most gifted of all.

He was not happy, however, for he fell in love with a

niece, and although she returned his affection, his brother did not approve of the marriage, and she became the wife of another. Moses left his home and wandered about, expressing his sorrows in beautiful Hebrew verses, as

But Moses could be gay and light-hearted too, as some of his other poems show. His finest and most serious work has found a place in our modern prayer-book, which contains many of his hymns and prayers. 

Jehuda Ha Levi

Of all the poets in the Golden Age of Spain, the one whose songs were the most beautiful and beloved, and whose fame was the brightest, was Jehuda Ha-Levi. Indeed, he has been ranked as the greatest Hebrew poet since the days when the psalms of the Bible were written. 

Jewish congregations in almost every part of the world chant his poems about God and Zion. He has written songs and prayers for almost every season of the year, and has thus touched the hearts and inspired the minds of countless Jews, for his poems have been recited in synagogues since the Twelfth Century. 

Jehuda was born in the city of Toledo at about 1085.

In that same year, Toledo was captured from the Moslems by the Christian king, Alfonso VII. Alfonso was a kind and broad-minded ruler. He knew that in those troubled days of war between Moslems and Christians

for the possession of Spain, it was wise to have the help and friendship of the Jews.

Jehuda thus grew up in a kindly atmosphere where his talents and abilities were encouraged. As a child he must have enjoyed wandering about the quaint city of Toledo which was built among the hills and overlooked the Tagus River. It was a busy and prosperous city and became one of the chief centers of Jewish life. No doubt 

Jehuda often strolled through the heavy gates of the city which opened on the river bank. He must have enjoyed crossing the two well-built Moorish bridges which spanned the river. From these bridges one could get an interesting view of the city which was built upon the top and sides of the hill. With its high stone walls and many towers, it must have seemed somewhat like a grim fortress to the boy.

Jehuda left Toledo for a while to study the Bible and Talmud at a Jewish college near Granada. He also learned Greek and Arabic, but he chose Hebrew as the language for his poetry. He began writing at an early age. Once he sent some of his work to Moses Ibn Ezra at Granada, and the older poet praised his efforts. The friendship between the two continued for many years. 

Jehuda studied medicine, and when he returned to Toledo, his duties as a doctor occupied much of his time. 

He was not too busy, however, to write many poems and to spend happy hours with his numerous friends. Jehuda had gay and charming manners and was very popular. 

He was asked to write poems for every occasion. Over forty wedding songs which were written for his friends have been collected. No party seemed complete without the presence of Jehuda Ha-Levi. He was always ready to amuse people with clever riddles or bright sayings or a

And so the great Hebrew poet died in making his wish come true. But the beautiful songs he wrote have lived on through the centuries and have made the name of Jehuda Ha-Levi beloved and honored.

Abraham Ibn Ezra 

Abraham Ibn Ezra was related to the family of Moses Ibn Ezra. He was one of the outstanding Jewish scholars and poets of this period in Spain. 

Abraham mastered many fields of knowledge. He not only knew the Bible, the Talmud, and Hebrew grammar, but was also well educated in mathematics and astronomy. 

His interests were many and his abilities made him an important figure even in those days when scholars were numerous.

Abraham led a life of restless wanderings. He visited many lands. The difficulties and dangers of travel in those days did not stop him. He went to Africa, Egypt, Palestine, Arabia, and even England. Abraham had many misfortunes and disappointments but his courage and good spirits seldom failed him. In the little poem given here, he laughs at the failure which always seemed to follow him. "My labor's vain,

No wealth I gain. 

My fate since birth 

Is gloom on earth. 

If I sold shrouds 

No one would die. 

If I sold lamps, Then in the sky, The sun for spite, Would shine by night."

Abraham Ibn Ezra wrote books on mathematics and astronomy. He is best known, however, for his explanations of the Bible. Like the other poets of his time, Abraham wrote beautiful hymns about God. One of them is given here:

"I see Thee in the starry field,

I see Thee in the harvest's yield. 

In every breath, in every sound, An echo of thy name is found. 

The blade of grass, the simple flower, Bear witness to Thy matchless power."

Moses Maimonides 

The greatest Jew of the Middle Ages was probably 

Moses Maimonides. He was like an uncrowned king of

Israel, to whom Jews in all countries turned for advice and inspiration on questions of Jewish life and thought. 

Like the Geonim of Babylonia who had once united the Children of Israel in all lands, Moses Maimonides became the guide and leader, to whom letters from afar were constantly sent and whose advice was respected and obeyed. 

Moses was born in the city of Cordova, on the eve of Passover, in 1135. His father was a well-known scholar and traced his descent from the family of Judah, the Prince. As could be expected, Moses received a very excellent education. His father was a noted Talmudist and the son followed eagerly in his footsteps. Moses attended the University of Cordova where he studied sciences, languages, medicine, and the books of famous Greek and 

Arabic thinkers.

When Moses was still a young boy, the city of Cordova was captured by the unfriendly and cruel Almohades, Arabs from Northern Africa. The Jews either had to accept the Mohammedan religion or leave the country if they wished to escape death. Maimon, the father of

Moses, gathered his family about him and began a life of wandering. They lived in different parts of Spain for a number of years. Moses continued his education even during his travels. He had an amazing memory which

served him well since he could not carry many books with him from one place to another. By the time he was twenty-three, Moses had already written a number of books on the Talmud, and Spanish Jews were turning to him for help and advice.

Maimon then decided to leave Spain and seek a home in other lands. With his daughter and the two sons, Moses and David, he sailed for Northern Africa. The family settled in the town of Fez in Morocco. But they found to their sorrow that the change was not a fortunate one. Morocco was also under the rule of the Almohades, and Jews and Christians could not openly observe their own religions. It was at this time that Moses Maimonides wrote a "Letter of Consolation" to his fellow Jews in Spain and Africa. He told them that it was better to observe their religion in secret than to give it up altogether. He added, however, that it was not good to lead a double life and those who could escape to other parts of Spain should do so and live openly as Jews. His letter was a great comfort and help to many Jews and the name of Maimonides became more and more known. 

After a few difficult years in Morocco, Maimonides and his family had to leave. Their true religion was discovered and they had to escape under the cover of night. 

This time they sailed for Palestine. The voyage was a long and stormy one, lasting an entire month. In the 

Holy Land they visited many places which history had made familiar and beloved to them. They went to Jerusalem and prayed at the wall of the ancient Temple. 

Maimonides did not wish to remain in Palestine, however. It was in the hands of Christians at this time, and few Jews were living there. The family finally settled in Cairo, Egypt. Here two sorrows befell Maimonides. 

First his father died and then his brother David was

drowned at sea. For many years Maimonides could not forget his grief at the loss of his beloved brother.

The years in Egypt were very busy and crowded with work. Maimonides' fame in the field of medicine had become so great that he was appointed one of the royal physicians at the palace of Saladin, the Caliph. This ruler respected men of learning. He is known for his wise and kindly government and his love of knowledge. He established colleges and collected a great library of manuscripts. 

Besides his duties at the palace, Maimonides had many other patients. A letter which he wrote to one of his friends gives us a glimpse into the busy life he led. Every morning he went to Saladin's palace which was about a mile and a half from his home. He returned in the afternoon. "Then," he wrote, "I am almost dying with hunger. I find the ante-chambers thronged with people, both Jews and Gentiles, nobles and common people, judges and bailiffs, friends and foes-who await the time of my return. I dismount from my animal, wash my hands, and ask my patients to excuse me while I take some slight refreshment, the only meal I take in the twentyfour hours. Then I attend to my patients, write prescriptions and directions for their various ailments. Patients go in and out until nightfall, and sometimes even, I assure you, until two hours and more in the night. I talk to them and prescribe for them while lying down from sheer fatigue, and when night falls I am so tired that I can scarcely speak. Because of this, no Jew can have any private interview with me except on the Sabbath. 

On that day the whole congregation, or at least most of the members, come to me after the morning service, when

I instruct them as to their duties during the whole week. 

We study together a little until noon, when they depart.

Some of them return and read with me after the afternoon service until evening prayer. In this manner I spend that day." 

Perhaps it is not surprising that when the poet, Abraham Ibn Ezra, came to Cairo and tried repeatedly to visit 

Maimonides, he met with the disappointment which he described in the following lines: "I call on my lord in the morning, But am told that on horseback he's sped; 

I call once again in the evening, And hear that his lordship's abed. 

But whether his highness is riding, Or whether my lord is asleep, I am perfectly sure disappointment 

Is the one single fruit I shall reap."

His medical duties, however, heavy though they were, made up only a small part of his daily work. Maimonides was appointed Chief Rabbi of Cairo, and was later given the title of Nagid, or Prince, over all the Egyptian Jews.

It is for his scholarly writings that Maimonides is chiefly remembered and honored. His books have influenced 

Jewish life to the present day. One of them, called The 

Light, is an explanation of the Mishnah. It was written in Arabic which was a popular language among Jews at that time. In this book, Maimonides listed thirteen principles of Judaism which he thought every good Jew should believe. These have become a part of Orthodox 

Judaism. They were made into a religious poem which is now recited as part of the synagogue service. 

Another of his great works is the Mishnah Torah. 

This work took him ten years and occupies fourteen volumes. In it Maimonides arranged all the laws of the 

Talmud in order, so that they could easily be found and

understood. He thus made it simpler for Jews to understand and observe the laws of their religion. 

Maimonides' third great book is called Guide to the

Perplexed. It explains that religion must have in it both faith and reason. Faith means believing and reason means understanding. We must depend upon our reason as much as possible, and when reason can go no further, we must have faith.

Even in his own lifetime, Maimonides became the outstanding figure in Jewish life. Many scribes were busy copying his books and sending them to other countries. 

Much of his work was read by Moslem and Christian scholars as well as Jews. 

In spite of his fame, Maimonides remained modest. and was humbly grateful to anyone who pointed out the slightest error in his work. In his own life he practiced the noble truths which he taught to others.

It is interesting to picture Maimonides, dressed in his long cloak and turban, moving to and fro from one task to another, in the colorful city of Cairo. Did he ever find time to slip away for a few silent moments and look upon the mighty sphinx and the vast pyramids which were just outside the city? Surely he must have often thought of the days long ago, when the Children of Israel were slaves in that very land, and another Moses was a prince among them. Now Jews dwelt once more in the land of Egypt, but they were contented citizens under the kindly rule of the Moslem Caliph, Saladin. And of Maimonides it was truly said, "From Moses unto Moses, there was none like Moses."

Nachmanides 

Nachmanides lived in Northern Spain in the period following the death of Maimonides. He was one of the

leading Talmudists of the Golden Age. The Christian king, James I of Aragon, regarded Nachmanides with favor. His brother was also well-known at the royal court and was a tax collector for several towns in the kingdom of Aragon. 

When Nachmanides was only sixteen years old, he wrote several explanations of the Talmud which proved that the writer was a young man of great talent. 

Nachmanides was of a very gentle and peace-loving nature. When his fellow Jews disagreed about some point in religion, Nachmanides tried to make peace among them. And yet this gentle rabbi was himself drawn into a debate which affected the rest of his life. A monk who was the king's guide in religious matters tried to persuade the Jews of Northern Spain and Southern France to become Christians. He sent out travelling priests among them who preached to the Jews. These priests met with very little success. The monk at the royal court was determined to prove that Christianity was the true religion. He asked the king to arrange a debate between Pablo Christiani, a priest, and Nachmanides.

The rabbi had no desire for this debate. He knew that such discussions were dangerous and might lead to persecution of the Jews. There was no way out, however, and Nachmanides finally agreed to take part if he would be allowed perfect freedom to say just what he thought. 

The king granted this point.

The debate lasted four days. The king, the officials of the court, the heads of the churches, were all present. 

Nachmanides performed his part with great dignity, courtesy, and cleverness. With the help of his fine scholarship and clear-thinking mind, he completely defeated Pablo. The king praised Nachmanides and rewarded him with a gift as a sign of respect. The monks, how-

ever, were greatly displeased. They declared that Pablo had won the debate. They insisted that Nachmanides had shown disrespect to the Christian religion and should be punished. To satisfy these monks, the king exiled 

Nachmanides from Spain for two years. But this punishment was not severe enough for the angry churchmen. 

So Nachmanides was exiled from his homeland forever. 

He was an old man at this time, being over seventy. 

And yet he had to leave the land where he had spent all his life, and part from his family, friends, and devoted pupils. Nachmanides took the long journey to Palestine. 

The Holy Land was still in the hands of the Christians. 

It was a land of many ruins and sad memories. Nachmanides settled in Jerusalem where a few Jewish families were living. He found comfort in beholding the city of his fathers, even in its ruined state. In one of his books he wrote, "I left my family, I forsook my house. There, with my sons and daughters, and with the sweet and dear children whom I have brought up on my knees, I left also my soul. My heart and my eyes will dwell with them forever. But the loss of all this, and of every glory my eyes saw, is made up by having now the joy of being a day in thy courts, O Jerusalem, visiting the ruins of the

Temple and crying over the ruined Sanctuary, where I am permitted to caress thy stones, to fondle thy dust, and to weep over thy ruins. I wept bitterly but I found joy in my tears.'

Nachmanides wrote some of his best books explaining the Bible and the Talmud while he was in Palestine. He died at the age of seventy-five and was buried in Haifa. 

Benjamin of Tudela

The Jews of the Middle Ages were great travellers. In sailing vessels, in wagons, on camels, mules, or horses

they went on their slow and dangerous journeys. Sometimes they travelled to escape from a land where Jews were unkindly treated. Often they travelled for purposes of trade, or to study the Talmud with some great teacher who lived far away. A number travelled because their spirits were restless and they wanted to see the world. 

At a time when roads were poor, when robber bands were ready to swoop down upon the lonely wayfarer, when pirate ships threatened the safety of vessels at sea, it was a brave and daring act to undertake a long journey. Yet 

Jews were among the most frequent travellers in the 

Middle Ages. 

One of them, Benjamin of Tudela, in Northern Spain, kept a careful record of his journey. His notebook, written in Hebrew, gives very interesting information about Jewish life in the Twelfth Century. Benjamin was a merchant and travelled for his business affairs. But he was interested in how Jews lived in different lands, how they earned their living, whether they were educated, how they were treated by the governments under which they lived, and other facts about their lives. The information he gathered gives us some valuable knowledge of where and how Jews lived in those days. 

Benjamin's journey took him thirteen years. He sailed from Barcelona, Spain, to the south of France. From there he went to Italy, Greece, Turkey, and then on to Syria, Palestine, Arabia, and Persia. On his way back, Benjamin travelled by way of the Indian Ocean, the 

Persian Gulf, Egypt, Sicily, and then back to Spain.

Benjamin informs us that in a number of towns students of the Talmud were clothed and fed by the people. 

In the East, many Jews made a living by manufacturing silk and purple dyes and fine glassware. He describes palaces, cities, and ancient Jewish monuments.

A whole century before Marco Polo made his famous journey to China, the Jewish merchant, Benjamin of 

Tudela, wrote about his adventurous travels. 

Jewish Translators

There were many other great men in Israel during this 

Golden Age of Jewish life. One group deserves special notice, the translators. A translator is one who takes a book which some one else has written and writes it in another language. In this way he makes it possible for many more people to read and understand great books. 

Many Jewish translators were busy at this task. In 

Southern France and Italy as well as in Spain, the work of translating books into different languages went on. 

Scholarly Jews knew many different languages. They translated the books of Greek and Latin writers into Arabic and Hebrew so that Arabs and Jews could understand them. They translated Arabic writings into Hebrew and Latin so that Jews and Christian scholars could read them. In this way, the Jews had a large share in spreading knowledge throughout Europe in the Middle Ages. 

PART THREE THE AGE OF SORROWS IN SPAIN Why Jewish Life was Insecure 

The Golden Age of Jewish life in Spain was over. The 

Fourteenth and Fifteenth Centuries might be called the Age of Sorrows, for this land which had been the happiest home in Europe for the Jewish people, now became the scene of unbelievable suffering.

One might ask how it was possible for such great changes to take place in the same country and within a fairly short time. As you read this section see if you

can decide how these changes happened to occur. The 

Jews have always been a small group living among people from whom they were different in religion. These differences often made others misunderstand and dislike them. As you have read before, most of the people in the Middle Ages were uneducated and could not think clearly for themselves. They generally accepted the opinions and beliefs of their leaders. Since the far off days when the Jews were a self-governing nation in their own homeland, they have ever been dependent on the will of rulers in the foreign lands where they dwelt. When the kings under whom they lived treated them kindly, the Jews were happy and all was well. When a narrowminded or unwise king governed, the Jews frequently lost their rights and were made to suffer great hardships. 

Thus their lives were ever uncertain and insecure. 

In the Middle Ages the church, also, was very powerful and was closely connected with the government of every Christian land. When a kindly pope was the religious head of the church in Europe, Jews could live in greater freedom and safety. But if it happened otherwise, and the head of the church at Rome was unfriendly to the Jews, then unhappiness, suffering, and even death might be their fate.

We must turn now to the sad story of Jewish life in Spain during the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Centuries. 

The Story of a Marrano Girl 

Inez was working busily in her garden. She looked with delight at the purple beauty of the morning-glories which were beginning to climb over the high stone wall into the street beyond. "Inez, Inez," a voice called, and the little girl jumped to her feet. "Have you forgotten that it is time for your

studies and your mother awaits you?" The nurse's eyes were stern. "But it is my birthday," Inez protested. "I thought there would be no lessons today." "Your mother desires to see you at the usual hour," the nurse replied. Inez walked slowly to the large stone house. After washing her hands and straightening her hair, she climbed the staircase which led to one of the upper floors. She entered a large room richly furnished. 

A huge, four-poster bed, very beautifully carved, stood on a raised platform, its head-piece against the wall.

The Final Day 

On August 2nd, 1492, all the Jews had to leave Spain.

In Other Lands of Europe

At first the Jews made their living by working at trades and by being merchants. Later on, as the towns grew and trades developed, each trade formed a guild or club.

Christians were forbidden to enter this business because the church thought it was wrong, and so the Jews were almost forced into it. The modern world could not get along without bankers, and in the Middle Ages, too, it was important for people to have some place where they could borrow money when they needed it. This business, however, caused the Jews much grief, for the people who owed them money often wanted to get rid of them so the debts would not have to be paid.

In order to understand the life of the Jews in Europe during the Middle Ages, you must know what an important part religion had in the lives of the people. The church was very powerful and it had a large share in the government. The pope was the religious head of all the lands in Europe. Even kings and princes were supposed to obey him, although they did not always do so. 

In those days, the clergymen of the church believed that the religion they taught was the only true one. They thought that anyone who believed differently was wicked

and wrong and would never get to Heaven. Certain 

Christian groups whose ideas were a little different from those the church taught were severely punished and many lost their lives. Monks and travelling priests tried over and over again to make the Jews change their religion to Christianity. It seemed a sort of dishonor to the Christian church that the very people from whom Jesus had been born should refuse to worship him. Because the

Jews remained steadfastly true to their own beliefs, the church passed many severe laws against them. They were not allowed to have Christian servants in their homes, or to have much to do with any Christians for fear that the 

Jews might teach them wrong ideas.

The story of Jewish life in Europe during the Middle Ages shows how the Jews were disliked for two chief reasons-because of their religion, and because many were successful in making a good living in spite of the special hardships they had to endure. And yet, when the 

Jews were very badly treated in certain towns and wished to leave, they were often forbidden to do so. Do you know why? The Jews were forced to pay very high taxes and the government did not wish to lose these taxes.

The greatest scholar and leader of these lands was Solomon ben Isaac who lived in the French city of Troyes, in the second half of the Eleventh Century. He is better known as "Rashi" which is formed by the Hebrew initials of his name.

Rashi was very poor and suffered much during his youth. Often he did not have enough to eat or proper clothing to wear. He wandered from town to town in order to study with wise teachers of the Talmud. No doubt he often slept on the hard benches of synagogues at night, or spent the hours poring over the pages of the 

Talmud by the pale light of a candle or an oil lamp. Like the brave Hillel of long ago who was also very poor, Rashi loved learning and let nothing interfere with his studies.

Finally the student returned to his home in Troyes. 

He was made the rabbi of the community. His fame grew and many wrote to him asking for his opinion on matters of Jewish law. He did not receive any pay for his work as a rabbi, but earned his living by working in a vineyard. France was one of the places where Jews could own land at this time, and many worked on farms and in vineyards.

Rashi spent most of his time in studying, writing, and teaching. Many students found it difficult to understand the Talmud. They had to give years to this study before they knew it well. Rashi knew how to explain the Talmud in a simple way. Students came to him from many lands and as Rashi taught them, he wrote down the explanations. After a while, he had clear and simple explanations written out for the whole Talmud. These helped more than any other man's work to make the Talmud understood by many students. In the pages of the Talmud today, Rashi's explanations are printed right

next to the Talmud itself and both are studied together. 

It is said that the Talmud without Rashi is like a lock without a key. Rashi also wrote an explanation of the 

Bible which is greatly valued for its clearness and wisdom.

The later years of Rashi's life were saddened by the deep sorrows which came upon the Jews at the end of the Eleventh Century. Perhaps it was best that he could not look ahead into the dark and troubled future which lay before his people, Israel.

The Crusades 

Let us see what some of the events were that took place in the lives of the Jews from the Eleventh to the Fifteenth Centuries. These years are among the saddest in Jewish history. They were filled with sorrow and bloodshed for the Jews. They may well be called the "Sad Centuries."

A great deal of the misfortune suffered by the Jews of Europe in these years was caused by the Crusades. The 

Crusades were expeditions or journeys to Palestine made by Christians. Their purpose was to capture from the 

Mohammedans those places in Palestine which were holy to the Christian people. Palestine was the land where 

Jesus had lived and died. Christians often made pilgrimages to worship at the sacred shrines connected with the life and death of Jesus. The Mohammedans or Moslems mistreated these Christian pilgrims. Reports spread that the holy places of the Christians were also not being treated respectfully by the Moslems. 

These reports caused much excitement all over Europe. of the Eleventh Century, the pope called a council to consider the matter. He urged the Christians in Europe to unite in a Holy War and capture Palestine from the

"infidels" or "unbelievers" as the Moslems were called among the Christians.

The monks and priests travelled among the people and spread the message preached by the pope. Everyone who would join the Crusades was promised forgiveness for his sins and a great reward in Heaven. The knights and nobles of Europe, who were ever ready for adventure and excitement, prepared to leave for lands which they had never seen. The peasants were glad to escape from their hard lives by joining the Crusades. Tramps, beggars, and criminals were freed for this purpose and joined the mighty throngs that were forming all over Europe. The sign of the Crusade was the red cross which they sewed on their clothing. Thousands who had never left their homes before, were ready to depart on this great adventure. No doubt there were many who were truly filled with the religious desire of freeing their holy shrines from the Moslems. Many, however, went for other reasons, as you have seen. The knights hoped to find wealth and adventure in the rich lands of the East. Slaves and prisoners found their freedom speedily. Peasants found a new interest to fill their weary lives. And others were rewarded by forgiveness for their sins by the pope himself.

Before there was even time to organize these large groups of people and start in an orderly way, great bands gathered all over France and Germany. Soon they were robbing and plundering as they went on their way.

Leaders arose who urged the ignorant mobs to attack the 

Jews. They said that the Jews were also "unbelievers" like the Mohammedans. They blamed them for the death of Jesus. The leaders declared that it was a good thing to kill the "unbelievers" who lived right among them, before departing for those in far off Palestine. The crude

peasants did not stop to do much reasoning. The Jews could be attacked and robbed without fear of punishment. 

The Jews of Germany and France had been increasing in number and prosperity. But their days of peace were now at an end. The first Crusade, which was started in the year 1096, was the beginning of a long period of persecution which brought death to many. 

The Jewish population of Europe was rapidly reduced.

Fathers stabbed their beloved wives and children to save them from a worse death at the hands of the mob. Homes were robbed and destroyed. The sacred scrolls of the

Only a few saved themselves by agreeing to be baptised. The rest preferred death rather than giving up their religion. Their last cry was, "Hear, O Israel, the 

Lord our God, the Lord is One." 

Scenes of unbelievable horror took place. A number of Jews tried to find safety in the palace of the Bishop of Worms. The palace was surrounded by crusaders. 

The bishop begged the Jews to be baptised. They asked for a little while to consider. When the time was up and the bishop opened the door of the large hall where they were hiding, he found only dead bodies. They had killed each other for the sake of their faith.

Nearly eight hundred Jews died at Worms at that unforgettable time. The crusaders were excited by their victory and ready for more bloodshed and stolen riches.

They moved on to their next victims at Mayence. The same dreadful scenes of suicide and murder were repeated. Again, very few were willing to save themselves by becoming Christians. About thirteen hundred died as martyrs, with the name of God upon their lips.

All through Germany the mob continued on its bloody way. It is believed that about ten thousand Jews perished in Germany at this time. This division of the First 

Crusade never reached Palestine. As they moved through 

Austria and Bulgaria, their cruelty and greed roused the natives to fight against them. They were scattered in all directions. Many were killed or enslaved. Others died from the hardships of the journey. 

Other bands of the First Crusade finally reached Palestine after three years of travelling, fighting, and plundering. When they got to Jerusalem and captured the city, they massacred a large part of the inhabitants. Many 

Moslems were killed and hardly a Jew was left alive. 

A Second and Third Crusade was organized in Europe when part of Palestine again came under Mohammedan rule. Then the Fourth and Fifth Crusades followed. 

Each of them again brought terrible suffering and death to many Jews. A number were protected in the strong castles where nobles or bishops lived. The Jews paid large sums for this protection, in many cases. In other communities, countless lives were lost. In England, too, riots broke out and many names were added to the list of martyrs who died for their religion. 

You must not think that all the Christians approved of the violent acts of the crusaders. Many were filled with horror at their deeds. Many helped the Jews to save their lives and property.

Some of the rulers tried to protect the Jews. They restored a part of the stolen prop-

erty when they could and also punished some of the leaders. 

In Spain and Italy, the Jews were more fortunate and were hardly touched by the Crusades. Those in the other lands of Europe did not easily recover from the dreadful experiences they had gone through. They never knew when another wild mob would turn against them. Life seemed to have lost its joy and peace and hope. 

It is no wonder that not many great scholars and writers grew up in these lands of Europe during the years of the Eleventh through the Fifteenth Centuries. Lives were too filled with sorrow and fear, for peaceful study. 

How much more fortunate were the Jews of Spain who were enjoying the period of the Golden Age during the

The Sad Centuries 

There is much more to be told about the sad fate of the Jews in Europe during these centuries of the Middle Ages. But we shall not dwell too long on these sorrowful events. A few of them, however, must be mentioned that we may better understand the story of Jewish life in these sad centuries.

In the year 1215, the pope ordered the Jews in all the Christian lands of Europe to wear a badge whenever they were out on the streets, so that all might know they were Jews. The badge most commonly used was a round piece of yellow cloth sewed on the front of the outer garment. It was a badge of disgrace intended to set the 

Jew apart from everyone else. This cruel law hurt the self-respect of the Jew and caused him much suffering. 

Fortunately, it was not strictly carried out everywhere and many Jews succeeded in paying sums of money for the privilege of not wearing the badge. Those who were

forced to wear it, often suffered from the insults and attacks of mobs.

More suffering was in store for the unhappy Jews. In those days when most of the people were ignorant and superstitious, they believed the most ridiculous lies that were told by their leaders. The Jews paid by the loss of many lives for one of these false stories which was spread among the peasants.

In the middle of the Fourteenth Century, a dreadful sickness called the Black Death swept over Europe. People in large numbers caught this disease and died. It is believed that one out of every four people in Europe died from this terrible sickness. One writer estimates that over twenty-five million perished.

The Jews also suffered from the Black Death. In many countries they suffered as badly as the Christians. 

But in some lands the number who died among the Jews was fewer, perhaps because they followed rules of hygiene more carefully than the other people. A story spread that Jews had caused the Black Death by poisoning the wells of water all over Europe. This ridiculous falsehood was believed by many. The ignorant peasants again had an excuse for killing and robbing the Jews.

Throughout Europe massacres and riots broke out against them. The awful scenes that took place at the time of the Crusades were repeated. Again, thousands of Jews were slain and entire Jewish communities wiped out. 

But Jewish sorrows were not yet at an end. Another way was thought of for disgracing the Jew and separating him from the rest of the world. The Jews were forced to live in a separate section of each city, which became known as the ghetto. The ghetto was shut off from the rest of the town by wall and gates. When the gates

of the city were locked at night, the gates of the ghetto were also locked.

Long before the Jews were forced to live in ghettos, they had generally lived together in a certain part of the town. They preferred to do so. At that time they had 

Christian neighbors and lived on friendly terms with them. But now things were very different. Christian families were compelled to move outside the Jewish district and Jews who happened to be living in some other part of the town had to give up their homes and live in the ghetto. Jews were even forbidden to have Christian servants. To be set apart from other people in this way was another cruel blow to the pride and self-respect of the Jews. 

It has been hard to tell you about the dreadful sufferings of the Jews in the Middle Ages. It is even harder for you to understand, as you are a child of the Twentieth

Century and the world has made some progress since those far off days. Even in our own time, however, the suffering of the Jews are not over. You know how badly the Jews in Germany have been treated during the past years.

As you read about Jewish life in the Middle Ages you must remember that they were the Dark Ages. The world of eight or nine hundred years ago in Europe was a more ignorant and uncivilized world than the one you live in today.

But you must not imagine that Jewish life during those centuries was always sad and gloomy. That was not so, at all. There were many periods of peace. Life in the ghetto was not a cheerless one, as you shall see. 

You stopped at 1500 C.E. Climb up now to about

1560 C.E. Most of the ghettos were already established by that time. The following story takes place in one of the famous ghettos of Germany, in Frankfort-Am-Main. 

As you read it, remember that ghetto life was very much the same in most of the lands of Europe because the Jews all lived according to the Jewish religion and laws. 

A STORY OF LIFE IN THE FRANKFORT 

GHETTO 

A Glimpse of the Judengasse

The twins, Miriam and Mayer Baer, walked along the Judengasse, the "Street of the Jews." They were going to the baker's for bread and were swinging an empty basket between them. The Judengasse was an extremely long and narrow street. The houses which rose on either side were four or five stories high. The upper floors extended over the first story and shut out much of the light and sunshine. Behind these houses, stone walls rose to a height of thirty feet, separating the Judengasse, or ghetto, from the rest of Frankfort. There were three gates in these walls, one at either end of the long street and one in the middle section of the wall. 

The street was lively and somewhat crowded with people. Most of them were dressed in black clothes. A number of the men had a round piece of yellow cloth sewed on the front of their cloaks. Some of the women wore veils on which two strips of blue cloth were sewed. 

These were the Jewish badges which the Jews had to wear when they went outside the ghetto. Anyone caught without a badge was severely punished by the Frankfort government. Carts rattled along the street, travelling from one gate to the next. Several students dressed in long black cloaks passed by. They had come to Frank-

fort from other towns to study Talmud with the rabbi who was a famous scholar.

The twins passed a number of people who were sitting on stools outside their homes, engaged in various tasks. 

Some of the front rooms of the houses had been turned into shops and stores where people could buy certain articles necessary for daily life. There were several bakers and butchers and a few tailors. The people in the ghetto who tried to earn a living by working at a trade had a very hard time. The non-Jewish workers complained that the Jews were taking business away from them and so the Council which governed Frankfort passed many laws forbidding the Jews to work at most of the trades. 

Some of the people in the ghetto were wholesale dealers and handled spices and grain. Several of the wealthy men of business dealt in precious stones, gold and silver, lead and brass. These products were often shipped to other lands.

One of the chief ways of earning a living was by banking, which was better known as money-lending. The government of Frankfort made the Jews agree to enter this business as the church would not permit Christians to lend money at interest. Since the Jews were not allowed to be farmers or work at most of the trades, many became bankers.

Others of the Judengasse earned their living by selling second-hand clothing and household articles. They were not permitted to rent stores outside the ghetto, so they peddled through the streets of Frankfort and in the neighboring villages.

A number could not find any way of earning a living and had to be taken care of by the Jewish community. 

The twins got their bread and then walked slowly home. "Let's play our guessing game," Mayer suggested,

He spoke in German, the language which was used in the ghetto as well as everywhere else in the country. Hebrew words were beginning to creep into the German, however, for Hebrew was studied in the schools and was the language of the synagogue and of the religious ceremonies and prayers in the home. "Let me guess first," Miriam cried eagerly. She covered her eyes with one hand and called out, “A lion!" "Right," answered Mayer, looking at the house which they were passing. They were guessing the signs which hung upon the doors of the houses. On every house of the Judengasse there was an emblem of some kind, generally carved on a slab of wood. The people who lived in the house were named after the emblem on the door. 

The sign on the Baers' home was the figure of a bear. 

He was standing on his hind legs, holding a rolled-up scroll of the Law in his front paws. Animals were often used as emblems. On the doors of the Judengasse one could see the carved figures of a lion, a goat, an ostrich, a stork, a swan, a parrot, and other animals. Names of fruits, plants, and household objects were also much used. 

The twins passed houses having such signs as a nut tree, a flower, a bunch of grapes, a green hat, a ring, a drum, a pitcher, a boat, a red shield, a boot, and many others. 

When people died, the emblem on their homes was carved on their tombstones. The twins had often wandered in the Jewish cemetery of Frankfort which lay at the end of the street, enclosed by a wall. They liked to study the signs carved on the stones. "We are much too slow," Miriam decided at last, when they had wearied of their game. So the two quickened their steps. "You have been playing on the way again," their mother greeted them reprovingly. "Hurry back to your

lessons, now, Mayer. Miriam, your household tasks are not yet completed."

In the Baer Home 

The living room where Mayer was studying was pleasant and comfortable. The furniture was made of pine wood and was painted a dark green. The extension table could be made larger by inserting boards. There were several armchairs as well as a number with straight backs.

Fine pewter plates and cups were displayed on shelves along the wall. From the ceiling hung a brass chandelier with six branches in which either oil or candles could be burned. On a small shelf against the wall stood a few greatly valued Hebrew books. "I have news for you, Mayer," the mother interrupted him after a while. "Father and I have decided to send you to school instead of having a private tutor. We think you will accomplish more by working steadily all day as you will be required to do in school." "I'm glad," Mayer declared. "It will be more lively than studying all by myself." "Where is little Hirschel, mother?" Miriam asked, coming in with her embroidery. She was making a richly decorated cloth to be used for covering the loaves of bread at the Sabbath meals. "The child is resting in the bedroom. See if he is comfortable, dear."

Miriam went into the next room where a small boy of six lay in a large four-poster bed. A canopy of blue and white checked linen hung around it. The room contained a wardrobe for clothing and a chest for linens. 

There was also a wash-stand with a pewter basin and pitcher. The wooden floor was covered with a carpet. 

Miriam tip-toed over to the bed and looked at the child.

who was fast asleep. He was not very strong and so had not started school at five years of age, as children usually did. But father had promised that Hirschel would soon begin. 

Miriam wandered into her own room, which was small but neat and pleasant. It contained a cot bed, a chair, and a small wardrobe. Miriam sat down by the window and peered into the busy street. The same houses, the same faces! She turned slowly away from the too familiar view. She wondered what it would be like to live in a different part of the town and see new sights and new faces. All her life would have to be spent in this one street, the Judengasse, shut off from the rest of the world by high stone walls. 

Preparation for the Sabbath

The next day was the busiest in the week for it was Friday and many preparations had to be made for the Sabbath. There was one duty which the twins always enjoyed. They had to carry the large copper kettle containing the Sabbath dinner of meat, beans, and peas, to the bakehouse. The man in charge of the big oven was jolly and treated them like good comrades. He allowed them to shove the kettle into the depths of the huge oven with a long pole. Many were the kettles that disappeared from view in its great mouth, for the bakehouse was used and supported by the entire community. In the lower part a huge fire would soon be built. Before the Sabbath the fire would be covered with ashes. The food would remain hot until Saturday morning after the synagogue services. Then people would stop at the bakehouse and each would take his shalet, as food cooked like this was called. The twins always slid the handle of the pot through a pole and each took one end of the pole. It

was not against the law to carry in the ghetto on the Sabbath, for the Judengasse was regarded as a common home for all the Jews, and the law permitted carrying in one's own home and yard.

The twins raced home to see who could get there first and arrived breathless and laughing. "Come, come, there is no time for nonsense," their mother called to them from the kitchen, her arms white with flour up to the elbows. Little Hirschel was standing at her side deeply interested in seeing how she made the pasdida. These were a special kind of cookies which Mrs. Baer always made for the Sabbath. They had a delicious flavor which no other cookies seemed to have. Mr. Baer laughingly called it the "Sabbath flavor." He insisted that all foods tasted better on the Sabbath than on any other day. 

Miriam was soon busy with her other duties. Mayer was even allowed to leave his studies to help her shine the pewter plates and cups. Mrs. Baer sang a delightful little melody as she worked.

Suddenly the door opened. The father appeared with a large basket on his arm. His face was pale and troubled, his breath was coming in quick gasps, and he seemed to be trembling in every limb. “Aaron, what is it? What has happened?" Mrs. Baer rushed to him, the song forgotten on her lips. She removed the basket from his arm and led him gently to a chair. "It is nothing. Do not be alarmed," he said in a whisper and rested his head on his hands. Little Hirschel burst into tears of fright. The twins stopped their work and a heavy weight seemed to creep into their hearts. The mother hurried to a cupboard and taking out a bottle of blue-berry wine, filled a small glass and raised it to her husband's lips. The color gradually came back into his face.

"I went to see Prince Wilhelm and his wife," he told them. "You know they always sell me the clothes that they no longer wear and I can find good customers for the lovely garments of silk and lace which I get from them. They were very kind to me and I stayed and chatted with them for a while as I always do. They told me that their doctor is a Jew. Many Christians, you know, have far more faith in Jewish doctors than in their own. Well, after I left, I decided to go to a market some distance from the ghetto in order to buy fish for the Sabbath. I could not get any yesterday in our usual market closer to the ghetto. The shop keepers raised their prices greatly because they know that every Jew wants fish for the Sabbath. So I refused to buy. "I did get some very fine fish today, dear wife," he added, his face brightening for a moment. To have good food on the Sabbath was to honor that day. "On the way home, I was careful not to walk in the places forbidden to Jews. I avoided the open square where the main church stands, and the other streets leading to the smaller churches. I also remembered not to go on the beautiful new avenue, lined with trees and flowers, the 

Stadt Allee. What a pleasant place that must be for a walk in the evening, after work is done! I hurried quickly through the narrow side streets, with my heavy burden on my arm. Suddenly I heard a shout, 'Jud! Jud! off with your hat!' A group of boys playing at a game of ball, had caught sight of the yellow badge on my cloak.

I pulled off my hat to them and hurried on. They pursued me, hurling large stones and shouting, 'Jud! Jud! back to the ghetto!' If one of those stones had struck me on the head I would have been seriously hurt." The mother's face paled as she listened, although it was not the first time such a thing had happened.

Mayer clenched his fists in fierce anger. "Why didn't you catch one of them and beat him well?" he demanded. "I have answered that question for you before, my son," his father replied. "No Jew dares to lay a hand upon a Gentile child. The cry would soon arise that we had cruelly injured him and the whole ghetto might be attacked and many lives lost. I dared not risk such a thing. In the Synagogue 

On most mornings and late afternoons, Mayer went with his father to the schul or synagogue for services. 

The women attended only on Sabbath mornings and holidays, since it was not considered as important for them to go as for the men. The chief duties of women and girls were in the home and even services in the synagogue took second place to those.

The synagogue was the only stone building in the ghetto, all the other houses being made of wood. As 

Mayer and his father entered one pleasant spring morning in the following week, they found a number already at prayer, swaying gently back and forth, with their white fringed prayer-shawls around their shoulders. The arched roof rose high above them. Light entered through thirteen windows. There were three entrances to the synagogue.

The Ark containing the sacred scrolls of the Law was covered by a richly embroidered cloth. Before it hung a silver oil lamp which was always kept burning. Not far from the center of the room stood a platform on which the reading-desk was placed. From here the Chazan chanted the prayers in tuneful melodies. 

During a pause in the services, the Schul-klopfer came up to the reading-desk and made some announcements to the congregation. "The following articles have been re-

ported lost," he called out. "A pair of silver candlesticks, a gold necklace that can be wound three times around the neck, and a large copper kettle. Anyone who knows where these articles might be or anything about them are commanded to give this information to the officers of the synagogue."

It was a regular custom in the Frankfort ghetto to have lost articles announced in this way. 

Several people then arose to announce donations of money which they were giving to charity. Mr. Elkan 

Oppenheimer, a wealthy man, gave a very large sum. “I wish half of this money to be used for supporting the students of the Talmud who came here from other towns to study," he said. "The other half is for the care of the poor."

After services were over, Mr. Baer and his son stopped before a large box which was kept in the synagogue. 

Mr. Baer dropped several coins in it. "This is a thanksoffering for my escape from danger last Friday," he told

Mayer. On many occasions of both joy and sorrow, the people put money into this box. It was used for purposes of charity.

The synagogue was the center of Jewish life in the ghetto. Here the Jews felt at home and at peace. They came for daily prayers, on Sabbaths and holidays, at times of joy and sorrow. They came for study as well as prayer. Since the people of the ghetto were governed by the Talmud, their religious Law, the synagogue was also the center of their government. The officers of the synagogue, who were elected by the people, made the rules for the whole community, with the advice and agreement of the rabbi. Several times a year, the rabbis of important Jewish communities in Germany would meet in some city and make laws for all the Jews in the land.

There were many laws governing the daily life of the people. Some of these even described the kind of clothing and the amount of jewelry which the Jewish women might wear. It was considered wrong for them to dress too richly. One law forbade people to beg from house to house. A third declared that prices must not be raised on things that were necessary for daily life. A very important law stated that no one could offer the owner of a house a higher rent than was being paid by the family already living there. This was to protect poor families who might not be able to find another home since they were forced to live in the Judengasse. 

The new laws were announced to the people during services on week-days. Anyone who disobeyed them was punished by the Beth Din, or Court of Justice. The most severe punishment which the court inflicted was excommunication. A person who was excommunicated was treated like an outcast. No one was allowed to speak to him or have anything to do with him. Such a person could not send his children to school. He could not take part in the synagogue services. He was completely separated from the rest of the community. For some offenses a person might be excommunicated for thirty days. 

For more serious offenses, the punishment lasted longer. 

The ceremony of excommunicating a person took place in the synagogue. A public announcement was made before all the people. The guilty person was dressed as a mourner. Ashes were strewn about his feet. Candles were lighted, the shofar, or ram's horn was blown, and other solemn ceremonies took place. The man was declared to be henceforth separated from the congregation of Israel. Anyone who associated with him in any way would also be punished with excommunication.

Gentile courts of Frankfort were regarded with great disapproval by their fellow Jews and were often excommunicated for this offense. 

One of the most important problems of the ghetto in which the synagogue also had to take the lead was how to pay the heavy taxes which were demanded of the Jews. 

The Jews were taxed directly by the emperor of Germany or by any prince or city government to whom he gave this privilege. They were taxed more heavily than any other group of people. One of the great burdens of their lives was collecting the large amounts of money which were constantly being demanded of them. The whole community was taxed a certain sum and then the officers of the synagogue had to collect it from the people. For this purpose, meetings were held in the synagogue to decide how taxes should be collected. Generally a committee was chosen to decide the amount which each one should pay. The rich had to give large sums and the poorer ones less. The officers of the synagogue were held responsible by the government of Frankfort for collecting the entire amount.

The synagogue also appointed committees which took care of the poor, the ill, and the wandering, homeless Jews. Everyone in the ghetto had to contribute the amount he could afford for the care of the poor. Even those who received this help also gave a small share, for it was considered a duty for all to take part in charity. 

Thus, the synagogue was the center of Jewish life. It was the house of prayer, the house of study, the place of government, and the home of charity. This was not strange, for the Jewish religion was not just for Sabbaths and holy days, but a religion for every day and for every act of life.

Mayer's Adventure 

One morning Mayer gave Miriam a signal that she was to meet him in the usual place. This was in the small back yard of their home. "What is it?" asked Miriam in a whisper. "Remember, you mustn't tell," Mayer warned her. I am going to leave the ghetto today all alone." "Oh Mayer, what for?" "I want to see the fair. It is held only twice a year and I have never seen it. The fair grounds are not far from the ghetto. I have some money, too, that father gave me for knowing my lessons well." "Please don't go, Mayer. I'm afraid something will happen to you." "I'm not a baby. I'm ten years old." "Then let me go with you," Miriam pleaded eagerly. "You couldn't do that. You're a girl and it's safer for girls to stay home. But I'll bring you something from the fair," he promised generously. "Are you going to wear the yellow badge?" she asked. "No," he said, after a moment's thought. "Nobody will know I'm a Jew and it will be safer to go without it." "But the gate-keeper won't let you out of the ghetto without it, and if you are caught on the streets outside the ghetto, father will have to pay a fine.” 

I'll wear it until I get out of the ghetto," he decided, “and then I'll take it off.” "Please don't stay too long," she pleaded. "You know the gates are locked at sunset." "I'll be gone only an hour or two," he assured her.

In a short time Mayer had finished his preparations. 

The yellow badge was pinned on the front of his blouse. 

He filled his pockets with cookies in case of hunger.

As Mayer approached the gates he felt a bit uneasy. 

No one stopped him, however, and he drew a breath of relief on reaching the other side. He quickly unpinned the yellow badge and put it in his pocket. Then he breathed deeply of the clear fresh air and looked about him. If only he could always be as free as this! He walked through the new part of the town where the streets were wider and pleasanter than in the old district. 

Whenever anyone turned to look at him, his heart beat a little faster. But he soon lost his fear. The streets were rather crowded, for many strangers came to Frankfort at the time of the fair. The fair grounds were right outside the town, not very far from the ghetto. He found the way easily by following the stream of people who were moving in that direction.

When Mayer reached the busy scene, he forgot everything else in his great interest. Traders from every part of Germany, from France and England, from the countries of the North and the lands of the South were there to exchange their products, to buy and sell from each other. Cattle and grain, clothing, household goods, food, all were on sale. Booths had been built for this event and before each stood groups of people bargaining and buying, and often coming to blows. There were soldiers present, however, to keep order.

The fair was also a carnival. Musicians played gay tunes upon their instruments, and wizards performed magic tricks. Candies and cookies were on sale. The

Jews could be seen here and there, wearing their yellow badges. Mayer hoped that he would not meet his father who might be here. The

boy knew he would be severely punished for this prank, but he would not allow that thought to disturb him now. 

Mayer thought that he had never had such an exciting and interesting time. He wandered from one spot to the next, watching the people in their strange costumes, listening to the talk, the music, and the noise that went on. When he felt hungry he chewed on a cookie. He did not forget his promise to Miriam and bought her a dainty little embroidered apron.

Finally Mayer was so weary that he lay down to rest beneath a shady tree. What a joy it was to be out of the narrow Judengasse and to stare up at the blue sky through the green branches of a tree! Before long, he was sound asleep. The hours sped swiftly by, and when 

Mayer opened his eyes, the sun was already sinking in the west. He jumped hurriedly to his feet and began the trip back to the ghetto. Somehow, the way seemed much longer than it had in the morning. He wasn't sure of the shortest way back and he dared not ask. The short nap had rested him a little but he still felt tired and his feet ached. One idea only was in his mind. He must reach the ghetto before the gates were locked. On and on he sped, and finally the walls of the ghetto came into view. Never before had those walls looked so good to him. He hurried toward the gates. The child's heart gave a great thump as he saw that they were closed. Mayer could not realize that such a thing could really happen to him. He pounded on the heavy gates and shouted loudly. There was no reply. It was getting darker now and the child trembled with weariness and fear. The gates would not be opened until morning. He would have to spend the whole night out in the dark, alone. 

The tears came thick and fast now, as he stood there, a lonely little figure beside the great gates. On the other

side was his home and mother and a comfortable bed. "Oh God, please let me get home," he prayed, “and I'll never complain about living in the ghetto again." But the gates and the stone walls were untouched by his tears and prayers. At last he sat down on a large stone and rested his head in his hands. Several people passed and looked at him curiously but he did not dare to ask for help. He thought of his mother. How frightened and worried she must be! Miriam was probably in tears. He wondered how long a whole night was, and wept again, Finally he stretched out on the ground and fell asleep. 

Mayer was startled out of his slumber by the light of a lantern flashing in his face. Several people were bending over him. Then he heard his father's voice saying, "Thank God, it is he." His mother began to sob. Mayer was carried home in his father's arms. They went through the little wicket gate which was set inside the larger one. Mayer could remember nothing else until he awoke in his own bed the next morning.

At the breakfast table he related his experiences rather timidly, for his father's face was stern. "It is fortunate," Mr. Baer said, "that the key to the small gate is left in charge of a Jew inside the ghetto so that one can get out in case of great need. Otherwise you would had had to spend the night out-of-doors."

No further mention was made of punishment as his father no doubt thought he had been punished enough.

So this time Mayer escaped the whipping, the usual form of punishment both at home and in school.

A Purim Celebration 

One of the gayest times of the whole year in the ghetto was the holiday of Purim. In the synagogue, at the school which Mayer was now attending, and in every home of

the Judengasse, Purim was celebrated. The whole ghetto was the scene of the Purim carnival. 

For several days before, Miriam had helped her mother bake many kinds of cookies and other good things to eat. 

These were nicely arranged in packages. The twins, with Hirschel trotting beside them, delivered these packages to the homes of their friends. This pleasant custom of sending Purim dainties was observed throughout the ghetto. The poor were remembered with larger and more useful gifts.

On the eve of Purim the Book of Esther was read from the scroll in the synagogue. It was Miriam's favorite story. She loved to hear about the brave and beautiful 

Queen Esther who had saved her people from death, about her wise uncle, the noble Mordecai, and the wicked

Haman who planned to destroy the Jews but was himself punished. After the reading of the Megilloth, as the 

Book of Esther was called, merry-making began. Celebrations went on in the synagogue itself. A great many of the young people and a number of the older ones too, wore masquerade costumes of all kinds. Miriam and 

Mayer exchanged clothes. Generally it was against the 

Jewish law for men to wear women's clothing, but on Purim the rabbis were not severe on this point. A number of young men whirled gaily around in women's skirts. 

Later in the evening, the doors of the synagogue were flung open by a merry young group. There was Queen

Esther dressed in an old gown of fine silk and lace, with a gold paper crown upon her head. At her side walked the mighty King Ahasuerus. Mordecai was there and 

Haman, and others who just added to the merriment. 

Without plan or preparation the young actors put on a Purim play for the entertainment of all. They knew the story well and did not have to learn their parts. Right

words came easily and the few times when they didn't only increased the merry spirit. The fair young Esther pleaded with the king for the life of her people. The king assured her that she had found favor in his eyes, and the Jews would be saved. Haman would suffer the punishment which he had planned for the faithful Jew, Mordecai. 

Miriam listened, hoping that some day she would play the part of Queen Esther. 

Another group caused much laughter by imitating the dignified rabbi and the way he taught his students. Only on Purim would such a thing be permitted. 

All the next day the celebrations went on. The streets were filled with people in masquerade who performed all sorts of amusing tricks. Clowns tumbled about, knights in armor carried swords and spears, jugglers performed their tricks, and musicians played upon their instruments. 

In the dance hall, dancing went on in a gay spirit. The women generally danced by themselves, in a line or circle, and so did the men. There were no regular steps but they followed the rhythm of the music. The dance hall was a very popular place in the ghetto. Here weddings and other happy events were celebrated. 

The homes, too, had a share in the Purim carnival. 

Much visiting went on and goodies were generously served. At Purim the Judengasse was more than ever like a big united family. Sorrows and cares were drowned in merriment. Perhaps the Jews would again be saved as in the days of Esther. Perhaps the gates of their ghetto would some day be unlocked and the Jews find themselves once more a free people. Purim gave them courage to wait and hope.

School Life in the Ghetto 

Miriam awoke one morning with the feeling that something special was to happen that day. Then she remembered. It was Hirschel's first day of school. Yesterday it had been Miriam's special privilege to bake three cakes of fine flour and honey for him. Now she jumped out of bed and began to dress. From the next room she could hear Hirschel's excited young voice asking questions while his mother dressed him in a new suit of clothes.

Soon the rabbi himself arrived to take Hirschel to the synagogue for the morning services. The rest of the family followed proudly. Hirschel was placed near the reading-desk. Later the little boy was taken to the school nearby. Here he received his first lesson in 

Hebrew. Some letters of the alphabet were smeared on a slate with honey. As the child repeated these, he ate of the honey cakes, fruit, and other goodies, so that the words of the Law might ever be sweet to him. The rest of the family stood around watching this scene with much delight. The next day his regular school life began. 

Mayer wondered if Hirschel would always find school so pleasant. The school day was very long. In winter 

Mayer had to get up before daylight. There were lessons until it was time for the morning prayers. The teacher then took his pupils to the synagogue. Sometimes a private service was held in the school. After prayers the children had a short period to go home for breakfast.

Lessons then went on until eleven o'clock. The boys went home for lunch and came back at twelve. There was a short recess period between two and three o'clock. 

Studies then went on until the hour for evening services. 

After this the children returned home. They often had

lessons to prepare at home too. The chief study for the younger children was the Hebrew Bible and prayers.

The teachers were stern masters and the rod was frequently in use.

In spite of their long hours at school, the boys found time for play too. There were ball games in the street. 

Often they played blind man's buff. A favorite game was choosing sides, selecting a leader for each side, and then trying to catch the members of the other team. On festivals, especially, the children liked to play nut games. 

The nuts were placed in a heap and each player tried to knock them down. The boys of the ghetto spent many merry hours in these games. Passover

The Passover season was the busiest of the whole year in the Judengasse. What cleaning and scrubbing there went on! What scouring of pewter and brass! Miriam was up early these days, helping her mother in all the household tasks. Mayer and Hirschel were busy at school studying the Hagaddah, the service for Passover night. 

One of the special events was the day when the Baers baked their matzoth. Several households generally performed this task together. Baking matzoth was a happy occasion for it meant carrying out a religious duty. A certain time was agreed upon with the man in charge of the bakehouse. The families gathered there at the appointed day, men, women, and children. The women mixed the dough in large vessels and then rolled it into round flat cakes. Then they pierced holes in it with iron pronged forks. The men were ready to do the baking. 

Into the big oven went each matzo on a long wooden shovel. In a few minutes it came out crisply baked. The

children danced around their elders adding somewhat to the confusion but also to the merriment. 

Finally the eve of Passover arrived. The Schulklopfer was knocking on the door with his wooden hammer to summon the people to the synagogue. They soon appeared, fresh and clean in their new holiday clothes. 

In the Baer home everything was ready for the Seder. 

On the white table-cloth stood the seven-branched candlestick of shining brass. A special Passover plate decorated with gay figures contained the herbs and other articles necessary for the service. Miriam had set seven places for they were having two guests. These were homeless 

Jews who had wandered in from another city and were now staying at the Inn for the Poor. All those in need were given special care for the holidays. Every household in the ghetto, no matter how poor, must have matzoth and wine for Passover. There were special societies formed in the synagogue which took charge of these matters. 

When Mr. Baer returned from the services the Seder was begun. Hirschel asked the Four Questions and Mr. 

Baer chanted in Hebrew the old, old story. Part of it he translated for them in German. The children learned how the Jews had once been slaves in the land of Egypt, but the Lord had freed them with "a mighty hand and an outstretched arm." Mr. Baer lifted a piece of matzo and chanted, "Behold, this is the bread of affliction which our ancestors ate in the land of Egypt. Let all who are hungry come and eat thereof." In memory of the bitter days which the Israelites had suffered in Egypt, they solemnly ate the bitter herbs on the table. In memory of the brick and mortar with which the Hebrews of old had been forced to work, they tasted the "Haroseth,"

a reddish-brown mixture made of chopped almonds and apples mixed with a little cinnamon and wine.

As each part of the story was told, the children listened with eager interest. They took part in the ceremonies with delight. After the supper was served, Mayer slipped quietly out of the room. The father filled the Cup of 

Elijah and bade Hirschel open the door so that the Prophet might enter. In came Mayer dressed as a traveller and smiling broadly. He had a staff in his hand and a wallet upon his back. Mr. Baer regarded him with pretended surprise and asked, "From whence comest thou, O pilgrim?" "From Egypt." "Are you freed from bondage?" "Yes, I am free." "Where art thou going?" "To Jerusalem." "Nay, remain with us a while to read the story of the 

Passover." And so Mayer took his place again at the table. The Seder went happily on. The guests told 

Passover stories and joined in the beautiful songs. In the past Israel had been delivered from slavery. God would not desert them in the present. Their hearts were filled with new hope and courage as they ended the Seder chanting, "Next year in Jerusalem!"

Pictures of Ghetto Life by a Travelling Preacher 

One afternoon when Mrs. Baer and the children were in the living room, the door opened and Mr. Baer came in. As usual, the children rose to their feet respectfully. "Peace be to you," he greeted them. "To you a goodly blessing," they answered together. "I have interesting news," he went on, sitting down. "A travelling Maggid just arrived at Frankfort. They

say he has been all over Europe and has seen much of how Jews live in other lands. Tomorrow he will speak to the whole congregation. He is staying at the home of the rabbi and I have been invited to meet him there tonight." "Will you take me with you, father?" asked Mayer eagerly. "Yes, my son. Gladly." 

That evening when they arrived at the rabbi's home they found the living room crowded with visitors. All were eager to meet the guest of honor, Joshua ben Moses, who was noted for his famous preaching and story telling. He travelled from ghetto to ghetto, addressing the congregations of Israel.

Mayer and his father sat with the rest around the table, from which tea, cookies, and other good things to eat were being served. "Tell me first how my fellow Jews in Frankfort are faring," the guest suggested. "Life here is neither better nor worse than elsewhere," the rabbi told him. "Taxes continue to fall heavily upon our shoulders so that our people groan beneath the burden. The laws forbidding us to work at trades grow more numerous. For the poor man in the ghetto, life is a great struggle." "Conditions are much the same throughout Europe," the guest replied. "Everywhere the Jews are huddled together in dark and gloomy ghettos, separated by walls and gates from the rest of the people. "As you know, England, France, and Spain have expelled their Jews entirely. It is fortunate for us that the districts in Germany are not so well united. When the Jews are made to leave one city, they are permitted by some other town to settle there."

"Yes," added the rabbi, "and after our people are sent out, the towns want them back because they miss the taxes of the Jews. But tell us more about our people in other lands." "I have just come from the ghetto in the city of 

Prague," Joshua ben Moses went on. "It is one of the largest and most cheerful in Europe. Not just a single 

Judengasse but a whole section of the city is set apart for the Jews. They have a number of synagogues, a town hall, and their own guilds. The Jewish community makes its own laws and has its own courts of justice, as you have here in Frankfort. But even in Prague, Jewish life is not secure. The Jews have been expelled from their ghetto a number of times when different rulers came to the throne. But they were always allowed to come back. "Prague, as you know, is a center of Jewish learning. 

Many students meet in the schools and synagogues to learn the Talmud from great rabbis.” "Have you been to Vienna, in Austria?" one man wanted to know. "Yes, I have passed through there. You know its sad history. It used to be one of the largest Jewish communities in Europe, and had many schools and great scholars. About fifteen hundred Jews lived there then. 

The rulers treated them kindly and even gave them some special privileges. Then a young duke came to the throne.

He wanted to enrich himself with the property of the Jews. Some falsehoods were started about the Jews being disrespectful to the Christian religion. This was an excuse for arresting them and seizing their property. You have heard about the dreadful tragedy of the year 1421. 

Hundreds were killed and burned at the stake. The rest fled into exile. The Jewish community was wiped out. 

It is now over a hundred years since that time, but there

are only a few Jewish families living in Vienna now." 

His hearers listened with sad hearts although the story was not new to them. At last one of them asked, “Have you also been in the ghettos of Italy?" "Yes, many times. The ghetto of Rome is only a few years old. The Jews were forced to enter in the year 

1556. It is a miserable place. There are a few dark and narrow streets along the left bank of the Tiber. At certain seasons of the year, the Tiber overflows its banks. 

The lower stories of the houses are often flooded and the people must escape to the upper floors. These floods bring disease and illness with them, and our poor people lead comfortless lives." "Is it true that in Rome many efforts are made to convert the Jews to Christianity?" one of the visitors asked. "That is very true. Those who forsake their religion are offered many rewards. They no longer have to live in the ghetto, or wear the hated yellow badge. They may freely enter any business or profession. The new name which they choose may be selected from among the noblest families in Rome." "Are there many who are tempted away from the religion of their fathers," Mr. Baer wanted to know. "There are some, but not many." "At least," one of the people said, "the Jews in Italy have been spared the horrors of massacre during the Crusades." "Yes," Joshua ben Moses agreed, "the pope could not permit bloodshed in the name of religion in the very land which he ruled.” "Tell us about the ghetto of Venice, the city in the north of Italy," another visitor suggested. "That ghetto is the oldest in Italy," their guest answered. "It was established in the year 1516. The ghetto

is on an island but is connected with the mainland by bridges and boats. It, also, is enclosed by walls and gates. The right to live in Venice is granted to the Jews for only a few years at a time. At the end of that period, they pay a large sum for the privilege of being allowed to remain for another few years. At any time they may be told to go and hundreds of people may find themselves without a home. This is not likely to happen, however, for the Jews in Venice pay tremendous amounts into the treasury of the city. "In Venice, also, most trades are closed to the Jews except banking and selling second-hand articles. "There is a special duty which the law requires of the 

Jews in this ghetto," Joshua went on. "They must keep several loan banks open for the sake of the poor who want to borrow money. The person who gets a loan leaves a pledge of some kind as security, such as an article of clothing or some household object. The amount of interest which the Jews are allowed to charge is very small so that these banks cause a serious loss of money to the Jews. But the law demands that the banks be continued because the poor cannot get along without them. The 

Jewish poor, however, are strictly forbidden to use these loan banks. "Life in the ghetto of Venice is the same, in many ways, as it is here," Joshua continued. "It is a larger ghetto than you have here in Frankfort. There are three chief groups of people, the German Jews, the Marranos from Spain and Portugal, and the Jews from Turkey and other lands of the Near East. This last group is made up mostly of merchants who carry on a large trade with the countries of the Near and Far East. They have helped to make Venice the greatest trading city of the world.

These merchants are given many special privileges which other Jews do not have. "Each of the three groups has its own synagogue. 

There is a court for the entire ghetto made up of seven of the oldest rabbis. "I remember one Sabbath day that I spent in Venice. 

On Friday afternoon, about an hour before sunset, a man walked through the streets of the ghetto blowing loudly upon a trumpet as a signal that all work should cease. 

Men in their yellow hats came hurrying home. All Jews have to wear a yellow hat as their Jewish badge. The noise and bustle of the ghetto streets changed to quiet. 

The people gathered in the synagogues for services. "On Saturday afternoon it was the custom to give a talk or sermon in the synagogues. Not only the rabbi, but other scholars were called upon for this privilege. "In some respects, the ghetto of Venice is better off than the Judengasse of other lands," the speaker went on. "It is not so shut off from the rest of the world. Venice, as you know, is one of the richest and most powerful trading cities of Europe at the present time. Many Jewish merchants and travellers come there and bring news of Jewish life in other lands. Letters are also sent by special messengers to different parts of the world." "Our worthy guest brought many letters to the Jewish families in Frankfort," the rabbi put in. "When he was travelling through the other Jewish districts on his way here, people who have relatives and friends in Frankfort asked him to take these letters with him. "I am most eager to hear about the Hebrew printing presses in Venice," the rabbi went on. "My volumes of the Talmud were printed there and they are my most prized possessions." "The first Hebrew printing press was set up in Italy

in 1475, about twenty years after Gutenberg's press became known in Europe," Joshua ben Moses told them. "This Hebrew printing press was started in a little town which was governed by Venice. Venice itself soon became the chief center for the printing of Hebrew books. 

These presses were owned by Christian firms but they employed Jewish workmen and scholars by special permission. The laws which limited the Jews to certain trades prevented them from becoming publishers. The most famous printer of Hebrew books in Venice was Daniel Bomberg, a Gentile. He was the first to print the entire 

Talmud. This great undertaking was completed in 1523, after four years of work. Bomberg's Hebrew books are highly valued for the beauty of their type, the fineness of their paper, and for the worth of the material they contain. "Later on a number of other Hebrew presses were started by non-Jews. Two of these printing companies got into a quarrel and one of them, in revenge, falsely told the government that the Talmud contained passages against the Christian religion. The Talmud was being published by the other company. The officials of Venice now ordered all copies of the Talmud and any other Hebrew books connected with the Talmud to be surrendered at once. Those who failed to do so would be imprisoned and then forever banished from Venice. "The Jews protested and pleaded against this injustice. 

It is said that they offered large sums of money to have this order withdrawn. They were not successful, and in the year 1553, hundreds of volumes of the Talmud were burned. Other Hebrew books met the same fate. Just a few months before, the Talmud had been seized and burned in the city of Rome. You can imagine the feelings of the Jews as their most precious possession went

up in fire and smoke. It was a blow from which Jews all over Europe suffered, for the Hebrew books of Venice had reached many lands. Hebrew printing did not die out in Venice, however, but is continuing on a much smaller scale.”

Murmurs of sorrow and protest arose among the peo- ple as Joshua ended. The rabbi sighed deeply. “Truly we live in difficult times," he said. "It is a world of constant warfare. Peasants fight against the lords for their right to live as human beings. Princes fight with each other for richer and broader lands to conquer. The new church of the Protestants fights against the Catholic church of Rome. How can the Jews hope to escape in this war-hungry world? They are a small group, different in religion, and without a land of their own. Is it any wonder that those who fight with each other, also fight against the Jew? But we must try by our own example to teach the lessons of peace." The silence which followed the rabbi's words was so sorrowful that he added in a more cheerful tone, "Come now, we will speak of happier things. We must never lose our hope or faith in God. Some day the Lord will lead us back to our own land of Zion, and there, as the prophets said of our people, "They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks. Nation shall not lift up sword against nation; neither shall they learn war any more'.” 

And now we must leave our friends of the ghetto. You have seen how they worked and played. You have read of their joys and sorrows. Although they lived on the narrow Judengasse, they never forgot the ancient glories of Jerusalem. The holy days brought back memories of

their beloved Palestine and they hoped that some day the land of Zion would be rebuilt. 

Even the walls of the ghetto could not keep some of its inhabitants from rising to wealth and power. One of the famous families that grew up in the Frankfort ghetto in the second half of the Eighteenth Century were the Rothschilds. The five sons in the family became the heads of banking houses in five important cities of Europe. For many years the House of Rothschild was the most powerful banking house in the world. In the Frankfort Judengasse, the Rothschild house still stands, in memory of the past. On the front door hangs the emblem of the red shield from which the family got its name.

For several hundred years the Jews lived in their ghettos. Once they had been nomads in the free air of the desert. Once they had been farmers, tilling the soil of Palestine. Once their king had ruled in glory from 

Jerusalem. They had built a new home by the rivers of 

Babylon. In the land of Spain, their Hebrew poets had sung. And in the ghetto they had learned to be strong in faith and brave of heart. They had learned to be merry even amidst the gloom.

They were ruled by many governments but their own law was the Talmud.

Many nations who lived in ancient times have disappeared from the face of the earth. They had mingled with the other nations until they were no longer a separate people. The Jews have been different. They remained true to their faith and their religion, even in the midst of terror and death.

In the Eighteenth Century new ideas of human rights and liberties spread among the people. Only then did the walls of the ghetto begin to fall. The Jews once more

walked the earth in freedom. With the fall of the ghetto, the Jews came into the modern world. But their experiences in this new world will begin another story. 

And now you have climbed to a place in the Hole of 

Time where you begin to see the light of Modern Days. 

You have a better understanding of why You are You

From far, far down the Hole of Time you have come into the Present. Your history as a Jew began about four thousand years ago, in the days of Abraham.

Today, with new faith and courage, the Jews are rebuilding their ancient homeland in Palestine. Time and the Jew march on together.

GHETTO LIFE IN EUROPE Under Christian Rule 

The Eleventh through the Fifteenth Centuries bring much suffering to the Jews in many lands of Europe. It is the period of the Crusaders, the Black Death, and of severe laws imposed upon them by the popes.

Activity for Review 

Plan a pageant or play on "Why You are You."

Show how every period you have studied in this book had an important part in making the Jewish child what he is today. Here are some suggestions you may wish to follow: Father Time and a child of today are standing near the Hole of Time. He says, "So you want to know why You are You. 

Very well, I will show you." He summons from the Hole characters from each period who explain to the child what they have contributed to Jewish life today. Remember that in Palestine the Mishnah was produced, in Babylonia, the Talmud, in 

Spain, many beautiful prayers and songs, and in the ghetto, there developed a strong spirit of loyalty and bravery in the midst of persecution. Show how the Jewish holy days and ceremonies were observed in all the periods you have studied in this book.
