Dedicated To all who are contributing to a more cooperative democracy

Introduction 

Even after a hundred years of growth of consumers' and producers' cooperation, there is, among cooperators as well as others, a lack of clear understanding and expression, as well as a lack of uniformity, of cooperative terminology. We have long needed a Cooperative Dictionary. For cooperation is bringing into use a largely new economic and social language, and it is important both to understand its meanings clearly and to use its expressions accurately and intelligently.

The cooperative movement is greatly indebted to Dr. Emory S. Bogardus for his extensive and clarifying writings in the field of cooperation. He has now added to his many contributions the first American Cooperative Dictionary. There will naturally be some disagreements about the definitions he uses. 

No doubt he will have occasion to make some changes in future editions. He has, however, made a valuable beginning in this field, which will be added to and revised from time to time. 

Just as the formation of a true philosophy must precede the development of sound practices, so also must the formulation of accurate phraseology accompany every advance. It prevents conflicts by furthering democratic discussion, and thus speeds up the progress of any new movement. This is particularly important with such a movement as cooperation, which is not a transitory idea, as are so many, but which has proven by a hundred years of practical growth that it is destined to succeed. The more rapidly it grows, the more rapidly will the economic causes of poverty, unemployment, tenancy, disease, crime, and war be eliminated.

This Cooperative Dictionary has a great purpose behind it-to help speed the day of plenty and peace. Dr. Bogardus' efforts in its preparation should bear much fruit. 

E. R. BOWEN,

General Secretary The Cooperative League

Statement 

Cooperative education demands the study of all aspects of cooperation and the examination of the various sides of subjects upon which differences of opinion exist. 

In such studies, issued by the Cooperative League, the author is accorded full freedom of expression. The publication of this book does not mean endorsement by the League of any of the opinions which it contains; the author alone is responsible.

Preface 

A dictionary of cooperation is called for at the present time because of the recent and current growth of the cooperative movement in many countries such as the United States, Canada, Latin American countries, China, and Great Britain. It is called for also because the cooperative movement, if rightly understood, bids fair to make a noteworthy contribution to postwar reconstruction. The movement has grown to the point where many terms are used in different senses and where the use of initials and other abbreviations is confusing to the uninitiated. Likewise, many cooperators are somewhat confused in their thinking about cooperative terms. 

This dictionary constitutes a set of guide posts for any person who wishes to increase his understanding of the cooperative movement. It aims at clarification of terms. It may constitute an important step in the development of uniformity of usage of both terms and thinking in the cooperative movement. 

It may be used also as a study guide for discussion groups who wish to consider cooperation from A to Z in its basic trends and current phases. 

For the first time, cooperative managers, educational directors, editors, and auditors have recently been working upon a uniform terminology. The writer who has had access to the results of this thinking hereby indicates his indebtedness. Also, deep appreciation is expressed to E. R. Bowen for writing the Introduction, and to Wallace J. Campbell, C. Jack McLanahan, and Merlin

G. Miller for reading the materials in this dictionary when they were in galley proof, and for contributing valuable ideas.

In preparing this dictionary the aims have been as follows: 

1. The definitions and descriptions have been kept as succinct and to the point as possible. It has been necessary to be selective and to emphasize definitive materials. 

2. The field of consumers' cooperation has been kept to the forefront. 

In order to offset as much as possible a widespread neglect of the consumers' needs, the emphasis in this book has been placed squarely upon the consumer and his attempt to improve his condition. 

3. At first it was planned to supplement the dictionary proper with a descriptive list of leading and representative cooperative institutions. A short list of selected leaders (not living) of the cooperative movement was also contemplated. Further consideration led to the decision that it would be easier for anyone who wished to use these lists if they were presented alphabetically as a part of the dictionary proper. The leaders whose names are included are

limited to persons not living. Eight exceptions are made, because of their long, pioneer, and widely known services in the cooperative movement, namely, Palmer of England, Johannson of Sweden, Gebhard of Finland, Jaeggi of Switzerland, Kagawa of Japan, Tompkins of Canada, Ribas of Colombia, and Warbasse of the United States. 

4. In the choice of titles to be included a great deal of thought has been given to both distinctive and representative factors. The historical approach has been maintained. The materials presented have been screened from a large body of available data. 

The writer is aware that errors may have crept into some of the statements and invites suggestions for the improvement of the dictionary. "Dictionaries are like watches, the best cannot be expected to go quite true, but the worst is better than none."-Samuel Johnson.

PREFACE TO THE SECOND EDITION 

The new edition contains about eighty new terms and names which bring the total number of items to approximately 600. About an equal number of items have received an enlarged definition or description, either enriching the earlier statement or bringing the facts up to date. A few typographical or other corrections have been made. The second edition may be considered about twenty per cent more useful than the first. Special thanks are expressed to those persons who have helped in any way to improve the Dictionary, particularly to Gilman Calkins, publications director of the Cooperative League. 

Neither these persons nor the Cooperative League are in any way responsible for the choice of terms and for the method of treating them. 

The procedure that was followed in the first edition has been continued, namely, of combining supplementary materials with the definition of terms. 

These additional materials are of an elementary encyclopedic nature and foreshadow a joint dictionary and encyclopedia of cooperation.

Accounts payable. The amounts of money that a business owes at any particular time, excepting those sums secured by notes or other indentures. 

Since a cooperative association normally buys for cash, its accounts payable remain low.

Accounts receivable. The sums of money that are owed a business at a particular time, excepting notes receivable or other secured amounts. Because a cooperative association normally sells on a cash basis, not on credit, its accounts receivable are kept low.

Capital. Accumulated wealth used in the production of economic goods. 

Consumers' cooperatives believe in the essential relation of capital to business enterprise and in paying fair wages (interest) to capital as well as fair wages to labor. They limit the rate of interest to capital, assigning the voting privilege in a business to the investor of capital as a person and not in proportion to the amount of capital invested. They allow no speculation in capital and rely on the 14 mutual-aid interest instead of the competitive profit interest as the major motive for the development of whatever business activity a community needs in order to meet and improve the conditions of living.

Capital, revolving. A procedure in cooperative societies whereby patronage refunds are left in the treasury of the society as working capital for a period of time. A method of temporarily increasing the capital of a society without cost to the members, called "revolving" because the first refunds so deferred are the first to be paid out, as other deferments are made. 

Capital, social. See Social capital. 

Capitalism. An economic system in which capital plays a dominant role in production as evidenced (1) by the ownership of shares in a corporation, (2) by control through voting by the owners in proportion to numbers of shares, and (3) by a profit motivation.

Cartel. A name used in Europe to refer to "trusts" and other combinations of business for the purpose of controlling the market and exacting monopoly prices from all consumers. By mass production and efficient methods cartels may lower prices but still hold them at monopolistic and unnecessarily high levels. Thurston Arnold has defined cartels as "any ring of producers or distributors who gain control over wide markets by agreements on price and output."

A widespread awareness by people in a given region or nation that they are divided on "have" and "have not" economic levels, and that as a result the two levels possess different and antagonistic attitudes.

Cooperation. From operari, to work, and co, together. To work together. A social process of working and thinking together toward universally advantageous goals. A social process that has its elemental origins in early human groups and in higher forms of animal life. In fact, the biologist points to universal evidence supporting the belief that even the simplest forms of life survive and thrive because of "working together." Among human beings cooperation takes place on four levels of motivation: (a) of survival of individuals, (b) of "getting ahead of the other fellow," (c) of "getting

ahead with the other fellow," and (d) of mutually helping to build together a more constructive and harmonious human society. 

Cooperative, a. A voluntary organization of people formed for the purpose of meeting their common needs by mutual action, democratic control, and sharing of economic returns on the basis of participation. A group of people voluntarily organized to meet their needs by following the Rochdale principles (q.v.). "A voluntary association in which people organize democratically to supply their needs through mutual action and in which the motive is service, not profit" (Warbasse, Co operative Democracy, Harper & Broth ers, 1942).

Credit union. A cooperative association of people of kindred interests, usually members of the same occupational group, as teachers in a given city, or of the same organization or community, as a cooperative or a rural town, which accepts deposits on a lowinterest rate and which loans small sums to its members also at low interest rates, on short-term bases and on character and reputation. It encourages thrift, enables its members to borrow at a minimum rate of interest, and is controlled by the members.

Lassalle, Ferdinand, 1825-1864. A Ger man economist and socialist who opposed consumers' cooperation on the ground that it would promote individualism and free enterprise and hinder the coming of socialism and state enterprise. He advanced the Iron Law of Wages, namely, that wages tend to be maintained at a "minimal subsistence level." Hence, to lower the cost of living by consumers' cooperation would lower wages and thus would lower standards of living. The argument neglects the upward push given to wages by organizations of laborers and the improved quality of goods that consumers' cooperation makes possible.

Motivation, cooperative. The motivations for cooperative living are manifold.

They are revealed in professional men and women who work for salaries instead of profits, and for the joy in their work. They are found in the laboring classes who work for wages instead of profits, and who find satisfactions in work well done. They function in every group where group opinion emphasizes the importance of cooperative behavior rather than competitive strife. They are revealed in the innate urge of mankind to work together and to engage in mutual aid.

Mutual insurance. A procedure whereby a number of persons form a society and pay to it pro rata sums of money to cover losses, such as those due to fire or sickness, that may befall any member. The farmers' mutual insurance societies in the United States are examples where the premiums are paid by annual assessments. As a mutual society develops, it charges prepaid premiums. Refunds are paid in case the premiums have netted a surplus. In general, a mutual insurance society (1) is formed without capital, (2) makes no earnings, (3) receives nothing but contributions, and (4) can indemnify risks only up to the amount of the contributions. The large mutual insurance companies for the most part have fallen away from an observance of Rochdale principles.

Mutualism. A social theory according to which everyone feels a genuine interest in the welfare of all and willingly undergoes sacrifice to be of needed assistance. Mutualism exists extensively within families and fraternal groups but as the group grows large the spirit of mutualism tends to decrease. Mutualism grows "by putting into operation acts of justice.'

Preliterate people, cooperation among. 

The basic tendency of primitive peoples is to cooperate, as well as to compete in order to survive. This mutual aid behavior among early peoples in human history and current preliterate groups is supported with many illustrations by Peter Kropotkin (q.v.). In her book, Cooperation and Competition.

Among Primitive Peoples (McGraw- 

Hill Book Company, 1937), Margaret

Rochdale principles. (a) The twenty eight Rochdale Pioneers (q.v.) developed not only six Rochdale aims (q.v.) but eight principles or rules of procedure for achieving the aims.

These have become the basis for the consumers' cooperative movement in the world. They have stood the test of time and place. Cooperative achieve ment follows their adoption. They were formulated chiefly by Charles Howarth, one of the twenty-eight Rochdale Pioneers. The original statements were: (1) open membership without restrictions regarding politics, religion, or sex, (2) each member to have one vote but no more, (3) capital to receive a limited rate of interest, (4) net savings to be distributed to members in proportion to patronage (5) cash trading at market rates, (6) accounts audited and reports made regularly to members, and (7) frequent discussion meetings of members to consider the society's activities. (b) At the International Cooperative Alliance Congress, held in London in 1934, it was decided that "the most important principles of the Rochdale 

Cooperative System" are: (1) open membership, (2) democratic control, (3) patronage refunds on the basis of purchases, (4) limited interest on share capital, (5) political and religious neutrality, (6) cash trade, and (7) education in cooperation. It was also decided that the first four named are the most important and that the others are secondary. (c) At the Biennial Congress of the Cooperative League (U.S.A.) in 

1934 it was decided to add an eighth "principle" to the ICA list, namely, "continuous cooperative expansion."

Russian cooperatives. Consumers' cooperatives began in 1865 among the wellto-do. After the unsuccessful revolution in 1905 many peasant people turned to cooperatives as a means of help. The Czarist regime was not fa vorable. When the Revolution of 1917 

came and a Constitutional regime assumed power under Kerensky, the cooperatives were numerous and strong enough so that they were able to respond to the request to take over the economic affairs of the country. Centrosoyus, the Central Union of Cooperative Societies, was organized in 1917. 

When the Bolsheviks came into power, the cooperatives were gradually undermined, but in 1921 they were given new consideration in order to help save Russian economic life. Under the first Five-Year Plan, with its emphasis on production, they declined. Under the second Five-Year Plan there was an emphasis on consumption, and cooperatives were given a larger role. 

But in 1935 the urban cooperatives were suddenly cut off in favor of state-owned and state-operated stores; the rural cooperatives were permitted to continue for the time being under state control.

German economist born in Delitzsch.

He added the name of his birthplace to his name in order to distinguish himself from other Schulzes. Self-help cooperative. While all cooperatives employ the principle of self-initiative, the self-help cooperative has a special meaning. As such, it refers to people who, being in a state of unemployment and without money, pool their laboring ability and engage in barter with their fellow self-help cooperators, so that the products of the labor of one group are exchanged for the labor products of other groups and all become in a measure self-sufficient. 

The members of self-help cooperatives try to maintain their self-respect and to acquire economic livelihood rather than accept relief from the state. 

Self-help cooperatives have developed twice in the United States: first, in the form of labor exchanges during the years 1893-1896; second, beginning in

Seattle in the summer of 1931 and reaching a climax in 1933 and 1934, with self-help cooperatives being organized in Ohio, Michigan, Washington, Colorado, Utah, and California.

They developed in many cases with the aid of government grants. See C.

Social capital. The net savings of a business, to which all the persons who have had anything to do with the business as consumers or producers have a claim. The question is raised: What is an equitable way of distributing social capital, especially in view of the fact that the existence of society itself and its functioning have made possible the development of a business? However, it has been a common practice for social capital to be distributed to stockholders, while cooperatives distribute social capital to consumer-patrons. Cooperatives widely 58 use the term to refer to that portion of the net savings which is not distributed but left in the business as interest-free reserves.

Socialism. Socialism is a system for cor recting the injustices of large-scale capitalism by state ownership and control of economic enterprises. Some socialists believe that private interests can be controlled only by the state. 

Others, such as Christian socialists, would make religious motivation guarded by the state the main thesis. 

Socialists have sometimes used COoperatives as a means to better the condition of the laboring classes, but on the other hand, cooperatives as a rule insist on autonomy (q.v.). In recent years in the United States there has been a shift in socialist thinking away from a heavy emphasis on state ownership, because its vast economic power "enhances political power, and because of its tendencies toward bureaucracy and dictatorship."

Socialization. 

A basic social process that develops the we-feeling in people and that builds up social welfare attitudes of persons, putting them in control of self-centered attitudes. Socialization is a process of developing personal responsibility and control in line with social welfare through voluntary activities and agencies, not through force and state domination. It is the essence of cooperative procedures.

Swedish cooperative housing. The rise of cooperative housing in Sweden has occurred since 1916, with Sven Wallender as its chief leader. Four main types of housing projects have been created. 

A-type is the best, requiring the payment of 10 per cent down, and renting for amounts ranging from $10 a month to $72; B-type requiring a 5-per-cent down payment, with rents ranging from $10 to $40; C-type calling for no down payment, and with rents running as low as $7 a month. D-type is built only for families with children. City and national governments furnish the capital at a low interest rate. After twenty years rents are cut, and the remaining income is used to cover repairs and upkeep. The result has been "better housing at less cost."

Tariff. A charge imposed by a government on goods imported into a country in order to prevent their undue competition with native products, and as a means of keeping up wages and profits. A high tariff often keeps needed goods out of a country and makes monopoly control possible. It prevents free interchange of goods between countries. In contrast, the consumers' cooperative procedure favors as few restrictions as necessary on the interchange of goods between countries in the same way that the different states in a given nation, between which wide variations of standards of living exist, are not permitted to put up tariff restrictions against one another. 

Taxes and cooperatives. Cooperatives. pay taxes similar to those paid by other business enterprises and corporations, including city and county real estate taxes, state and federal social security taxes, state corporation franchise taxes, federal capital stock taxes. Federal income taxes are not paid on patronage refunds that are returned or assigned to patrons (these are overcharges paid in on purchases) and do not represent income or profit to the cooperative.

Women cooperators. Women in many countries have shown a special interest in consumers' cooperation because they are the main purchasers of food and other household goods. They have given attention to quality and low prices and to consumer education. 

Through their guilds and related committees they have organized discussion circles and sponsored social gatherings for the promotion of cooperative interests. There are many women presidents and managers of cooperatives today, particularly in the United 

States and Switzerland.

Women's Cooperative Guild. An associa tion organized in England in 1883 at a meeting of the Cooperative Congress of the Cooperative Union, enabling women to take a new part in cooperative life. The aims are to spread a knowledge of the advantages of cooperation, "to keep alive in ourselves, our neighbors, and especially in the rising generation, a more earnest appreciation of the value of cooperation, to improve the condition of women.' The Guild has grown until in 1940 it had 65,000 members in 1,800 branches. It is said to represent the most definite body of public opinion supporting the cooperative movement in Great Britain. It has conducted its work to a degree in terms of a series of "campaigns," for example, for cash trading, for a low patronage refund policy, for cooperation among the low income classes, for woman suffrage. Its best-known president has been Margaret Llewelyn Davies, who was also the first woman president of the Cooperative Union (1922).
