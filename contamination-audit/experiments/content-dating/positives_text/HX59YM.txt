CHAPTER I 

HE clock on the modernistic radio cabinet struck eleven. 

The bright rays of the sun crept across the floor of the penthouse apartment overlooking Central Park until they reached the rim of a straw hat. The rim was dangling from a limp ankle. The ankle belonged to a slim young man draped ungracefully over a modernistic chair. The young man's blond curly hair, the envy of many a girl, looked as though the rats had nested in it. 

His best friends would not have recognized the chipper and spruce Jack Gibson whose ingenious little-boy smile was the [1]

means of transferring copious quantities of Grade-A, gilt-edged bonds from the coffers of Renny & Co. to the safe-deposit boxes of susceptible widows and hard-boiled business men to the not inconsiderable profit of the young man himself. His dress shirt was completely split up the back, and the tails were hanging out. His tie was twisted completely around to one side, but had managed, somehow, to retain something of its pristine freshness.

Strange noises came from across the room. Noises peculiarly reminiscent of a buzz saw. They came from another young man asleep on a couch-a tall young man whose usual neat mass of lacquered black hair was hanging in dejected rat-tails across his forehead. A brightly figured dressinggown was drawn about his throat, concealing his crumpled dress shirt and trousers.

The collar and tie clasped in the hand dan- [2]

gling over the edge of the couch were, presumably, his own.

At last the sun, in its steady journey, put the buzz saw out of commission by shining boldly in the operator's face. The young man stirred restlessly, turned uncomfortably, groaned and then, unable to shake off the clinging rays, sat up dolefully. "Cripes, what a head," he groaned thickly, trying unsuccessfully to rub the offending member back into a semblance of normality. 

With the movement came an opening and shutting sensation in the region of his temples which made him regret that he had not gone on sleeping. Even if he had never waked up, he thought, he could not have felt so very much worse. His eyes swept the room, and his melancholy increased. 

Vivid morning was flooding the room through the big casement windows. It made bright every nook and cranny. The sun [3]

glare brought into merciless relief a sight to sicken any born housewife. The room looked as though a hurricane had struck it. A good reproduction of a bad example of modernistic art hung from the molding by a single cord. A gilt cage containing a stuffed parrot hung from one of the wall lights. One of the panes of the French door leading to the terrace was smashed, and on the tiling outside lay the shimmering iridescent fragments of what had once been the Ming vase that went through it. The gorgeous apricot-colored Chinese rug was stained hopelessly with cigarette ashes and bad liquor. Judging by the stacks of empty bottles and glasses scattered about and standing in disconsolate little groups on the red-and-black lacquered modernistic bar in the far corner of the room, the apartment had been the only oasis in a thirsting metropolis. A chromium floor lamp that looked like a cross between a [4]

streak of lightning and a dying faun stood on the long mahogany table. A pair of dress shoes rested rakishly upon its shade. The telephone stand was overturned, and the telephone, lying on the floor, was adorned with a dejected-looking taffeta bow that obviously had been a part of the cool green drapes on one of the windows. 

The young man blinked and shuddered. "Mura! Mura!" he croaked hoarsely. 

There was no answer. "Where's that damned Jap?" he demanded of the world at large. 

He gazed about him drearily. Then his eyes lit upon the half-filled bottle on the floor by his side. He poured himself a stiff drink and tossed it off at a gulp.

Then he faced the spectacle of the room again. "It must be real," he muttered in awe, pushing the rat-tails out of his eyes. He studied the scene again. "It is real," he de- [5]

cided dismally as fitful flashes of memory brought back the cause of the wreckage. 

Slowly he got to his feet. "Gosh, what a birthday party!" he groaned, as the full impact of the room's destruction penetrated his brain. He was a more powerful man, when you inspected him closely, than one would be led to suspect. One of the accomplishments of his lean face and slender build was the impression-when you were away from him that he was a man of medium height and quite thin. You had to look at him carefully to realize that he had shoulders like a stroke and to appreciate the easy grace with which he moved. Further inspection led one to suspect that this red-eyed, bleary-looking individual was none other than the erstwhile handsome, polo-playing, hell-raising son of the late John Christopher Douglas. 

Just at the moment, however, there was very little grace about him as he stooped [6]

over, picked up a cushion and threw it at the recumbent figure on the easy chair. The cushion failed of the desired effect and next time Douglas shied a book. "Come on, sweetheart, wake up. Spring is here," he croaked. "Aw, leave me alone," the sweetheart grunted, none too sweetly, and turned so that the brim of the straw hat around his ankle broke off.

Douglas got to his feet, somewhat unsteadily, it is true, and picked his way across the room to the friend who shared the apartment. Putting the bottle and glass he carried on the floor, he seized Gibson's feet. "Come now, Papa spank," he said, working the legs back and forth like a couple of piston rods. "Come on, wake up. I crave company in misery." my 

Jack Gibson opened one eye and scowled. 

He made a move to sit up and Douglas [7]

promptly dropped the feet. They came to the floor with a thud. "Oh, I'm so sick," Gibson moaned.

With an effort he raised himself to a sitting position, looking, for all the world, like a cherub suddenly stricken with maturity. "Where's Mura," he grunted. "I need a couple of aspirins or a bromo selzer or something. I think I'm going to die." "The damn little Jap hasn't shown up yet," Douglas said thickly. "But I'll fix you up. What you need is a little of the hair of the dog that bit you," and he poured out a drink with a critical eye, if unsteady hand. "Thanks," Gibson muttered, gulping it.

The treatment seemed to revive him. He ran his hands through his tumbled hair-a characteristic gesture. Then: "Never again!" "Until the next time," Douglas said, stoop- [8]

ing painfully to pick up a scattering of small coins from the floor and surveying them wonderingly. 

Gibson put his hand to his head. "What a headache I've got," he moaned. "Do you expect me to break out in a rash of loving-kindness and good cheer over your headache, when I've got a worse one myself," Douglas told him, deciding that if the coins in his hand hadn't been his originally, they were now. "Besides," he went on, "you should worry about a little thing like a headache, when to-day's the day you inherit more millions than a dog has fleas. Just the thought of that would be enough to cure anything in me, from leprosy on up." 

Despite his aching head, there was the beginning of his usual cheerful grin on 

Gibson's gay, satiric, scoffing mouth that was always sharp-edged and firm-angled under its light mockery. Then the beginning [9]

of the grin faded. "Lord, I can't remember a thing. What happened, anyhow?" "You gave a party," Douglas answered sweetly and dodged by a scant inch the book which Gibson cast. "Don't try to be funny, I'm too sick," the thrower said. "What happened?" "Search me," Douglas said, eyeing with an air of startled surprise the woman's evening slipper which had just caught his eye under the edge of the couch. It was golden brown in color, of some crinkly silken material with a knot of what looked like tiny topazes for a buckle. He eyed Gibson cautiously and pushed the slipper furtively under the couch. 

Not that he had a guilty conscience. Just an uncomfortable conscience and an innate sense of caution. And a lady's slipper in this most masculine apartment might lead to unexpected, if not embarrassing, consequences. [ 10 ]

"It looks like plenty happened," Gibson said, gazing about the room sourly. "It looks like a case of the children improving on the sins of the fathers. Gosh, what a mess. I don't remember anything after that bicarbonate blond did a hula-hula on the table. 

She ought to wear longer skirts," he added as an afterthought, "and her ankles were a disaster on land or sea." 

He produced a crumpled cigarette, lit it and let a cloud of smoke drift away on the vagrant air that came through the wide 

French doors to the terrace. Then he looked at the slender tube critically. "You always did have rotten taste in tobacco," he observed conversationally. "It's your liver," Douglas told him sourly. "Grind up a mess of onions in a meat chopper and drink the juice." 

Gibson shuddered at the thought. 

Douglas reached for his own cigarette [II]

case and eyed, with a thoughtful face, the smashed and battered piece of etched gold which he hauled out of his pocket. He racked his brain to remember whose cigarette case that had been. He had never seen it before. 

There was growing within him an uneasy feeling that there was something about that party the night before which had set it apart from other parties. "Just what do you remember?" he asked cautiously, still eyeing the cigarette case. "I don't remember much," Gibson answered slowly. He ran his hand through his hair and frankly appraised his surroundings with dark, rakish eyes. The more he thought of the matter, the more a little surge of uneasiness within him grew. "For instance, where in hell did that parrot in the cage come from? And I don't remember seeing that floor lamp on the table, either." "I don't, either," Douglas agreed. "I can't think." [12]

"Well, if at first you don't succeed-don't try again," Gibson said with a touch of his old flippancy. 

Douglas did not answer. The things that he didn't remember, coupled with the things that were coming to light now, were growing more and more disconcerting.

He walked across the room and flung open one of the windows; gazed from his high aerie at the greenness of Central Park below, at the boats dotting the lake, and the befogged cliffs of Central Park West beyond.

His eyes lit upon the fragments of the vase on the terrace. He shuddered. That vase had been a treasured possession.

Irene had given it to him on her return from Europe last year. It had marked the end of their friendship and the beginning of a deeper interest. Irene would be furious that the vase was broken. He hoped, rather doubtfully, that her anger might be mended more readily than the fragments of shim- [13]

mering porcelain out there in the sunlight.

Vague apprehensions and dismal forebodings filled him. He wished, listlessly, that he was comfortably dead. He felt like the devil and things were developing that were beyond the reasoning powers of his numbed brain.

The voice of Gibson, slumped in a big easy chair, broke into his reverie. 99 "I've a funny feeling about last night,' the blond youth said slowly. "I've a feeling that something strange happened after we passed out." "That's funny," Douglas said, turning. "I've been thinking the same thing myself."

The fact that this had been a party of parties—a party to go down in history as a party to end parties-had been creeping over Gibson for the past few minutes. It had begun to stand above the ordinary level of [14]

daily occurrences as a tall and slender tower dominates the level plain on which it stands. "Something queer happened at that party after we passed out," Gibson went on with conviction. He was beginning to be haunted by a distant premonition of disaster. "Something unusual. I wish I could think, but my head feels like a drum." "I wonder," Douglas said suddenly, "if

Mura could have been in and slipped out without waking us. Maybe he made some coffee." "Bo, howdy, what a life saver coffee would be," Gibson agreed, easing himself to his feet and following in the wake of his friend toward the door of the kitchen. "Say, what's the idea," he demanded crossly, as Douglas stopped short, causing 

Gibson to bump into him. "Put out your hand if you're gonna slam on the brakes that way." [15]

Then his eyes lit on the thing that had brought Douglas to such a sudden halt. A square of white paper pinned to the door of the kitchen. A note, across which sprawled 

Mura's spidery handwriting. Peering closer over Douglas' shoulder, he read it. "Please Honorable sirs Mr. Douglas and 

Mr. Gibson," the note read. "I go home. Not like get killed. See you maybe jail. If not then funeral. So long good-bye." 

It was signed, "Mura." "Now what can the little fool mean by that?" Douglas demanded angrily. 

For once Gibson had no answer ready.

Apprehension was gathering about him like a dark cloak. [16]

CHAPTER II 

ITHOUT waiting for an answer, Douglas pushed open the door of the kitchenette. The tornado had struck there also. Piles of dirty plates and dishes were in the sink . . . . except those that were broken and lay on the floor. Remnants of food, never an appetizing sight, and now doubly less, were profuse. Someone had left the door of the refrigerator open and the frosting about the ice-cube compartment had melted, overflowing the little pan beneath and spilling onto the floor. No sign of the little Jap's deft fingers here. "No coffee?" Gibson asked anxiously. "No coffee," Douglas returned sadly. "What do you suppose he means by 'no [17]

like get killed'?" Gibson asked thoughtfully, closing the refrigerator door. "Search me," Douglas returned, turning on the cold water faucet. "And 'see you in jail,' what does he mean by that?" Gibson persisted. "I don't know. He's nutty enough to mean anything," Douglas responded, between gulps of water. "But who will he see in jail, and what for?" Gibson persisted. "And whose funeral is he talking about? Yours, or mine or his?" "Oh, dry up, can't you," Douglas said crossly. "What am I? A mind reader? Perhaps he's just expressing himself in his own quaint Oriental way.' 99 He pushed through the door back to the living-room again. Gibson followed him. "Jail! Funeral!" His lips were moving as though to memorize the words. They moved [ 18 ]

with a peculiar expression of reluctance, of distaste. "I wish I knew what he meant," he said unhappily. "Come on, calamity howler," Douglas said, slapping him on the back with an attempt at lightness. "God's in his heaven and all's right with the world. Stop belly-aching and let's get the place straightened out a bit." "O.K." Gibson agreed, without enthusiasm, eyeing the room. 

Try as he might, as he listlessly began picking up the débris of the party, he could not keep out of his mind the wording of 

Mura's note, and for some strange and inexplicable reason it carried with it a tinge of terror, veiled, elusive, yet almost hideously real, like a childish dread of the dark that had never been quite outgrown. Foolish to feel that way because it was such a small [19]

matter. And yet there are times when small matters are but the foundation for greater issues. (And it would not have improved his mental state had he been able to glimpse what was going on in the apartment below.) 

Meanwhile, Douglas had crossed to the table and taken down the floor lamp. Setting it in its accustomed place, he took his shoes off the top of the shade and proceeded to put them on. There was an obstruction in the toe and he ran his fingers in and pulled out something long and sheer and altogether feminine. This was too much. "Look!" he cried, and there was abject wonder in his voice, and in his heart a queer, inexplicable premonition of catastrophe, of disaster.

He held up before Gibson's apprehensive gaze a pair of filmy woman's hose. "They're not mine," he insisted firmly. [ 20 ]

"Of course not. They're a woman's," 

Gibson scowled. "So your lady friends take off their stockings at your party." "It wasn't my party and they aren't my lady friends," Douglas said decisively. 

He eyed the stockings a bit uncertainly for a moment. He was nonplussed. Added to the two other incidents, the stockings would seem to have a definite significance. 

But, try as he might, he could not total them up. And the more he tried, the more uneasy he grew. He shook his head. "I'm too woozy to try and think," he said finally. "I'll stick my head under the shower and see if that will freshen me up. And you'd better step on it, too. You've got to meet your Uncle Dudley at the lawyer's office at three." "Yeah, I know," Gibson said without enthusiasm. "Gosh, he's going out a pauper and com- [ 21 ]

ing back a millionaire, just by signing a flock of papers," Douglas marveled, "and he shows about as much enthusiasm as a sick pup! It's always the wrong fellow that gets the breaks. Boy, what I could do with that money. Why wasn't I born rich, instead of handsome?" "Don't flatter yourself," Gibson growled.

Douglas slipped off the dressing-gown and started for the bathroom.

At this moment the apartment house was blown up by a bomb. . . or, what amounted to the same thing as far as the effect on his nervous system was concerned. Gibson had let out a frightened squawk, like a chicken that sees the axe poised. He was staring at 

Douglas, goggle-eyed. "What's the matter with you?" Douglas demanded impatiently. "How did you get that blood all over you?" his friend stammered. [ 22 ]

"Blood?" 

Douglas' eyes followed the pointing finger and horror grew in his own eyes as he discovered a huge splotch of crimson across the front of his dress shirt. "Oh, I knew it. I knew it. I knew something terrible had happened," Gibson said frantically. "Tell me, Jack-you can trust me"-he came closer and his voice was like that of a beaten ghost-"Jack-did you kill somebody last night?" "Don't be an ass," Douglas said impatiently, then added more slowly, "Now how in the world did that blood come to be all over my shirt?" 99 "Jack, why didn't you tell me sooner,' 

Gibson said, eyeing the crimson blotch with gruesome fascination. "Now I understand the reason you've been acting so strangely, dodging questions, avoiding the issue. I saw you hiding that slipper, and you would have [ 23 ]

hidden the stocking, too, if I hadn't seen you pull it out. Jack! What did you do? 

Tell me. Maybe I can help you." "Don't be an utter idiot," Douglas said impatiently. "I don't know how I got all full of blood. As for the other thingswell-knowing the kind of mind you've got, there was no need in putting ideas into your head. False ideas," he added quickly. "You know you can trust me," Gibson went on reproachfully. "I'm prepared to be broad-minded-even broad-minded enough for this." "But I tell you I didn't do anything—that

Gibson sighed dolefully. "Something terrible happened at that party. I had a premonition. Oh, it's awful to be in trouble and not know what the trouble is." "For heaven's sake, shut up," Douglas [24]

snapped impatiently. Two vertical lines appeared on his high forehead above his eyebrows. They gave his face a look of acute distress. "Let's wait until the trouble starts." "It has started," Gibson declared firmly. "Oh, Jack, why did you do it? And right at my party. If you had to kill somebody, why didn't you pick out the Central Park Casino, or the Waldorf Astoria? Why my party?" "Will you shut up? I don't know any more-" Douglas stopped, his voice trailing off into an awed whisper as his eyes rested on Gibson's right hand. He grabbed it and waved it accusingly before its owner's startled face. "Say, when you start accusing me of murder, why not explain how that blood got on your hands?" 

Gibson surveyed his bloodstained hand, his muscles bound in a rigor of dreadful apprehension. Silence wrapped the room about like a tight swathing. [25]

"Something did happen," he whispered weakly, after a moment. "Who did you kill?" Douglas asked grimly. "Jack," Gibson said slowly, "do you really suppose we killed someone and don't remember anything about it?" "You're as crazy as a coot," Douglas shouted, but there was uncertainty in his voice. He sank into a chair, pressing his hands to his throbbing head. "God, why can't I remember something?"

The shrill sound of the telephone cut through the silence of the room. The two started, then looked at each other foolishly. "Yes, it's the telephone," Gibson agreed with a sheepish grin. "And if it's another of those automobile salesmen or insurance agents that have been making my life miserable ever since the tabloids got hold of the [ 26 ]

story that I was due to inherit my money to-day, that wire's going to melt with the heat of what I tell him." 

But he made no move to answer it.
