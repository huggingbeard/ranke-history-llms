Grace was sleeping upon it. Nor could she turn on her side. Movement in any direction was practically impossible. She felt almost like the meat in a sandwich, Grace sleeping on one side of her and

CHAPTER II 

EORGIANA, the oldest, twenty-one, was tall and so beautifully formed she might have found a place in the Ziegfeld ensemble without being out of her niche. Tawney, lovely, she reminded one of a sinuous panther. Her hair was golden-brown, her eyes so dark that they were almost black and her complexion slightly coppery from constantly being out of doors. Though her features were well-formed, her mouth was too sensuous and red. Georgiana loved life. To her it was wine which it was a joy to drink. She seldom read books, knew scarcely anything about history or the arts but she was an ardent student of men, always playing up to them, always interested in their conversation. She was an excellent listener, so naturally she was rated as being clever and wise. Her renown was acquired by bowing to the ego of other folks. 

She was thoroughly modern, by nature polygamous, held in check by fear. Carlene believed that her sister was good but careless. On the other hand Grace declared that Georgiana couldn't be trusted any further than you could throw a brick house. 11

From her own attitude toward life, Grace formed her opinion. She was willing to try anything once. 

She believed that nothing was wrong unless you were found out. Of course she did not put all of her theories into practice but at least she had a few of them working steadily day in and day out. Grace was a schemer, not mean, nor petty but she liked to wear a personal cloak of glamour. Frequently she told the most outlandish tales about her experiences which were the purest fiction but which she almost believed in herself. Into the gullible ears of the boarders she poured many a prepostrous tale. Perhaps sometimes she had her tongue in her cheek as she breathlessly told her fabrications.

Slightly smaller than Georgiana, she was equally as good looking in a more somber way. Her hair was jet black; black too were eyes; her face, olivepale. To some extent she helped out nature with generous applications of powder. She mauve-tinted her eyelids to emphasize her pallor. Her lips were extremely red by comparison to her ivory white skin.

Even though she lived in the country she never grew sun-burned. The sun seemed to pass her by utterly as though in disgust. "The sun does not like me," she frequently told the boarders, "because I am really a daughter of the night, a daughter of the moon. The stars woo me."

The boarders who should have had ears as large as the handles of loving cups, looked at her in amazement.

Laura Klein who had been summering at "The

Happy Nest" for years was vexed. She liked to classify every one she met. Her mind was as orderly as a card index. But Grace she could not classify. She couldn't decide whether she was a genius or slightly mad. She did not stop to realize that the two are almost synonymous. A genius controls his dreams, a madman is controlled by his dreams. Dreams are the motive in each case. 

Men found Grace very easy to look at. She was two years younger than Georgiana but she looked older, more sophisticated, more a woman of the world. Seldom was she home in the evening. "If," Georgiana used to say, "a month had only thirty days, Grace would have dates for thirty-one." 

Carlene, just past seventeen, was the youngest of the trio, a strange, adorable mixture of child and woman. Her hair was copper-brown, her eyes were dark blue, almost black, laughing, roguish. Her sense of humour was so acute it got her constantly into difficulties. 

Second only to her love of laughter was her love of reading. She absorbed ideas and impressions like a sponge. The boarding house was a splendid college. What she didn't learn about life at "The 

Happy Nest" wasn't worth knowing. Much that she did learn was equally of no importance. 

Carrie Dixon was very fond of her girls but that did not prevent her from constantly ranting, fretting and finding fault with them. "You won't have your old mother long," was one of her pet expressions. There were tears in her

eyes as she said it and usually a catch in her voice. 

In view of the fact that Carrie Dixon was not yet fifty and in the pink of health, it was a silly utterance. But many of her actions were of similar caliber. 

Carrie Dixon could have been a rather handsome woman if she had taken any pride in her appearance. 

Her complexion was like that of a young girl. Her eyes were large and beautiful but her mouth was weak. Constantly she complained and whined and incessantly she wore a soiled apron splashed with grease. She had a most provoking habit of wiping her hands on her apron when she was excited. She used it as a towel. As a rule she presented a rather tawdry, sloppy appearance, her hair straggling about her face, rather breathless and constantly perspiring. 

Deep within her there were hidden many splendid qualities but they were so submerged under petty things it was almost impossible to discover them. 

Carrie Dixon's life had been rather fruitless despite her three handsome daughters. She had mated with Tom Dixon and given him children, yet in her entire life she had never known a perfect night of love. Love was alien to her. She scoffed at it. 

Carrie's marriage had been a miserable mistake.

She had married Tom Dixon for purely mercenary reasons. He had inferred that he was wealthy, had money invested in rich oil lands in California. Carrie succumbing to cupidity, had married him only to discover that his investment had been in orange-blossom dreams that refused to grow. He actually

owned only a hundred oil shares which had cost him twenty cents each. The certificates were beautifully engraved. Apparently it was a fine company but it didn't have any oil. Thus Carrie sold herself into bondage for a few shares of worthless stock. She married a man she detested, almost loathed to secure an empty bubble. One thing must be said in her favor. She continued to live with him, even suffering the agonies of birth for him. The children were beautiful even though they were not conceived in love.

So complex was the character of Carrie Dixon it is hard to define it. She loved to go to funerals. 

It mattered little whether she knew the deceased well or not. Her grief was always extreme. Audibly she sobbed through the service. Her nose became red and shiny, her lips quivered. Everyone felt sorry for her, believing that she must be a great friend or near kin. But it was sympathy wasted for Carrie Dixon enjoyed a good cry. Her viewpoint on life was slightly morbid. She thrived on melancholia. Her marriage to Tom Dixon had been an added spur to a gloomy nature. 

Even in her cooking, she was complex. Her puddings, pies and cakes were so excellent the boarders never stopped talking of their qualities. But she never cooked a bit of fish or meat that wasn't swimming in grease.

Georgiana frequently declared that her mother took all the taste out of the meat and put it into her pies.

"But," argued Grace, "you can't live incessantly on pie."

Her soups too were tastless, greasy, unpalatable. "Ma raves about her home cooked meals," mused

Carlene. "After eating a dish of her soup one can believe it. It tastes as though she cooked the home right in it, cellar, shingles, chimney and all." 

Carlene was somewhat of a dreamer. From earliest childhood she had always imagined herself in the place of the heroine of the story she happened to be reading. She was "The Sleeping Beauty," "Little Red Riding Hood" and Scherazade. Later she was Elaine, Guinevere, Heloise and Juliet.

Some day she would travel all about the world, visit 

China, India, Negri Semblan. Pretty sounding words she loved, Malabar, Samarkand, Hyderabad, Taj Mahal, Madras, Kashmir, Khyber, Mandalay. 

All held glamour for her. Just to utter them was music. She longed to visit forgotten places, to thrill at strange sights, to walk alone through the desert, to sleep outdoors beneath a torrid moon. She was insatiably romantic. Some day she would go to New 

York. She would become a great dancer. Throughout the world her name would be known. She would interpret love-poems in her dancing, she would be a flower, a jewel, a breath of perfume, dust of moonrise. Through her dancing she would interpret every emotion.

Carlene had never taken lessons in dancing and yet she danced divinely. The flowers taught her to dance. She danced gracefully even as they swayed

in the wind. For the most part she danced without anyone watching her. Perhaps the sun itself glowed a bit more warmly to behold her lovely body. 

Of all things of earth, she loved quietude the best, yet quietude was the one thing she seldom could find in her own home. She had to sleep with her two sisters and always in the middle. During the day or early evening no matter where she sat in the rooms, some boarder would intrude upon her thoughts. "My home," she used to reflect, "is not really in this house but out in the forest. Even though the Dixon girls had been born and raised in Ellenville they were almost as sophisticated as the average city girls. Constant streams of boarders passed and gushed through their lives. HADD CRANE drifted into "The Happy 

Nest" with the intention of remaining over a week-end. Instead he stayed on and on. He had been touring around the mountains in his car, consumed with ennui, in search of a thrill or at least hoping that he would find something that would arouse his interest a trifle at least. Then he met the 

Dixon girls and lethargy commenced to slip from him. 

Shadd was an idler, magnificent if you wish. He was young, handsome, faultlessly attired. His father was wealthy, a broker in Wall Street. Therefore it was unnecessary for Shadd to work. The thought never crossed his mind that there might be a certain pleasure in working, a certain exhilaration in accomplishment. It was his boast that he hadn't read a book in five years. Another of his favorite observations was, "Although I wore a uniform and went to war, I haven't the faintest idea what it was all about. I believe I killed a few men but whether they were friends or enemies I don't know and it doesn't matter because there wasn't much difference between the guys on either side. They were villians, vile wretches, murderers only to the extent that liars 21

could paint them. All the nations ought to be ashamed of the sorry spectacle they made of themselves. Ever since the war, I've been trying to find out who won. Some have a vague idea that we did. 

Perhaps. But when a guy wins a court case, does he usually have to stand all the expenses? It is all very confusing and amazing. We went to Europe to make the world safe for democracy. Now we've got to be saved from the Republicans. When we got home again we found a gangster in every telephone booth. That's what Prohibition has done for

America. The country was sick and the doctor prescribed Prohibition. It's a swell medicine but it looks like the patient's dying."

Shadd was a cynic. The war had done that for him. He had no morals. Ethics didn't bother him.

He had hosts of friends. Women loved him until he was tired, but not tired of women. To him all girls were toys, to amuse one for an idle hour. He lied delightfully, telling every good-looking girl he met that he loved her. He usually succeeded in loving her, before finally quitting her for pastures new.

He had plenty of money and plenty of time and he squandered both. His father was an indulgent parent who called him his precious boy but made no effort whatever to hold him in check; nor had he any idea of the life that his son really led. Even if he had it would not have made the slightest difference to him, for he himself kept two mistresses. He was a well-known figure in banks and boudoirs and

knew how to conduct himself in either with equal facility. 

Shadd was twenty-eight and boasted that while he had never been around the world, the world had been around him. He had loved girls from almost every country of the globe right in little old New York.

Of course the Dixon girls did not know the unsavory reputation he enjoyed, yes, even relished. He used to claim that of the ten commandments, he'd broken eleven. The war had made his score complete by permitting him to kill a man in the name of humanity. Perhaps the children of the man he killed did not appreciate his efforts to uplift the world. Many acts committed in the name of humanity, must make humanity hide its head in shame. 

Much to the chagrin of Grace and Georgiana, it was Carlene to whom Shadd paid the most attention. Her unspoiled beauty was a tonic to him. Her charm, her wit, her supple slenderness, the way she wore her clothes, her disarming frankness. In his own mind he decided here was a choice field for conquest. The fact that she was only seventeen increased his enthusiasm. It was distinctly a delight to make love to a girl who he was sure was still a virgin. From his own experience, he had decided that in this modern age, virgins are almost as hard to find as four-leafed clovers. 

He laid his plans of conquest deliberately. The war had taught him strategy. He was careful not to rush her too quickly. For the first few days he

merely lingered about the porch, talked with her when occasion presented itself without deliberately seeming to pick her out. They walked a bit in the evenings about the garden and talked on various inconsequential subjects. Carlene found his conversation stimulating and interesting. Many of his observations were cynical. Some of his opinions coincided with her own. He was careful not to voice his true attitude toward sex, love or marriage. 

He had no intention of scaring her away, rather he was subtley trying to coax her to him. Not for a moment did he doubt his eventual success.

Finally he commenced driving her down to the village when she had shopping to do for her mother. 

So that it would not seem to obvious he invited some of the boarders to go along. These short drives could not have been less casual and they never aroused any thought in Carlene's mind. 

From that point it was quite easy and logical to make the rides longer. Soon he ceased to ask the other boarders to accompany them.

One afternoon they drove up to Sam's Point, the highest spot in Ulster County. Here one can gaze off for miles toward the distant peaks of the Catskills. The cliffs drop down sheer for fifty or sixty feet and then on down again. As one looks over the bare cliffs edge only the fronds of the treetops can be seen.

The vista that spreads out before one is charming, a rolling peaceful country, fields of hay and wheat, truck farms with here and there a tiny house or a few barns that seem almost to belong

in a nursery. Tiny toy-houses and little cows grazing about in the fields, a peaceful homelike picture. 

Threads of white winding roads like strips of ribbon flung about, joyous runaway roads losing themselves in the distance, off to meet the sun or the mountains of the morning.

The cliffs were deserted when they reached the summit. They had left the car when the road became too steep for them to ascend farther and finished the rest of the journey afoot. That mountain top is a strange, weird place, as level as a table with Lake Maritanza denting it in the middle, a blue lake under the soft blue sky. On the cliffs no trees grow, nothing but dwarfed and stunted bushes sweeping off in endless monotony. 

Shadd Crane drew Carlene down to a seat beside him on a natural rock-bench. "God, it's good to be alive on such a day," he cried. "A perfect day, a perfect moment, a perfect girl. As I look at your profile I imagine that you are a lovely cameo, so perfect are the lines of your features. The air is crisp and cool. Do you not hear the love songs which the treetops down there are crooning? They are singing of my love for you. 99

At her taunts, his anger flared out beyond all reason. He jerked her roughly to her feet. "God, how I want you," he cried. "And all you do is sit there cold as ice and laugh at me. But one

Some unknown force seemed to be urging her onward, forcing her to do things she did not wish to do. She had no control over her fate.

Listlessly she walked up Fifth Avenue. She simply must find work. She turned East at Twelfth Street and walked toward Third Avenue. She had no definite goal in view. She simply wanted to walk and walk, to get away from herself, to find rest and comfort. How she would have joyed to have been back once more in Ellenville, to bathe in the mornings in the sink, to lie for a few moments rest on the rock couch afterwards. But Ellenville was as far from her reach as last night's sunset. It was perhaps gone out of her life forever.

She rose every morning with a bad headache and no appetite. The very sight of food was obnoxious to her. She still had her position addressing envelopes for which she was thankful.

She didn't feel well enough to go around looking for any other sort of work. It was a period of utter gloom. There were countless things to distress her, and several unusual things were causing her mental alarm.

As Carlene walked home across the Park she felt almost as though she were living in a dream. She who was oh, so lonely was going to have a baby, a bit of flesh to love and cherish, to hold in her arms, to croon to. It would keep her from going mad.

Doctor Burns had cheered her up considerably. 

Knowledge of her condition was easier to bear than endless, everlasting fear. Once she almost sobbed as

she thought of Grant Lee. How had it been possible for him to be so vile when he had been such a perfect lover? Within her heart she still had a bit of affection left for him. Nature so constitutes a woman that she is drawn irresistibly toward the father of her child during her period of confinement. 

It is usually the period when lovers are closest, more nearly one. The child is the link that binds them. 

And Grant Lee didn't even know. He thought he had gotten rid of her forever but through this unborn child they were held together. Odd that such a thing could be.

Impulsively she decided she would not go back to her room right away. Beaulah would perhaps be home. From Carlene's expression she might suspect. Carlene was sure that her face was unnaturally flushed. Then too Mrs. Case might stop her in the hall for a chat as she frequently did. She must keep her secret locked within her. It was hers, hers alone, hers and the baby's.

She walked leisurely up to Broadway. For a moment her nerves seemed to be resting. Odd that she should not feel more upset since she had definitely Tearned that she was with child. But she was unafraid. There was a bit of the eternal mother in her. She felt a sort of divine peace, unexplainable. 

She wanted to be alone. If anyone had spoken to her at that moment she would have shrieked.

Through the dust and heat of the city she walked in beauty. She smiled at the barefooted children who ran scampering across her path.

As she reached Broadway there was a car waiting for the lights to change. It was nearly empty. At that hour the wave of traffic is mostly uptown. 

That night the voice of the city was a voice of music. It was beautiful, restful. It lulled her almost into slumber. Before she realized it the car had reached the Custom House. She alighted and walked slowly toward the Aquarium. The sight of it stirred old wounds. She remembered that happy day when she and Grant had walked about viewing the exhibits. How joyous he had been as he talked about "Poor Fish." And Grant, where was he now, where was he tonight? Some part of him was within her body. What would he say if he knew that he was soon to be a father? He mustn't know. He must never know. The child was to be hers alone, hers to love and cherish.

She turned her back on the Aquarium so that memory might be dulled and walked along the seawall toward the ferry houses. It was the hour of sunset and the water looked calm and tranquil lapping gently along the sides of little ships. A snow white bird circled low over her as she passed. Of course it was only a sea-gull, one of the countless fishhunters that haunt the water's edge but she liked to pretend that it was a fairy-bird of good omen. "Little man," she whispered, "see there is a small white dove already searching for you. He will guide your life. He will be your star." 

There were tears in her eyes, tears of happiness.

She felt as though the city had suddenly become a

Dream City, all purple and gold with the roseate hue of sunset flashing on the turrets of palaces! 

What matter that the turrets were only the steeples of stately office buildings looming up to meet the sky?

She gazed at them as through a mist, a mist of tears and sunbeams. At that moment she felt as though she were very close to God. Somewhere she had read that when a mother is carrying a child she is sitting outside the Gates of Heaven peering in at the sweet-flowing rivers and the fragrant flowers. Sometimes the view is too enticing and the mother steals within. Those of earth mourn her but she is happy with her child in that wondrous garden. 

Was it only her imagining or was there music in the wind? Was the wind blowing in from the sea singing a lullaby? She closed her eyes, afraid to move, afraid to utter a sound, afraid to breathe, for she did not wish to miss a single fragile note. 

Yes, there could be no doubt, there was music in the wind, a lullaby, a lullaby for her baby. 

As in a dream she sat as night crept in from the sea. One by one the lights of the ferries and tugboats flickered into view. Beyond her the lights flared out in the numerous offices where men were still working. The harbor became a magic place. 

The lights on the ships were fireflies dancing in a meadow. That moment was one of the happiest 

Carlene had ever experienced. And in the wind there was music. Strange emotions stirred within her. She closed her eyes. Beauty, beauty everywhere. Nothing sordid, nothing but beauty. Beauty

and a lullaby. The breeze laughed across the grass, over the sleeping form of a tramp who lay spread out at ease under the broad canopy of the sky. In his pockets there were perhaps only a few pennies but no Ceasar ever had a more magnificent, airy room in which to sleep.

A young couple passed very close to Carlene. His arm encircled her waist and they were whispering.

So absorbed were they in themselves they might have been alone in a deserted city for all the notice they took of anyone else. As they passed, the young man gazed furtively about and then quickly kissed. his companion. 

Tonight was a night of love. The sky was in love with the sea. Love. Love. All Nature seemed to feel the joy of it. And in the heart of Carlene there was a new wonder. The wonder of motherhood. A new love. The love for her coming baby.

At last she rose to her feet. The screech of a tug-boat whistle had penetrated her consciousness, had recalled her from the Hills of Dream where she had been loitering among the slumber shadows. 

Mechanically she retraced her footsteps to Broadway. She had eaten nothing since luncheon and yet she was not hungry. Neither was she tired. The sea air had been a tonic to her. She decided that she would walk leisurely back to MacDougal Street. 

Time didn't matter. Time meant nothing. Tonight she felt as though she would like to remain in the streets all night where she could turn her face toward the stars.

OMEHOW for Carlene life seemed to have sweetened. Everything looked friendly. The future was no longer dark. Her attitude would have been extremely interesting for a psychologist to ponder over. Perhaps it would have interested 

Grant Lee who was somewhat of a student. She was not crushed by knowledge of her condition. Not for a moment did she think of the possibility of changing it. This child that she was to bear had been conceived in love. It must be born in beauty.

When Carlene awakened the sun was pouring in through the bedroom windows. She gazed sleepily about her. Then she realized where she was. Hastily she rose, went into the bathroom and took a cold shower. Then she dressed once more in her own clothes. She hesitated for a moment about putting on the Chinese costume again but decided against it. 

No use trying to force issues. She had tried her best and failed. He had not come to her.
