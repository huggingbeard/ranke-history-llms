INTRODUCTION 

IN N the late 'nineties, a red-haired, freckle-faced boy, full of the spirit of adventure and faced with the test of competition, discovered a fundamental principle which shaped the destiny of his life and others associated with him. Too young to realize the significance of this discovery, he unwittingly applied the theory in his daily play and, later, to minor tasks confronting him.. 

This all happened back in Missouri, during a foot race with several other boys. Our boy was skinny and wiry, capable, no doubt, of winning a race with others his size, but he never seemed quite able to make the grade. He was thirteen then-the time of youthful romance when nearly every boy discovers his first love, and he had found his, a blue-eyed blonde of perfection. She was standing on the sidelines that day watching the race which was to determine the championship of the school. 

All the boys were lined up, ready to start. He 11

knew the girl of his dreams was there. The crowd itself was for him only a clear picture of her beautiful face. She was all he could see as the race began. Each stride brought him closer to her. His love for her seemed to urge him on. He just couldn't bear to lose that race; he must call on every ounce of strength within him to win!

For the first time in his life he did win, and with distance to spare. He could hardly believe it. He had never run so fast before. How had he managed it this time? What force was at work that enabled him to accomplish this task? Then the idea was born that has carried through his life ever since. He realized that he won the race because his love depended on it. He was to learn well, in later years, how great a factor is love. When one's effort is based on love, it rarely fails. 

It never occurred to the boy when he entered business that this same power existed there. He looked at a job as a necessary function. For many years, in order to make a living, he worked because

13 work was expected, never for the love of the thing he was doing.

It was not until love, urging him to make the work under his hand the very finest he could, again inspired him, that the lesson of the foot race came back to him-his first boyhood success and what had prompted its accomplishment. Yes, this power existed in business, too, and he would look for the things he loved to do.

Now grown to manhood, he began to analyze this power and to talk about it with his friends. 

When he finally found the business that filled his heart, he was amazed at the rapidity with which it grew. He knew now for a fact that when one's heart is harnessed to the task-even in businesssuccess is bound to come. He had tried it and found that it works.

It seemed such a simple principle. Almost anyone could apply it. So he prepared a speech on the subject of "The Third Commodity" and began making it before groups of businessmen in order

that they, too, might benefit as he had by this simple discovery.

First made some five years ago, the speech has now been delivered several hundred times to 

Rotary Clubs, Chambers of Commerce and other business organizations throughout the United States. It has met with such wholesale approval everywhere that it has been decided to make it available to a larger audience by publishing it in book form. It is given here for your benefit, and all who read it may be assured that they possess all the necessary faculties to enable them to profit as the author has done.

HAT I want to do throughout this book is to point out the amazing power that radiates from the heart. The power of the mind has been so praised and exalted among men that most of us bow down before it. 

We take it for granted that everything starts with the mind; that all progress and success originate in and develop from cold, logical reasoning. Therefore, those of us who have not been granted the gift of exceptional intelligence as most of us have not-are inclined to believe it inevitable that we shall be mediocre all the days of our lives. 

The truth is quite the contrary. Analyze a thousand men and women who have achieved great success in the usual callings and you will find that 15

some have fine intellects, minds that work quickly to sound conclusions and that foresee the consequences of a proposed course of action; but probably only a few score-say one hundred-of our sample thousand have this ability. The other nine hundred will prove to have only ordinary mentalities. 

What's more, this proposition holds true if we first pick one hundred of the greatest successes from among the thousand and analyze their mental capacities. Ten may be unusually intelligent, the other ninety just average from the neck up.

But, if we start from another direction and analyze this sample of a thousand successful people, we find all these men and women have one quality in common. Every last one of them is sincerely in love with his job. Preacher or plumber, teacher or wheat grower, artist or politician, each one believes that his is a fine calling-one of the best on earth. He sees his work as a real benefit to mankind. And his belief in his usefulness gives him

an enthusiasm that becomes contagious, affecting others. 

No matter what you are doing or where you start, there is no satisfactory accomplishment until your heart is harnessed to the task. 

Lots of people work and "get by," as we say. 

Great numbers work hard for years, busy as donkeys on treadmills, but if that love isn't there, they can't accomplish much, because an essential factor is absent. That's what I am going to try to tell you about.

I was brought up to be a minister. Yes, my parents planned it before I was born. Their ambition never wavered. I was taken to church regularly and clergymen were invited to our house. On the brightest of Sundays when the rest of the gang, even my own brothers, were out playing ball and swimming, I had to sit in the house and read the Bible. My nose was held to that religious grindstone until I was seventeen, but I did not want to be a minister!

Most of the boys in our town had youthful ambitions to be locomotive engineers, or policemen, or even President of the United States. Not me. 

You know what I wanted to be? I wanted to be a

Hot-Tamale Man. Surely that was the finest job on earth. Did you ever see a gang of twelve-yearolds grin while they quit playing ball long enough to eat hot tamales-all the tamales they had money to buy? When I was a boy, back in Missouri, that hot-tamale man came around every day. He spread happiness among the kids everywhere he went. 

That's why I thought he was grand!

After seventeen years' struggle, my parents finally admitted I would never make a good preacher; so I went on to get whatever job I could find. The first was menial work with an express company. This lasted three or four years but did not allow me to take care of a wife and baby the way I wanted to care for them. My belongings were few, principally an old typewriter. Changing from one company to another, I landed in a big city without money enough to pay expenses. First,

luxuries had to be given up. Then, because there was no money for even the necessities, a food shortage was experienced. That's one of the first effects of insufficient income-hunger. I was hungry then. 

And believe me, when a man gets hungry he really begins to think! 

Have you ever been hungry? I mean, have you ever been hungry because you did not have enough money to buy the food you need? Of course you have been hungry because a meal was an hour late, or because you got stuck somewhere on a country road, or because something happened that postponed your normal eating time. That kind of hunger does not count. That's merely an evidence of good health. But when you are hungry because you are broke, and when the hunger persists day after day because short rations continue day after day, you have your eyes opened to some of the grim fundamental truths of life. 

You hear people talking about economic fear, or you read about it in books. Some writers say it is the worst of all our fears, and the most common.

What do they mean by economic fear? They mean fear of hunger for yourself and for those dependent on you. Food is a primary necessity. Man must have it, and he must have it amply or his morale will break, and then his health will break. 

Perhaps, in the process of disintegration from hunger, he becomes what we call a dangerous man. 

If he does, I think I can understand, and to some degree sympathize, even though I may not excuse his actions.

Think your way back to the times of primitive man. Then consider the ways of Nature. Hunger is an unpleasant sensation, isn't it? Perhaps Nature made it like that so man would bestir himself immediately when his body began to need food. The need of food is a need that cannot be ignored. 

Maybe the old cave man would have died slowly of starvation if hunger had not been such a miserable sensation-more disagreeable even than the physical effort required to get food. 

Well, one day I was hungry! I stopped on a sidewalk and looked into a restaurant window, as

thousands of other hungry men have done. And as I looked I saw something-a chance for a job! 

The menu pasted on that window glass was poorly typed, messily typed. At home I had a typewriter.

Perhaps I could trade some neat typing for some sustaining food. This restaurant was immediately across the street from where I worked, so the setup seemed perfect to me. Should I try out my idea on the proprietor? Why not? The effort could not make me any hungrier than I was. 

I stirred up my courage, threw my shoulders back a little, walked into that place, asked for the proprietor, and when he appeared to be an ordinary human, not a monster, I told him that I'd like to type his bills of fare for him every day in exchange for a daily lunch. He gave me a looking over and agreed to my proposal. "When do I start?" I asked. "Wait a minute. I'll get my bill of fare for tomorrow and you can work on it right away."

That night, with the thought of a hot lunch in mind, I began work. I typed slowly, carefully,

planning the spaces, the capitalization and general appearance of the card. Over and over again that bill of fare was typed. 

Food depended on it.

The work had to be good. 

Well, sir, it did please the man. So much so that he showed it to other restaurant men near by. They decided that they, too, wanted such bills of fare, and in two days I had orders for daily menus from ten different restaurants in the neighborhood. I could not very well eat ten lunches, so I set a price of twenty-five cents per day for each of the new customers. This meager beginning was the start of a business which eventually took me away from the express company altogether. 

You understand, I wasn't in love with typing menus. No, but because I was hungry I made a beautiful job out of that first one. It was important to me to give satisfaction and I think this caused me to throw my heart into what I was doing.

The result was amazing. This menu-typing business-and who had ever heard of such a vocation as that?-grew so rapidly and substantially that with-

in two years I sold it to a man for five thousand dollars, despite the fact that its tangible assets added up to no more than four hundred and fifty. Yes, sir, I had put my heart into the simple, unexciting job, and out of it I got experience, knowledge, guidance toward my life's work, and financial profit. Suppose I had done the typing grudgingly, hating the task, and telling myself all the time that it was work for a dumb cluck, not for me. What would I have got out of it?

My association with restaurant men, as typer, printer and arranger of their bills of fare, developed into a close personal acquaintanceship with at least a hundred of them. They were fine fellows, most of them, but the feelings they had for one another were sour, and among them cut-throat methods were common. It seemed to me the trouble was due to the fact that these men did not know one another, never met around discussion tables, never pooled their experiences or developed their potential co-operative strength. That conclusion gave me another idea, so I set out to form an association

of restaurant operators. I could put my heart into that task from the outset, and I did. 

This association was a success from the beginning. After seven years it drew such favorable attention throughout the country that a general request was entered to make it a national organization. For the purpose of building this good work into a national organization, a restaurant magazine was established. Within a few months a convention was held and the national association was formed. For ten years, I served as secretary. It was this experience that drew me to the business which really filled my heart. 

Remember the hot-tamale man of school days?

Somewhere in this business of successfully feeding people was the same opportunity to spread happiness I'd seen him spreading among kids in my childhood days.

An experimental restaurant was established by the National Restaurant Association in 1925. It was to be operated for three months to determine the value of college training in restaurant work.

Six college professors and twenty-two students from some of the leading colleges took part in the experiment. It was my duty, as secretary of the association, to manage the entire affair. I was thoroughly involved in all branches of restaurant management. I participated in the baking, cooking and salad-making, as well as in the service. It was the most interesting work I had ever done. A year later, I resigned as secretary of the association when one of the country's most prominent restaurateurs offered me the opportunity of helping him run his 

Chicago business. I jumped at the chance. We did well, too. We opened for his company a new restaurant of a type that both of us had been thinking about. It made an immediate success. Now I was in a business that I whole-heartedly enjoyed. 

In September, 1930, I stood in the basement of another restaurant in Chicago and listened as the auctioneer asked: "What am I bid for this restaurant that cost sixty-five thousand dollars to build." 

There was no response. A quick look told me that of the other people present none was a res-

taurant man. Unless some stranger was acting for one, there would be no bids. In 1930 almost all business was in trouble; more enterprises were failing than were being started. People were afraid to take chances. Naturally, almost anyone with good sense would have hesitated to buy a restaurant that had housed three or four consecutive failures. But the opportunity seemed too good to miss. I had twenty-five hundred dollars, all I had saved in my working life. On the auctioneer's second call I bid twenty-five hundred dollars, and a minute later was surprised to find I was actually the sole owner of the restaurant.

It seemed unbelievable. At last I owned a business that I knew I could love. I took the news home to my wife that night. Instead of being dismayed she, too, was filled with joy. Our two children had grown up. My daughter had married and left home, so my wife suggested that it would be a real satisfaction to her to help in this new enterprise. I had not thought of that. But why not? Home duties no longer required her full time or interest.

Perhaps she needed a new job to keep employed the great abilities she had originally possessed and steadily increased as a homemaker. Energetic and able women can't idle away their time and also retain their happiness. Yes, we decided it would be a good idea and it was; one of the best I ever had sense enough to accept from any other person. 

She made her own place in the business, worked into it naturally, and that place was keeping the books and caring for the money. Because she handled the payroll she became acquainted with all of the twenty-five employees we began with. A short time later she was calling them by their first names, and after that she began to mother them in a sensible, not a sentimental, way. She did her work so well that I was left free to concentrate on food and service, which suited me exactly. Thus "Mom" and I started in a business we both loved; and thus Mom and I continued an active, equal partnership we had started when we were married. 

Today our enterprise occupies all four floors of the building in which we operate. We have one hun-

dred and twenty-five people on our staff, and our restaurant is widely known as one of Chicago's best, if I do say it myself. I don't mean to brag. 

I'm just thankful.

Both Mom and I believe that this quick rise to success has been due to the third commodity we put into our business. That commodity is devotion. 

We have discovered something. Our discovery has been fine for us, and we believe it will be fine for others. Therefore, in humility but with zest and confidence we write this book to offer to you who read it a usable "recipe" for the third commodity, without which there cannot be much satisfaction in work, or in life.

OMMODITIES, according to the dictionary, are articles of commerce. Everyone, whether he works for an employer or is in business for himself, necessarily has two commodities for sale. First is the article, and second the service or activity of delivering that article. The first commodity of a shoe store, for instance, is shoes; the second commodity is the service, or the activity of fitting those shoes to feet in order that the shoes, the first commodity, may be used properly.

The third commodity seems to exist only among the leaders of businesses, professions and trades. I do not know a better way to define the third commodity than to refer to it as a sort of business per- 29

sonality. It's really an excuse for existence in business. 

I wonder what your answer would be if I should ask you these questions: Why are you operating a business? Why, Mr. Professional Man, do you seek clientele? What, Mr. Employee, is your reason for being on the company's payroll?

It's very likely that the honesty in most of you would prompt you to answer: "Why, for profit.”

Well, that's a practical reason, but it's hardly a justifiable one, if it stands alone. To ask business patronage or seek professional clientele, or even to be on the payroll just for the selfish reason of your own profit, is not enough to carry you to any satisfying achievement. There must be something more than that—something more than the item that is sold, and also something more than the mere physical activity involved in acquiring it from the producer, and handing it on to the purchaser. The public to which you cater is not seriously interested in the money profits you make, and will not be guided to your doorstep merely to assist you to pile

up such profits. The public is interested in what it gets for the money it spends, and it goes to the place where it gets the goods or services it desires, plus something else.

I have a friend who often drives to Florida in winter, and en route there and en route back he always spends a night, and eats at least two meals, at a small, cheap-looking hotel in a small uninteresting town. He still does this, even though the newest and fastest highway does not now pass through the town. When a motorist goes out of his way, the lure must be strong, for motorists are an impatient lot. I'll let this man explain why he and his family always turn off for that little hotel. "The first time I drove south with my family I had no idea where to stop for the night. The indecision caused me to drive too late in the evening, and as a result both the small children were fretting. The next large town was twenty miles on, but a small town was nearer and in that town I found a hotel. 

The appearance of the place discouraged me, but we stopped anyway and asked for dinner.

"The proprietor said the dining room had been closed an hour, but he would see what he could do.

And he did plenty! That was the best meal we had on the trip and there were special dishes for the children. 'I saw the children were hungry,' the proprietor explained, 'so I asked my wife to come down and lend a hand in the kitchen.' "Next morning we had a good breakfast, and, as we were leaving, the proprietor's wife called the children to her and gave each of them a little package of food to eat as we drove along. All that for few dollars." a very

Now do you wonder that my friend always stops at that hotel? The stop there has become an enjoyable event for him and his family. And they have told many other people about it, some of whom have learned for themselves that the proprietor and his wife give what I call the third commodity. Many other hotels have better rooms, better beds and, perhaps, better food. But this one has that something extra, that something plus. 

Do you like maple syrup? If you do, and if you

buy yours from a variety of places, probably you have found some of it better than the rest. There may be several factors explaining the difference. 

First-run sap and second-run sap do not give identical syrups. And so maple-syrup fans have told me-sap from trees growing on the north side of mountains cooks down into better syrup (or worse, I've forgotten which it is) than sap from trees on the south side of the same mountain. And trees that grow in shallow soil on top of granite are supposed to give sap of some special quality. Perhaps these factors and others affect the final quality and taste of the syrup, but there's another factor, an essential ingredient without which there can be no best-grade syrup, and that is the care with which it is made.

Last summer a friend of mine, while visiting in New England, decided he would buy a supply of the syrup directly from one of the many makers of it there. One day as he and his host were driving a country road, he saw a sign "Maple Syrup for 

Sale." There are, of course, hundreds of such signs.

"Let's stop a minute," my friend suggested. "I want to buy some syrup from this farmer." "Maybe you want to buy some syrup,” his host said, "but not from that farmer." "What's the matter with him?” "He's all right, but his syrup isn't." "Why isn't it as good as any?" "Because neither he nor his wife is interested in making good syrup." "I don't care whether they are personally interested in the process of cooking maple sap or not," my friend remarked. "All I'm interested in is the syrup. If it's good, I'll forgive him his dislike of his job." "But good syrup can't be made by people who do not like to make it," said the host. "It has something more than boiled-down sap in it. There's art in it, or skill, or something that comes from the maker and not from the tree." 

And there you have it again—the third commodity is essential. Without it, somebody will be gypped, and the most likely victim is the fellow

who fails to supply that third commodity, that something plus.

The old story of the three stonemasons is to the point. The first, asked what he was doing, said: "Earning my wages." The second said: "Laying stones." The third: "Building a cathedral.” The first two men were delivering the two commodities necessary for holding their jobs; the last man was giving the third commodity along with the bare essentials. And, in the natural course of events, delivering this extra value for the same pay as the others probably brought him a promotion or the chance to set up in business for himself as a builder. 

But suppose it did not bring him either a raise in pay, or head him toward a business of his owndid he lose anything by giving that something plus to the work he was doing? Certainly not! He gained, and what he gained was satisfaction. He had a good time at his work.

One of the best talks I have heard was delivered to a group of schoolteachers in a state where schoolteacher pay is low, and where it cannot be really

high, because the public hasn't the money. The speaker admitted the pay was too low, even for that state. He said he wished all schoolteachers were paid whopping salaries. In this I agree with him, but the importance of work to society is not always reflected in the pay check. "You teachers," said the speaker, "have a choice to make. All of you want, need and deserve more money. But your choice is not between what you are getting and a larger income. There isn't any way at present for you to be paid what you are worth as teachers. Neither are there other jobs open for most of you. Nevertheless, you have an important decision to make: Will you have a good time teaching, or a poor time? Will you go at it with zest, or with boredom, or with actual detestation? That's the choice you can make. The money you receive may not be increased or decreased by your decision. But the fun you get, the satisfaction you experience, and even your health will increase and improve if you decide to teach with zest, to put your heart into it!

"How can an educated person, how can any sensible person," asked the speaker, "justify an unintelligent attitude toward the work he or she must do? And isn't it unintelligent to make a task a drudge when it might be a stimulating adventure? 

Isn't it nonsensical to poison your mind, and maybe your body, by detestation when you might sweeten both by devotion to what you are doing? Work is easier, not harder, when one puts one's heart in it.”

All of us who are grown up have had a number of teachers. That is, we have sat in classrooms presided over by a number of persons. But it is a lucky man who has had two or three real teachers. 

Think back to your school days. You were not impressed by all your teachers, were you? But one, or two, or three stand out from the others, and know that they have influenced you. What sets them apart? Probably they did not know more than the teachers you have forgotten. Perhaps they had less impressive degrees than some of those you have forgotten. But they had something the others did not have-they had love for their work. you

No teacher can teach a student much, but a real teacher can inspire him to learn much, and only a real teacher-one who has his heart in it-can do that. Inspiration does not proceed from those who are bored.

Call it an ideal, call it love for the job, call it business personality, call it pep, call it whatever comes to your mind, and still you will be forced to define it as the extra factor or value that comes from putting your heart in your occupation. It is this something plus that raises an individual above his fellows, and sets him up as a leader in his field. 

This is true whether his field is business, a profession, common labor or sport.

How this third commodity shows itself depends on the individual's special leanings and on the particular situation that he faces in his life. You remember, a few years ago, the moving-picture industry presented a star known as the "It" girl. She was well named. When she came into a room, there seemed something in the air that told you an unusual person was present. Her personality vi-

brated into every inch of space. It could be felt, too, by an audience watching her performance on the screen. A perfect expression of the third commodity.

When the result of your business activity reaches this point you may well feel that you are applying the third commodity properly. All who meet you will immediately sense your business personality and even in that first step you will be giving them something that cannot be duplicated by someone else. When such personality or commercial "It" develops into a service to mankind you are "on your way to town.”

It is frequently true that a person who falls short is to blame only for picking the wrong occupation. 

Let me illustrate:

About twenty years ago I was publishing a small monthly magazine. There was nothing pretentious about our company. It was a tiny enterprise with five of us crowded about our desks and tables in one small room. We had an artist who made illusrations for advertising copy and comic squibs for

the editorial pages. His name was Walter. I remember one day some advertising copy was sent down to a coffee firm for approval. It carried a drawing of two people sitting at a table drinking coffee. Walter had made it. I shall never forget the notation put on this copy by the advertising manager of the firm. It read: "Ye gods, what crimes are committed in the name of art!" I called

Walter over and showed it to him. "You ought to be ashamed." I told him, "You can do better than that." "I am ashamed," he admitted. "Why don't you do better work, Walter?" "I'm doing the best I can." your "That's the first time I ever knew anybody to defend his best work by that excuse," I pursued. "You could do better if had you heart in your job—you just don't seem to be interested. I've often seen you playing with a little pack of cards and making marks on them. Why don't you make better drawings, Walter, instead of fooling around with those cards all the time?"

At mention of the cards the boy's expression brightened. He beamed with enthusiasm and seemed to speak clear from his heart when he said, "Oh, those cards! Here, I'll show you what I'm doing!" He dug down into his pocket and brought up a little pack of plain white cards held together with a rubber band. On the top card he had drawn a rabbit. He held the deck before my eyes and flipped the cards with his thumb. As I looked down into the crevices between the moving cards, the rabbit seemed to jump up and run away. It was amusing, but it wasn't what I had paid him to do. 

A little nettled, I said: "Well, of all the gadgets for an eighteen-year-old boy to be playing with!" 

His face turned red. "This isn't a gadget-it's my ideal!" 

Such enthusiasm overpowered reproof. Before

I knew it he was bubbling over about the movingpicture machine in his garage at home and how he was working all hours of the night to perfect it so he could produce a clear motion picture of those cards on the screen.

"When I get this thing working," he went on, "I'm going to be able to make movies out of drawings, and in those drawings tell the stories that have been written for children. Children, and grownups, too, will understand these stories and enjoy them as they never could by just reading them in books. You see, Mr. Carder, these pictures will do things you can't do with a movie camera now—and it's the one thing I want to do more than anything else on earth." 

Yes, you've guessed it. That young artist was 

Walt Disney, eighteen years old then, and tied to a job that did not really interest him. He was doing his job the best he could-for wages. But down in his heart he was working at something else and for something else. You who read this know the rest of the story. If your opinion of the man is the same as mine, you must regard him as one of the great Americans of our time. 

Well, now, what's the difference between Walt

Disney today and the young artist who worked for the wages I paid him? Simply this: that youngster

found an ideal and built it into a business with the kind of personality I call the third commodity.

Without knowing much about movie profits, I am guessing that Snow White made more money for 

Walt Disney than all the pot-boiler cartoons ever earned for men who ground them out for profit alone. Why? Because the third commodity always pays better than the two bare essentials of a sale. 

Just about the time of my experience with Walt

Disney, or perhaps a few years before, another man started making funny-looking things. His one desire was to manufacture a motor vehicle at a price within reach of the masses. He felt that, if peoplethe common people could buy it, they would make better use of it than the rich men who were buying it for a plaything. He knew what he wanted to do and he did it. He made it possible for men of low incomes to have automobiles. In the process, he became one of the world's richest men. 

Maybe Walt Disney and Henry Ford did think of profit when they started. It's only human to think of making money. But I can't believe that

money was their guiding principle. Neither of them could have imagined the amount of money he has since made. If either thought of money at the outset, it was because he felt he could do his job better if he was not cramped for cash. Did that stop him? Not a bit of it. He wanted with all his heart-with real heart interest-to do something he felt would benefit mankind. And each of them, today, gets his greatest happiness, not out of his wealth, but out of achieving what he wanted to achieve.

One who knows Mr. Ford well, as it is my good fortune to know Mr. Disney, has told me that 

America's most interesting industrialist rarely thinks or talks of money. His mind always is on, and his heart always in, something he is doing. 

Newspaper and magazine interviewers learned years ago that it is impossible to get a good interview from Mr. Ford on a subject that is not, at the moment, "hot" in his mind. If he isn't interested, he just isn't interested at all. But if he is interested, the interest is whole-hearted. Nothing is more

characteristic of Mr. Ford than his singleness of purpose. By that I mean that when he is planning a new engine, he is doing it with all his heart; when he is adding something to his Greenfield Village, that task claims all his attention at the moment; when he is experimenting with tung trees and other plantings on his plantation in Georgia, that is the thing of consuming interest.

Whatever he does, he does with all his faculties, and while he is doing it, so his friends have told me, he is having a spirited, delightful time. Read what is written about him, and you will realize that throughout his life this has been a major theme: 

Most persons have abilities to do some things better than they can do other things, and every person who wishes to do his best must find the work he likes, for a man does best what he likes best to do. 

Isn't that the same idea I have been presenting?

Faith in human possibilities. Faith in life, and in one's ability to fit into it somewhere, sometime. 

Jacob Riis, the immigrant who became such a splendid American thus describes, in his book The Making of an American, his landing at New York: "It was a beautiful spring morning, and as I looked over the rail at the miles of straight streets, the green heights of Brooklyn, and the stir of ferry boats and pleasure craft on the river, my hopes rose high that somewhere in this teeming hive there would be a place for me. What kind of a place I had myself no clear notion of. I would let that

The love of change belongs to youth, and I meant to take a hand in things as they came along. That's my thesis! Riis had not, at that time, found the work he loved best to do, but he had faith that it existed, and that he would find it, and he did. 

Every reader of this book who is on the maturer side of forty remembers Jacob Riis, the writer, the lecturer, the tireless, effective worker for improvement of conditions for the poor. He was an idealist.

Gladys started working for us as a receptionist at a depression wage, and she took to the work from the very beginning. Before she came to us she had been a stenographer and after a while I made her my secretary. She remained in that position for several years, developing steadily and always showing deeper and deeper interest in the business. Naturally, inevitably, she saw the possibilities ahead for our business and just as naturally, just as inevitably, we saw the possibilities ahead for her in it. 

It came easily, for her work brought her into direct contact with the important things going on.

Her ability drew broader duties; soon she was helping the manager with many of his responsibilities. 

When the time came for an extensive expansion, Gladys was the logical person to become assistant manager. Today she is manager of our Los Angeles restaurant. She handles all our personnel work there, has complete charge of all service, hires all

Any work, anywhere, necessarily has its less pleasant tasks. In its course there are many calls to do things that cannot be enjoyable. There are days when everything goes wrong. That's when it's easy to throw in the sponge, to give up, to quit. But troubles rarely are serious. The person who knows how to put them in their proper position of unimportance comes out from under them successfully. 

What do I mean by "proper position of unimportance"? Simply this: if you are really working with interest from the heart the few unpleasant things will certainly not be magnified. You will soon

It may occur to you that the experiences of screen stars are not applicable to ordinary life, but they are. There never has been an actor who achieved greatness on the stage or the screen who did not throw himself whole-heartedly into his work; and that's what I have been talking about all through this book. You will find that whole-heartedness pays in the professions, in the arts, in big business and in little business, and in sports. Usually it pays off in money; always in satisfaction. 

You know what made Bob Feller one of the greatest pitchers in baseball, don't you? He became great on the mound because his dad put his heart into the job of teaching Bob how to throw a ball, and because Bob caught his dad's love for the game, and put his own heart into it. Every great player, no matter what the game may be, puts his heart into it. 

You remember Babe Ruth's playing days, of

course. There used to be a saying that a strike-out by Babe Ruth was worth a home run by most of the other players. Why? Because whatever Ruth did, he did with all his might-with all his heart. 

When he swung, he swung and when he missed, he missed! Everything he did was a thrill to the crowd. A fly ball hit to him in right field caused excitement because Ruth went after it so mightily. 

Once I asked a baseball man why Ruth was always such a box-office attraction. "Because," he replied, "the Babe makes each play as if his job depended on it. Watch him go after even an easy pop fly, watch him throw, watch him run, watch him bat, and watch him razz the other fellows. Then you will understand why he draws the crowds, and gets what he gets. It is because he gives everything he's got."

All the great players who are also great attractions are like that. They never hold out on the game.

One afternoon I was watching a football practice session. One of the players looked like an All-

American to me. He was big, strong, fast and fancy. But a friend who was standing with me told me the boy never would be used except in case of necessity. "Why?" I asked. "Because he does not like the game enough."

Winners like what they are doing. They must, or they can't keep on winning. That is true in sport, and in business. If you have been working many years, try to recall to mind your first associates, and then locate them in their present tasks. It is true, isn't it, that the men and the women who had the pep, the zest, the desire to do what they were doing are better off now than those who used to say, "Aw, what's the use?"

How did they manage to hold on, and to grow,

while others did less well? You will discover many reasons, but one of them will surely be this: the survivors eagerly wanted to survive in the businesses they were in and still are in. That one reason-their liking for their work-may be the main explanation.

The child likes to learn when there is no harsh compulsion, and no sour boredom associated with study.

I am not, however, advocating that a man take only the easy ways of life. All I am trying to set forth is

Of course, we all recognize that we ought to do our duty, and we all know there are times when duty's demands are not pleasant. But as a rule that is not true. As a rule, duty does not demand that we perform distasteful tasks. To attempt to do something distasteful when no moral principle is involved, is likely to result in disappointments all around.

How could it be otherwise, when half of our energy has been lost in contending against ourselves? The wise thing is to blend duty and desire together, mold them into a single purpose. This will, of course, eliminate all conflict between them.

Therefore, before turning one's back on duty, it is well to examine her face and see if she may not bear considerable resemblance to desire and if one may not, after all, learn to love her. 

Some related desire can sweeten duty when sweetening is needed. There is the desire to succeed. The desire to be a useful and commendable

member of the community. The desire for a share of the good things of life. People who discourage their own ambitions by denying themselves all luxuries may discover that they have slowed down their progress, and in so doing have lessened their incentive to work.

But of all the phases of desire which help one along the path of duty, there is nothing that can beat a genuine affection for somebody to whom your success will mean gratification and happiness-the devoted parents whose pride in your achievements you like to satisfy, the friends who are interested in you, and above all, the wife and children for whose sake you would gladly undertake any kind of task.

They find a tremendous inspiration in the desire for a lady's favor.

Many of the older generations were kept so far from their desires that they were finally weaned completely away from them.

This was most unfortunate, as nothing is so perfect a clue to our real selves as our desires. We are what we desire and if we truly express ourselves in life, it must be along the line of our desires. 

People who are indifferent and listless are unlikely

ever to accomplish anything worth while. They are in reality sick at heart.

They went blind because they could no longer bear to look on the horrors of war, or became paralyzed, with no injury to muscle or nerve, because they could go no farther along the paths of the inferno.

The complete mastery of anything, a perfect understanding of it, and competence in handling it, produces a mental and emotional satisfaction that may often turn into a delight. HAVE said much about loving the work you are doing, and have told various true stories of widely differing persons in an effort to make clear the big discovery of my life—the spiritual and practical value of devotion to occupation. Now I wish to show how this devotion, how this third commodity may be applied in a business enterprise. To do this I must ask your permission to refer again to the restaurant business which is, after all, the natural business for me to talk about, and the one I am qualified to talk about, because it is mine. 

The first commodity in this business is food. The second is service. It is necessary, of course, that these two be satisfactory to customers. It would be 142

folly for any restaurateur to attempt to succeed, except meagerly, without the third. Almost any person can go through the motions of any business, and a considerable number of persons can manage to hang on after a fashion. But when a business zooms along you will find at the head of it a man of whom people say: "He was cut out for that work!" 

Isn't that true? Think over the restaurants you are best acquainted with in your community. Some of them are doing well, and some are not doing well. What makes the difference? It is not in the locations necessarily, nor in the adequacy of the physical layout. The final explanation-I think you will find this true if you investigate—is the zest, or the business personality, or some other intangible quality, possessed by the heads of the zooming enterprises and lacked by the heads of the others. Out of acquaintance with hundreds of men and women in the restaurant business, I can tell you positively that it is the third commodity which makes the difference between failure and gratifying progress.

Every restaurateur who blends and dispenses all three of the essential commodities, knows this: Any restaurant, even at its best, is only a substitute for home. 

These words do not look very important, do they?

But let's think them over and attempt to find out exactly what they mean. 

Edgar Guest has said: "It takes a heap o' livin' in a house t' make it home." I think we all have seen the expensive mansion on the hilltop where money was lavished without restraint. It contained costly draperies, expensive rugs, fine furniture and marble statues; yet it seemed to lack something. As you entered this palatial abode there was a cold feeling. 

Something was missing-something, apparently, money could not buy. That something was homelike atmosphere. 

And I think all of us have seen the humble little cottage down near the railroad track, of just two or three rooms, where, perhaps, a laborer and his family were spending a very happy life. It contained much homemade furniture, possibly a rag

carpet and some comfortable old wicker rocking chairs, a hand-painted job of decoration and, instead of marble statues standing high on pedestals, only a china cat on the mantelpiece. Yet there was warmth there. When you entered the front door, you felt it immediately. Something was in the air that pleased you. It was homelike atmosphere. 

There was love in the air: the love of these people for each other, and their love for the home in which they were really living. Yes, and love of the very furnishings, some of which were made with their own hands, or purchased with money that had not come easily and had been put to "a heap o' livin'." 

Even around the little ten-cent china cat on the mantelpiece there radiated sentiment, perhaps because it was a present from one of the children, maybe the first a child had bought with his own money.

In your motor travels, you have no doubt wondered at the great difference in the appearance of roadside dwellings. Some of them are characterless, some are almost repellent, and some invite you

to stop and come in. The differing effects of the home places on the passer-by is not explained by the greater cost of some and the lesser cost of others, nor is it explained wholly by architectural perfections or imperfections. No, there's something else to be considered. That something else, that quality which makes a house inviting or uninviting, lies, I believe, in the residents and through them it becomes manifest in the residence and its surroundings. Where a family loves its home, the love takes visible form in the beauty of the dwelling, and in what I cannot describe other than as general atmosphere and charm. But where the home is not loved by the family, the appearance of the house is likely to be uninviting, no matter how much money that family may have.

So, you see, when a restaurant adopts the plan of substituting for a home, it takes on itself a real job. 

A house becomes a home when it is filled with love and the manifestations of love; any house will become a home under such conditions. Therefore, the restaurant that would perform certain functions of

the home must offer more than just food and the serving of food. Let's see how this can be done. 

First, take an ordinary job in a restaurant; the receptionist's job will do. The receptionist is the first employee you see when you enter, for she is the girl who meets you at the front door. Well-regulated restaurants require of her certain qualifications. She must be attractive, easy to look at, and cheerful. She should be a little taller than the other girls, so that she may be easily seen in the crowded dining room. Her background is important. She should have some high-school education and should reflect good home training. To find such a girl perhaps twenty-five or more may have to be interviewed. When one is selected she receives, direct from the proprietor or manager, instructions like these:: "The position we want you to fill has peculiar requirements, and I think the best way to explain them is to ask you to visualize, or make believe, so to speak, that I am a very dear friend of yours, and as a dear friend you have invited me to your house

for dinner tonight at six o'clock. Your regard for me is so great that you got up early this morning and went to market. Yes, you went to pick out with your own hands the food you want to prepare for this unusual dinner. You selected only the things you knew best how to prepare. You picked, also, those things I would like best. You went home with your armful of supplies, and all day today you have been completely absorbed in the task of preparing this dinner, working in the hot kitchen, taking care of this and that, to be sure that nothing would be overlooked to make the meal a complete success. Then, after you had set the table and adjusted the flowers, the doorbell rang. You looked up and saw it was six o'clock, the hour for me to arrive. Neither is the hand-shaking answer right. The restaurant man himself usually has to give the answer and generally it is like this: "No matter what would do or say, you after you had been working all day as I described, preparing this meal, your heart would speak a welcome to the one for whom you had prepared it. You see, with this simple little example, one employee (perhaps a very important one) has been given the initial training in expressing the third commodity in her daily work. 

Another instance is the training of waitresses. 

Each selection is something like that of the receptionist except that it differs as to the girl's size. Most successful restaurateurs have found that a small

girl, not too heavy, makes the best waitress. She gets around quickly and can move with ease in narrower spaces. It has been said from the stage that the purpose of the small waitress is to make the portions look large. I hardly think that this would influence many restaurateurs in their selections! They generally pick girls not to exceed five feet three or four inches, and to weigh not more than one hundred and twenty pounds. Many confine the age limit for a student waitress to twenty years. Some restaurants, of course, employ experienced waitresses, but most employers prefer an inexperienced girl whom they can train themselves, as they are fearful that the other fellow's training may not fit a waitress to express the restaurant's policy of the third commodity.

A student waitress is given simple work to do, such as helping with the setups, passing rolls or salads or relishes, sometimes pouring water for guests, during the period when she understudies the instructor. This is all done to accustom her to work around people; it eliminates stage fright and gen-

erally prevents her from spilling soup down the neck of the first customer she waits on when she becomes a full-fledged waitress.

When this period of instruction has been completed (its length varies in different restaurants) she is given one or, generally, two tables as her station. She is taken to the section of the room where these two tables are located and is told by the proprietor, manager or service supervisor, that they represent her own home. She is asked to draw an imaginary line around them and to realize that whenever a person passes this line he is entering her home and immediately becomes her personal guest. She is told to be governed by this principle in everything she does. "Treat that customer as you would a guest in your own home." You would be surprised, I know, how much this covers. Any successful restaurateur will tell you that the cases are rare indeed when a waitress, trained in this manner and imbued with the spirit of this ideal, will fail to make patrons feel at home.

It is with equal importance that the third commodity exists in the kitchen. There is a very great difference between making a batch of soup for profit and attempting to make a pot of soup a masterpiece. The restaurant guided by the spirit of profit will have a stock-pot on top of the stove all day, into which the cook throws various odds and ends as the preparation of the meal progresses. 

Cooks become so adept at throwing things into the stock-pot that they can hit it from any angle in the kitchen. One time, perhaps, it is a ham bone. 

The next shot is a lamb shank. Then a few little odd pieces of beef, and so on. Of course the contents of the soup pot reflect exactly this careless attitude. And that's the reason, perhaps, why every soup tastes practically the same, in some restaurants, regardless of the name it travels under. 

Now, let's look at the preparation of a pot of soup where the third commodity is involved. As we said before, the restaurant at best is only a substitute for home. The cook is trained to know that the very health of the restaurant's guests depends

on her care and devotion to the idea of cooking. 

She has been trained to cook at home, she has probably cooked for her own children, and you don't need to think for one minute that she would ever prepare anything for them that was not completely wholesome! Her training has taught her better. 

To prepare the soup, she must first make her stock, and the ingredients of the stock-pot depend on the kind of soup she wants to make. If it is that most commonly known, vegetable soup, a beef shank must be secured and allowed to simmer slowly for many hours. In most cases, it is left on top of the stove and watched during the entire night, to be ready for the morning cook when she arrives.

With this stock the cook begins her work of art. 

Each ingredient is prepared carefully and added to the stock according to the time required to cook it.

It is not an easy job to produce a good bowl of soup. Much care must be taken in its preparation: care that must be given to each operation. But the proper cook does not object to this, for she is pro-

ducing one of the finest examples of her art that she can offer as evidence of her ability. 

You can see, as we go along in our analysis of the third commodity applied to or used in a particular business, that it proves to be equally important in all departments. It reaches into every phase of the work. Even the dishwashers can be taught to realize that the fine products purchased by the restaurant, the care taken with the preparation and transforming of these ingredients into finished products, the expedient and careful service of the waitresses, all will be in vain if the dish on which the food is served is not spotlessly clean and sanitary. 

The members of many dishwashing departments throughout the country feel that their work is most important of all. I know that dishwashing doesn't sound like an attractive occupation, but many of you readers would be surprised to see the modern facilities for washing and sterilizing dishes, and to learn of the satisfaction of some of the people working with these machines. They realize the importance of their jobs. Yes, they, too, are dis-

pensing the third commodity. You would know it very soon if they ever failed to do it. For, when thousands upon thousands of dishes are washed daily, it would be a very easy matter to slip up and let some go by half-done. Let's all hope that this will be the last place under the sun where the third commodity fails.

The proper use of the third commodity requires much more than even the most diligent training of the employees. They must understand the reasons behind the training, and must acquire, if they do not come equipped with it, an interest in the work that will guard them against making errors, and, what is of equal or more importance, prompt them to do the correct thing when unpredictable conditions are met. But they will not have, or maintain, this essential interest in their jobs, unless the boss himself has it. And he can't fake it, either.

Sometimes he tries to fake it and, to conceal his own defects as a manager, insists on obedience to rules, even to the point of absurdity. And that suggests an amusing story, illustrative of over-training.

The proprietor of the restaurant in which the episode occurred had decided to make all his waitresses salesgirls. They would not only serve the food, they would sell it. This would enable him to prepare certain food features in quantity and dispose of them profitably. Before beginning the new method, he made elaborate preparations. He brought in speakers to address his waitresses on the art of selling. He had personal conferences with the waitresses, and had them speak their pieces in presence and in the and in the presence of others. All of this was to get them in perfect training to sell whatever he told them to sell. He was eager for profits. his One day, after the selling plan had gone into effect, he was startled when he heard a customer break into unrestrained guffaws. He looked toward the man who was laughing, and so did everyone else in the restaurant, but there was no apparent explanation of the noisy mirth. He was seated alone at the table, was looking at nobody, reading nothing, saying nothing. But he was laughing.

The proprietor naturally wanted to go over and see what the fun was about, but he held back. 

After all, if a fellow wants to laugh that's his business. If he is making an unpleasant disturbance, that is something else again. But the proprietor hung around, hoping to learn what had happened. 

In a few minutes the guest left the table and came over to the counter to pay the check. Probably he was a little abashed at having been so conspicuous. 

Anyway, he opened up, himself. "I suppose you thought it was funny," he said to the proprietor, "the way I was laughing." "Well, yes," answered the proprietor, “it did look strange to see a man having so much fun all by himself. What happened?” "I know it looked queer," the customer admitted, "but I could not stop the outburst after what the waitress said to me." 

That made the boss uneasy. What had the waitress said? He had spent money to train her to say only the correct things. "I had finished the main course of my lunch,"

the customer continued, "and was wondering what to have for dessert. I turned to the waitress standing at my table and said, 'What would you suggest?' At this question her face beamed with enthusiasm. She seemed to speak clear from the heart when she said, 'Oh, Nesselrode Pudding.' 'All right,' I said, 'bring me an order.' Came the answer: 'I'm sorry, sir, but we're out of that.""

The waitress, you see, had been over-trained in the technique of selling, but under-trained in genuine interest in her work. Before the lunch hour she had been instructed to sell Nesselrode Pudding.

The instruction was dingdonged at her until she repeated it automatically, like a talking robot, not like an interested human being. Her heart was not in her work, and her mind was not on it. There was "nobody home," and the real fault was the boss's because he had put on pressure to sell the selected feature and not to inspire interest. His own proper interest was wanting. Pressure is no substitute for intelligent, alert interest. Nothing is.

The controlling idea in any restaurant where the third commodity is appreciated is to serve the customers as they wish to be served, and what they wish to be served. That is vastly more important than spot selling of selected dishes. There is some extra and immediate profit, of course, in selling hundreds or thousands of orders of the same item; but that extra profit is of little importance when compared with the sustained profit that comes from pleasing customers day after day, week after week, month after month, and year after year. It is amazing to me that some food dispensers appear never to learn this obvious fact. 

One of my friends is allergic to onions. If not physiologically so, he is mentally so. Anyway, onion, even in tiny flavoring portions, makes him definitely uncomfortable. He says that, for him, there is no such thing as a little onion. Any at all is much too much. Many persons have similar food idiosyncracies, recognized as real by the medical profession, I believe, and also by intelligent servers

of food. But my anti-onion friend has told me that dozens of times he has asked if certain dishes were prepared without onion, and being assured they were free from that ingredient he has ordered them, to learn later, or immediately, that they did contain onion. Now, what is the justification for treating a customer like that? Such treatment makes him unhappy and gives him an unfriendly, distrustful attitude. His disposition, henceforth, is to stay out of that place. The sale of the unwanted dish has proved a costly, not a profitable sale.

The restaurant proprietor, or the chef, or the headwaiter may think it foolishness for a man to say he cannot eat any kind of wholesome food; and it may be foolish in some cases. But it is not the business of the proprietor, or the chef, or the headwaiter, or anybody in any restaurant to pass judgment on the customer's digestion or on his food prejudices. Restaurant keepers are not supposed to be reformers of their customers. Their business is to please the man who comes in to buy the food

they prepare and offer; or to admit they cannot give him what he desires. Any restaurant governed by the principle I describe as the third commodity will please him, or admit frankly and without shame that it does not have what he is seeking. 

Of course, restaurant men encounter some of the most remarkable cranks, and some of the most unbelievable food preferences and prejudices, but that only makes the business more interesting. 

Every night there are entertaining stories to be told of the day's adventures. I remember reading an interview with a restaurant man who told a story of a customer who nearly knocked a waitress over when he ordered for dessert a dish of ice cream with mayonnaise on top of it. She took the order to the kitchen, swore to an astonished attendant she had not misunderstood what the gentleman had said, got her dish of ice cream with mayonnaise on top, and served it to the customer without question, comment or so much as a lifted eyebrow. "Ah!" said he. "I see you give a man what he

asks for and do not argue with him. I'll come again."

All kinds of people with all kinds of taste make the world interesting, and they make the restaurant business especially interesting. I'm crazy about it!

Chapter 10  

HE darkest shadow that hangs over most persons is the fear of adversity. They see others lose jobs, lose health, even lose loved ones on whom happiness has depended, and they live in dread that fate will someday deal them a hand from the bottom of the deck. The dread of that possibility spoils much of the pleasure they should be getting from their good fortune today.

No one can argue sensibly that adversity is pleasant. But there is a hopeful side to adversity, even though sometimes years must pass before he who has been hit hard can gain the perspective to realize it. I have heard it said that any misfortune, barring death and incurable insanity, may be turned some- 164

how to a useful purpose. I do not mean that the compensation for misfortune is always great enough to balance the suffering caused by misfortune, but I do believe that in nearly every instance something fine may be salvaged from misfortune.

Years hence students of business will be saying that many of the most successful enterprises of their time resulted directly from the depression that began in 1929. Mind you, I am not advocating bigger and better panics! My point, at the moment, is simply this: when disaster comes salvage as much as possible from what it leaves, and learn as many as possible of the lessons it teaches. Strength is developed by a proper meeting of adversity.

Here was a man who had held a position for many years as a business executive when his company failed, he was thrown out of work, his savings were almost swept away, and he was nearing old age. All he had left was his home. You know what the prospects were, in 1931, for anyone to find a new job! Fortunately, this apparently un-

fortunate man had a hobby-woodworking. He had in his basement some fine woodworking machinery. The craze for jigsaw puzzles was on, and he whiled away time putting them together. In fitting the pieces he noticed what seemed poor workmanship in many of the puzzles people were buying. He believed he could make better ones, himself, but they would be expensive because he was not equipped for mass production. 

At this time his son returned from college, with no job in sight. Father and son had plenty of time to mope around. Instead, they experimented with the making and marketing of jigsaw puzzles. The father did the making. The son did the selling from door to door in the more prosperous streets.

What he had was too high priced for ordinary folks. But the boy picked his prospects and made his sales. At times he sold faster than his father could turn the puzzles out, and then for a few days both would concentrate on production in the home basement. After a few weeks it became necessary for them to spend nearly all their time in the base-

ment. Re-orders were flooding in from the delighted customers, and from friends to whom these customers had talked, and to whom they had shown the superior puzzles. With the use of ingenuity, skill and courage, these two men earned—and I mean earned a good living for their family when depression was at its worst. 

When conditions grew a little better, the father looked around, found a small factory for sale at a low price, borrowed money and bought it. Ever since he took it over the business has done well. 

Today he is making more money than he made in pre-depression years, and having the happiest time he ever had. Meanwhile, the son continued to make puzzles until he found a sales job that seemed to have a fine future. It was not so hard for him to find it, for when a smart employer heard the story of how he had sold high-priced jigsaw puzzles in dead-broke times, naturally he wanted him on his sales staff. The son has made good, too, and has been rewarded with several promotions. 

It would be possible to fill page after page with

stories of men who lost their jobs and turned to their hobbies as a means of support. Many stamp dealers got their start as commercial philatelists when, in order to tide themselves over tough years, they set up with their own collections as stock in trade. Many antique shops are conducted by men or women who, in the big pinch, fell back on what had been a hobby. I suspect that a big part of the trade in all kinds of hobby materials is carried on by people who first pursued the hobbies for fun, and as vocations only after they had suffered heavy losses. And if you look around you will find a number of attractive little inns conducted by former brokers or bankers or merchants.

I have spoken of Vash Young, who deliberately quit a job that he disliked, cut himself absolutely free, even though he had no money saved, in order to find employment to which he could harness his heart. My personal knowledge leads me to believe that hundreds of persons have done this. I know of one young man who left a good job for what

seemed a better job, but discovered after a couple of years that he did not and could not like the second job. He was on a spot, as many another man has been. What should he do? He faced his situation resolutely. He gave up his place, went into a business at which most persons had failed, and has done marvelously well in it for fifteen years. Instead of worrying along, doing half-heartedly what he did not like, he took what seemed a long chance. But would it not have been a longer chance if he had continued to do what he disliked?

The men and women who build up their courage sufficiently to go out and tackle jobs that fill their hearts, often, I think usually, win satisfying victories. Security and ease never make the strongest men. Strength or greatness is a product of hardship, effort and endurance.

Faith, courage, ambition and determination are deadly enemies of adversity. Therefore, I urge that people try to be optimists, always. The optimistic way is the loveliest way of life, and optimism, even

when it does not seem to come naturally, may be cultivated as a habit. I pity the pessimist, forever pursued by fear. 

All my life I have been an optimist-all my life except one day, and I shall never, never forget that one day. It was in June of 1932. That month there had been thirty-six bank failures in the city of Chicago. Banks, money, safety and losses were the topics of conversation on every corner. Life savings were being wiped out so fast that all sense of security seemed to be fading away.

In my own neighborhood, however, there was a feeling of safety. The bank in which many of us had our money enjoyed a splendid reputation. 

Nearly everyone believed that it would weather the storm. But it didn't. One Saturday morning the doors were locked against us. When the news spread the entire neighborhood was shocked almost into panic. Saturday was payday for most of the workers, but no pay checks could be cashed. All the money my business had was in that bank. 

Ordinarily, Saturday is a busy day with us, but

on that Saturday only a few customers came in, and they were the most depressed lot of human beings I ever saw. They came to the restaurant, presumably, to get food, but their minds were not on food. They didn't seem to care much what they ordered. 

Gloom, gloom, gloom. The air was thick with gloom, and I fell for it, like everybody else. As I stood in the dining room that noon and watched people with solemn faces walk slowly by (the very same people who had, many times before, come in with cheerful faces to eat their lunches) I became as pessimistic as anyone could be. I began to look down, instead of up. For the first time in my life

I saw only the black side; and my attitude, inevitably, was reflected by the employees. I could feel their worry and fear, and yet at times I thought they looked at me with sympathy in their eyes. Why sympathy for me? Was I worse off than they? No, but I thought I was.

Finally I could not take it any longer out in the open, so I secluded myself in my private office under the sidewalk. As I sat there and brooded the

walls seems to be closing in on me. Never before had those walls been so cold; never before had the little office been so dingy. I had the sensation of one about to be crushed under some huge object. I tried to figure a way out. With pencil and paper I worked, but the figures showed conditions worse each time I set them down. I was in despair! That day's sales showed about one-fourth of normal. 

Next week there would be bills for supplies. Next week there would be wages due all employees.

Next week there would be expenses of all kinds. 

Would there be income enough for me to stay open? 

Over and over I said to myself: "If business will only keep up we can work something out." But always there followed this thought: How can business keep up? Other people have no more money than I have. They can't come in here to buy food because they have nothing to buy food with. 

Those were black hours. I never can forget them. 

It was about midnight when I left my office, and as I turned toward the street after locking the res-

taurant door-perhaps for the last time—a crippled beggar stepped up to me. "Could you help a fellow get a bed?" he asked.

In those days so many people were begging on the streets that the custom was to say "No" whenever any stranger addressed you, and keep on walking. But for some reason I stopped to look at this man. There was something in his voice that corresponded to the distress in me. Adversity had made us kin. "Are you hungry?” I asked. "No, I'm not hungry. A kind gentleman just fed me, but I am trying to get together enough to get a place to sleep." "I wonder," I said thoughtfully-or gloomily— "if you are in as bad a fix as I am."

Then, urged on by some strange impulse, I began to tell him of my own misery. I filled his astonished ears and mind with my pitiful story; a story of a long climb, and of a sudden fall not my fault: a story of ruin that had overtaken me without warning. After a while I saw the crippled beggar hold-

ing a dime in his dirty hand, and holding it out to me. "Here," he said. "I think you need this more than I do." 

There is no way for me to describe my humiliation. There I stood, known by all who know me as an optimist, a man who had been boasting and boosting optimism for forty years, and yet I had fallen so low mentally and emotionally that I had placed myself on the same level as a chronic street beggar. Yes, even lower than that, for the beggar was offering me alms! I do not know quite what I said to him, but I do remember saying this: "Oh, that's all right. I guess I have over-sold you troubles." on my

The beggar put his dime back in his pocket and, coming closer, laid a hand on my shoulder. "Listen, Mister, you are the greatest salesman I ever met. You could sell anything to anybody. 

You're not whipped. You're just not trying, that's all." 

What a rebuke, and from what a source! But I

had it coming to me, so I thanked the fellow and started on my homeward walk, about twelve blocks up Michigan Avenue north of the river. I do not know how that street would have appeared to me if I had not met him, but after the meeting it seemed—and was—a wide, brilliantly lighted thoroughfare, humming with traffic and crowded with people walking or riding to some sort of achievement. These people were not giving up. The air was fresh and clean because the breeze was from the lake. I stepped up my pace, but even before my swift walking had got me home, I knew what I would do, and how our business would continue. 

Let me give you the solution for our problems as it came to me in those few minutes of walking.

We had always paid cash for our foods and other supplies. We had been in business for two years, and naturally had built up a good reputation among dealers who knew they would get their money when the goods were delivered. I would make emergency arrangements with them. Early Monday morning I telephoned to a number of them, told

them of our plight due to the bank's failure, and explained that we could not go on without their cooperation. All the dealers to whom I telephoned offered credit for a month, or for as long as needed. 

That simple arrangement tided us over.

Several years passed before we received any part of our money on deposit with the closed bank, and then the payment was only about twenty-five percent. But the jerk that crippled beggar gave me has been worth, to me, more than all the money I lost in the bank. Out of adversity developed a new and firmer belief in the philosophy that has sustained me. Because of adversity I got the shock I needed to force me to make good.

Adversity can, sometimes, do wonders for people. It can and often does enrich human relationships more than any other experience except love. Even love does not reach its full depth until hardships, heartaches and troubles have been faced. 

The husband and wife who believe their love is so great it could not be greater, find it somehow

deepened and sweetened after they have stood side by side in trouble. Yes, despite the misery that accompanies it, adversity is one of the greatest developers of character and of strength. Its full effect rarely can be measured until months or years have passed. The slowness of the action suggests the permanence of the results. 

And now, if I may, I wish to offer a little concluding sermon. Instead of fearing adversity, look on it as something that may benefit you in the long run, however dreadfully it tries you at the moment.

As best you can, ignore the pain that it brings today, supported by the knowledge that the pain will pass, leaving you a finer individual than you were.

And remember also, even while it causes you suffering, that as often as not what seems a needless and cruel harm may well prove to have been the first step toward a greater success and a greater happiness than you have ever known.

Try to be guided in everything you do by devotion to the task. Exert every effort for perfection,

even in minor accomplishments. Put your heart into your work in order that you may make good in it, and in the glorious adventure of living. And if adversity comes, or when adversity comes, meet it bravely, intelligently, get from it what you can that is useful and fine.
