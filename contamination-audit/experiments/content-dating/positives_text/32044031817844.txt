PREFACE 

Experience in teaching the law of trusts by the case book method has prompted the author to publish in concise form the informational matter which should form the background of a student's approach to this subject. It has been found that much valuable time has been consumed in the class room presentation of such material, time which might be more advantageously devoted to an interpretation of the cases. 

In presenting this information, an attempt has been made to substantiate it, either by citations of the leading cases in point, or by appropriate references to authoritative legal literature. These citations and references must be consulted in order that full benefit be derived from the use of this book.

Grateful acknowledgment is made to Miss Betty Argar for her able and untiring assistance in every phase of the work.

THE LAW OF TRUSTS CHAPTER I THE ORIGIN OF THE USE AND TRUST § 1. The Use 

A use, as it existed prior to the enactment of the Statute of Uses¹ may be described as a device for the ownership of property, whereby one person became the owner of the legal title, which he held for the benefit of a third party.² The use was most frequently created by a feoffment to A and his heirs for the use of B and his heirs. It could also come into existence by the levy of a fine or the suffering of a recovery.³ In cases where A would grant property to B, no consideration being paid by the latter, a use would be said to 'result' in favor of A on the assumption that the implied intent of the grantor was that the property should be held for his benefit.* 

Likewise, in a 'bargain and sale' transaction, where the full consideration had been paid by the purchaser to the seller of the land, equity considered the seller as holding the land to the use of the purchaser pending the transfer of seizin."

From the beginning, it will be observed that the basic element of the use was the duality of interest, i. e.. the interest of the legal owner of the res, and the interest of the beneficial owner. The practical applications of the use have been numerous and varied, ranging from the avoidance of Feudal obligations to the establishment of

§ 2. Theories on the Origin of the Use 

It has been stated by substantial authority that conveyances of land to the use of another were occasionally made shortly after the Norman conquest in 1066 A. D.8 

The source of the 'use' concept, however, is a matter of historical debate. Without attempting to judge the valid ity of the various contentions, the better-known theories and solutions as to the origin of the use are briefly described. 

According to (a) The Theory of Justice Holmes." 

Justice Holmes, "the feoffee to uses of the early English law corresponds point by point to the salman of the early 

German Law." 10 The salman was a person to whom a grant was made for the purpose of conveying the property granted, to a designated person after the death of the grantor. It seems to be more than mere coincidence that 'nearly every feoffment mentioned in the Calendars of Proceedings in Chancery down to the end of Henry VI is for the purpose of distribution after death'." The relationship between the salman and the feoffee to uses is further established by the conclusion that 'in the reign of Edward I the distinction between an executor and a feoffee to uses was still in embryo'.¹ 12 It is argued further, that the introduction of the Feudal system, transferred the jurisdiction over the rights of the beneficiary in possession from the common law

CHAPTER II THE TRUST RES AND THE PERSONS INTERESTED § 7. Definitions 

An attempt to define a modern trust meets with the same result as attempts to define any legal relationship. It is either too narrow or too broad. It is no sooner formulated, than it begins to decompose under the process of judicial reaction. In fact, legal definition, if it is to be used at all, should serve as a compass, with full allowance made for variation and deviation, rather than as an inflexible mould into which the facts of life must be poured. 

In defining the trust concept, Bispham¹ states that a "trust, in its technical sense, is the right, enforceable sole in equity, to the beneficial enjoyment of property of which the legal title is in another". An analysis of this definition reveals that there are four items to be considered in connection with a trust; (1) the property which is to be enjoyed; (2) the party who has the legal ownership, hereafter called the trustee; (3) the party for whose benefit the property is held, hereafter designated as the beneficiary; (4) and the party responsible for the creation of the right, hereafter referred to as the settlor.³

CHAPTER V TRUSTS AUTHORIZED BY STATUTE § 32. Legislative Policy 

It is apparent from the statutory enactments in reference to express trusts in land, that the legislative policy is one of disfavor towards dual ownership.¹ Only such trusts in land were permitted, as would, in the opinion of the legislature, require the trustee to have title. All other trusts in land except those as might arise by implication of law were expressly abolished.2 

§ 33. The Passive Trust 

In cases where, a grant of land is made to one, to hold in trust for the benefit of another, the trustee, having no active duty to perform, a passive trust is said to result. By Sections 92 and 93 of the Real Property Law, the legal title in a passive trust passes through the trustee and vests in the beneficiary.³ The vesting takes place by operation of the statute; no conveyance is required from the trustee, nor will one be decreed, where the deed on its face indicates the nature of the trust.*

CHAPTER VI THE CHARITABLE TRUST § 40. The Charitable Trust in General 

The charitable trust is to be distinguished from the express trusts above discussed,' both in the purposes for which it may be created and the persons who may be the beneficiaries. The purposes of the charitable trust admit of a fourfold classification, i.e., religious, educational, eleemosynary, public. Each of these classifications must be interpreted in the light of the origin and development of the charitable trust, before the dimensions of the four purposes can be fully measured. In the express trust, the beneficiary must be definite or ascertainable while in the charitable trust, its very nature demands that it be for the benefit of an indefinite number of persons. Such persons may include either the entire public or a definite classification thereof.2 

§ 41. Origin of the Charitable Trust

However, a fair interpretation of the known facts seems to indicate that there was a significant Roman-Christian influence in the development of the charitable trusts. The English Parliament first took legislative cognizance of the charitable use, when in 1601, it enacted the Statute of Charitable

§ 43. The Charitable Trust in New York 

In 1788 the New York Legislature repealed the English Statute of Charitable Uses insofar as it applied in this state, and in 1830 enacted the Revised statutes which by their terms abolished all trusts in land except those expressly authorized by the statutes. The statutes as revised made no mention of the charitable trust. Almost immediately, there sprang up a conflict as to whether or not the charitable trust as it was known in England had been abrogated by our statutes. CHAPTER VII IMPLIED TRUSTS (a) RESULTING TRUSTS § 53. Implied Trusts 

91 The term 'implied trusts' has been characterized by substantial authority as 'an ambiguous phrase.' Technically, an implied trust may mean one where the language used is held to spell out a trust by implication. In such a case, the settlor really aims to create a trust, but his language is not expressive. Nevertheless, a trust is implied, from the intent which has been manifested. An implied trust may also mean one where the court places the legal owner in the situation of trustee, either to carry out what the law believes (not interprets) the intention of the transferror to be, or to prevent one who has fraudulently acquired title from enjoying the fruits of same. 

It is with the second stated meaning of 'implied trust' that this and the following chapter are concerned.

§ 78. Contract Liability of Trustees 

Due to the particular situation of the beneficiary, which excludes him from any jurisdiction over the subject matter of the trust, his estate is not normally liable for the executory promises of the trustee, made in behalf of the trust estate. To rule otherwise would be to subject the beneficiary's interests to jeopardy, without giving him an opportunity to consent to, or disapprove of the transaction.22 The trustee, then, when he contracts on behalf of the trust estate acts at his peril. He must respond personally to the other contracting party and will be entitled to reimbursement from the trust estate, only in the event that he has acted within the scope of his express or implied powers. Upon his accounting to the beneficiary, the latter will be entitled to test out the propriety of the contract from the viewpoint of the trust.23 

It sometimes happens that the trustee is either unable or unwilling to incur personal liability as a party to a contract, for the benefit of the trust estate. He may then enter into a contract with a third party, whereby he subrogates the third party to his lien for reimbursement against the trust property. In such a situation, the third party may assert his lien against the trust estate. The claim will be allowed to the third party, if it would have been allowed to the trustee.

§ 94. Life Insurance Trusts 

Within the past decade or so, there has sprung into existence a form of trust which has come to be known as the life insurance trust.37 Such trusts are usually created in one of two ways; either the settlor, who continues to pay the premiums, designates as his beneficiary a trustee who is to administer the funds collected pursuant to a trust agreement; or the settlor deposits with the trustee a stipulated amount, the income of which is to be used in the payment of the premiums of the policy, and at the same time, he designates the trustee as beneficiary of the policies under certain trust agreements. 

The first method is designated as the unfunded life insurance trust; whereas the other is called the funded life insurance trust. 38 In a life insurance trust, the res consists of the contingent interest of the beneficiary to receive the proceeds of the policy upon the death of the insured." Even in those cases where the insured reserves the right to change the beneficiary, the trust will be upheld. All that is requisite to the validity of the trust is that the interest in the policy, vested or contingent, shall vest in the trustee, and apparently, that the beneficiary of the trust be 40 39

By Sec. 15 of the Personal Property Law, it is, among other things, provided, "that when the proceeds of a life insurance policy, becoming a claim by death of the insured, are left with the insurance company under a trust or other agreement, the benefits accruing thereunder after death of the insured shall not be transferable, nor subject to commutation or incumbrance, nor to legal process except in an action to recover for necessaries, if the parties to the trust or other agreement so agree." Inferentially, then, an insurance company may be trustee of the proceeds of a policy of its own insured, and may mingle such proceeds with its general funds.
