INTRODUCTION 

-1- The Study of Social Work Interpretation in Pittsburgh was, so far as we know, the first study of the kind to be made in any city. If we accept public information as an important function of social work, it seems reasonable and desirable to include it in community-wide surveys. Therefore, the opportunity to carry out such an experiment as a part of the Pittsburgh Social Study was welcomed not only for its own sake, but also because of the chance to try out a method which might later be useful to other communities. The Study has proceeded more slowly than might otherwise have been the case because the method employed was untried. 

The plan was explained at the beginning of the Study to members of the Pittsburgh Social Work Publicity Council, and developments have been reported to its members from time to time. Their cooperation and help has been most generous at every stage. 

The Study has been directed toward future planning. An effort has been made to include in it a minimum of critical comment on past performance. 

However, interpretation has lagged so far behind other aspects of social work everywhere, both in volume and in quality, that it would be hard to find a city where an examination of the publicity would not call attention to many omissions, as well as to some ineffectiveness in both content and method of presentation.

With the objective of concentrating as much as possible on those factors important to developing a sound program for the interpretation of social work, the Study has been concerned, first, with the content of publicity, that is, with what the public has been told about social problems and services; and second, with the available and needed resources for carrying on an adequate program of interpretation for both public and private social work.

A limitation set for the undertaking was the definite exclusion of criticism and advice to individual agencies. This service, which was asked for in the beginning, did not seem possible or desirable to include in a study of this kind. Later, however, when a great deal of publicity supplied by agencies had been examined, informal consultation for comment on this material was offered to those who wanted it, and as a result brief interviews with representatives of some 30 agencies were held during a four day period. 

The Study covered interpretation of public and private social work in the city of Pittsburgh only, since the staff time available was insufficient for a county-wide study. It was confined also to the forms of publicity which aim to enlighten the public, omitting a great deal of publicity in the form of dramatic or spectacular events, posters, slogans, and other efforts primarily of the attention-getting variety. 

The analysis of content which was the basis of Part One of the report was carried on in New York with the assistance, for short periods, of Baird Middaugh of the National Social Work Publicity Council, Suzanne Wunder of the publicity department of the New York Children's Aid Society, and Mary E. Culp, who has assisted the Social Study, Community Fund, and other agencies in Pittsburgh in their publicity work. 

The field work of the Study of Resources reviewed in Part Two of the report was done by Miss Wunder.

The Pittsburgh Social Work Publicity Council, under the leadership of its chairman, Eleanor Webster Brown, has assisted greatly in collecting material from the agencies, arranging appointments for consultations, and keeping representatives of social agencies who are members of the Council in touch with the progress of the Study.

Part One What the Public Has Been Told About Social Work

Part One What the Public Has Been Told About Social Work Introduction What 

Public opinion affects more than the financing of social work. many different individuals or groups throughout the city believe about social work, social agencies, and social workers is reflected in the quality of the social work of the community. It tends to increase or lessen the usefulness of agencies in countless ways. Without doubt the effort to put the conclusions of the Social Study into effect will be influenced by the degree of understanding interest and favorable opinion that exists among various sections of the general public. 

A study of public opinion about social work would be a very desirable, but also a most difficult and uncertain undertaking. We can, however, look more closely than we ordinarily do at the ideas and facts to which we expose the public. For example, what help do newspaper readers receive in forming sound opinion on unemployment relief or crime prevention? Are contributors told what happens when they do not give enough? In a period when changes in scope, methods, and personnel of social work are many and rapid, is public confidence being sought by the interpretation of those changes? 

To find answers to questions like these, the attempt was made to learn what the public had been told during a ten-months' period. The advantage of such a study of content of publicity seemed to be threefold. First, it is natural for all of us to slip unconsciously into the habit of telling the public about the most easily understood needs and services and to postpone the more difficult task of attempting to arouse interest in the unfamiliar. An analysis of what actually was told would show whether or not this is the case. 

Second, an appraisal of its content is a wholesome substitute for the common practice of measuring the job of public education by the inches of space which

have been filled with printed words, the number of speeches made, and the number of persons who have listened to speeches. The third and most important use of such a study is that a plan for interpretation which might grow out of it will have a logical starting point in a resume of what has been presented, with its inevitable gaps in some places and over-emphasis in others. 

It was to be expected that much of the information put out by social agencies would relate to already familiar forms of social service. From the standpoint of raising money and of retaining goodwill, it is important that familiar appeals and obvious information should be kept before contributors. 

This very fact makes it all the more necessary to interpret and gradually gain acceptance for the less easily understood needs and services. It is with this type of public information, rather than with the whole output of social work publicity, that the analysis was chiefly concerned.

This material gives by no means a complete record of all that the public was told during these ten months. We did not secure the whole output of local agencies and undoubtedly overlooked some articles in newspapers. Many citizens must have received important social information during this time directly from national magazines. They were also exposed to much misinformation and highly prejudiced comment from these sources. National radio broadcasts and motion pictures contributed to the impressions formed about situations and programs of which Pittsburgh social work is a part. 

The selection of the ten months' period for this Study disregards of course, all that was told before and after this time. It takes some stories out of their setting. Thus we know that conditions in state institutions had been much in the news in the past so that somewhat brief references during the Study period reached a public which read them against a background of familiarity with the subject. Still another limitation to the significance of this analysis is that it takes very little account of the attention-value and clarity of the material itself. 

While fully recognizing all its limitations, this section of the report is submitted as a fairly typical picture of the kind of information reaching the general public, and the sources from which it comes. The method of this section of the report illustrates a procedure available to any agency or group of agencies for an occasional checking of what is told and what important facts and ideas are overlooked in the year around publicity.

How the Analysis was Divided 

The analysis of the social information which reached the public is divided in relation to different media in which it appeared, rather than by following through some one topic as it was presented in all of them. 

The newspaper and the radio reaching the general public are considered first; then the publications, house organs, meetings, reaching more selected groups. At the end, major topics are summarized crossing the lines of the different media. 

What Newspaper Readers Were Told 

Our clippings from the three daily newspapers totaled over 3,000, about 85 per cent of them being secured during the first six months in 1935. They were grouped for study by topics, and the clippings relating to each topic considered in chronological order. The topics in turn were broken down into what we called stories, meaning by a story a particular event, issue, or campaign, which was continuously or intermittently in the news over a period of several days, weeks, or months. We did not count inches of space, partly because the total amount of space did not seem significant for our purposes, but also because in so many instances only a small percentage of the space given to an article told the reader anything which we classified as social information.

Unemployment Relief in the News Newspaper readers were told far more about unemployment relief than about any other social problem or any other form of social work. It seems safe to say, indeed, that the newspaper attention given to the subject exceeded that devoted to any other single topic in the news during the first six months of 1935.* Front page stories, editorials, cartoons, letters to the editor, and *The analysis of newspaper reporting of relief covered the period from 

January 18th to July 1st, 1935.

feature articles reported, criticized or upheld, and interpreted many aspects of this question. Our clippings from the three dailies totaled 1,300 items. 

Under these circumstances, one might suppose that the people of Pittsburgh had an extraordinary opportunity to become enlightened about the plight of some 75,000 families in the county, and the millions in the country as a whole in need of public aid, and about what was being done and what should be done for them. Actually, relief as a social problem was scarcely in the news at all. The news values were found in political battles about appropriations, the cost of relief to the taxpayer, the hoped for improvements through work projects, and the changes from month to month in the number of persons on relief. 

The unemployed as human beings became news only when they were under fire. This was true, also, of the relief workers. A minor note of sympathy for the distress of the jobless appeared now and then in editorials, cartoons, letters to the editor, and the reported speeches of the Governor, but was withdrawn at the least hint of suspicion that some few of them were "chiseling" or had refused work.

Federal Work Relief Appropriation 

The huge and unprecedented size of the four and a half billion dollar federal appropriation for work relief naturally made this measure top news. 

First, the administration's fight for its passage; then the plan of control for spending the money and delays in getting underway; and finally what 

Pennsylvania would get out of it in improvements, were the chief elements of news interest. The intended beneficiaries of the vast sum of money were referred to briefly when controversy over the security wage versus prevailing wage arose. Even at this point the emphasis was not on the living standards of the unemployed, but rather on a debate between organized labor and the administration over this issue.

Brief and obscure paragraphs sometimes reminded the reader that the unemployed are human beings. For examples: "The people (relief recipients) are getting the habit. It's going to cost the taxpayers far more in the future than any four billion or twice four billion, unless we put them to work."

And, "The allotment of 600 million for the Civilian Conservation Corps will be generally approved. No other type of relief has proved quite as satisfactory as the CCC. It has kept on its rolls more than three hundred young men of relief families, many of whom otherwise would have become street-corner loafers or homeless wanderers. The CCC has paid extra dividends socially as well as in the conservation of forests and soil." 

News and comment from Harrisburg in connection with the Work Relief Bill was chiefly concerned with the desire that the state should get its full share of the money and with proposed projects for Pennsylvania. Stories about applications for PWA grants gave some attention to the conditions in state institutions, especially the long standing neglect of the mental hospital with its overcrowding and waiting lists. The average reader must have been confused much of the time as between PWA and WPA projects. 

Local news during the spring months concerned the attempt to carry over those employed on work relief until the new PWA absorbed them. 

Headlines

threatened layoffs of more and more of these workers as the continuance of ERA funds became uncertain, but mainly reported delays in getting started, suspicion of political corruption, and accusations by private contractors of waste and inefficiency. As the time approached for transfer of some 66,000 relief clients to work relief, we hear of some 20,000 unemployables to be transferred to the care of city and county relief boards, although it is brought out that nobody knows how it can be done. 

In June, the required registration for jobs of everyone on relief brought the unemployed into the headlines. Delays in registration were attributed to reluctance of those on relief to take WPA jobs. "Relief Slacker Will Lose

Dole" said a Press headline, "No Work, No Eat, Edict Put in Effect for Those 

Who are Employable."

During June, the Post-Gazette ran a critical series of signed feature articles on RWD, which parenthetically is called Reckless Waste Division, instead of Relief Works Division. The theme, illustrated with many specific instances, was that unemployed persons were encouraged to go on relief in order to qualify for jobs, while many who do get them are not anxious to keep them.

The Press in a series of feature articles on projects for white collar workers, described an impressive number of useful achievements. 

State Appropriations for Relief 

The Governor's legislative fight for relief appropriations, accompanied by a great deal of dickering with Mr. Hopkins, and reports on the size of the job from the SERA, was a close second to WPA in news importance. There was plenty of editorial backing for demands for efficiency and economy in the administration of relief at the same time that a series of eleventh hour appropriations, each covering a two-week period, were reported.

While attention was centered on the bitter appropriations fight which reached its high point in April, with a deadlock between the Governor and the Senate, the number of unemployed, estimated variously at 1,700,000 and 2,000,000, was a set of figures repeated like a chorus, but never brought to life except in a prediction of food riots and general disorders if their needs were not met, or in repetition of phrases like, "Their bitter need," in quotations from speeches.

Two front-page articles in the Press by its Harrisburg correspondent, reviewed the relief situation with some evidence of interest in the unemployed themselves. The articles reviewed the steps in relief from the breadlines to home relief, next to the addition of clothing and shelter to food orders, then to cash relief, and the progress from local to state, and then to national responsibility. 

The Relief Administration 

News from SERA and ACERB during this period consisted for the most part of statistical reports of numbers on relief and amounts spent. 

Talking to the Democratic Women's Luncheon Club in Philadelphia, Robert L. Johnson, state relief administrator, declared, according to a Pittsburgh 

Press story, that most of the members of the legislature are not "really interested in relief." In March, he said, he offered to supply all members of the House and Senate with weekly reports on the relief situation. Only 29 replied, he charged. "I believe this fact is significant in the light of the sharp criticism levelled at the relief administration in the last two months," he said.

Stories from other states helped to center attention on the relief administration as the arena for political quarrels, especially between the federal government and the states. The three most dramatic stories, and those longest in the news were: the fight between Governor Davey and Mr. Hopkins in

Ohio; the deadlock in Illinois with relief cut off and hunger marchers converging on the Capitol; and the boondoggling story from New York which drew such headlines as: "Goody! Goody! 19 Million for Ballet Dances;" "New York's Relief 

Millions Go to Pay for Ballet Lessons and Hobby Guidance."

Investigation of Relief in Allegheny County The "probe," as the headlines called the legislative investigation of relief, was a news event of importance in March and April. This was the one occasion when the unemployed themselves were important figures in the news. 

Social workers, also, were talked about in the course of the public hearings. 

Both clients and workers were headlined for their sins and not their virtues. 

Reports of the hearings told that cash relief had resulted in a 40 per cent decrease in milk consumption; that coal orders were sold; that cash relief frees the relief workers' time so that they have more time to investigate families; and that cash relief increases drinking. Court action against "chiselers who had been identified was hailed as an achievement. easily overlooked items about clients being cheated on their coal orders, and the theft of relief checks from clients, were in the news at the same time, but these were minor human interest stories and independent of the investigation.

Brief and Headlines, articles, and editorials made much of an apparently reluctant estimate by County Relief Director Mills that "under 10 per cent and possibly under five per cent of all relief clients were chiselers." This was translated as the story progressed into a positive statement supposedly based on accurate records which showed that 10 per cent or 30,000 chiselers were getting from $100,000 to $200,000 monthly. 

Relief workers were blamed editorially for the existence of chiseling, although it was admitted they were carrying case loads of 150, whereas a load of 75 had been recommended as sufficient. Low salaries were also admitted in

editorials but neither of these were accepted as excusing them for not finding the chiselers. 

Newspaper reports of hearings gave generous space to a disgruntled coal dealer who charged waste in coal distribution, a grocer who claimed to have made a study of 100 cases and found that all were chiselers, and a discharged relief worker who claimed she was dismissed for taking a day off to appear in a suit against a taxicab company. 

The total results of the investigation appeared to be that the modest total of $11,249 was returned by 260 relief clients (out of over 250,000 on relief.) A few persons were prosecuted. The readers, however, would have to study the news carefully to learn this. It was not pointed out editorially or otherwise.

The investigating committee's report, which came out about a month after the investigation was over, was summarized in news stories. It recommended unrelenting punishment of relief chiselers caught in the act; home rule for 

ACERB; curbing power of Miss Nell Scott, county supervisor, to hire and fire relief employes at will; consolidation of relief activities under one head; and putting representatives of Public Relations Office in every district to hear complaints and give power to settle disputes. 

Newspapers also reported legislative investigations in nine other counties. News from these counties contributed its share toward creating the impression that chiseling was widespread among persons on relief. 

Remedies for chiseling proposed in bills or suggested by volunteer investigators which were reported included: (1) making public the records of persons on relief, (2) requiring affidavits of income, (3) putting up a bond, (4) relief recipients to carry a card - those refusing decent offers of employment to be made to give up their cards. A law, with penalties attached, was passed which required all applicants to sign a sworn statement as to their financial condition.

In May, a proposed resolution aimed primarily at social workers, was reported, which said that "in the sacred profession of social service" experience and common sense were disregarded for requirement of three years! schooling. "This most sacred fraternity is enjoying an era of abundant prosperity while the remainder of our people are enduring poignant suffering. • • · · 11 The resolution accuses the board of hiring social workers or members of well-off families, "while the Governor and Legislature are 'sweating blood' in an attempt to enact a tax program to keep thousands of families from starving." Increases in salaries of social workers, some running as high as 40 per cent, are reported.

The SERA Releases 

There is evidence that the SERA was making a serious effort to present its side of the picture during this period of agitation. Governor Earle was quoted as not believing that relief rolls had grown primarily because of chiselers. "Our relief rolls have grown simply because a number of our citizens finally exhausted every resource and had to turn to their fellow citizens for help." per cent.

Figures were given out by Administrator Johnson showing that in three counties studied, the percentage of chiselers is about two and three tenths The relief administration supplied figures showing that the relief cost was 39 cents per person per day; that the Pennsylvania system of relief costs less than in 45 other states; and that much less was being spent in 

Pennsylvania than by any other state administration. 

Mr. Johnson's assistant, Alvin Guyler, was quoted in the Post-Gazette of 

May 21st in a report of the hearing of the state-wide investigation committee as opposing publication of names of persons on relief and as favoring trained social workers.

Defending the use of trained social workers as supervisors of distribution of relief, Guyler said that of 11,000 of the SERA payroll only 25 are "social workers." Fifty are being trained at the federal government's expense and will be ready to join the staff next month, he said.

In one county report, the administration was criticized for importing social workers from other states. In another, social workers were said to be "seeking to build the state relief board into a permanent organization for their own benefit."

Some Striking Omissions It is to be expected that newspaper readers as taxpayers would be interested in the cost of relief and their own part in paying for it. They would be receptive to any information or expression of opinion which would support the conviction that less money should be spent for relief. On the other hand, if the needs for relief were made vivid enough, and if the victims of unemployment were sympathetically treated now and then in the news, this defensive attitude might be offset to some extent. 

For example, the public learned nothing of the courage and resourcefulness of thousands of relief clients in contrast to the almost negligible number found to be chiselers, or refusing to work. The effect of the depression on children was represented by one obscure item, reporting a study of malnutrition in the families of the unemployed. 

The public was not made aware of the intricate problem involved in transferring employables to work relief, or of the difficulties of distinguishing between employable and unemployable persons. The case for competence in relief administration as it affects the clients and their future restoration to self support was not brought out. 

The only material found on the important transient problem was a series of five feature articles appearing in the Press in January called, "Hobo for a Week." A reporter told of his experience as a tramp seeking shelter in Pittsburgh.

Sources of Relief News 

Most of The chief sources of news about relief were the national press services, and the Washington and Harrisburg correspondents of local newspapers. the interpretation of the news, as well as the reporting, was done by political commentators and reporters. SERA and ACERB provided statistics which were duly reported, and told the reader how the size of the relief load varied from week to week. Almost no information, comment, or interpretation was credited to social workers, or appeared to bear the stamp of their professional understanding of the situation.

Old Age Assistance 

The subject of old age pensions was in the news most frequently in November and December, 1934, when applications for pensions under the state law were being filed. All the newspapers carried information about methods of applying for pensions, human interest stories about the applicants, and photographs, which in themselves told stories of helplessness and poverty. The 

Sun-Telegraph carried a cartoon, "Journey's End?," showing two old people on their way to the poorhouse unless their application for old age pensions was successful.

In three feature articles in the Pittsburgh Press, Gilbert Love illustrated an interpretation of the pension plan with human interest stories concerning the applicants. The first of the stories made the point that the applicants were of all types; some had been poor all their lives, some were persons of education but had lost money in the depression, etc. The second forecast the benefits that old age pensions would bring, not only to the old themselves, but also to relatives just above the relief level who were making great sacrifices to provide for aged parents. The third described the difficulties of proving eligibility for pensions. It recounted the story of

an aged colored woman who had been born in slavery and consequently had difficulty proving her age. Many persons had lost citizenship papers in odd ways. Others were so old that their contemporaries were gone and they had no one to vouch for the date of their birth. 

After January 1, when the Old Age Pension Law went into effect, and the first rush of applicants subsided, old age pensions appeared in the newspapers only occasionally. The more lengthy stories during this period, for the most part described the state legislature's alarms and excursions in the process of making an appropriation for payment of the pensions. 

The careful reader in these months might have learned that of the 11,569 applications filed in Allegheny County, 3,907 had been approved, 1,643 rejected, and 6,019 were on the waiting list. A paragraph in a brief story headlined "Aged Pensions Nearing Quota," explained that allowances not exceeding $24.96 a month for indigent citizens over seventy years were divided into four units on a budget basis, namely food, fuel, clothing and rent. If the applicant had ways of providing his own fuel, for instance, the pension covered the other items of food, shelter, and clothing. Under this arrangement, the average old age pension for the state was $20.59 a month. A sympathetic story by a columnist described an old lady spending her first pension check. 

Social Security Measure 

The term, social security, not at all a familiar one to the public, came into the headlines during this period. The main features of the administration's Social Security Bill were listed, rather than described, fairly often, but comparatively few articles explained it in any but the most technical terms. The bulk of the news, as in the case of the Work Relief Bill, had to do with its progress through the House and Senate. The Townsend Bill also received considerable attention, and was explained in a much more human and understandable way.

When the Social Security Measure was finally passed, each of the Pittsburgh dailies explained it in more or less simple terms. An excellent question and answer article told applicants for old age pensions what they would need to know. Walter Lippman's column in the Post-Gazette explained the unemployment insurance section, The Sun-Telegraph explained the old age pension plan simply. 

A feature of the Social Security Bill, providing an eight million dollar appropriation for public health was commented on editorially when Josephine Roche, assistant secretary of the Treasury, brought out facts from a survey of mortality among the unemployed from 1929 to 1933. "The sickness rate was 50 per cent greater in the groups hit hardest by the depression than in families whose incomes had not been materially reduced." "Not so long ago," says the editorial, "government officials were even assuring us that the depression had improved the physical condition of the population. Such figures, of course, were taken from full-time workers and other favored groups... Apart from the human suffering and injustice involved in these figures, the economic waste and financial loss to the nation is incalculable."

Topics of Minor News Importance 

When we shift our attention from unemployment relief and social security as news topics, to other aspects of public and private social work, with few exceptions we turn from the front page to back pages, and from a topic continuously before the public to social information found in the news intermittently, and for only a few days at a time.

What is printed on these other topics seems to depend much more on what was offered by interested state and local organizations, and less on the initiative of press services and local editors. For example, the local Public 

Welfare Department, which apparently offered little information to the press,

was in the news very briefly when accusations of graft in purchasing supplies were made.

We found the Mothers' Assistance Fund in the news briefly and when under criticism, although its work was important and timely enough to rate consider- The County Commissioner objected to the staff's request that their new automobiles should be inconspicuous of color and without county able attention. seals. His comment, as reported in the Sun-Telegraph, was: 

If they get their cars I want them painted the county colors. 

They won't chase around on private business with the big seal on their cars. Why, not long ago the Mothers' Assistance people wanted to hire some experts from New York. Why go to 

New York, Arizona, Mexico or Kansas? We have plenty of experts in this county. What crimes are committed in the name of social welfare. 

An editorial in the Sun-Telegraph, a day or so later, criticized the Commissioner's stand. "County Commissioner McGowan casts an unwarranted reflection on integrity of the social workers employed by Mothers' Assistance Fund." The editorial explained their desire to spare the feelings of the widows, old folk, and blind persons whom they visited who otherwise would be known to the neighbors as objects of charity. 

Proposed County Welfare Boards 

Closely related to emergency relief and social security is the provision for relief on a permanent basis, a matter of first importance in relation to the immediate future of social work. 

A bill before the legislature proposing to set up county welfare boards was sponsored by the Public Charities Association, and provided an occasion for interpreting this subject to newspaper readers. A state-wide survey of the present poor relief system had been completed, and the report was released in January, 1935. Here we had both an occasion and source material for news. 

The legislative measure was presented to the public as an administrative problem with emphasis on efficiency and economy. A cartoon pictured the old

system of poor relief boards as a patchwork quilt of 531 overlapping agencies. 

The fact that it was proposed to replace 425 independent local boards with 67 county boards was reiterated many times in some 22 news articles and 10 editorials which were examined. 

The need for this reform was treated as a human problem, having an important bearing on the recipients of public assistance, in only two articles, both in the Press. One of these, reporting a WPA survey, told of the scores of "lost" children which the study discovered - children who had been placed in free homes by poor boards and exploited. The other, with a headline, "Poor 

Board System Pictured as Monster Preying on Indigents," told of the humiliation to which applicants for relief were subjected, the incredibly low relief budgets, and the wretched conditions in almshouses. Housing Low cost housing and slum clearance were topics of especially timely interest during this period because of the hoped for PWA's ten million dollar housing project for Pittsburgh, and also because several local studies provided facts and figures as a basis for news. 

The terms, low cost housing and slum clearance, or "slum war" were in the headlines often enough to become familiar. 

Technical aspects of enabling legislation, so that it would be legal to accept government money for PWA projects, received much of the newspaper space. The Sun-Telegraph ran a striking cartoon called "Cleaning Time" which pictured slums as breeders of disease germs and crime germs. Differences between the various government housing services were explained, although hardly made clear. The Federal 

Housing Administration kept constantly before the public the programs of its local unit in stimulating building repairs, but its information had no relation to better low cost housing. The constant association of the word, housing, with this program might easily have confused readers.

About housing conditions as a social problem, readers were told the results of an investigation of 400 homes in the Hill district in which there were known cases of tuberculosis. Accepted minimum standards of decent housing, as to open space, cross ventilation, sanitary equipment, sound construction, and space for room privacy were explained. A diagram and a feature story showed that more than one half of the renters in the county pay less than $25.00 a month, and gave figures on distribution of rents at different levels, and numbers of renters and owners. A report of a government study by Dr. Wood said that 15 per cent of some 25,000 Pittsburgh houses inspected were unfit for use.

Juvenile Delinquency 

Crime prevention and juvenile delinquency were brought into the news on the occasion of the Crime Conference in January. As reported, each speaker advocated a different remedy for crime, or attributed it to a different cause. 

The homes are to blame, parents are lax, children should be kept out of prison, police corruption is responsible. Of the Morals Court we learned chiefly that the Mayor would not listen to a report. 

The Juvenile Court figures prominently in the news for brief periods three times between September, 1934, and January, 1935. The official report on the famous 1933 investigation of the Court's foster home department was released. Various groups sought to force the building of a Juvenile Detention 

Home. A quarrel arose over the Court budget.

Although the headline, "Report Bares Cruelty to Court Wards," (Post- Gazette for September 17, 1934) intimated revelations, the reader learned that 5,000 copies of the report on the 1933 investigation, printed by authorization of the county commissioners, were now ready for distribution. The contents of the report were already well known because of the furore of publicity which had accompanied every finding of the 1933 investigators; hence the announcement that a change in court personnel would be made soon received the major emphasis. The report itself was reviewed in a single sentence: "The report, 158 pages, tells of 631 cases of children living in foster homes where they were abused or badly cared for." The judge of the Juvenile Court was quoted as saying that all reports of abuse of Court wards from any source had been thoroughly investigated and "as far as I know at the present time there is no ward anywhere in the county suffering mistreatment."

All Court employes, it was revealed, had taken examinations during the summer and each member of the new staff would have the approval of the National Probation Society and other specialists in the field. 

The fact that both new appointees and members of the old staff took a #rigid test," both oral and written, was noted in the Post-Gazette, also that this system of competitive examination, one of the first ever instituted in any U.S. juvenile court, was approved by civic and social service groups. An editorial in the Sun-Telegraph praised Judge Schramm for making "personal fitness for the job the guiding consideration." A Press editorial also approved giving jobs to qualified social workers selected after careful examination. The unqualified, untrained workers, selected primarily by political methods were obviously unfitted to supervise dependent children, according to the Press.

The The series of stories concerning the agitation for a new detention home opened the last of November and reached its peak the middle of December. point at issue, the reader learned, was the delay of the commissioners in

selecting a site for the new home, because that delay was endangering a PWA grant of $98,000. Since the movement for the new home had begun months before the period of this survey with articles which revealed the old home as a fire trap and a menace to health, etc., the need for construction of a well-equipped, modern building was generally known and accepted; consequently, these stories now under consideration told only a little about conditions at the old home. "Delay in New Juvenile Home Stirs Women," said the Post-Gazette on December 13, 1934. "While the commissioners argue and fuss about a site for the new home the child wards of the Court are being kept in a fire trap that endangers their lives and in building inadequate for their protection," the president of the Federation of Women's Clubs was quoted as declaring. She cited the recent escape of two girls, one of them feeble-minded, and one of whom took poison before being returned to the home, as proof of her point that "children in the present detention home cannot be protected even from themselves." Eight other children, she said, had escaped from the home in the past month. In a later Post-Gazette interview, she pointed out that lack of isolation wards or any means of isolating a new child until he had been examined, exposes all children in the home to the dangers of infectious disease. For example, 20 boys were kept in the "dingy building under a mumps quarantine for twenty-one days because a new boy had to be put in the same building before he was examined." Efficiency is disrupted during the frequent quarantines because no children can be admitted to the home and none go out a boy may be arrested but goes free because with the home quarantined there is no place for him.

Sixty women representing the Legislative Council of Western Pennsylvania, the Sun-Telegraph for December 14th stated, presented the commissioners with a petition for the immediate building of the new home. "Present conditions are a disgrace to the community," the leader of the delegation said. The quarters are wholly inadequate, competent supervision is impossible, the

building is in a dilapidated condition and facilities prohibit even the least modern methods of detention. 

About ten days after the excitement over the detention home subsided, another tempest rushed through the newspapers, this time criticism over the proposed Juvenile Court budget by women members of the advisory committee appointed by the judge. 

A leading front-page Post-Gazette story, with a four-column head, and pictures of the principal figures concerned, told little about the budget and a great deal about the dispute. For several days columns were devoted to the question of whether or not the judge had consulted his detention home board and his advisory committee.

The case for a proposed increase in the budget, one learned by reading carefully, was that an adequate number of qualified workers for the foster home department would be the only real basis of preventing the Juvenile Court from becoming the dumping ground for more and more children with more cost to the taxpayer and less service to the child. Each worker had at the time 200 children who must be visited four times a month. Each worker has to spend about six days a month at court hearings so that with other duties there are left eighteen days in which to make 800 visits. 

Obscure references to the budget itself were not very clear. Inconspicuous also, was a statement by a member of the Social Study staff, upholding the proposed increases in staff.

Between January and June little of interest concerning the Juvenile Court appeared in the newspapers. The most important was the announcement, illustrated with a column photograph, of Elizabeth Cosgrove's appointment as director of the Court's foster home department. The story, a minor one, included the information that there are 600 homes in the department and 2,000 wards.

The Penal Affairs Committee discussed juvenile delinquency at its annual meeting, A full column report of the meeting mentioned briefly that neighborhood recreation reduces crime, prisoners should be segregated, and racketeer lawyers should be kept out of court. Occasional reference to crime prevention was found in syndicated feature articles. 

Recreation and Character Building 

Announcements and reports of athletic events, pictures of plays, festivals, and games, reports of a pet show and a hobby show, and stories and pictures about camping, all reminded readers that recreation in many forms is offered by social agencies. The athletic news was far in the lead as the form of recreation receiving news space. 

These events were allowed to speak for themselves so far as their value as social service was concerned. One had glimpses of the value placed on heroism. A review of achievements was contained in the considerable amount of publicity given to the Fortieth Anniversary of Irene Kaufmann Settlement. The purpose of the Settlement is defined as "the advancement of civic, individual and social welfare of the surrounding community an institution in the service of its friends, a school in the work of its teachers, a club-house in the social uses of its neighbors, a civic organization in the interests of the community, and settlement in the choice of its location. Scores of varied activities, from the distribution of milk to instruction in the arts, are carried on in the settlement buildings." · • Less familiar, and less generally appreciated aspects of recreation, such as the value of competent leadership, the extent of unmet needs, the relation of leisure time activity to unemployment were not brought out. One reads briefly now and then of day camps, but only one story about day camps in Mt.

Service to Individuals 

Social case work for families and children is one of the least understood forms of social service and one involving much confusion now that relief, which has been so closely associated with case work in the public mind, is largely taken care of by public funds. Only three or four stories were found which threw any light on these questions. One story based on the annual report of the Family Society, wove into the statistical record brief and illuminating glimpses of individual services. On the occasion of the fiftieth anniversary of the Children's Aid Society, one learned that in 1885 ladies of the Society looked on boys and girls rescued from jails and reformatories as "little inheritors of sin and sorrow," and that now the same agency makes physical and mental examinations of these same children, who are called "problem children" and chooses foster homes to fit temperaments of specific children. 

Brief references to services which case working agencies performed for families of the unemployed were found in Community Fund campaign stories. 

Social Work as a Profession 

The fact that we do not have enough trained social workers was stated several times in interviews by leaders in social work. What the public health nurse does, especially in instructive nursing service for mothers and babies, was told in one article. 

Two social workers were under fire, a relief supervisor and a Y.W.C.A. industrial secretary (the latter being vigorously defended with the aid of one newspaper.) Criticisms of the profession in legislative hearings were reported. 

Distinction Between Public and Private Social Work 

The Community Fund campaign publicity in the fall of 1934 emphasized the distinction between publicly and privately supported social work. The pressure to make clear that private agencies are needed, led to repeated statements of

what public funds do not do. For example, ", .unemployment relief is not taking over the providing of homes and proper care for orphans and children from disrupted homes. It has nothing to do with character-building agencies such as settlements and Boy and Girl Scout activities, which have always required help." And again, "In short, the agencies of the Community Fund start in at the vital point where the service of the public funds stops. 

They build and restore health, putting the individuals aided in condition to accept employment when it is available and become self-supporting." 

What is left out makes explanations like these misleading, since private agencies are urging government to take over and perform adequately social services to care for children, restore health, and provide recreation and other character-forming activities. Nothing was found in the newspapers during or after the campaign which helped to clarify the distinction and at the same time give full recognition to the continuing work of the public agency. 

Unmet Needs Before and during the Community Fund campaign, readers were told that the failure of the campaign to meet its goal the year before resulted in cutting down the work of nurses and increasing the case load of nurses far beyond what is reasonable; that the intake of a children's agency had been limited. Probably there were other similar stories of this kind which we overlooked. After the 1934 campaign fell short of its goal, nothing appeared during the following months to indicate what was happening because of this shortage. 

The Character of News from Private Agencies 

While relief as a social problem was subordinated in the news to its political aspects, in news about private social agencies social information was overshadowed by descriptions of particular occasions, and administrative matters. The bulk of our clippings tell about election of officers, changes

in personnel, announcements and progress of money raising campaigns, accounts of benefits, together with pictures of socially prominent men and women, descriptions of anniversary events, and announcements and reports of speeches of out-of-town speakers. In such stories as these, one occasionally finds brief general statements about the aims of agencies. Thus, an agency's camping program "teaches self-reliance among young girls, and gives them healthful outdoor activity which builds a better mind and body." A community center "is a haven for thousands of boys and girls who would otherwise have been deprived of a center for recreation and social contacts." 

News and social information coincide when social studies are reported, and several of these provided the best material found. Conferences held in 

Pittsburgh would seem to provide an excellent occasion for getting social information into the news. The State Conference of Social Work held in Pittsburgh in February, 1935, was well reported, but the chief news lay in the speech of the newly appointed state emergency relief administrator who had little to tell about policies or problems at that time. work were treated technically in discussion meetings of this conference and did not get into the news.

The analysis of what the radio public was told is based on approximately 70 radio programs.* These were all presented by local privately supported social agencies. Juvenile Delinquency 

The Penal Affairs Committee, in a radio interview, advocated a new crime prevention service. Its aim would be to protect youth with properly supervised poolrooms and dance halls, and to serve as a liaison bureau between public and private agencies in helping to build good citizens. It was proposed that the police force carry on the experiment. 

The Committee's legislative program, described in another broadcast, proposed to raise the juvenile court age from sixteen to eighteen years. Boys and girls of this age group are still immature and should not go to criminal courts or reformatories. Children charged with murder should be sent to the juvenile courts for trial. Parents should be permitted to bring to the court wayward and disorderly children above sixteen not charged with specific crimes or misdemeanors. Court and prison reforms were also advocated. The talk covered a long list of briefly explained items in the penal program. 

Services of Community Fund agencies to problem boys referred by courts, schools, and parents, have reduced the bill for juvenile delinquency by 20 per cent, according to a speaker representing the Catholic Boy Welfare Bureau. 

Cost of this follow-up and crime prevention work is $10.12 per boy-year while the cost in correctional institutions, detention homes, etc., is $425-$500 per boy-year.

Volunteers, mostly fathers of families, serve as big brothers. A story is told of a boy, unemployed, turned out of the house by his father, and planning to tramp through the West. Service to Radio Listeners

Only in a few instances were radio listeners regarded as a public whose members might use the services of social agencies. Here are several examples of talks which offered service or gave advice and useful information to the radio audience. "Service to the Hard of Hearing" explained the difference between the deaf and the hard of hearing. 

The League for Hard of Hearing helps the discouraged make use of a remnant of hearing, and prepares them for vocational and recreational activities in which they can succeed. They are the first group of handicapped people to work out a solution of their own problem independently of the more fortunate who are not handicapped. Lip reading is the first aid. By-products of lip reading are self-reliance, quickening of mental facilities, better understanding of human nature, and loss of inferiority.

Vocational advice is given according to age and type of hearing difficulty. The adequately trained among them usually do a better job than those not handicapped, as illustrated by the story of a young woman who completed twenty-four weeks' business school course in seventeen weeks, and was promoted by her employer in one month, instead of two as agreed if she made good. Recreation is important in rehabilitation of hard of hearing.

Public health nursing service is available to listeners as the following summary of a talk makes clear:

The newer knowledge of disease prevention is taught to families by the Public Health Nursing Association. The nurses teach not only how to care for sick but the principles of personal hygiene, home sanitation, disease prevention, and health promotion. This is especially important in conservation of maternal and child health. Six to seven mothers die for every 1,000 babies born alive in Pittsburgh. Two-thirds of these deaths could be prevented. Among cases cared for by 

P.H.N.A., only two to three mothers die for every 1,000 babies born. A young couple who followed nurse's instructions saved their frail, almost lifeless baby, now a husky normal little girl. A mother was taught how to care for herself so she could nurse her baby boy who grew strong as a result. 

Nurses also give nutrition instructions, illustrated by story of two children who gained on skimmed milk and oatmeal. 

The nurses' services are open to all. The fee for those who can pay is $1,00 per visit. In five months this year, 108,000 visits were made to 31,000 patients. 

One talk offered useful information about the emotionally unadjusted child to those not dismayed by the psychologist's use of such sentences as, "An instinct may be througt of as being composed of three parts: a stimulus, a reaction and a feeling tone or emotion.!" The speaker told that: 

The emotional maladjustment of an adult goes back to childhood where symptoms should be recognized. We must understand the emotional development of the child, and methods of dealing with emotional problems, like shyness, stealing, temper tantrums, which may indicate the child's need for affection, security, recognition, or independence. Then try to satisfy this need in a healthy way. Often the brightest children, rather than the dull, have emotional behavior problems. Treatment varies according to the individual. The child is torn between the desire to achieve independence and the desire to remain in the irresponsible state of infancy. Prime needs of childhood are security, feeling of being wanted, loved, of being part of the family group, of being understood. Things which undermine a child's security are marital disharmony between parents, too strict discipline, nagging, inconsistent handling, unfavorable comparison with brothers and sisters. 

Parent-teacher associations, parent education and child study groups help parents understand the child's needs also psychiatrists, the mental hygiene clinic, and the child guidance clinic which sponsored the talk.

Other Topics 

The Goodwill Industries, in a series of programs chiefly musical, made direct appeals for clothing and furniture for specific families whose need of these particular articles was vividly described. The Pennsylvania Association for the Blind in a weekly program, presented blind entertainers, and told about its workshop and entertainment service, and now and then briefly referred to its preventive work. 

We know that other agencies whose services are widely familiar, like those of the Salvation Army, were on the air more or less, and we may assume that they presented a readily understood picture of help given to persons in distress.

With the addition of scattered spot announcements and occasional programs, we believe that we now have before us a fairly representative picture of what social agencies told the radio public during the ten months of the Study. 

Appraisal of Content of Radio Programs It is hard to guess what the radio audiences might have learned from So much depends on what the listeners contributed to the these broadcasts. educational process.

Some talks called for little more than ready sympathy; others demanded close attention and ability to think in abstract terms to which only the "socially minded" are accustomed, or to follow the thread of a case story in which several problems, and four or five characters are involved. 

Certain of the programs would be sure to find a public, although not a very large one, made up of people already responsive toward the topic or the

agency. Those who like the kinds of games described in the Recreo Hour must have enjoyed these programs. Requests for furniture and clothing to meet specific needs, whether made over the radio or in newspapers, nearly always find their way to persons who are glad to respond. The Boy Scouts have their own public, no doubt enlarged for the story of adventure and courage in their radio pageant. Broadcasts like those on the home customs of foreign born groups would find listeners among those who travel or read of these countries, and among the foreign born themselves. 

When we turn from these readily accepted themes to the talks about social programs, it is harder to visualize any considerable number of listeners who would grasp and retain the facts and stories for later retelling. Well dis- posed listeners without following closely any details of the talks might perhaps have thought, "They do a lot of good - these agencies." They also may have become familiar with phrases like medical care, foster home, keeping families together, and so have become more responsive to campaign appeals for support of such services. To some, the specific instances of unmet needs which were cited, might have been compelling. 

Although the value of trained case workers was stated many times, it was asserted rather than made clear. The number and variety of examples of needs and services cited to show case work, might, in so rapid a recital, create an impression of social workers moving people about like puppets, in and out of hospitals, foster homes, and clinics. One might readily conclude that a social worker would have to know her way around to do all this, but scarcely be convinced that she needs long and expensive training. 

The service figures would be difficult to remember and would not tell the listener anything about the size of a particular problem or make clear the extent to which it was being met or neglected, especially since these figures always related to a single agency.

Commercial broadcasters repeat a single idea again and again with variations in illustration or approach from day to day or week to week. The informative broadcasts of the social agencies were largely single presentations of certain ideas or information. The Human Values talks, which had a degree of continuity, were spread thin to cover as many agencies as possible. More experienced broadcasters also use a very simple and limited idea and much repetition in a given program, always providing some tie to the experience and interest of the radio listener. The broadcasts which have been summarized here cover so much ground and shift emphasis so often that the listener has no chance to get a firm hold on the theme. 

Accepting the programs reviewed as a fair sample, we would conclude that the radio had been used effectively to present familiar and readily accepted needs and services, and ineffectively to enlighten radio listeners on social questions insufficiently understood. 

We have seen also that a number of important topics which could be made interesting to radio listeners, as health, housing, and unemployment relief, were scarcely mentioned at all.

Greater Pittsburgh

Business men who read Greater Pittsburgh, monthly organ of the Chamber of Commerce, were informed about only two social problems in the 10 issues examined, namely housing and health. 

An article on "The National Housing Movement," by Charles F. Lewis discusses three divisions of the government housing program subsistence homesteads, slum clearance, and FHA - with slum clearance receiving the most space. 

Mr. Lewis does not believe that the subsistence homesteads and slum clearance projects of the New Deal will provide better housing for the poor. 

He can see many obstacles to the success of the PWA's slum clearance program, chief among them the huge cost. No one knows how to build urban housing of acceptable standards in the eastern cities of the U.S. to rent on a sound basis for less than $10 "per room a month," but such a rent would be impossible for the present slum dwellers, most of whom are on relief rolls. Government subsidies for building costs would, therefore, have to aggregate 100 per cent and frequent subsidies for operating costs would be necessary. The attempt to provide new housing sufficient to take care of all who need it would also bring serious social maladjustment -the classes above the relief clients would see that government-financed dwellings were more attractive than their own and demand their share of government money for building. "The realities strongly indicate that the most which may be hoped for from the proposed governmental subsidized housing is a series of demonstrations. These demonstrations, from the

standpoint of physical plan and management, will leave little to be desired. From the standpoint of sound economies, however, they will prove nothing, except that if you have houses to give away you can find people who will live in them."

This discussion is based on an analysis of 17 annual reports for 1934.

Case Work Agencies 

"The case worker takes people as she finds them, and helps them to make the best of themselves," wrote one superintendent of a case working agency in

her annual report. Some effort was made in several reports to tell what it was possible to do to help people in this way. 

The dilemma of the writer of these reports appeared to be a choice between over-simplifying case work, or, at the other extreme, making it appear too complex to be understood at all. Over-simplified is the explanation which uses the case worker's discovery that a child's irritability is due to bad teeth to illustrate her skill, and again the failure of a child to gain weight is traced to his not being sent to bed early. On the other hand, an explanation technical in concept and in expression, tells that the family agency "believes it is especially equipped through an integrated case work approach to give special service to families presenting problems of disorganization." Formerly this agency dealt more with inadequate personalities, and now more with adequate personalities in an unsatisfactory social situation. "A satisfactory adjustment was made" is the way one organization summed up a case work process and its results. 

Settlement Reports 

One settlement report told something of the philosophy of its work. 

Penetrating the social work vocabulary, and the graphs, charts and lists of statistics that feature its report, we learn that the Brashear Settlement staff is definitely striving to create community spirit on the South Side, a policy and practice worthy of note.

The Brashear staff, the report reveals, cooperated with local printers in inaugurating publication of a neighborhood weekly which not only helps to build community solidarity, but also serves as an excellent medium for Settlement publicity. The staff was also the moving spirit in two community festivals, one at Christmas and the other in May. The value of the festivals, the author feels, lies in the fact that the sponsoring committee, composed as it was of representatives of civic, religious, educational and business groups, brought together many sides of the neighborhood life.

In a report which is also an appeal for funds, Kingsley House tells that one branch of its work, Lillian Convalescent Rest, which cares for children suffering from malnutrition, and for women and girls recuperating from illness or operations, is so seriously in need of money that closing may be necessary.

Rest is the only solution to this problem. 

"Suppose the Rest were not open to the hosts of undernourished children who are the supremely tragic symbol and product of 

Depression?.... What Was Left Out 

There was a tendency to take for granted a familiarity on the part of readers with projects of the year instead of seizing this opportunity to describe them. Some of these are merely mentioned. A demonstration in its second year of nursing care for pneumonia, is referred to. A settlement mentions that "the annual neighborhood survey was conducted as usual during the spring months, giving statistics on nationality, religion, employment and relation to social agencies." The same report briefly states a predicament which might well be elaborated: "We are faced with the problem of either doing a big job and spreading it thin, or of choosing the most essential factors of our program and doing them well." 

One of the most baffling and least discussed social situations, that of unemployed youth, is disposed of somewhat too easily in this paragraph from one report:

"Our feeling is that the young people, with whom we come in contact, have adopted a position of resignation. It is our concern to analyze their situations carefully and to get them to face reality. By so doing we are able to take away their feeling of loneliness and that gnawing sense of personal blame for the inability to get a job and to attain a feeling of independence. By being friendly, by offering opportunities for recreation and by making helpful suggestions, we attempt to instill a sane appreciation and to create a healthier attitude toward things in general." 

For the most part reports give an accounting of the amount of work accomplished and the money spent, and express appreciation of the support and volunteer service received. They do not attempt to interest readers in outstanding current needs and services. One gets an impression that writers set down accustomed phrases about what their agencies find to do and why they do it, instead of reviewing thoughtfully the experience for the year covered to see just what it contains of enough significance to be reported.

Public Assistance 

Newspapers on their own responsibility sought out and brought to their readers' attention information on old age assistance, especially at the time of the passage of the new state law. They gave information of help to those who would be benefitted and told human interest stories to the general reader. 

Social security was the theme of the State Conference of Social Work meeting in Pittsburgh in February, 1935. In none of its aspects was it interpreted through any other channels than these meetings and the newspapers, so far as we could discover.

The proposed state legislation to set up county welfare boards, a most important and urgently needed reform, was described and supported in a well organized set of talking points which should have made a strong appeal to persons with an intelligent interest in public affairs. The same facts were told in the newspapers, in printed matter widely distributed, and in talks to many organizations; also, on one or two occasions, to radio listeners. Except for two feature stories in which the writer warmed up to his theme, the presentation although logical and convincing, was without human interest. Very little use was made of specific instances or close-up views of conditions found under existing forms of poor relief. Throughout this period there was evidence of a lack of resources for a vigorous educational campaign to arouse in the public a sense of the urgent need of the legislation. 

Public assistance, in all its forms, is so little understood and is of such growing importance that an educational program on the subject would seem to be urgently needed.

Housing Several important housing studies provided significant facts and figures about housing conditions in Pittsburgh for educational use during this period. 

This wealth of material, plus greatly increased public interest in housing

because of the government's activities in this field, opened the way for a vigorous program of public education on a long standing and all too familiar social problem. Apparently these advantages were offset by other factors with the result that housing conditions, although brought to the attention of many different groups, did not take on great public importance as a topic. 

The emphasis in what was told was on existing conditions, and on minimum standards for good housing, rather than on specific plans for the immediate future. The complexity of the low cost housing problem which makes it so difficult to explain, may have accounted for the lack of more discussion of future plans. The prolonged period of uncertainty regarding the proposed PWA 

Pittsburgh housing project was also a factor in delaying efforts to interest the public in housing plans for the low income section of the population. 

Hospitals and Public Health 

We have indications, although not much evidence, that each of a number of hospitals kept a group, closely concerned with its affairs, in touch with its needs and accomplishments through meetings, and in a few instances, occasional bulletins. Hospitals were also occasionally in the news or favorably mentioned to radio audiences. 

The financial situation of the hospitals and the shortage of beds for free patients, were brought to the attention of an inner group of physicians, hospital superintendents and social workers, and to newspaper readers, at the time when the hospital study report was made public. The sponsoring committee which took under consideration the recommendations of the Social Study did not undertake any public education during this period. 

The Public Health Appraisal, in its chapter on Community Health Instruction, indicated a much wider use of lectures, pamphlets, bulletins, and the press, for public information during 1933 than appeared to be the case during 1934 and 1935. The Pittsburgh Health Department's monthly bulletin had been

dropped when our study began. 

Reduced budgets probably accounted for lessened activity in the whole field of health education. We did not find any evidence of active campaigns on such topics as cancer, diphtheria, heart disease, or the venereal diseases, under either public or private auspices, and only a moderate amount of educational work on tuberculosis was noted. Our study did not take account of health education in the public schools, nor of the educational work of insurance companies, the Dairy Council, and other semicommercial organizations.

Child Welfare 

The impression gained from news stories, radio talks, and other material studied, was that the most serious current problem of children was the condition of malnutrition found among children on relief. Shortage of funds, we learned, curtailed the services to neglected and dependent children. Through case stories and various brief articles, we learned something of modern foster home care.

Educational work on child guidance was carried out extensively among parent groups. On the whole, child welfare problems merged into other topics as social case work, relief work, public health, and delinquency. 

Social Case Work 

Case stories and statements in annual reports gave brief glimpses of what case working agencies are trying to do. These explanations were especially effective in describing the tools the case worker uses, arrangements for health and clinic treatment, a housekeeper's service in an emergency, a temporary job. What was told otherwise about social case work is no negligible that we have only to summarize what was left untold. Why are graduates of schools of social work rapidly filling more and more positions in Pittsburgh family and children's agencies? What of the greatly increased skill and resourcefulness which training and careful selection of personnel is bringing to bear on problems of dependent children, and of families in trouble? Only members of a small inner circle are given answers to these questions. 

In a period when mass treatment of people on relief is all too widely accepted as a reasonable economy, there is added reason to explain how differently trouble and hardship affect different families and different

persons. The case worker has an obligation to interpret the needs of people she deals with as well as to gain an acceptance of her own techniques. As the more familiar services connected with relief giving recede farther into the background of the private case working agency's job, or are transferred altogether to the public agency, interpretation of what these newer services are, and why they should be extended, would seem to be not merely important, but necessary.

Conclusion 

It would be both unfair and unwise to base any conclusions on this analysis of the content of public information without knowing what factors in the organization of social work itself might have accounted for a given emphasis or omission, or for the use of one outlet and the failure to use others. Also, the suggestions submitted as to what the public should be told, should be considered in relation to current situations existing in social work. 

We know that in a period of transition, and of rapid changes in policy, such as public social work has been undergoing, there is a natural tendency to postpone interpretation until things settle down and procedures become more clearly defined. On some occasions when it would seem that social workers could clarify public thinking, the supporting figures and evidence to make their statements convincing cannot be assembled at the moment when they are most needed. Again, the continued existence of a few sub-standard agencies working side by side with those who are doing excellent work creates embarrassment in asking the public to demand these higher standards. Because of these limitations, the results of this study of content should be useful primarily to those who know best the social work of the community, and to those who are planning and directing social programs. 

Certain impressions gained from this experimental analysis might have been expected. Thus, in other cities, as well as Pittsburgh, political

aspects of relief dominated the news. Again, there would be no reason to expect in Pittsburgh, or elsewhere, that a great deal of interesting and useful information on current social programs would be presented in popular form, since no planning is done for an orderly and integrated presentation. machinery has been set up for carrying on social work interpretation of this kind. No Results of the analysis which were unexpected, at least in degree, were: 

The obscure position in the articles or speeches of which they were a part of many of the passages which have been quoted or summarized; the small percentage of interpretative material in relation to the total volume of words; and finally, the lack of variety, frequency and force, with which important facts were set forth.

If any one member of the Pittsburgh public, knowing little or nothing about social work, had chanced upon all the fragments of information reproduced in these pages, we believe he might have been considerably enlightened on a fairly wide range of social problems. We may be sure that no one person could have encountered more than a small part of it. We may conclude, therefore, that social workers have before them the task of building almost from the beginning a program of public education which keeps pace with advances in social work, is well balanced in its coverage of important social problems, and adapted to the interests and understanding of the publics to which it is addressed.

Outlets for Social Information The main outlets or channels for reaching the general public are the daily newspaper and the radio. Selected groups with special interests of their own, and in some cases, special relationships with social work, are reached through class publications, house organs, meetings, and special events of various kinds. We have given some attention to these outlets in connection with the study of what the public was told in a ten-month period. Here our aim is to find answers to such questions as: How large a public may be reached through a given outlet? What characteristics has it which present advantages or limitations to its use as a medium for expressing social information? How can each of these outlets be used more effectively?

Opportunities for Social Work Interpretation 

The Pittsburgh newspapers have shown great willingness to cooperate with social agencies. In our conferences with editors they expressed their desire to report social work news, and they have given generous support to the Community Fund campaign. Special events, such as anniversary celebrations, have been well publicized. Editorial comment in support of social measures has been on occasion, vigorous. Editorials in praise of social agencies and their leaders have been numerous. With the outstanding exception of the attack on a Y.W.C.A. worker by one newspaper, news treatment during the period of the Study was favorable to privately supported social work. 

Although the newspaper is an important, and, on the whole a willing outlet for social work information, the space it has to give is decidedly limited Ordinarily a large proportion of the space devoted to news is used for material received from Washington and other sources through national press services. 

Syndicated columns and features interpret topics of the day. State news bureaus, and during legislative sessions, Harrisburg correspondents, are also important news sources. The space left for local news must be divided between politics, crime, local government, human interest stories, and many other topics, as well as departmental news 

sports, society, business.

Another serious limitation for social work in using the newspapers as a channel of social information is that news values lie chiefly in conflict, the unusual, or the dramatic. To bring these elements into the headlines and the lead, the facts are often thrown out of balance. Again, much of what social workers want most to tell the public does not take news shape. 

With full recognition of these limiting conditions, we believe social workers can do much to improve their relations with the press. More total space than they now secure may not become available, but more effective use of what is to be had should be possible. 

Social Work News 

The most fruitful sources of local news are meetings, and special events, like exhibitions or visiting days and weeks. One characteristic of social agency news noted in the Study was that much more advance news was printed about coming events than reports after the event. Possibly the agencies themselves have encouraged this practice regarding newspaper mention primarily as an opportunity to advertise their meetings and dramatic performances. The newspaper cannot manage to cover all events, even the more important ones, and must depend on the agencies themselves to supply reports of what occured and what was said. An account after the event ordinarily gives much more opportunity for interpretation than the advance announcement. When social work agencies hold meetings at which interesting or important information is brought out the reports should be supplied to the press, usually by advance arrangement with newspaper editors. 

The social work conventions held during the Study period were reported, as a rule, in news stories consisting of a series of brief paragraphs giving a single point from each of a number of speeches but not enough of any one topic to make it interesting. These stories appeared to be based on abstracts of speeches supplied by the conference press service. Longer reports of a few

important speeches, with brief mention of such others as there is space for, would be much more effective. Such articles might be secured if the important speeches were properly spaced during the convention period, and also, if editors were told in advance what papers promised to have good material for their readers 

all this in addition to supplying abstracts of all speeches. 

Public agencies appear to have information with news value fairly often but lack means of getting ready for the newspapers anything but routine statistical reports. Private agencies are so numerous and their work is carried on in such small units as to lessen the significance of their activities to the general reader. As the practice of combining the resources of social agencies increases, social work should take a much more important place as a news source than it does now. In the meantime, groups of agencies with a common interest in a particular social situation or type of activity can combine their facts, figures, and comments to make more impressive and more interesting stories than single agencies can produce separately. 

Public Statements 

Newspapers often welcome statements from persons who can speak authoritatively on current questions. Both board members and executives in social work have the opportunity to express themselves on current social questions by this method. Perhaps this is done from time to time, but the only example found was a statement of protest by a group of individuals against the attack on the Y.W.C.A. in connection with the Youth Congress. 

In the present period of confusion growing out of the whole muddled relief situation, authoritative statements giving the important first-hand observation and judgment of the leaders of social work would help to clarify the thinking of the public. Often, editorial writers would welcome such statements as background material for editorials. 

More local cooperation with national agencies in bringing to the

attention of editors important information which has a local bearing might be desirable. Frequently, national agencies send news directly to the newspapers, or through press associations, material of importance, and at the same time supply copies to local interested agencies. Newspapers would often pay more attention to this material if its local interest and value was brought to their attention.

Opportunities for Feature Articles 

Series of from three to five feature articles covering rather fully some one subject of timely interest appeared from time to time in all of the Pittsburgh dailies. Sunday editions also carried single feature articles. work contains excellent and unused material for such articles. Social Each of the newspapers has capable special writers who would no doubt welcome the help of social agencies in collecting source material and presenting different points of view. Exclusive ideas for a single article or a series of articles should be submitted to each of the newspapers in the course of the year.

The feature article is an especially good medium for follow-up stories on social studies. It is well adapted to discussion of current changes, such as those which are taking place in social work as a profession. 

Human Interest Stories 

Pittsburgh newspapers are unusually cooperative with social agencies in protecting clients from being identified in news stories in unfortunate ways. 

This is especially true in their policy of not printing the names of young persons who get into court. This attitude increases the responsibility of social agencies to supply such human interest stories as they can without identifying people. It is to their advantage to have stories published which illustrate the greater understanding of human behavior and the greater respect for personality which characterizes social work today. very little. treatment of people in trouble was shaped in an earlier period and has changed Terms like "waif" and "poor unfortunates" in the headlines which treat the poor as a class, are not in keeping with the present methods and attitudes in social work, but no change can be expected until social workers themselves help to bring it about. 

A News Sense 

Whether newspaper publicity takes the form of news, statements, feature articles, or human interest stories, it should have a quality of timeliness. 

Perhaps one of the weakest elements in all of our social work newspaper publicity is that it fails to get into the current of what is being talked about. 

Now and then topics on which wider public understanding is important, break into the news perhaps unexpectedly and drop out again before social workers have sensed the opportunity to offer the newspapers their specific facts or experience bearing on the situation. 

The Radio as a Channel for Public Information Pittsburgh has, or had, at the time of this Study, five radio stations, three belonging to national chains, and two unaffiliated stations. The 1930 census listed approximately 81,000 radios among Pittsburgh's 155,000 families. 

Among habitual radio listeners, there are probably many persons who do not read newspapers regularly, so that social work presumably increases its general public in telling its story over the air as well as through the press. 

Opportunities for Broadcasting

Although the stations have a limited amount of free time to give, social agencies appear to have as many opportunities to broadcast as they can use to advantage. In addition to regular weekly programs like "Human Values," there are frequent opportunities to appear on such programs as the "Home Forum"

hour, women's hour, and news broadcasts, and the possibility of arranging special programs on important occasions. 

Types of Programs Desired 

Directors of the five stations were in fairly general agreement on the types of programs they would welcome from social agencies. Three of them expressed a preference for interviews between the announcer and speaker, or for a dialogue between two persons. With varying degrees of emphasis, all disapproved of amateur dramatizations of social work. These radio plays, if given at all, must be written by well qualified professional writers, they said, and performed by professional radio actors. They also agreed that talks should be brief, and preferably preceded and followed by good music or other entertainment. By a brief talk is meant one lasting about five minutes. 

Anywhere from three to seven minutes of speaking time on a fifteen minute program would be acceptable. One director pointed out that "spots" between programs provide an excellent opportunity for 20 or 25 word announcements. 

The requirements of the broadcasters are that the request for a broadcast should be made from four to six weeks ahead of time, and that manuscripts and details of programs be received five days in advance of the broadcast. 

Quality of music must be up to radio standards, except when children are performing, when a lower standard is accepted. The station must have five days in which to check on copyrights and reach agreements with unions about musical programs. 

They would welcome a central clearing service for all social agency programs. 

The Radio Programs of Social Agencies 

Talks seem to be the type of program preferred, or at least the form most Those submitted for the purpose of the Study used by the agencies themselves.

varied widely in the degree to which the material itself and the style were adapted to the miscellaneous and casual radio audience. For the most part speakers told about agencies and their services. A few programs have been noted which offered entertainment or information for use. The interviews submitted for study were much more interesting in manuscript form than the straight talks. 

Suitable Programs for Social Work 

The radio does not seem nearly so well suited to publicizing the individual agencies, as it does to bringing before an important section of the public such social problems and services as may be of direct value to the listeners, or of timely interest to them. 

Those agencies which have "information for use" to offer are in the best position to reach the public through the radio. Programs of health information are especially well adapted to radio. Group work agencies might sponsor a series of programs to tell about the local resources for recreation. The budgeting of small incomes, and child guidance are other topics on which social work has helpful ideas to give radio listeners. 

The interview, for which the program directors expressed a preference, should be an especially good method of interpreting some aspects of the Social Study in a series of broadcasts. Interpretation of the relief situation, and information about volunteer opportunities in social work, are topics which could be well handled in interviews. 

Radio forums in which three or more persons discuss a timely question informally would seem to be especially well suited to interpretation of current situations. Since the Study, KDKA has launched its round table, or "town meeting" which seems to be an unusually successful program of this kind. 

Social workers have very little time to prepare radio programs. Aside from the time factor, however, one gets the impression that social workers do

not expect the quality or finish of their programs to be judged by standards set for commercial programs. The competition with the commercially sponsored programs is so direct and immediate that unrehearsed speeches, presented in technical phrases, and delivered without much warmth or ease of manner, are in too great contrast with neighboring programs. Since the chances of definitely displeasing radio listeners with poor programs must be considered, the radio may be listed as a liability rather than a "resource" for social work interpretation, unless more time, thought, and skill are devoted to its effective use.

Class Publications 

Daily and Sunday newspapers go to all sections of the community and to nearly all types of people. Class publications are issued weekly, or less frequently, and appeal to particular neighborhoods or sections of the population. Many of these are trade papers. 

Pittsburgh has its full share of class publications, some listed in Ayer's Directory with impressive circulation figures. There are the weeklies of general circulation, such as the Bulletin Index, and This Week in Pittsburgh; 

Catholic, Jewish, and Negro papers; foreign language, and neighborhood newspapers; trade journals, club magazines, and others. A number of partial lists were in use at the time of the Study, but there was no complete up-to-date classified list of these publications. 

The class publications are important outlets for social information from several viewpoints. Information adapted to the special interests of their respective readers can be made more specific and therefore more interesting than articles addressed to a general public. The fact that editorial standards are sometimes less exacting is an uncertain advantage, since hurriedly prepared material published in the form in which it is submitted is not so likely to be read.

Those agencies which sent material to class papers showed a tendency to send the same material to all, or at least several of them, regardless of their different interests and editorial policies. 

There should be more study of these papers to adapt material to the style and policies of the publications and to their particular groups of readers. 

As a common service to social agencies a complete card list of these papers is needed, giving the names of editors, publication dates, deadlines, and type of material used. Sample copies should also be filed. Such a listing and examination would no doubt bring out that some of the religious, trade and other special publications, have no space for any outside contributions; others would use more than they now receive. Out of the whole list the more important ones could be selected, and dealt with individually. 

Examples of Class Publications 

Among the papers which welcome social work information we have noted a few examples.

The Bulletin Index draws upon a middle and upper income group for its circulation. It covers music, theater, politics, society, business, and selects its news with an eye on the well-to-do and influential citizen. With such a circulation, it should be an important medium for information about social work. Its editor expressed an interest in reports of social research and in stories about social problems. The interpretative background which can be given in a publication of Bulletin Index type has a distinctive value to social work. Social agencies, the editor says, have not learned to recognize the Bulletin Index's special slant, and seldom offer suitable material. 

Although accentuated in this case, it is likely that editors of most newspapers and class publications would be happier if social workers and others were more conscious of their individual styles and policies.

This Week in Pittsburgh, a magazine for hotel guests, tells where to go and what to see. Its articles are necessarily brief, never more than a few paragraphs, and frequently limited to a sentence or so. Its news items are in most cases condensed versions of stories which have already appeared in the newspapers. 

Several social work organizations use This Week in Pittsburgh regularly, chiefly for the announcement of coming events, as, for example, the Boys' Club 

Fair, the International Institute's Old World Market, the Kingsley House May 

Festival. The Salvation Army and Y.M. and Y.W.H.A. campaigns are also heralded here; the photograph of the Y.M. and Y.W.H.A. campaign chairman graces the cover of one issue, and his biography together with his prediction that the drive will be successful, is presented a few pages later. 

The Jewish papers, of which the Jewish Criterion has the largest circulation, give considerable space to Jewish social agencies. Catholic newspapers publish all that is offered them by the Catholic Charities. Negro newspapers do the same for the Urban League, which, unfortunately cut off to a considerable extent from mention of its affairs in the daily papers, can at least reach its Negro constituencies through the Negro press, and does. 

The South Side Journal is "published weekly in the interests of the people of the South Pittsburgh district," and has a free circulation of some 6,000 copies. Brashear Settlement is editing the paper as an experiment in providing a community newspaper which helps to make the Settlement an integral part of the neighborhood. News of churches, the Legion, the schools, political clubs, and social clubs is covered, and the Settlement has it own special column. It is both a neighborhood paper and a settlement house organ. 

As a whole, the class publications render distinctive service for social work, as well as for their avowed constituencies, without duplicating functions of the newspapers.

82House Organs of Social Agencies 

Twenty-five social agencies reported the publication of 28 bulletins or house organs. This does not include bulletins reported as being temporarily discontinued and others about to be started. The number of copies distributed ranged from 150, the smallest number reported, to 40,000 copies of the quarterly bulletin of the Goodwill Industries. More than half were addressed to members and others who use the agencies' services, and the rest to inner groups of leaders and volunteer workers, to contributors, or to all three groups combined. The publication schedule included 13 house organs issued monthly, four at quarterly intervals, and others occasionally. Nine made no report of frequency of publication. 

In form, most of them are quite unpretentious. They are mimeographed or cheaply printed, and only a very few showed care and skill in editing and production. 

The Uses of House Organs 

Many of these bulletins serve a definite purpose in conveying information to a picked group not to be reached through the broader channels of the newspapers or class publications. There is hardly a question about the need of such publications on the part of settlements and other community centers, and of membership organizations like the Urban League or the League for the Hard of Hearing. Decision to publish such a bulletin on the part of other agencies calls for consideration of several elements. When the agency has something to say at frequent intervals to an already interested or potentially interested constituency, such an organ is very useful. Family and children's societies in a few large cities issue monthly bulletins which interpret their work very effectively to an inner group. 

If a number of agencies send house organs to a considerably overlapping

mailing list, probably all of them are less read, and may convey an impression of wastefulness. 

It seems inexcusable for even the simplest of these bulletins to receive careless editing and poor mimeographing, which not only make the house organ uninviting, but also reflect on the standards of the organization itself. 

Community centers in some instances at least, might use these bulletins as projects of clubs and classes of the centers. It seems likely that volunteer service could be secured for projects of this kind from persons with training and experience in advertising or journalism. The In applying for budget items for the publication of house organs, agencies in the Community Fund might well be asked to submit plans for competent editorship, pleasing design, and neat and readable appearance. 

Informer, of the Urban League, sets a good standard. It lives up to its title, and is good-looking and inexpensive. 

Two bulletins which deserve special attention are Human Values, and The Federator. Both speak for a large section of the social work field, but each has a distinct function. 

Human Values 

Human Values, the illustrated bulletin of the Community Fund, is issued monthly. The mailing list at the time of the Study consisted of approximately 

14,000 contributors of 10 dollars or more. Certain professional groups have been added to the list, and anyone who wishes may also receive the bulletins. 

It is difficult to edit a free publication for such a miscellaneous list of readers. The amount of the gift does not give any clue to the degree or nature of the contributor's interest in social work. The readers have in common only the fact that they have given money. The house organ has as its aim to tell them something of how their money is spent. 

It maintains a balance between pictures, text, general information,

editorial comment, news, and reports, in charts or statements of income and expenditures. Selection of the contents and form of treatment in the issues examined seemed to be aimed at the top third, the socially minded givers, rather than at the average general reader. The pictures, many of them excellent, have a much wider appeal. The form is both dignified and attractive. 

The value of a publication of this kind is a question on which there is much difference of opinion. The fact that an attempt a few years ago to determine reader interest brought many favorable replies, may be an argument in favor of continuing it in its present form. A more inexpensive and simpler publications like the small bulletin of the Dayton Chest called "Three Minutes," might do quite as well for such a large and mixed public. This might leave more time to spend experimentally on preparing material for the class publications in which information can be adapted to the particular interests of known groups of readers, and also release some money for other publicity uses. 

The Federator 

The Federator, published by the Federation of Social Agencies, has the advantage of a small and definite constituency, and performs a needed service in relation to this group. It keeps its professional delegates, and lay member delegates informed about current and coming events, and changes in personnel, policies, and services in the social work field. It seems reasonable that without changing its concentration on this particular group, it could to advantage enlarge its circulation to reach more of the social workers and lay persons to whom the information it contains would be useful and interesting. 

The editing of the Federator was not at the time of the Study the special assignment of anyone who could give much time and thought to planning ahead or to news gathering. Specific suggestions about editorial policy are contained in a separate memorandum to the Federation. What seems important to note here

is that good editing and full coverage of local social work news is sufficiently important to justify provision of the necessary editorial service. 

With the increased emphasis on county-wide social service there is an added problem of news coverage to make the Federator serve the county as a whole.

Other Printed Matter 

In addition to house organs, other forms of printed or duplicated material may be included among desirable resources for interpretation. These include: annual reports, reports of studies, booklets containing the essential facts about a movement or an agency, leaflets, and so on. Drastic cuts had been made in budgets for printed matter at the time of the Study so that apparently little of it was in use, and what there was showed rigid economy. 

Printed matter, or some other form of duplicated material, is indispensable to maintaining relationships between social agencies and their particular constituencies. An annual or a biennial report, attractive enough in appearance and content to be read, is important, not only to give an accounting of funds spent, but also to explain changes, progress, and plans. Studies, legislative programs, campaign appeals, all require some simple and easily read printed statements which can be placed in the hands of persons who are important to reach, and who can only be reached with certainty by mailing or delivering printed matter to them.

The idea that social agencies should not spend money for good printing is bound up with the discarded idea that direct relief is the purpose of all forms of social work. With this concept printed matter is looked upon as a form of "overhead," diverting funds from the purpose to which they were contributed. 

Public education is an essential part of social work, not a luxury, and its effective use of the printed word depends on inviting appearance and readable text. There is a great deal of waste in badly prepared and carelessly distributed printing.

Cooperating Organizations as Resources 

Most privately supported social agencies in Pittsburgh, and some public departments, have close affiliations with one or more religious, civic, fraternal, or educational organizations, of which there are many hundreds in the city. The relationship of the Junior League to the Public Health Nursing 

Association, as well as to several other social agencies, is an example. Some of these organizations give volunteer service, supply equipment, or take active leadership in legislative campaigns. Others merely receive speakers from social agencies at their meetings. Many local clubs are associated with national bodies which are committed to certain social programs. Rotary 

International is interested in boys' work, and the League of Women Voters in extending civil service to public welfare departments. 

These organizations can be of the greatest help in the spread of public understanding and interest in social work. Since most of them, however, have programs of their own which occupy the greater part of their attention, social agencies can count on securing only a small margin of unassigned time and interest for special causes. This margin of potentially available interest varies widely with different organizations. Some can give only perfunctory attention to campaign talks. Others might take on important projects to be carried out by committees of members. Some would be much more interested in one branch of social work than in others. 

Recently a committee of the Pittsburgh Social Work Publicity Council began work on a classified card list of organizations. It was intended to compile information for these cards which would indicate an organization's special interests, what departments it has for any form of social service, the characteristics of its membership, the types of meetings held and their frequency. If information of this kind is collected in some central place, social agencies through a speakers' bureau of their own, could plan their

speaking programs intelligently. They might also join in encouraging the outside organizations to specialize in their service to social work. 

Sources of Social Information for Publicity Use 

Part One of this Study, reviewed the social information offered the public during one ten-month period and cited some important topics on which very little was said. Whether or not the public is told what most needs telling is a matter of chance under a system of unplanned publicity. The odds are in favor of the use of information which is easiest to present and that which lends itself to expression in accustomed phrases. "What we do," a few illustrative case stories, the monthly or yearly service figures, and a statement of purposes of the organization, make up the content of typical articles and speeches.

Pooling Ideas and Information 

The source material for effective interpretation of needs and services is sometimes found in the files of a single agency, but more often it can be brought together from the records or observations of a group of agencies. 

Meetings, collected reports, technical conference papers, and replies to questions, may provide suitable material. Family societies, children's agencies, and settlements could all throw some light on the current misconception that people on relief do not want to work. 

You are not likely to find in current popular magazines, articles about clubs, trade organizations, government bureaus, or "agencies" of any kind. 

You will find such subjects as teachers' oaths, stolen goods, the crisis for our youth. "Strength in Family Life," a title used in a social work magazine, suggests one of many such topics which would afford an opportunity for interesting treatment of social case work. How social case workers look for and find what there is to build on in restoring a family to self-reliance

could be its theme. This use of topics crossing the line of the interests of the work and experience of separate agencies, is not only more interesting than articles about individual agencies, but it also offers a fresh approach to a public which has come to associate publicity about social agencies solely with appeals for money.

To secure and use material of this kind effectively, some one whose primary job is interpretation, should be in close touch with the current movements in the general field of social work and current social situations. 

To tap the less obvious resources for social work publicity involves finding a way to get at the thinking, the observations, and the collected experience of social workers as these relate to questions of current interest, The different divisions of the Federation now provide for pooling of knowledge and opinion to improve the practice of social work. It is to be hoped that out of the work of these divisions will come much more joint interpretation of social needs and services to supplement, and, to some extent, to replace the agency-by-agency method of telling the story. 

Studies as Source Material 

Information already collected through social studies and routine compilations, such as the service figures collected for the U.S. Children's Bureau, is an obvious and important source of public information, but by no means an easy one to use effectively. Most of the data contained in these studies is technical, both in form and in the purpose for which it is intended. This is well illustrated in Social Study reports. These contain a great deal of material not for general circulation; but interspersed with the more confidential or technical material are facts and figures well suited to publicity. 

Making use of Social Study reports requires careful selection, translation into simpler terms, and in some instances, assembling the material in new groupings which cross the lines of separate reports. It is equally true of

much other technical material, that it needs "processing" before it is ready to be turned into popular articles. This intermediate step in dealing with source material for public consumption is illustrated in the abstracting of scientific articles for press use. To attempt interpretation of reports without getting the material into an easy form for reporters and special writers to use, is to waste a very good opportunity for public education. It is not just a perfunctory task of preparing a few newspaper releases, but a special sort of job to be done with skill, judgment and imagination. 

Repetition is an important factor in securing and holding interest in the results of studies. A report is not news for very long after it has been made public, but there is still an opportunity to reshape it in many other forms. 

When the Social Study reports are all assembled, it should be possible to prepare in addition to news releases, one or more series of public lectures, outlines for discussion groups, a number of radio programs in the form of interviews or forums, special articles, and charts to illustrate articles. 

Provisions for full use of the results of studies in these varied forms This is seldom made, partly because it requires the time and skill of some one who can humanize and enliven technical reports without destroying accuracy. somewhat obvious situation is emphasized here in the hope that full recognitior will be given to the importance of providing this service. 

Publicity Service 

Publicity service for private social work is provided by the publicity department of the Community Fund, and by part-time publicity workers in a few agencies. Public departments - health, welfare and emergency relief employed no one for this purpose, nor were staff members with other duties assigned to it at the time of the Study. Interpretation of those subjects which are the common interest of public and private agencies is, in a few instances, the responsibility of a committee or an organization for social action, such as

the Penal Affairs Committee, or the Housing Association; but even when such a sponsoring group exists, interpretation is an added duty of a busy executive or staff member. No service is available for joint efforts of interpretation in the fields of health, recreation, dependency, and other major branches of social work, except as an incidental part of Community Fund publicity, which is, in the main, directed toward money raising. 

Community Fund and Member Agencies 

In Pittsburgh, and elsewhere, the division of responsibility for publicity between the community funds and their member agencies has never been very clearly defined. It is generally accepted that it is neither practicable nor wise for the Community Fund to carry the whole load. In Pittsburgh, the agencies are expected to interpret their own work, with some assistance from the Community Fund, and to participate in the joint interpretation conducted by the Fund. 

Agency executives consider interpretation an extra job, one for which they have little time to spare in their crowded programs, and for which they have not been trained. Appropriations to these agencies for publicity services have been discontinued during the depression years. Certain agencies receive stimulation and assistance as members of national organizations which supply ideas, and sometimes ready-to-use material, such as plays for the radio, and printed matter with space for a local imprint. 

Interpretation of their work to board members is accepted by some executives as part of their administrative responsibility, and this part of the task is well done. In fact, to some of them the board member group reaches to the outer rim of the circle that constitutes their "public." especially group work agencies, devote their efforts to the groups which use their services. Others,

Publicity committees are set up from time to time by some of the agencies. 

It is not easy for these volunteer committees to function without a plan, a budget, or staff leadership for their efforts. Individual board members and volunteers are giving active publicity service to a few agencies. 

In spite of all these limitations it should be said that many executives expressed a keen desire to do a better job of interpretation, and have welcomed suggestions and help. Several have encouraged members of their staffs who show some ability for writing or speaking to develop their skill through study and practice. The fact that 80 persons attended a two-day institute on publicity methods conducted by the Public Relations Section of the Social Work Publicity Council, indicates a definite interest in the subject. 

Publicity Department of the Community Fund 

The publicity department of the Community Fund, with a full time staff of two members, and a varying amount of additional staff service during part of the year, devotes practically full time to campaign publicity for about four months of the year. During the remaining months, its major activities include the publication of the monthly house organ, Human Values, regular newspaper releases, one or more weekly radio programs, a poster service to industries (all of these continued during the campaign also,) and in addition, assistance to the member agencies in their own publicity. The service to the member agencies includes advice and help in preparing articles or talks, active assistance in connection with publicity for special events, and some training of staff members of agencies in writing case stories. It is proposed to extend this training service to other techniques. 

The time consuming factors in a publicity service of this scope are not fully appreciated or provided for. In addition to the more highly skilled work there are many chores, such as making arrangements to place special

articles, arranging details in connection with special broadcasts, designing printed matter and seeing it through production, compiling mailing lists and other necessary lists and keeping them up to date, making advance arrangements for meetings, and so on. The major job of writing and editing has to be done in the atmosphere of continual interruption which these tasks involve. Some of the writing is of a routine nature, but some of it is of the more creative kind which cannot be rushed through under great pressure. 

The Community Fund publicity department occupies a position between the member agencies on the one side, and the giving public on the other. Its staff is more closely associated with the problem of public support, than it is with social work. Moreover, the department has access to the public by way of editors, radio program directors, program chairmen of clubs, and other intermediaries who have definite ideas of what they want and how it should be presented, which do not always agree with the social workers' point of view. 

Because of this position, also because of pressure of work and the lack of easy access to the best source material, the Community Fund publicity department is not in a good position to plan and carry out in its own name interpretation which look toward better understanding of current social work and the important changes in it which are taking place. Also, because of this position, the service of the Fund publicity department is indispensable to the shaping and carrying out of such a program. 

Volunteers for Publicity Service 

Opportunities for volunteers in publicity service for both skilled and unskilled helpers are many and varied. Social work interpretation can take on a dramatic form which calls for the help of playwrights, actors, directors, and designers. Much of it can be presented through photographs, motion picture films, sketches, cartoons, and other pictorial forms. Writers, speakers, good organizers, can be extremely useful.

Pittsburgh appears to have its full share of talented writers, artists, musicians, and little theatre groups, who might, and perhaps do, contribute service to social work. It is not easy and seldom time-saving to take advantage of volunteer service, but it is sometimes possible to obtain a much higher degree of skill through this means than is available to social work in any other way. The problem is to be able to choose one's volunteers as one would an employed staff and to get them to accept whatever oversight or criticism of their work is necessary. 

Social Work Publicity Council 

For several years the Federation has sponsored an informally organized group now known as the Social Work Publicity Council. Its purpose is to raise standards in interpretation. The Publicity Council is one of about 17 such groups throughout the country, loosely affiliated with the national Social Work Publicity Council which serves as a clearing house of publicity information and ideas. Most of these local councils are sponsored either by community funds or councils of social agencies. They are the expression of a growing recognition on the part of social agencies of their common concern in bringing about wider and more intelligent public interest in social problems. 

A steering committee plans programs for meetings of the Pittsburgh Publicity Council and manages its affairs. A chairman is elected annually by members of the group. The publicity director of the Community Fund acts as secretary. The present membership is not very clearly defined. The mailing list for its meetings consists of names of persons who responded to an invitation to take part in one of the three sections of the Council setup for 1935. 

These are concerned with interpretations to boards; inter-agency interpretation; and public relations. This last section concerns itself chiefly with helping the members to increase their skill in the use of publicity techniques.

During 1935, the Council participated actively in the Study of Social Work Interpretation. Its chairman and members of the steering committee have helped to collect material from the individual agencies and have kept in touch with the progress of the Study. There has been considerable discussion of the purposes, methods, and possible reorganization of the Publicity Council itself. 

As it now exists, the Pittsburgh Council is not fully representative of both public and private social work. There is nothing in its form of organization, however, to prevent its becoming more inclusive. Also, its steering committee might bring together a more representative planning group, and already has this idea in mind. While the publicity department of the Community Fund works closely with the Publicity Council, there is nothing in the way in which the Council is set up to assure the Fund any share in its control. 

The fact that this Council has no staff assistance, and depends on the volunteer efforts of busy executives and staff workers, prevents it from functioning continually or altogether effectively. Since there is no immediate pressure to carry out its program of improving the quality of publicity and stimulating new projects of interpretation, its continued existence or usefulness is always uncertain.

Part Three Recommendations 

The recommendations which follow are directed toward these objectives: 

1 - To provide sponsorship for campaigns of public education on social problems which especially require public understanding and interest for their solution. 

2 To give more substance to the content of social information, and to 

enliven it, through providing a service of collecting and making available for publicity uses important and timely facts, figures, specific instances, and other source material. 

3 To make possible: (1) more effective use of existing resources for

The Placing of Responsibility 

The proposals here submitted for a division of work among the agencies concerned can at best merely suggest the general outlines of a flexible plan to be adapted to the social programs of which interpretation is a part. 

Certainly none of the recommendations submitted are workable unless the Federation of Social Agencies and the Community Fund find in them the basis for a plan in which their common interest goes much deeper than surface agreements. We see no way to stake out a field of effort with sharply drawn lines separating the respective responsibilities of these two coordinating agencies. 

However, the plan which follows contemplates a definite assignment of specific tasks as part of a broad program of community-wide interpretation. 

Community Fund 

The Community Fund recognizes a responsibility for carrying on publicity with the cooperation of its member agencies, to secure and maintain financial

support. In efforts directed toward this end, the Fund itself is in the best position to interpret the decisions of the budget committee; report on how the Community Fund money is spent for different types of services; interpret the standards of work which the Fund seeks to maintain; point out serious needs not taken care of by present budgets and explain the need for supporting private social work in a period of rapid growth of social services supported by taxation.

It is recommended that the Community Fund carry on a year round program of interpretation on these topics. It is also recommended that an outline of the Fund's publicity program for six months or a year ahead be prepared and duplicated so that the Fund itself, the Federation, and other agencies concerned, may have copies for reference use. 

It is recommended that the Community Fund plan the keynote and the talking points of its campaign publicity well in advance and in consultation with representatives of social agencies. In this connection it would be an advantage if directly after the campaign the Community Fund publicity department would collect the clippings, samples of publicity used, and records of campaign events, in a convenient form for study as a basis for follow-up work and for decisions regarding next year's campaign publicity. 

The Community Fund should encourage and assist its member agencies to publicize their work, but should center its own publicity program on important and timely questions such as those listed above, drawing upon individual agencies for illustrative material as needed. 

Publicity Service of the Community Fund 

A common service should be supplied by the publicity department of the Community Fund for all Fund and Federation agencies to include: 

1 - A clearing bureau for press and radio relations through which arrangements may be made for the publication of special articles,

publicizing of important events, assignments of broadcasting periods, and so on. 

2Advisory service to the agencies in their own planning of printed matter, news releases, and other publicity. If possible this service should be limited to announced hours so that it is not a continual interruption to other work. It should be possible to make use of competent volunteer consultants in this service. 

3A speakers' bureau which would function for at least six months of the year, as a clearing bureau through which individual agencies or groups of agencies may offer speakers, plays, films, and tours to appropriate organizations. The bureau should have advice and help from a committee of social workers and representatives of the leading clubs in the county. 

The maintenance of up-to-date lists for common use of agencies. 

These would include lists of class publications and organizations described on pages 80 and 86 of this report. 

Broader and improved service in mimeographing and other forms of duplication, which would help to raise the standards of house organs and other publications of all agencies. 

Publicity Staff

The service outlined above is more extensive than is practicable with the present staff, especially when we remember that several months of the year are given entirely to campaign publicity. It does not make any provision for county-wide interpretation which involves preparing material for community newspapers, and working with local units throughout the county. 

The proposed service might be saved from too great scattering of energies by concentrating it into definitely scheduled projects. For example, work on the annual revision of the proposed lists might be a part of the preparation

for the campaign, since good lists are a campaign necessity, and also, the staff is considerably expanded at that time; radio programs might be scheduled chiefly for the winter months; and so on. 

Federation of Social Agencies 

The responsibility of the Federation for social work interpretation includes: collecting and making available source material. for public information about needs and services, inter-agency interpretation, the development of special educational projects chiefly through its divisions and the interpretation of its own program. 

Through its research division, its contacts with both public and private social work, and the frequent meetings of its various divisions, the Federation becomes the logical source of supply for facts, figures, and illustrative material which make up the content of informative publicity. The various publicity projects proposed in a later paragraph as well as the publicity of the Community Fund should be so set up as to take full advantage of the Federation's unique position as a center for the collection and dissemination for social information. 

Service on a part or full time basis is needed to collect and make accessible social information, and to put it into convenient forms for writers and speakers. The staff member to supply this service logically belongs in the Federation, but the usefulness of such a worker depends on close working relations with the publicity department of the Community Fund. It is recommended, therefore, that an editorial assistant be selected by the executive secretaries of the Fund and Federation, and assigned by the Community Fund to The services of this worker should be available not only to the Federation. the Fund and the Federation but also as far as possible to individual agencies for consultation.

Group Projects 

It is recommended that divisions of the Federation undertake short time projects of public education on appropriate occasions, for example when a new social program is launched, or a study completed, or when some emergency situation calls for public support of action favored by a particular division. 

These campaigns of education, as a rule, should be completely dissociated from money raising. Some of the important topics mentioned earlier in this report as not now being interpreted, might well become the responsibility of the se Assistance in carrying out such projects should be made available if possible through the publicity department of the Community Fund. groups. 

Inter-Agency Interpretation 

Exchange of information among agencies goes on continually through the meetings and other activities of the Federation. It is easy to over-estimate the degree to which these contacts serve to bring about an understanding which extends to all board and staff members. It is recommended that the program of inter-agency interpretation begun by the Social Work Publicity Council, be continued with the active assistance of the Federation. 

The Federator is an important means of explaining agencies to each other and keeping all of them in touch with current questions of common interest. 

Steps have been taken for improvement in format and content of this bulletin since the Study was completed. It is recommended that an interested group, possibly a committee of the Publicity Council, assist in securing opinions as to how the bulletin can be made as useful and as interesting as possible to board members and staffs of agencies. 

The calendar of events which has been a regular feature of the Federator, if made up as long in advance as possible, might serve as what the city editors call their "day book," a listing of coming events to be covered. It should also be (and probably is) very helpful in clearing dates so that

important events are reasonably well spaced through the season.

Individual Agencies 

Individual social agencies, although they may be members of the Community Fund, need to maintain definite constituencies of interested persons from whom board members and volunteer workers may be recruited, persons who identify themselves with the work of a given agency while maintaining a general interest in social work as a whole. Interpretation to this end should be the responsibility of the agencies themselves. 

Some agencies have much more occasion than others to address publicity to those who use their services or to cooperating groups. Some agencies can use public information as an important means of reaching their social objectives. 

Some need to reach a larger public than others. These differences in requirements should be fully taken into account in planning publicity budgets of individual agencies within the Community Fund. 

The most readily available and accustomed methods for the individual agency to use are the annual report and annual meeting, public speaking, a house organ, and articles for such class publications as are most definitely interested in its subject matter or the group it serves. A minimum program of publicity for any agency should make possible good use of such channels as these.

It is recommended that member agencies of the Fund be encouraged to submit programs of interpretation which could be carried out with the help and advice of the Fund's publicity department and to ask for allowances for necessary materials, mailing costs and other reasonable expenses. 

Member agencies should know well in advance what they are expected to contribute to the common pool of information and illustrative material needed by the Community Fund for its campaign publicity, as well as for its all-yearround program of interpretation.

Public Agencies Responsibility for arousing public opinion in support of public social work is assumed in some instances by organizations of citizens set up for this purpose. In others, no such citizen sponsorship exists. Resources for interpretation of public relief, public health, crime prevention, and other forms of publicly supported city and county social work are wholly inadequate. Both the Federation in its social planning and the Community Fund in its responsibility for financing private social work are vitally concerned in educating the public on these questions. Their cooperation is needed in setting up campaigns of education in areas not now covered by other agencies. 

Provision should also be made within public departments the Juvenile 

Court, Public Health Department, Public Welfare Department, and Emergency 

Relief Bureau, for public information. Committees of citizens working closely with a public department sometimes help to create opportunities for officials to tell of their own work. Newspapers, as a rule, cover all public departments routinely. The missing link is often the lack of a staff member within the department, who meets reporters, works with a citizens' committee, and otherwise takes responsibility for public information. The initiative in securing this staff service might come from within the department itself, or through interested private agencies.

Social Work Publicity Council 

It is recommended that the Social Work Publicity Council be reorganized and strengthened for these purposes: 

1 To arrange meetings and institutes, and to carry on other activities directed toward increasing the skill of social workers as interpret- 2 

ers, and toward improving the quality of social work interpretation. 

Also, for this same purpose, to work with schools of social work in Pittsburgh in the development of courses of study in social work

interpretation. 

3 To initiate or recommend projects of interpretation which might later be sponsored by divisions of the Federation, or by other appropriate groups of social agencies. The Publicity Council's function in relation to these projects should be primarily an advisory one, although it might on occasion undertake demonstrations which later would be turned over to appropriate organizations. 

4 To receive this report, and discuss with a sub-committee on publicity of the Social Study, the action to be taken in regard to these recommendations. 

5 To recommend to the budget committee of the Community Fund needed appropriations for broad programs of interpretation to be sponsored by the Fund and the Federation. 

With the above objectives in mind, the reorganization of the Publicity 

Council should include: 

1Joint sponsorship by the Community Fund and the Federation of Social Agencies. This would involve representation of both on a responsible steering committee, and a recognition of the interests of both in its program. 

2Provision for the representation of public social work in the steering committee, and in the membership. 

3Provision for the representation on the steering committee of one or 4 

more laymen, preferably active members of publicity committees of social agencies. 

Provision of a new basis of membership which would be designed to secure an actively interested and participating group. Whether or not membership is on the basis of representation of social agencies, the aim should be to secure the attendance at its meetings of persons who share in making publicity policies and in directing and carrying

on the social work interpretation of the agencies. 

5 The Publicity Council should have at least part time services of an -D executive secretary who would be responsible to the steering committee. 

Volunteer Publicity Service 

Volunteer publicity service should be further developed. Possibly the 

Publicity Council might undertake a study of the possibilities of recruiting volunteers on the basis of their special abilities for writing, speaking, or any of the varied jobs outlined in "Jobs For Volunteer Publicity Aides," a memorandum of the National Social Work Publicity Council. A factor in this development would be provision of brief training courses, such as the Junior League conducts for its members, which would supply volunteers with a background of greater familiarity with social work itself. 

Interpretation of the Social Study 

Presumably the Citizens' Committee already has in mind the need of securing continuous publicity service throughout the period of active efforts to put the results of the Study into effect. 

This service should be something more than the preparation of news releases when sections of the report are made public. Advance planning of the various uses to be made of the material in newspapers, radio broadcasts, series of lectures, and discussion is urged.
