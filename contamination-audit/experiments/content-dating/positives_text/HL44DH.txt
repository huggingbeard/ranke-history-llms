December 1, 1966 The Judicial Conference of Oregon, formerly known as the Judicial Council of Oregon, acting pursuant to ORS 1.840, herewith submits its report for the period commencing January, 1959, through November, 1966. 1959 The annual meeting of the Judicial Council was held in Eugene in the Lane county courthouse on May 8th. Legislation affecting the judiciary passed by the recently adjourned legislative assembly was discussed by Mr. Gunther Krause and Mr. Philip 

Levin, members of the Oregon Bar. New provisions related to the use of pro tempore judges, retirement of judges, abolishment of the statutory terms of the circuit court and created two additional judgeships in Multnomah county and one in Marion county. 

Associate Justice Kenneth J. O'Connell made an planation of the Post-Conviction Procedure Act. 

Of significant importance to many of the judges was the new Juvenile Code. The provisions of this code were reviewed by 

Circuit Judge Ralph M. Holman who was a member of the interim committee on judicial administration which originally drafted the code.

The committee of the Judicial Council created to study the feasibility of uniform circuit court rules was continued and requested to make a further study. The desirability of keeping a record of all written circuit court opinions was discussed, together with the suggestion that a copy of each opinion be kept on file at some central location, preferably the Supreme Court library. 275580

1960 In 1960 the Judicial Council met twice; the first meeting was held in the Multnomah county courthouse in Portland June 

17 and 18.

Substantial time was taken to hear reports from members of the legislative interim committee on criminal law. The chairman of the committee, State Senator Carl H. Francis, Mr. James 

Craig, district attorney of Yamhill county, Mr. Harry Hogan, district attorney of Wasco county, and Judge E. H. Howell all discussed recommendations of the various sub-committees of the interim committee on criminal law.

A panel consisting of Associate Justice Kenneth J. 

O'Connell, Circuit Judges Val D. Sloper and E. H. Howell, Warden 

C. T. Gladden and Mr. Peter S. Herman, Assistant Attorney General, discussed the post-conviction statutes. The act had been in effect nearly a year. In Marion county during the period between June 19, 1958, and June 17, 1960, 108 petitions were filed of which only 12 were habeas corpus. Problems involved in sentencing under the act were discussed; also the possibility of a statute of limitation with respect to a petition for relief under the act was considered. The constitutionality of such a limitation was questioned. 

Mr. Roy F. Shields, chairman of the Oregon State Bar committee on judicial administration, addressed the members of the Judicial Council. He discussed the possibility of Oregon's adoption of the Missouri Plan relating to the selection and tenure of judges.

The committee on uniform court rules recommended that all judicial districts have written rules, and that such rules be uniform to the degree appropriate with reference to local conditions. The Judicial Council accepted this recommendation in substance.

Mr. Herbert S. Miller, secretary of the legislative interim committee on criminal law, discussed the recommendations which that committee intended to make to the 1961 legislative session. The Council opposed the mandatory five year sentence on felony charges; it did approve the recommendation that consecutive sentencing be within the discretion of the court. 

The Council's committee on post-conviction procedure recommended that no statute of limitation be adopted with reference to the Post-Conviction Act. 1962 The Judicial Council met at the Village Green, Cottage 

Grove, Oregon, on June 15th. Chief Justice Wm. M. McAllister, in making his report with regard to the status of administration of justice in Oregon during the preceding year, stated that the annual report reflects continued progress towards the elimination of all undue delay in the disposition of cases in the trial courts; it shows that in every judicial district in Oregon criminal cases are being tried very promptly, in most counties in less than four months. In 16 of the 36 counties the age of the average median civil case tried during 1961 did not exceed six months, in fact in only seven counties did the age of the average median civil case exceed eight months. The American Bar 

Association committee on docket congestion has set a goal of 12 months for the trial of the average case on a nationwide basis, therefore, Oregon has far exceeded that goal and gives every indication of improving its present excellent condition. 

Judge Charles W. Redding, chairman of the Judicial

Council committee on judicial retirement made a report dealing particularly with the retirement fund itself. Actuarial studies had been made and compared with the results of studies made by Mr. Kenneth Bragg, legislative fiscal officer of the state of 

Oregon, which probably will culminate in a recommendation by Mr. Bragg at the next meeting of the Legislative Fiscal Committee that the judges receive retirement benefits comparable to those which were in existence prior to the last salary raise, to-wit: 50% of the prevailing salary of circuit judges and 50% of the prevailing salary of members of the Supreme Court and also provisions for widows of one-half of that amount after certain conditions have been met in the way of longevity of service.

-- - 6 - Mr. Thomas H. Tongue, chairman of the Bar committee on judicial administration, discussed proposed legislation relating to involuntary retirement of judges for disability. 

The definition of the term "disability" would be broadened to include "incapacitated physically or mentally as to be unable to discharge efficiently the duties of judicial office."

The pro- posed law would further provide that either the Governor or the Judicial Council or the Board of Governors of the Oregon State Bar might file with the Secretary of State a request for investigation to determine whether a judge is disabled. 

A copy of that notice would go to the judge and unless he chose then to resign there would be a special commission appointed by the Chief Justice consisting of three members of the Judicial Council to make an investigation, hearing and report.

Many of the recommendations of the Constitutional Revision Commission directly affect the judiciary. This commission is presently considering the possibility of adopting the Missouri Plan relating to the selection and tenure of judges or some modification of that plan. Also the commission is considering the possibility of conferring upon the Supreme Court rule-making powers.

The Judicial Council's committee on probate matters made a progress report on the use of its recommended procedures for keeping track of current probate problems and for cleaning up the dockets on long pending decedents' estates. Several meetings have been had with county judges and also district judges who have probate jurisdiction and the procedures recommended by the Judicial Council's committee a year ago have been fully explained to these judges. At the time the program was put into effect statewide 55% of the decedents' estates pending had been pending for more than three years; at the time of this report the statewide percentage is down to 51.5%, nearly a 4% drop indicating that some progress in cleaning up the old estates has been made. Substantially greater progress should be made in the future for in the first year much time and work was expended in setting up the new procedures and educating the judges with respect thereto. The committee recommended that in the interests of consistency the reports should show only decedents' estates which were initiated for probate subsequent to January 1, 1940.

--  The recommendation of the committee with respect to the cut-off date of January 1, 1940, and the continuation of the program was unanimously adopted the the Judicial Council. 

A report was made to the Judicial Council by Judge Herbert M. Schwab regarding the changes in the Constitution recommended by the Constitutional Revision Committee as they pertained to the judiciary. A unified judicial system consisting of a Supreme Court and such other courts as the legislature may from time to time provide is set forth in the proposed revised Constitution and it is proposed that all judges be licensed to practice law and that all judges with the exception of municipal judges be selected in a like manner. This automatically eliminates juvenile and probate jurisdiction from the county courts and also eliminates justice courts. The system of selection of judges at the present time is not satisfactory, however, there does not seem to be any consensus with respect to a proper method of selection and tenure of judges. It was recommended that a judge be appointed by the Governor upon the advice of a law commission and such judge would serve not less than two years before standing on his record, thereafter the judge would run on his record every six years.

With respect to rule-making power the Constitution revision committee recommended what was known as the "Puerto

Rican Plan" under which the Supreme Court makes rules and they would become the rules and the law unless the legislature actively vetoed the rules that were made. It was recommended that a law commission be created to advise the Governor on functions dealing directly with the judiciary; such commission would be composed of judges, lawyers and laymen. 

The Judicial Council's committee studying the transfer of juvenile and probate jurisdiction made the following report: All juvenile court functions should be under the circuit court jurisdiction; so far as probate matters are concerned the committee also felt that eventually probate should be under the circuit court but with the following provision; that either by law or by order of the Chief Justice in certain instances present district judges should be appointed as pro tempore judges to work under the supervision of the circuit judge in certain districts where it was rather impractical for the circuit judge to

handle all the work.

There was some opinion expressed that it was not practical to attempt to transfer juvenile jurisdiction from the county court to the circuit court on a statewide basis but that it would be more feasible to select certain counties as they became ready for the change and accomplish piecemeal the ultimate transfer of juvenile jurisdiction to the circuit court. 

A committee was appointed by the Council to investigate the feasibility of an office of public defender, to make a report and to recommend whether or not the Council should go on record: (1) favoring or opposing the creation of such office, (2) limiting the duties of the public defender to post-conviction field or allowing the office to handle defense of indigent criminals generally.

It was suggested that a defendant be sent to the state penitentiary within forty-eight hours after execution of judgment or sentencing and be allowed to return only if the judge determines his presence is necessary during preparation of his appeal.

The Judicial Council called a special meeting November 

3rd in the Multnomah county courhouse, Portland, Oregon. Judge 

Charles W. Redding made a report with respect to the Judges'

Retirement Fund in which he stated that the Legislative Fiscal Committee is adamant in drafting the bill in form which will make the retirement benefits in a fixed amount rather than a percentage of salary; their thought being that should there at any time be an increase in salary that will not automatically result in increased retirement benefits. Thus, the only way to increase retirement benefits would be by amendment of the Retirement Act itself.

The report of the Judicial Council committee on the judicial article of the proposed Constitution was made by Judge 

Dean Bryson. Because of the many problems presented in the report and the desirability of avoiding unnecessary conflict with the action of the Oregon State Bar further serious consideration should be given to this matter. The matter was therefore referred to the executive committee of the Judicial Council for further action.

The Judicial Council committee on the public defender system reported that the counties, partly as the result of the Post-Conviction Procedure Act and partly as a result of increases in the fees allowed court appointed counsel, have a problem of cost. The committee attempted to get reliable statistical information relative to the amount of money each county budgeted for appointment of counsel, including but not limited to post-conviction and appeals, which it would then circularize to the members of the Council.

It was suggested that the state employ a public defender for post-conviction cases and appeals only; this would relieve the burden of the individual counties to some degree. 

Miss Kathleen Beaufait of the Legislative Council discussed the proposed legislation recommending establishment of conciliation courts in Oregon; also the creating of a crime reporting system through the Department of State Police was considered.
