The Bibliography Definition. 

A bibliography is a list of books, articles, pamphlets, etc., giving information as to author, title, facts of and sometimes of size, matters of illustration, publication, and price. Such a list is used in textbooks, in books and articles of scholarly research, and in classroom papers of various kinds.

There are two kinds of bibliographies, depending on the purpose the bibliography is to serve. One kind is a bibliography for a given paper. This kind merely lists those items that you the author have used in writing your paper and from which you have derived help. Such a list might better be called "References" or "List of References," for it is.a bibliography of the given paper, not necessarily of the subject on which the paper is written.

The second kind of bibliography, the kind most frequently met with and most often demanded in classroom assignments, is a bibliography of a subject, the subject on which a given paper is written. Such a bibliography is particularly valuable for any reader who may wish to pursue the subject further. It may well include not only items that you have consulted in writing your paper and from which you have derived help

but also items that you know are important in the field of your subject even though you derived no actual help from them and even though you may not have been able to consult them. But you know they belong in any bibliography purporting to be a firstclass bibliography of your subject. In such a case enter the items and star [*] them, making a note at the foot of your finished bibliography [*Not Consulted]. Such a bibliography then makes clear that you know all the important works bearing on your subject whether you needed to make any acknowledgment of indebtedness to them or not. (Your instructor should make of list is to be attached to your finished clear what kind paper.)

Of course, there are various "styles" for bibliographical data, both as to order of items and punctuation, to be found here and there. Facts of publication. 

The facts of publication consist of (a) the place of publication, followed by a colon; (b) name of the publisher, followed by a comma; and (c) the copyright date, followed by a period. 

In all ordinary work it is sufficient to list one place of publication, the first in case more than one is given on the title page. 

Write the name of the publisher exactly as it appears on the title page, except that the article The and such expressions as Inc. and Ltd. are omitted. But reproduce accurately such expressions as Company or Co., Brothers or Bros., and or &.

The copyright date is not necessarily the date at the bottom of the title page, which may be merely the date when this particular copy was printed and bound. The copyright date is the date given on the back of the title page in connection with the copyright notice. Occasionally, especially in work involving textual criticism, the date of actual printing is more important than the original copyright date.

Ante, post, supra, infra, Cf., See, Note, etc. 

Such terms as ante (Latin, "before"), post (Latin, "after"), supra (Latin, "above"), and infra (Latin, "below") may be used in cross-references in footnotes (or in the text placed in parentheses) within the given piece of writing. 

Ante and post are followed by proper page references; supra and infra usually refer to matter immmediately below on the same page and so are not followed by page numbers. These reference words are not abbreviations; they are italicized but are not punctuated with periods. 

A reference cited merely for confirmation of a statement is usually preceded by the abbreviation Cf. (Latin confer, "compare" ) or such an expression as See, But see, Note, See also, etc. They

are all italicized; cf., being an abbreviation, is punctuated with a period.

Double space between footnotes

Draw a horizontal line across the page to separate the footnotes from the text.1 lIf it were not for the fact that this method makes a typed page resemble a printed page, it would be far better for many reasons to use the same method that is used in a manuscript intended for publication and type the footnote in the next space below the reference, separating the footnote from the text immediately above and below it with horizontal lines. This method has a number of advantages: it keeps the text being footnoted and the footnote together; it prevents the untidy crowding at the bottom of the page when not enough space has been allowed for; and, finally, it is the preferred method for a manuscript intended for publication.
