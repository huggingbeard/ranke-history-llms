INTRODUCTION 

This Commentary reviews state motor vehicle and traffic laws applicable to railroad grade crossings. It is limited to provisions appearing in state vehicle codes and does not include laws appearing in state codes relating to highways nor railroads. 

Every attempt has been made to reflect laws that were in effect on 

January 1, 1978.

A recent report stated the safety problem at railroad grade crossings as follows: 

As of December 31, 1975, there were over 219,000 locations where public roads crossed railroad tracks. In 1975, there were over 11,000 vehicle-train collisions at the public gradecrossings, resulting in over 970 deaths and 4,100 injuries. In each of the years between 1965 and 

1975 over 1,100 people were killed and some 3,200 injured at grade-crossings. Grade-crossing accidents continue to be the major cause of fatalities in railroad operations, accounting for approximately 65 percent of the fatalities resulting from all types of railroad accidents during 

1965-74. An Evaluation of Railroad Safety 148 (Office of Technology Assessment, Congress of the 

United States, May, 1978). - 1 -

SAFE SPEED REQUIRED AT GRADE CROSSINGS 

The Uniform Vehicle Code specifies speed limits for drivers and, in addition, would require proceeding at a lower speed when it would be reasonable and prudent because of existing conditions and hazards. This latter rule, called the basic speed rule, then proceeds to require drivers to proceed at a safe and appropriate speed when approaching and crossing a railroad grade crossing. 

The Uniform Vehicle Code provision is worded as follows: 

§ 11-801 

Basic rule

No person shall drive a vehicle at a speed greater than is reasonable and prudent under the conditions and having regard to the actual and potential hazards then existing. Consistent with the foregoing, every person shall drive at a safe and appropriate speed when approaching and crossing an intersection or railroad grade crossing, when approaching and going around a curve, when approaching a hill crest, when traveling upon any narrow or winding roadway, and when special hazards exist with respect to pedestrians or other traffic or by reason of weather or highway conditions.

STOP WHEN TRAIN IS NEAR OR WHEN SIGNALED TO STOP 

The Uniform Vehicle Code requires drivers of vehicles to stop for trains and when signaled to do so at railroad grade crossings. This part of the Commentary reviews state laws that are comparable to the Uniform Vehicle Code description of these important duties which are contained in UVC § 11-701: 

§ 11-701 

Obedience to signal indicating approach of train (a) Whenever any person driving a vehicle approaches a railroad grade crossing under any of the circumstances stated in this section, the driver of such vehicle shall stop within 50 feet but not less than 15 feet from the nearest rail of such railroad, and shall not proceed until he can do so safely. The foregoing requirements shall apply when: 

1. A clearly visible electric or mechanical signal device gives warning of the immediate approach of a railroad train; 

2. A crossing gate is lowered or when a human flagman gives or continues to give a signal of the approach or passage of a railroad train; 

3. A railroad train approaching within approximately 1,500 feet of the highway crossing emits a signal audible from such distance and such railroad train, by reason of its speed or nearness to such crossing, is an immediate hazard; 

4. An approaching railroad train is plainly visible and is in hazardous proximity to such crossing. - 5 -

(b) No person shall drive any vehicle through, around or under any crossing gate or barrier at a railroad crossing while such gate or barrier is closed or is being opened or closed.

Uniform Vehicle Code STOPPING FOR STOP SIGNS 

Until 1975, the Uniform Vehicle Code expressly authorized the installation of stop signs at "particularly dangerous" railroad grade crossings. In addition, the Code required drivers to stop 15 to 50 feet from the nearest rail and to proceed only upon exercising due care.

The reasons for deleting UVC § 11-702 were stated in Agenda for National Committee Meeting, 27-30 (April 1, 1975): 

The reason for deletion of specific authority for stop signs at railroad grade crossings is the desirability of not encouraging stop signs at such locations. Research and experience clearly show that stop signs, except possibly in very unique situations which require thorough engineering study, should not be used at railroad grade crossings.

Throughout this discussion, it was stressed that other sections of the Code would authorize the occasional installation of stop signs at grade crossings (see UVC §§ 15-105 and 15-106) and the rule requiring drivers to obey all official traffic control devices (see UVC § 11-201(a)) would require drivers to stop for them.

Uniform Vehicle Code 

The Uniform Vehicle Code has contained a section requiring drivers of school buses and vehicles carrying dangerous cargoes to stop at railroad crossings since 1930. In 1971, the section was revised by deleting any description of the types of vehicles whose drivers must stop. This description was replaced by authority for the administrative designation of such vehicles to improve safety and achieve greater consistency between federal and state regulations. At the same time, exceptions to the stopping requirement were broadened to include crossings that have train-actuated warning devices and exempt crossings. 

The current section in the Uniform Vehicle Code reads as follows:

Civil Liability 

An Alabama law provides that no rule of the road for grade crossings is intended to abrogate or modify the doctrine of "stop, look and listen" obtaining in the courts of Alabama. That doctrine is probably used as a defense by railroads when they contend the driver was contributorily negligent and not entitled to recover his losses from the railroad.

Tennessee provides that laws relating to drivers' duties at grade crossings do not abridge nor affect a person's common law right of recovery against a railroad company. A similar law in 

Mississippi says that a violation will not defeat recovery and the question of negligence is left to the jury, the prima facie statute, and comparative negligence as in other negligence cases.

California provides that if a bus driver violates the rule on stopping at all crossings, that violation will not be imputed to, nor used against, a paying passenger. 

Virginia provides that nothing in its laws on registration of motor vehicles will affect one's right to sue for negligent operation of a motor vehicle or locomotive engine.

An Iowa law provides that the cost of signs may be paid out of state and local road funds. However, this law may apply only to stop signs.

Removal of Vehicles from Tracks 

California has a law authorizing police officers to remove a vehicle from a railroad right of way if the vehicle is on the tracks or within 7 1/2 feet of the nearest rail.

Motor Vehicles Transporting Railroad Employees 

A Delaware law requires motor vehicles provided by a railroad to transport its employees to meet certain standards. They must have adequate seating facilities, be heated, and a means provided to communicate with the driver. This law does not apply in emergency situations nor when the transportation is for less than five miles.

Alteration of Bridges Not Required 

Georgia provides that nothing in its laws regulating sizes and weights of vehicles, particularly permission for vehicles to be more than 12 1/2 feet high, will require railroads to alter their bridges, trestles or underpasses. 

Red Lights Near Tracks Prohibited 

A Minnesota law prohibits red lights and signs which are in view of a railroad line and which interfere with the effectiveness or efficiency of devices used in operating the railroad. The 

State Department of Public Services may cause such lights and signs to be removed.

Driver's License for Train Operator is not Required

A Mississippi law provides that no driver's license is required for a person operating a train even though it is proceeding down a street on tracks. This exception does not seem necessary in Mississippi or other states because their definitions of "vehicle" exclude devices used exclusively on stationary rails. 

See Traffic Laws Annotated § 1-184 (1972 and most recent supple ment). If a conveyance on rails is not a "vehicle," then it also cannot be a "motor vehicle" and it is drivers of "motor vehicles" which must have driver's licenses. See UVC SS 1-134 and 6-101 (a) 

Removal of Sight Obstructions 

A Utah law requires state and local agencies to remove all bushes, foliage and other obstructions which impair the view at railroad grade crossings. This law applies only to obstructions located on the highway. It would not cover obstructions located on private property as would UVC $ 15-113 (1968). 

Passing, Stopping and Yielding for Trains

Utah has several laws which are closely patterned after the rules in the Uniform Vehicle Code applicable to streetcars. See 

UVC SS 11-1401 to 11-1404 (1968). However, the Utah laws substitute the word "railroad train" for "streetcar" so that it provides for special passing, stopping and yielding rules where trains and other traffic use the same street.

Permits for Construction Work 

Virginia laws provide for permits allowing construction work on highways. Local authorities may issue them but not if the work involves a railroad grade crossing.

SUMMARY AND OBSERVATIONS 

1. Thirty-one states specifically require drivers to proceed at a safe and appropriate speed at railroad grade crossings. 

The remaining states should amend their basic speed rules to conform with UVC § 11-801 and Tennessee should adopt this important 

UVC section. 

2. The three states with a 15 mile per hour limit at crossings where the view is obstructed should repeal that limit, adopt UVC § 11-801, and post any reduced specific speed limit under provisions relating to speed zones (see UVC §§ 11-802 and 11-803). 3.

While most states restrict passing at railroad grade crossings in conformity with the Code, four states do not. These four states should adopt UVC § 11-306 or amend their laws to conform with that section. 

4. Forty-nine states have laws requiring drivers to stop when electric or mechanical signals warn of the approach of a railroad train at a railroad grade crossing. While virtually all of these laws probably are in substantial agreement, the wording of a few of these laws should be reexamined to make certain that they apply to all drivers as well as to all crossings with automatic signals. The two states without such laws should adopt UVC § 11-701. 

5.

Forty-five states require stopping when a crossing gate is lowered at a grade crossing. The other six states should adopt UVC § 11-701 or update existing laws using UVC § 11-701. 

Thirty-six states also ban driving through, around or under a gate at the crossing but one state allows it when safe and 14 states do not have comparable express prohibitions. 

6. Forty-six states have laws requiring drivers to stop for a flagman giving a signal indicating the approach of a train. 

However, there is disagreement as to whether a driver must remain stopped. The five states without this requirement should adopt it if flagmen are ever used at grade crossings. 

7. Thirty-six states have laws requiring drivers to stop for a train giving a signal but 15 do not. The 15 states should adopt UVC § 11-701 (a) (3).

8. Thirty-six states have laws requiring drivers to stop for a train that is dangerously close to a railroad. grade crossing. Thirteen states do not have this rule and two laws are not applicable to all grade crossings. Unless every grade crossing has automatic signals, it is incomprehensible that any state would fail to require drivers to stop for a train. 

9. One state South Carolina has not adopted any portion of UVC § 11-701 and thus does not require drivers to stop for trains nor for signals indicating the approach of a train. 

10. As to where drivers should stop and when they may proceed, several state laws are silent. Some laws allow stopping ten feet from the nearest rail though the Uniform Vehicle Code and federal regulations have long specified 15 feet. Several states do not indicate a maximum distance for stopping (such as within 50 feet from the nearest rail) so that drivers could claim they complied with the law by stopping substantial distances away from the grade crossing. 

11. Though the Uniform Vehicle Code and laws in 12 jurisdictions do not provide for the use and meaning of stop signs at grade crossings, 39 states do. One state requires stop signs at most grade crossings even though the National Committee found they should not be used except in very unique situations. 

12. Forty-nine states have laws requiring buses and vehicles transporting dangerous cargoes to stop at grade crossings and two jurisdictions do not. The two jurisdictions should adopt 

UVC § 11-703. Eight states have more than one law and, unfortunately, laws in several states prescribe different rules for the same driver. It is unnecessary and undesirable to have more than one law covering the same traffic situation. 

13. Drivers of school buses and vehicles transporting hazardous cargoes are required to stop at railroad grade crossings in 46 states. However, laws in five states do not require these drivers to stop at all grade crossings as would the Uniform Vehicle Code and laws in the other 46 states. 

14. As to exceptions from the duty of certain drivers to stop at railroad grade crossings, many states have not yet adopted all the exceptions provided by the Uniform Vehicle Code. The
