PAPER AS A MEDIUM FOR PRINTING 

The study of a much-neglected subject gAn address delivered by William Bond Wheelwright, at the Twenty-fifth Anniversary Celebration of the Department of Printing, at The Carnegie Institute of Tech nology, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. February 12, 1938

PAPER APER is the Godparent of Printing. It was invented in China long before printing and more than three centuries before the first use of movable type in Europe. H. G. Wells, in his Outline of History, declares that "it was the introduction of paper from the East that made practical the long latent method of printing."

An historical approach to the subject of paper may be found both interesting and suggestive, for as Socrates said, "the dead teach the living." But the "living" had no satisfactory medium for recording their knowledge before Ts'ai Lun's invention of paper in 105 A.D. Not until after the invention of printing from type about 1440, did it become possible for education to "reach the masses.'

Paper was not originally made for printing—but for writing upon with brush and fluid ink. Later it was used in China for printing from wood blocks, and afterwards from type. The same sequence was repeated

in Europe, after the introduction of papermaking in the twelfth century.

Gutenberg logically turned to paper as the most practical medium for printing. Perhaps he knew from the experience of others that the animal-sized, rag paper made for writing was altogether too hard for satisfactory printing. Possibly he experimented with some sheets which had not been dipped in the size, and was well pleased to discover that they accepted the ink better, and cost only about half the usual price. At any rate he soon learned that it was desirable to dampen the sheets before printing. This practice became universal among ancient printers and only disappeared toward the end of the nineteenth century, when the extensive use of rags in the making of printing papers was abandoned, and the softer wood fiber papers took their place. 

It is important to note this method of conditioning paper for printing, which is still occasionally practiced by a few craftsmen when using hand-made, or all-rag paper. William Morris dampened his paper, and according to Norman T. A. Munder who has also resorted to this technique, Frederic Goudy, who owns a press which once belonged to Morris, also moistens paper before printing. Yet Munder adds that "good printing, and even fine printing is by no means dependent upon dampened paper, but is dependent upon a strong and even impression."

The necessity for dampening hand-made paper is interestingly described in Horne's Introduction to

Bibliography, published in London in 1814. "The paper," he tells us, "is prepared for use by being previously dipped in water, a few sheets at a time, and afterwards laid in a heap, sheet over sheet, a thick deal board is laid upon the heap, on which heavy weights are placed according to its size. The reason why paper must be wetted before it is in a fit state to be printed upon, is that it may become sufficiently soft to adhere closely to the surface of the letter, and take up a proper quantity of ink, that it may receive a fair and clear impression. It is also necessary to wet the paper lest its stiff and harsh nature, when dry, should injure the face of the letters."

After describing the press as "a peculiar and complex machine," Horne tells how the form was inked by means of "balls duly prepared," and how "the pressman subjects the form to the powerful action of the press, which he pulls twice, that the impression may be complete."

He next describes the "rolling press," used for copper plate printing, "for the purpose," as he relates, "of taking off prints or impressions from copper-plates engraved, etched, or scraped. The ink used . . . . is composed of the stones of peaches and apricots, of the bones of sheep, and of ivory, all well burnt and mixed with well-boiled nut oil, by being ground together after the same manner as painters grind their coulors." After describing the painstaking process of inking and wiping the plate by hand, he continues, "the plate thus prepared, is laid on the plank of the

press; over the plate is laid the paper, which has previously been well moistened in order to receive the impression; and over the paper are two or three folds of flannel. The arms of the cross belonging to the press, are then pulled, and the plate with its furniture passes through between the rollers, which, pinching very strongly yet equally, press the moistened paper into the strokes of the engraving, whence it imbibes the ink, and the impression is, of course, complete." 

It is opportune to digress for a moment to see what lessons, if any, we may draw from the pre-conditioning of paper by wetting. From the technique as described there was a two-fold result. The sheets became sufficiently soft (a matter of rare consequence in modern printing since wood fiber papers are not hard like allrag stock) and they became more bibulous, thereby taking up "a proper quantity of ink." Futhermore such a dampening as described would have removed any waviness from the dry sheets on being subjected to the pressure mentioned. This accomplishment while not so important in letterpress work, must have been as essential as it is today for the so-called "rolling press, " for the same reasons that apply to lithographic or other processes where the paper has to pass between two plane surfaces without wrinkling. Unwittingly, the ancient printers also escaped the vexations of static electricity, which becomes trapped in dry paper after handling in a dry atmosphere. 

Now although wood-fiber papers do not admit of wetting like rag stock, they need to contain a definite

per cent of moisture to behave satisfactorily during printing and binding operations. Originally the moisture content must be determined by the papermaker. 

Unless he knows what the atmospheric conditions will be at the plant where the paper is to be delivered, the best he can do is to leave a normal per cent of moisture in the sheet as it is dried on his machine. 

His control over moisture content ceases the moment the paper leaves his mill. Assuming this has been properly controlled before shipment, his responsibility in this respect has been discharged. It then becomes incumbent upon the printer who receives the paper to ascertain the moisture content on delivery, and to control it as best he can.

For one reason or another, many letterpress printers appear either ignorant or indifferent to the need of conditioning their paper to prevailing atmospheric conditions in their plants. Through the research work of the Lithographic Technical Foundation our knowledge of such matters has been advanced. By means of modern ventilating, humidification, and in the most progressive plants, air conditioning, the difficulties occasioned by improper and fluctuating atmospheric conditions have been largely eliminated. In spite of the fact that some thirteen pressroom and bindery troubles are due to unstable or improper atmospheric conditions, little or nothing is done about it in the average establishment. Principally among lithographers and newspaper publishers has any research work on this subject been done.

A century and a quarter ago, printers through no sagacity, but out of the necessity of softening hard paper, stumbled upon the expedient of dampening paper which happened to emancipate them from the sort of troubles just mentioned. But they were not up against the exacting conditions confronting 20th century printers.

In the first place there were very few varieties of paper to choose from, and all the paper was made of the same sort of raw stock. The presses were all hand-presses which could only accommodate small sized sheets. The impression depended for its perfection upon muscular exertion, not upon pre-makeready, makeready, or mechanical precision. A variation in the thickness of successive sheets was of slight matter. 

Speed was a strictly relative term in those days when 250 impressions per hour was considered a high rate. 

But the era of leisurely craftsmanship was coming to its close.

In 1803 the first crude Fourdrinier paper machine was started. Ten years later Friedrich Koenig invented the first flat-bed cylinder press to run by steam. 

This was built for the London Times, and has been credited with making eleven hundred impressions per hour. Paper machines of 1806 turned out 6 cwt. of paper in twelve hours, while three men working at a vat could make only from 100 to 130 pounds. Roughly speaking, as much paper and printing could be produced in one hour as formerly in the regulation twelve hour day.

The early paper machines consisted merely of devices to form a moist web of paper which was wound upon a reel, from which it had to be cut off and hung in a loft to dry. This continuous process developed a distinct grain in paper, thus introducing a physical characteristic that has an important effect too often overlooked even today. It made paper two-sided because of the woven wire cloth upon which the fibers were formed and pressed by the couch roll. Only wove paper could be moulded on paper machines until after 1830, when the dandy roll was invented to help expel some of the surplus water and some one thought to adapt it both for impressing watermarks and laid marks as its weight imbeds them like blind printing upon the moving web of pulpy consistency. 

Artificial drying by means of heated cylinders began in 1823. The success of drying depended thenceforth upon the uniformity with which water was expelled from the web by its passage on endless felt blankets through pairs of press-rolls. The felts had to be kept porous, and uniformly clean, or else wet areas would appear in the paper, or moist streaks occur, which, on reaching the calenders, would become darker than adjoining areas of dryer paper. Frequently the paper was, and is overdried. It was a long time before papermakers became generally cognizant of this defect, largely perhaps, because the practice of dampening the paper before printing persisted so long. Now that the importance of making paper with a normal moisture content is appreciated this fault is less frequent.

Probably the most striking transformations in paper have come to pass as the result of industrial chemistry. This began with the substitution of chlorine bleaching toward the end of the 18th century, which displaced the natural method of sun-bleaching -often with disastrous results. At first the science of bleaching was imperfectly understood. Bleaching at too high temperatures gave rise to compounds which discolored and weakened the fiber. Chemical residues resulting from insufficient washing ultimately caused the disfiguration or ruin of books made of high grade materials. Technical control was conspicuously absent.

The introduction in 1820, of rosin sizing, involved new risks to color and permanence. It was not known at first that rosin should be sparingly used to avoid deteriorative effects. It was not discovered for some time that the tinting of lithographic surfaces could be caused by an excess of alum used to precipitate the rosin. Nor was the purification of the water supply for papermaking sufficiently understood. For years the self-styled "practical papermakers distrusted and resented the opinions of technologists and efficiency experts." Such prejudices have retarded progress both in papermaking and printing.

One person was still required to supply the ink, and another to work the press; but later a self-inking device was invented, by which the pressman, in running in the bed, raises a weight which, in its descent, draws the roller over the form. With this, one man with considerable exertion, may thus do the work of two. Mr. Applegarth was the first to abandon the plan of placing the type on a plane table, and built a press in which the type was placed on the surface of a cylinder." Robert Hoe constructed the first two-cylinder press in this country. After his death in 1833, his son Richard M. Hoe carried on, opening up a new era in the manufacture of printing machines, building eight- and ten-cylinder presses. The trend was always in the direction of greater speed and precision. The same thought impelled the paper industry. In England by the year 1860, hand-made paper represented only one-fiftieth of the total production. 

But speed was not all. In 1852, the invention of the halftone plate necessitated much smoother paper. 

This brought about the liberal use of mineral fillers and supercalendering. Coated papers were already being used by wall paper manufacturers, as were "flock" or velvet papers. These artisans were printing paper from rolls in many colors, one after the other, and drying them in festoons over steam-heated pipes long ahead of their contemporaries in the graphic arts. 

The first machines for coating one side lithographic papers were built in the United States about 1852, but the idea of making coated book paper was not put into practice until 1882. This process profited by the application of chemical science, but it was some time before all the difficulties connected with the manufacture and use of coated paper approached a satisfactory solution.

In the meantime a shortage of rags had inspired the search for other suitable fibers. Ground wood pulp, invented about 1840, was not made in the United States until 1866-67. Straw pulp, and pulp made by the soda process from poplar and aspen wood (known as chemical pulp), were introduced during the middle of the century. Sulphite pulp, made principally from spruce, while invented in America in 1866, was first made commercially abroad, and re-introduced into the States for manufacture under Swedish patents in 1882. The practice of defibering and de-inking used papers, by which means good pulp can be made was also resorted to, and now constitutes an important source of fiber. 

With so many new raw materials at hand, radically differing from rag, but often used in conjunction, the number of sorts and grades of paper multiplied enormously. The introduction of aniline dyes in place of vegetable, mineral, and animal complicated the choice of paper still more, until as today, the selection of suitable paper involves a great deal of knowledge and constant study. Without some understanding of both the chemistry and physics of paper, a buyer must depend entirely upon vendors, who themselves are not too well informed about the subject, and whose knowledge of printing requirements is often sadly deficient. 

Ordinary apprenticeship systems and vocational training could not keep pace with technological advances.

Absence of fundamental knowledge about paper was often reflected in discolored and disintegrating pages, printed on paper made during the last century.

Alarmed at this state of things, extensive researches were carried out by direction of the German Government, at Charlottenberg, between 1885 and 1896. In 

London, a Special Committee of the Royal Society of 

Arts issued in 1898, a report on the same subject—the causes of deterioration in paper. Possibly spurred on by such investigations, the paper industry in Germany and Great Britain established courses of technical studies at least a decade ahead of us.

The first instruction especially for paper technologists, was introduced at the University of Maine about 1913. Five years later courses on a collegiate level were begun at the New York State College in Syracuse, and in 1929 the Institute of Paper Chemistry was organized at Appleton, Wisconsin, as a graduate school. 

Harvard University was the first to establish printing courses on a collegiate level. Mr. D. B. Updike acknowledges in the foreword of his monumental work Printing Types, "that the lectures on which this book is based were delivered as a part of a course on the 

Technique of Printing in the Graduate School of Busi ness Administration of Harvard University, during the years 1911-1916." I am quite familiar with this course, having given some of the lectures there on paper. 

In a recent magazine article giving the history of the Printing Department of the Carnegie Institute of Technology, Harry L. Gage states that in the year 

1913 "less than half a dozen printing schools existed in the whole country." These were with the exception of Carnegie and Harvard, on a purely vocational basis.

As a matter of priority, however, the School of Lithography, established at the Winona Technical Institute, in 1904, deserves mention. Apprenticeship and vocational training is good as far as it goes, but apparently it leaves the novice with a very insufficient knowledge to meet the managerial and technical problems. 

A serious omission from the point of view of the graphic arts, in the colleges where paper technology is taught, is that the work is not correlated more directly with the use-requirements of the printing industry. We wonder, in schools where printing is being taught in this country, whether sufficient study is given to the subject of paper. 

In his foreword to my book Printing Papers, Dr. 

Otto Kress, Director of the Institute of Paper Chem istry, says in part, "While the paper manufacturer attempts to control his process of pulp and papermaking from the wood pile to the finished sheet, he unfortunately is not familiar with all the requirements of the printer, or with the pressroom conditions under which the paper is converted into the printed sheet. 

The printer, on the other hand is certainly not too familiar with paper, and is very apt to use arbitrary tests for evaluating the printing characteristics of the sheet, with the result that a situation often arises where two sheets of paper which apparently have quite the same physical characteristics react differently to the printing operation. . . . . The printer cannot expect, through the employment of a chemist who is not familiar with the intricate process of papermak-

ing and who will perform perfunctorially certain routine tests on paper, to have made available by this means the necessary knowledge which will enable him to evaluate the sheet to determine its suitability for a particular printing job. Only through thorough cooperation and interchange of information which should exist between the manufacturer of the paper and the printer as the converter of the paper, is there hope of placing the manufacture of paper on a scientific basis." 

He adds a paragraph which I quote unblushingly, because what he says about the book could be said with even greater validity about a course of study about paper: "it is very gratifying to know that there is to be presented to both the printer and the papermaker, real and authentic information on the subject of the relation of paper to printing. . . . . A careful study of the subject should do much to overcome the paper troubles which now exist, many of which can be corrected by relatively simple means.' · • 

At the world conference of the printing industries held at Carnegie Tech in November, 1929, Honorable 

George H. Carter quoted from a report of a committee of the Federated American Engineering Societies on preventable waste in six selected industries, which alleged that printing stood next to the head of the list.

He also said that "printing materials present more problems that chemical engineers can solve than are offered by almost any other industry. The extent of this field for research is apparent from the fact that the cost of printing materials constitutes more than a

fourth of the entire value of printing products." 

Taking the testimony of these two men together, each of whom is an authority in his sphere, can there be any doubt that paper as a medium for printing is a much neglected study?

Having noticed in the autumn number of Plaid Proofs that the Department of Printing of Carnegie Tech is going to be "developed and improved, with definite objectives for every program," I wish to bring you this thought to ponder. There is evidently an opportunity to correlate certain facts about paper and papermaking with the production technique in printing plants or other converting establishments, together with the functional properties of paper which render it suitable for any given purpose. All such data are available. It only remains to assemble and correlate the facts in a manner that will make them intelligible alike to students of printing and paper, and to discover men qualified by experience to teach them. 

Mr. J. R. Riddell, Principal of the the celebrated

London School of Printing, has very kindly replied to my recent enquiries as to their procedure in teaching this subject-paper. He wrote on January 24th as follows. "The manufacture of paper and its uses are exhaustively dealt with from the buyers' and users' point of view. Each evening there is a lecture of about an hour's duration and this is followed by laboratory work. In addition to this in each of the craft departments, such as, composing, press work, lithography,

ruling, binding and stationery, paper is dealt with as a departmental subject, so you will see that even the youngest student obtains some knowledge in his special department on the manufacture and care of paper when it reaches the factory. 66 'Sometimes we have a special intensive course for the workers in a paper mill which includes a short talk on the problems encountered in the various branches. of printing and stationery, supplemented by visits to respective departments to demonstrate how the printer handles paper. "Every instructor taking part is a man of practical experience in his particular department, and in the special class our instructor is Mr. E. A. Dawe who is 

Chief of the Paper Section of His Majesty's Station ery Office. He is a well known author on Paper and its Uses." 

Mr. Riddell omitted to mention the two special courses given in his school for "Stationers' Assistants," regarding which the Prospectus and Syllabus has this to say-—and it applies very well to the vocation of our paper merchants' salesmen as well. "It is imperative in these days of keen competition that those engaged in the distribution of stationery goods should have efficient knowledge of the articles they sell as will make it possible for them effectively to demonstrate their value to potential customers. 

This need has been recognized lately by the Paper 

Trade Association of Philadelphia which for the past two winters has been conducting lectures for a Paper

Salesmen's Study Course. Prior to that a Paper Sales men's Training Course was initiated by the LaSalle Extension University, which published an excellent set of textbooks, available however, only through enrolment in the course. But these two isolated examples represent the only organized attempt yet made along these lines in the United States.

If further evidence is needed to prove that the study of paper is an urgent matter it is to be found in the survey of Education for the Printing and Allied Trades, published by His Majesty's Stationery Office in 1935. "Paper," it declares, "is a substance of the utmost importance to those employed in the printing and stationery trades who order, handle, and use it in large quantities. Classes for the study of this material from their point of view are held in the Birmingham Cen tral School of Art and Crafts, the Bradford College of Art and Crafts, the London School of Printing, the Bolt Court School of Lithography and Photo-engrav ing, London, and the Municipal College of Technology, Manchester. "These classes are usually well attended and in the hands of expert instructors. The subject matter of the instruction includes the manufacture of different kinds of paper from the various fibres used and their suitability for work of different kinds and for use in different processes. The testing of their physical and chemical properties and the identification of samples by means of such tests form an important feature of courses in this subject."

If there are any equivalent courses in American schools of printing I have yet to hear of them. My conviction that paper as a medium for printing is a much neglected subject rests not solely upon personal observation, but has been recently confirmed in a letter received as the result of my review of the Fifteenth Year 

Book of the London School of Printing in the Inland Printer for December. 

Wrote R. Randolph Karch, Technical Supervisor,

Department of Publishing and Printing of the Rochester Athenaeum and Mechanics Institute, "I noted your comment on the teaching of paper. You're right, dead right, when you say that little is being done, or at least has been done in the past, in the teaching of paper facts in American schools. 

The need for such a course was apparent. 

All this evidence indicates a great educational opportunity in the United States. Here we have nearly 

47,000 printing plants, about 250 paper mills producing writing and printing papers that are distributed by approximately 700 fine paper merchants. Knowledge of paper is essential also in several related industries. In addition there are some 1,500 schools in the 

United States where printing is being taught. 

From the outline of present educational opportunities to study about paper, it seems unlikely that the teachers of printing could have been adequately prepared to give instruction in the subject of paper. It is obvious that most men entering the printing or the paper business have had to acquire their knowledge

of paper in the "school of hard knocks." Neither printer nor paper man being sufficiently familiar with one another's problems, as Dr. G. I. Riddell, Director of the Printing and Allied Trades Research Association of London has put it, “A situation akin to the Tower of Babel arises, for the two technicians do not speak the same language. . . . . What is most urgently needed is that some common language between the papermaker and printer be found. 

I have travelled here today because of my high esteem for the Printing Department of the Carnegie Institute of Technology, "the only school in the nation which awards a bachelor of science degree in printing." 

I am leaving the problem of adequate instruction in Paper as a Medium for Printing on your doorstep. In view of President Doherty's recent declaration of his intention to develop the study of printing "at a distinctly collegiate level and to maintain it with a truly professional curriculum," I have no doubt if any neglect exists here in this important subject of paper, that it will soon be placed on a collegiate level.

FOREWORD 

F IFTEEN fine paper merchants in Chicago stock 2081 brands of paper of over twenty-five different sorts. Each sort is carried in the standard substance weights and grades and in the colors typical of each. Thus the number of different stock items is many times more than that of the various brands. 

These papers are assembled from twenty states of the Union and from foreign lands, making it possible for local printers to obtain any kind in any reasonable quantity without delay.

Catalogs and sample cabinets for printers are provided. Sample departments stand ready to make up dummies, competent salesmen call daily on the printers to render service and acquaint them with the newest lines. Through this extensive service the printers are enabled to gain thorough familiarity with all that the paper market affords. From actual experience in printing many different brands they know what mere samples cannot tell—the printability, dependability, and suitability of a high per cent of the papers available for any given purpose. 

In all other arts it is customary for the patron to state the purpose for which the services of an artist are required. After having accepted the original plan of the artist the choice of materials and tools logically devolves upon the artist, for he is held responsible for the result. 

Paper is but one of the raw materials printers use. Not only is it the most important raw material, but upon the right choice of paper for a given purpose depends the appearance, the economy, and the functional ability of the finished product. But the choice is far more than a mere matter of taste; inks, engravings, and the very tools necessary for converting it into the final form all need to be taken into consideration. As a rule, the printer is the only one who is competent to make the wisest decision.

The patron who uses good judgment in the selection of his printer can best afford to place the entire responsibility for securing satisfactory work where it logically belongs-with the printer of his choice.

Hints on Choosing Printing Papers 

WHERE is a tendency on the part of advertising writers to encourage ultimate consumers to specify particular brands of paper. 

There has been a tendency also among production managers in advertising agencies and among advertising managers or purchasing agents to specify what paper the printer shall use. The right to do this is not questioned, but the advisability of so doing is often dubious. It should be thoroughly understood that the person who tells a printer what paper he must use assumes the responsibility for all the consequences involved in the final results, assuming that the mechanical handling of the job has been reasonably correct. pro- 

Dr. Otto Kress, Technical Director of the Institute of Paper Chemistry, has pointed out in the Preface of Printing Papers that "paper, which is the medium on which, through the skill of the printer, ink is applied by various mechanical processes, is probably the most important and least understood of the variables which enter into the duction of the finished printed sheet," and that "the situation often arises where two sheets of paper which apparently have quite the same physical characteristics react differently to the printing operation." He further stressed the point that "the printer cannot expect through the employment of a chemist who is not familiar with the intricate process of papermaking and who will perform perfunctorily certain routine tests on paper, to have made available to him by this means the necessary knowledge which will enable him to evaluate a sheet of paper to determine its suitability for a particular printing job." 

No one familiar both with paper and printing can question the soundness of this doctrine. But, quite in disregard of it, some consumers of paper have even undertaken to set up paper specifications for themselves which, in certain instances, are not in sympathy with the problems of the papermaker, even if they may be adapted to those of the printer or converter. The matter of adapting paper specifications to userequirements is a very technical and exacting one in which the difficulties have, up to the present time, been aggravated because of the absence of uniformity, on the one hand, in the instruments used for evaluation and because of the many variables involved, on the other hand, in the other principal raw material-printers' ink. 

Even when proper specifications have finally been evolved, the difficulties occasioned by atmospheric conditions remain to be solved. There is no need to review them here, but it should be appreciated fully that the final cost of any given paper is difficult to anticipate and impossible to know until all the costs of its conversion have been counted.

The contingencies both in papermaking and in printing are many. Accurate cost-finding is comparatively new and still far from universal in both industries. For want of it, losses and un-

necessarily high costs are frequently experienced. In the best paper-mill practice today, as well as in the more efficient printing establishments, the preliminary estimates on a job ticket are afterwards checked against the actual experience of the run. To select paper wisely presupposes a great deal of practical knowledge; without it contingencies cannot be foreseen or the obstacles to securing satisfactory results on a given paper be anticipated. 

Logically the printer should be the most competent person to choose a paper which will satisfy the technical requirements of the given job, whereas matters of taste are the prerogative of his client. When the paper suggested by a customer does not promise to comply with the mechanical requirements of conversion, it is the duty of the printer to explain the circumstances and suggest a suitable compromise.

This course may take courage in dealing with some egotistical or cantankerous customer, but, if the printer is sure of his ground and approaches his man tactfully, he should either win his client's respect and, possibly, his gratitude or lose a prospective customer who is good riddance!

It is doubtless true that some buyers of printing are more competent to judge paper than some printers, but it would seem that the wise buyer should select a printer not merely on price considerations but because of his demonstrated ability to give expert advice. At least, if a client insists on specifying the paper, he should expect to be told by his printer of any reasons why the choice is undesirable. If the customer displays bad judgment and the printer complies without a word, his silence gives consent and makes him accessory after the fact if a printing crime is committed!

We have no desire to surround the selection of paper with mystery or to exaggerate its difficulties. A few simple generalizations would doubtless cover a goodly proportion of the printing problems. To the experienced printer some of these may seem superfluous, but to the apprentice or the layman they may not appear amiss; so we ask our readers' indulgence toward passages which seem to them too elementary. 

At best these directions and warnings can be but as road maps and traffic signals which mark the way or warn against the ordinary hazards. Each must learn through trial and error the lessons which experience alone can teach.

GENERAL PRECAUTIONS 

When about to plan a job of printing, paper should be among the first items for consideration. Since it makes the background and provides the chief structural element, it should be selected on the basis of suitability both to the completed job and to the technical requirements of the process to which it will be subjected. What is the purpose of the job? What sort of illustrations, if any, are to be used? Are the engravings to be made for the job or must some previously used cuts be employed?

Is the work of permanent, semipermanent, or of but temporary importance? What limitations in expense must control the selection of paper?

In book-printing, conventions usually circumscribe the choice of paper; in advertising matter, the paper like the typography is often chosen for its attention value. In printing produced for the mechanical purposes of business, the physical properties of the paper frequently require very careful consideration because of certain functional fea-

tures involved in after-use. In every case the method of making the impressions will dictate certain properties which are definitely necessary. Letterpress printing, being from lines of type and scattered engravings with a direct transfer of ink to paper, imposes other restrictions than planographic, or offset, printing where the pressure on the paper is exerted over the entire area of the sheet, and suction tends to lift the surface, or moisture is transferred from the impression cylinder to the paper. Intaglio printing of all types also involves an over-all impression, and the paper must therefore lie flat to avoid creasing and must be sufficiently absorptive to withdraw the ink from the etched parts of the plate or roll, as the case may be. 

Web presses introduce other factors, such as the need for even, wellwound rolls of paper to prevent the occurrence of "dead metal printing" and of breaks in the paper web caused by uneven tension. Embossing and die stamping call for sufficient substancestrength to resist cutting through, or cracking of the surface, at the points of maximum depth of impression. All types of printing involving after-treatment, such as bronzing or varnishing, necessitate correct sizing for successful manipulation. All papers which have to be issued as folders must have extra strength either for simple folding or, as in the case of thick stocks, for scoring. 

Jobs for which Bristols or coated boards are used generally require stiffness. 

This property is determined in the finished piece not only by the character of the stock but by the direction of the grain in each piece.

Those who buy or specify paper must also take into consideration all of these technical details as applied to the process whereby the paper is to be converted. 

They must, also, find out whether the paper is obtainable in the stock sizes which can be cut without excessive waste and with due regard to the proper grain direction. Especially must the latter specification be scrupulously observed in all cases involving close register on account of the stretching and the shrinkage of paper under variable atmospheric conditions.

The buyer of printing paper, who is not thoroughly conversant with all the above named technical requirements, should secure the advice of his printer before specifying any certain brand or grade of paper. The printer, himself, who feels any doubt about the ability of a given sheet to comply with any userequirement would be equally wise to discuss the problem with some competent paper man before placing his order.

In every case where printing specifications are exacting, it is the part of wisdom to take counsel with the paper man, the ink man, or any other supply man whose product is involved. As 

Theodore Roosevelt used to say: "Ninetenths of wisdom is being wise in time." 

And the last stroke of wisdom before going to press with the selected paper is to inspect it before the forms are made ready and a press is waiting.

PAPER FOR LETTERPRESS PRINTING BookWORK: The matter of taste in the manufacture of books is too intricate to discuss in a general manner, but the question of suitability of paper for a given job may be presented in regard to its essential requirements. The price for which the book has to sell will immediately restrict the choice of grade; the number and type of illustrations will indicate the finish. Until these points have been decided, it is unwise to select the type face because certain types, like

Caslon for example, were designed for antique paper and were formerly printed with a strong impression on dampened rag, hand-made paper. Such types unless printed with a strong impression are poorly suited to calendered or enamel paper and with a "kiss-impression" do not look as the designer intended. It should be remembered that certain type faces were designed for use on handpresses before modern innovations in papermaking had been dreamed of, and by men who had no idea what our present-day papers would be like.

Paper was invariably made on laid molds before Baskerville introduced the first wove paper about 1750 and printed his celebrated edition of Virgil on it a few years later. A "period" book or other printed form of a style earlier than 1750 should, in most cases, go on laid, antique paper to be a faithful reproduction. Paper was hand-burnished before this time, but the cost of additional finishing was usually avoided unless fine copper engravings were to be reproduced. Baskerville introduced hotpressing. 

Machine-finish paper could not have been made before the introduction of steam cylinders for drying paper in 1821 or of the iron calender rolls in 1830.

English finish has been known scarcely over fifty years, supercalendering was a later development, and the first coated book paper appeared about 1882. Plated paper was made long ago, first by the ancient process previously described and later by the mechanical plater. In selecting type and deciding upon the style of illustrations, the appropriate finish of the paper is very important. 

As books are a continuous tax upon eyesight, they deserve to be carefully planned from the angle of reader comfort. For this reason very white color and highly reflecting surfaces should be avoided whenever possible. An eggshell or matte finish gives off a diffused light which does not annoy the reader. A natural or toned sheet is also agreeable, and the less gloss the better. If no halftones run through the text, an antique or eggshell finish is indicated. It shows off type attractively and, having greater relative bulk, enables one to use lighter weights, which not only saves money but makes a lighter book.

When a few halftones are to be scattered through a book, it shows good judgment to insert them on any suitable coated paper. If zinc etchings only are to be used, an eggshell finish will generally satisfy the printing requirements. 

A light machine or vellum finish, however, has no objectionable glare and may often be chosen with excellent results. 

Such a sheet will also receive acceptable impressions from deep-etched halftones of from 85 to 100 line screen. The usual machine-finish paper will respond nicely to 100 or 110 line screen halftones, and English finish is very satisfactory with 110 or even 120 line screen halftones if they are carefully etched and well made-ready and a suitable quality of ink is used. Supercalendered paper is good for either 120 or 133 line screen halftones. Needless to add, any paper that will take halftone printing will respond even better to the finer line engravings of zinc. Highlight halftones are somewhat easier to print on uncoated papers. 

When the cost is not prohibitive, photogravure inserts make especially attractive additions to a letterpress job, as also do those produced by the photogelatin process. Such inserts might be used and a book still held within specified price limitations if antique paper were chosen as economy will be realized through its lightness as related to bulk.

By the same token Featherweight book papers are less costly than antique because of their great bulking property, but the fibers, being loosely knit, cause more or less fluff on the presses, necessitating stops to wash the forms. COATED PAPER IN BOOK PRODUCTION: 

In certain books, such as scientific works where the utmost detail in the reproductions of halftones is desirable, it may be advisable to employ coated paper. It must be borne in mind, however, that coated paper will make a very heavy volume, is not considered permanent, and its use is justified mainly by its superior printing properties for halftone reproduction. Made with a semi-dull or a dull finish or overprinted with a tint, or stippled after printing, coated paper escapes the objectionable gloss characteristic of enamel papers. To do coated paper full justice, its merits for multicolor printing should be mentioned, although effects very nearly if not quite as good can be achieved by employing offset printing in place of letterpress. 

The effect of type from the latter method is, however, less interesting than impressions by letterpress direct from the type itself.

GRAIN DIRECTION IN BOOK PRODUC- 

TION: In specifying paper for a book it is important to make sure that the grain in the sheets will result in pages in which the grain runs parallel to the shelf-back.

It has been quite customary among English publishers to go contrary to this rule, and the practice has been defended on the grounds that with the grain at right angles to the shelf-back the sewing passes around the bundles of fibers and gives a stronger binding. The greater strength of such volumes does not compensate for their loss of flexibility, and, except in the case of Featherweight paper so notably lacking in cohesion, the average paper used for book manufacture is usually strong enough for its purpose. Unless grain direction is specified in a making or in any stock order, the mill or merchant supplying the paper may not be held responsible. 

When the quantity to be used is too small for a making-order, inquiry should be made to ascertain whether the paper stocked by one's merchant has the grain the right way of the sheet.

OPACITY: When it is necessary to use the lighter weights of paper, it is always important to examine the proposed samples for opacity. The possibility of the ink's penetrating far enough into the sheet to show through should also be considered and guarded against; for nothing is more destructive to good appearance in any printing than unseemly transparency. Titanium pigments recently introduced into papermaking have enabled paper to be made more opaque than when clay only was used for filler. 

PERMANENCE: The subject of per manence has been adequately covered elsewhere, but we may repeat that for semi-permanence, at least, the popular grades of book paper-made without ground wood or unbleached, chemically produced pulps—are considered acceptable if properly made.

HINTS ON CHOOSING PRINTING PAPERS DURABILITY: The distinction between permanence and durability should be borne in mind, not only for book work but for other sorts of printing. Durability has to do with the physical strength rather than the chemical purity of paper, and even fibers which are insusceptible to deterioration from chemical causes may be lacking in physical resistance to wear and tear of frequent use. Papers of good strength, on the other hand, which contain unbleached pulps or pulps which have been improperly bleached and washed, will gradually deteriorate in strength. It is only necessary to determine whether they will have fulfilled their mission. before their usefulness becomes impaired. In many instances the mere length of fiber may be the factor responsible for failure to withstand the handling to which the job is bound to be subjected. Yet as the beating of the pulp and the physical structure of the fibers have a fundamental effect upon durability, this property in paper may well be forecast by subjecting samples to the folding test which appears to be the most reliable index of physical durability. In really particular cases this test should be performed in the laboratory.

FINISH: The subject of finish is dis cussed elsewhere in considerable detail, but in choosing paper we must think about it from various angles. Where technical requirements permit, the question of appearance is the main factor. 

Ordinarily a paper not too smooth presents a more interesting texture and offers the advantage of being free from gloss, and may be obtained either wove or laid. The matter of taste is too difficult to discuss, but it is safe to say that unless halftones are to be used the smoothest finishes should be avoided.

The exceptions to this general rule would come where susceptibility to soiling as in school books or reference books needs to be anticipated, or where thinness is an important feature. In such cases the advantages of the smoother finishes are obvious.

HALFTONE PAPER: The theoretically level height of all halftone dots, regardless of the screen of the engraving, calls for a theoretically level sheet of paper in order that the printed results shall reflect the halftone values in the optimum manner. No paper is more than relatively level, but to achieve this desired state, a uniform body free from thick and thin areas is essential. No amount of smoothing can alter the original position of the fibers in the sheet or compensate for an insufficiency of mineral filler which fills the pores and helps to level off the surface under the heavy calendering of the paper. Even in enamel papers, a poorly formed body stock neutralizes to some extent the addition of the coating mixture to the surface. In examining samples of all papers intended for halftone printing the first thing to observe, then, is the formation of the sheet. The next thing is to be assured that its manufacturer is competent to keep his product uniform.

Isolated samples are no criteria for craftsmanship, and often experience with the proffered brands is the only thing on which to depend. 

Given a sheet of uniform formation, the first essential for good halftone work is present; then if the finish is also uniform in gloss the theoretically level sheet is obtained. In proportion as the gloss or finish is lowered, the screen of the halftone must be coarser as has already been mentioned. This rule applies equally to coated and uncoated papers. 

Lastly, the printer must see to it that

the best possible original photographs are provided from which engravings are to be made. The use of plates originally intended for other papers than the one selected should be avoided, and engravings which show signs of too much wear should be called to the attention of the customer, and he should be persuaded to permit new engravings to be made if he is really anxious to get the best possible results. It is good business to educate customers who are ignorant of such technical requirements.

The printer is faced with a problem very similar to the architect. He must consider the choice of raw material both in relation to aesthetic and functional requirements. When he has finished his work it may appear all right, but if it fails to function it cannot long satisfy the client. The printer may well adopt the engineer's motto, "Form follows function," while bearing in mind the saying which we do not hesitate to repeat: "Quality in paper is dependent upon its suitability to the given purpose." The beginner who always remembers these two things and constantly seeks to train his powers of observation, studies the best examples of ancient and contemporary printing, and keeps himself posted on the great and growing varieties of printing paper is likely to succeed. Such knowledge is of slow but cumulative attainment. It can be gained only by practical experience and constant application which is the secret of skillful craftsmanship.

Choosing the Right Paper I. WHAT AN AUTHOR SHOULD KNOW ABOUT PAPER 

BY WILLIAM BOND WHEELWRIGHT 

VERY author should know something about paper, since it is the vehicle for the printed word as well as the canvas upon which his word pictures must be painted. The appearance of the type and illustrations may please or prejudice his public; the quality of the paper will determine the durability and permanence of his volume; the finish of the paper will affect the legibility of the text and the presentability of the illustrations; the cost of the paper will be a goodly percentage of the total manufacturing expense. It is important for the author that the paper selected should be well suited for the proposed work. It is within the province of the printer to instruct and advise him.

The option of the author in the matter of paper will depend upon the nature of his contract with his publisher. Only in case he is assuming the publication cost personally, can his choice be unrestricted. Even then it cannot be satisfactorily made unless it conforms to the technical requirements of the printer and binder. 

In these matters the author should consult with a competent printer. In any case he is entitled to have a dummy made to suggest the final appearance of the completed work. 

If an author happens to be writing for some magazine, the paper question is closed, yet the author may, when illustrations are to be supplied by him, cooperate advantageously by furnishing the best possible originals for producing satisfactory engravings. 

If he is to supply photographs, for example, they should be developed on glossy paper. So even in such restricted circumstances he may be helpful, if he learns enough about paper and printing to appreciate the technical needs.

As it is impossible to know the restrictions which may be imposed by publishers in various instances, let us approach the subject from the viewpoint of an author-publisher in a position to make the final decision, if sufficiently well informed about paper and its use-requirements.

Only recently a pamphlet was published by a legal authority on the 

American Constitution. It contained no engravings, and is an outstanding example of how not to print similar jobs. The text paper used was a glossy coated paper of India tint! 

"Vision," states Walter W. Wildeman, of the Commercial Engineering 

Department of the Westinghouse 

Lamp Company, in the May, 1936 issue of the Paper Industry, "is purely a function of light reflection.' Good light reflectance is an aid to vision, but must not be confused with "specular reflection," which comes from a polished surface and is detrimental to accurate or easy vision. 

Any tinted paper has lower light reflectance than white, and any

glossy paper has a high degree of specular reflection. Measurements show that three times as much light is needed to give equal visibility between type printed on newsprint, which is a grayish white, and a bright, white shade of book paper. The inconvenience to a reader, therefore, due to the use of a paper of low reflectance, is sufficiently obvious. When this handicap to accurate vision is compounded by the use of small type set solid as in the larger telephone directories the acme of illegibility is achieved.

The proper function of printing being to produce legible pages, it is desirable to know about the light reflectance and the glare characteristics of the standard, staple grades and finishes of book papers. One may have to reach a compromise between having a pleasing reading surface and meeting the technical printing requirements, but in order to do this intelligently presupposes definite knowledge of how these properties are affected by the finishes obtainable in commercial grades of printing papers.

If we place reader comfort first, which seems a logical thing to do, satisfactory paper selection may be achieved by following the above suggestions. In a word, choose that paper with the minimum gloss and maximum reflectance which will satisfy printing requirements.

When the question of deciding between wove or laid papers comes up, the difference in reflectance and glare is slight, but a machine-made paper of laid finish is usually made with shorter fibered stock, and is weaker than a corresponding quality of wove. 

Laid paper takes an extra price differential as it is slower to make. The laid marking is strictly a decorative feature in machine-made papers, but in hand-made paper it is caused by the structure of the mold, and is therefore an inherent characteristic. In producing "period" design of books it should be recalled that wove papers were unknown before the year 1750.

At about this time the woven-wire mold was devised.

Whatever paper be selected, it should be reasonably opaque, for if printing shows through the page it not only looks badly, but interferes with the legibility of the matter on the reverse side. In most papers not lighter than Substance 60 (i.e. basis weight 25x38-60-500 sheets), the opacity should be satisfactory. Papers lighter than this should be examined for opacity, and the best test is to see printed specimens with both type and solids. Papers are made more opaque by the addition of mineral filler, and as a rule the smooth finish papers are fairly well-loaded to make them suitable for halftone printing. The maximum opacity is usually found in English finish of the staple lines. Bible paper is especially made with opacity in mind, but its flimsy character makes it difficult to handle on the press and in the bindery, if not for the reader himself. It is therefore suggested whenever possible that extremely light, thin papers be avoided.

The highest quality book papers will frequently be found to be less opaque, as in such sheets little filler is added, for filler interferes with strength and durability. The chief reason for putting clay or other fillers into paper, is to fill up spaces between the fibers and level off the surface to make better printing possible from halftones. At the same time it does lend opacity and makes a more mellow feeling sheet.

We now come to the matter of durability and permanence, which seems to be a much misunderstood affair, due to ignorance about paper among otherwise intelligent persons.

When we think of durability, we associate it with the withstanding of physical stresses, and when we consider the question of permanence, we think also of the chemical purity of the paper. There are many papers but moderate physical strength, but of high chemical purity. Converted into books which will not receive hard handling, such paper should last indefinitely. of If, on the other hand, we find a paper of great strength, but from which certain chemicals used in bleaching have not been thoroughly eliminated when the stock was washed, we know deterioration is bound to occur in due time. The original physical strength will thus be undermined. Again, in libraries we often observe stained or spotted pages in books made long ago, when only

rags were used for papermaking. 

Sometimes this occurs as a result of the presence of soluble iron in the water used by the paper mill, all of which goes to show the prime importance of chemical purity in paper which we wish to last.

This disbars immediately from would-be permanent paper, the use of mechanically-ground wood pulps, as their cellulose fibers are mixed with non-cellulose constituents of a very ephemeral nature. It excludes the use of unbleached, chemical wood fibers, because of unavoidable chemical residues which remain in the paper and will presently cause discoloration and disintegration.

Those book papers made of chemical wood pulps known as "soda” and "sulphite," may not be any too strong, but with proper manipulation are comparatively pure and may last indefinitely if not too roughly used. If the proportion of sulphite is high, the strength is proportionately greater, and if this be not reduced by addition of excessive amounts of filler, we have a semi-permanent paper. 

The most permanent papers so far as we can tell by actual experience, must be made from good grades of rags and with comparatively little mineral filler. But it is equally important to know that the paper is free from chemical residues of a harmful nature. Only actual laboratory tests can give any assurance on this point. 

The physical durability of any paper may be predicated on the socalled folding test, whereby a strip of paper is folded and re-folded until a break occurs. The paper specifications set up by the U. S. Government 

Printing Office were drawn up to provide paper suitable to their various demands, but at present no standard specifications have been adopted by the paper industry as a whole. 

Consequently among the available commercial grades of paper it is necessary to investigate in every selection of paper which needs to be permanent, and to obtain satisfactory assurances from the merchant or the mill from which the paper is to come. 

The American Paper and Pulp Association is at work developing paper specifications, but it will be some time before these are completed and adopted generally. In the meantime a number of book paper manufacturers are specializing on permanent book paper made of rag, and it is not difficult to secure such papers if the work can afford the extra cost of them.

The staple grades of ordinary book papers free from ground wood or unbleached fibers, are in no danger of deteriorating as do newspapers-containing ground wood-and provided the finish selected conforms with printing requirements, offer fairly satisfactory selection for the great bulk of our printed productions. Now as it takes years of experience to learn the printing trade, and infinite care in its successful practice, it would be extremely unwise for the author to assume too much responsibility in the selection of paper. If he will familiarize himself with the few fundamentals which have been pointed out, and assure himself that none of them are likely to be violated by his printer, he will at least save himself many disappointments. By W. E. LAVERICK RACTICALLY from the dawn of the human race man has tried to record his thoughts and to preserve such written records. 

As a result, we all have an instinctive respect for a bound book, regardless of its contents. In this human trait lies an excellent business opportunity for advertising printers. 

Book binding is one of the oldest. and one of the most interesting branches of the graphic arts. From the time of the origin of what we refer to as a book, that is, pages of written material enclosed between protective covers, almost every conceivable material has been used for the binding. Stone, such as onyx or marble, ivory, precious metals, wood, skins of animals and reptiles and even human skin. 

The introduction of paper, movable type, and the use of woven cloth for binding material has lifted the book industry from an individual enterprise, which tended to hoard knowledge, to a widespread industry covering almost every phase of human endeavor. Today in the 

United States there are several spinning, weaving and finishing mills whose facilities are devoted entirely to the production of book binding fabrics. 

The production of book binding fabrics is a specialized industry, starting from the raw cotton to the finished product. Specially selected long staple cotton is required. This is spun and woven into some 70 qualities and widths of base fabrics. All are woven to rigid specifications as to the number of threads per inch, the size of the threads, the twist of the yarns, and the strength of the finished fabric. It is also necessary that the fabric be free from any imperfections, such as knots or imperfect threads.

The filling materials used in the production of book binding fabrics divides the finished product into three classes-starchfilled, pyroxylin coated and pyroxylin impregnated.

The formulas for the filling material and the treatment of the cloth vary in each of these three classes.

BOOK CLOTH-This term has come to be known as designation for the starchfilled type of material. This is the original book binding fabric, and it is known to have been used as early as 1822. Considerable improvement has been made in this type of fabric since that time. In the production of book cloth, or starchfilled fabrics, the filling material consists of a heavy paste which is formed by mixing starch, gums and color pigments, all water soluble, into a paste about the consistency of a corn starch pudding. Tapa, or Kada is a cloth made of the beaten bark of the paper mulberry tree. Tapa was made by the natives of Hawaii, and many of the other groups of the South Pacific islands, and was also made in parts of South America and other tropical countries.

Among the Hawaiian and other natives of the South Sea Islands, tapa making developed into a fine art. The bark is stripped from slender saplings of the wauke (paper mulberry) and the green, outer layer of bark is removed, and only the tough, white inner layer retained. This is soaked in water and scraped with sharp edges and rough surfaces of various clam shells, or wooden scrapers. The strips of bark are then laid on a long, wooden anvil (kua kuka), and pounded with a wooden breaker (kuku), made of heavy native wood such as iron wood.

Strength is given the tapa by many layers of bark laid both lengthwise and crosswise, and pounded together with the beater, and sometimes pasted together with a starchy root called pia.

Tapa is ornamented with a great variety of designs, some showing remarkable development of primitive art. One of the earliest designs was that of the fern leaf, and other leaf designs such as that of the breadfruit are seen. Some of the designs used are symbolic, while a great many others are purely for the sake of ornamentation. Geometric designs are often used. Many of the designs are so old that their meaning has long since been lost. Tapa was sometimes stamped with bamboo markers, or the color was applied with crude brushes made of the hola nut. Another method was a crude form of block printing, the design being embroidered in relief on a mat of pandamus leaves, or carved in relief on a slab of wood.

The soft, white tapa is laid on this and the color is rubbed in. The part supported by the relief in the carved design absorbs the color readily, and the remainder is left white, or nearly so, giving a clear imprint of the pattern. 

Dyes and stains are made of red, volcanic earth, and from juices extracted from the barks of various trees which give rich, reddish browns. 

Blue is obtained from the juice of a berry, red from the seed pod of a small tree, and black is made of charcoal or of soot obtained by burning candle nuts under smooth pebbles.

Tapa was used by the natives for clothing, in such garments as the malo (loin cloth), the pau (long shirt), and the lava lava (short skirt used by South Sea Islanders). It was used for bed covering, many sheets being sewn together in the old Hawaiian kapa moe (bed tapa). It was also used for curtains in the houses for mosquito nets, and served as swaddling clothes for the new born infant, and as a shroud for the dead.

Tapa making was always done by the native women, and in ancient times the valleys echoed with the sounds of their rhythmic beating and to the songs they chanted as they worked. It is rapidly becoming a lost art except in a few remote islands of the South Pacific, and the marvelous examples of this ancient art can never be duplicated.

SCHEELE DISCOVERED COLOR 

BECAUSE the Swedish apothecary, Karl Wilhelm Scheele, was too poor to marry, printing is richer today by such illustration as distinguishes these pages.

For in his effort to overcome the poverty which separated him from the woman he loved, Scheele dedicated his days to working and his nights to musing on inventions which he hoped would earn for him a small independence. And it was while he was developing one of these, a poison derived from sulphur, that Scheele observed the action of light on silver chloride and educed the fundamental theory for the reproduction of color. 

Scheele never profited from this or any other of his discoveries. Hardships and unremitting labor brought about his death only two days after he married the woman for whom he had worked so long. But the generations which came after him have profited! On the theories evolved by him they have based color printing and the processes related to it which have done so much towards making fine printing possible at low cost.

Talbot found that by imprinting the mesh of such a veil on a sensitized copper plate and then photographing on this plate, the developed image would be broken up into a composition of dots. 

When etched with acid, these dots acted like the cross hatchings engravers gouged into the surfaces of their plates: when inked and impressed on paper, they reprinted as shadows and highlights.

"William Caxton, the father of English printing, is supposed to have been born about the year 1412; between his fifteenth and eighteenth year he was apprenticed to William Large, an opulent mercer or merchant of London; who was so satisfied with the fidelity of his servant, that he left him a legacy of twenty marks,-a considerable sum in those days. "On the termination of his apprenticeship, he went into the Low Countries, in 1442, either on his own account, or as agent to some merchants; and resided abroad about thirty years. In 1464, he was appointed by Edward 

IV. his ambassador to negotiate a treaty of commerce with the Duke of Burgundy, Edward's brother-in-law. "During his residence in these countries, Caxton acquired the knowledge of printing, the learning of which was facilitated by his commercial character: and his taste for literature could not but be increased in the polished court of the Duke of Burgundy, where he probably improved himself in the French language, from which he afterwards made so many translations. "Here he became acquainted with Raoul 

Le Fevre, chaplain to the Duke of Burgundy, whose Recuyell of the Histores of Troye he began to translate in 1468, and afterwards published in his English version, in 1471, at the request of his patroness, Margaret, Duchess of Burgundy. The original of this work was the first book Caxton printed. . "Previously to the year 1477, Caxton had quitted the Low Countries, and taken up his residence in the vicinity of Westminster Abbey. 

Caxton had, no doubt, brought the necessary materials and implements with him for his trade. The particular spot where Caxton first exercised his business, or the place where his press was first fixed, cannot now be exactly known. Mr. Dibdin thinks it more probable that Caxton, after the manner observed in other monasteries, erected his press near one of the chapels attached to Westminster Abbey: and as no remains of this once interesting place can now be ascertained, there is a strong presumption that it was pulled down in making alterations for the building of Henry VII.'s splendid chapel.

Alone through the mill, with his lamp and his clock The night-watchman prowled through the unfinished stock 

Till he came to a car that was filled with soft "broke" 

And concluded to rest for a moment and smoke.

When out of the dark there arose such a clatter He jumped from the car to see what was the matter. 

Of a sudden he saw a bright light in the mill, No more were the engines all quiet and still. 

The Fourdrinier hummed like a wild aeroplane

While the sight that he saw nearly drove him insane! 

For the paper it made was all painted with holly. 

The machine-tender looked most uncommonly jolly 

For he was none other than famous "Saint Nick," 

And he turned out the paper remarkably quick.

The finishing-room held another surprise- It was chuck full of presents way up to the skies; 

And as fast as the paper came off the machine

The presents were wrapped by some fingers unseen. 

Then Santa Claus whistled, shut down the machine,

And in less than a minute the whole place was clean. 

He made a huge bag from an old machine felt And into its mouth all the presents did pelt; 

Then all of a sudden a great cloud of smoke 

Hid Santa from view-and the watchman awoke! 

He jumped from the broke box, now bursting with flame

While the words which he used I would rather not name! 

He grabbed up a bucket and put out the blaze 

And received from the "Super." the highest of praise!

PAPER TRADE TERMS 

The terms or words included in this glossary are restricted to those which are mainly used in the paper trade as distinguished from the paper manufacturing industry. Technical terms commonly used in communication with sources of supply for printing papers, by printers, publishers, or other consumers of paper made primarily for printing, correspondence, or recording are included. 

For the convenience of those who require more information than can be comprised in a definition, reference is indicated by means of a dagger (†) to the Index of "Printing Papers."†

The vocabulary though principally confined to American terms, occasionally includes the British equivalents. For foreign paper terms refer to "A Dictionary of Paper and Paper-Making Terms," by 

E. J. Labarre, published by N. V. Swets & Zeitlinger, Amsterdam.

For American technical paper terms refer to "Classification and Definitions of Papers," edited by Clarence J. West, Lockwood 

Trade Journal Co. Inc., Publishers. 

"Printing Papers," by William Bond Wheelwright, published by The University of Chicago Press. Absorbent

An unsized paper in which an essential property is absorbency or permeability, as in blotting, filter, or matrix papers. 

Acid Freet Paper containing no acid or corrosive elements. Example, anti-tarnish tissue or bristol. 

Acid Proof Paper made to be resistant to acids. 

Adding Machine Rolls Paper put up in standard width small rolls for use on adding machines. 

Adhesive Paper Paper gummed for labels or tape by treating with animal glue, fish glue, or dextrine. 

Agalitet Mineral filler similar to talc, but more abrasive. 

Air Dried Paper which after being surface-sized, is dried artificially in a continuous web over skeleton dryers or in festoons.

Album Paper A cover paper made for albums, usually black, granite, or gray, having suitable pasting properties for mounting photographs.

Alkali-Proof Paper to resist alkali, or dyed with alkali-resistant colors.

Aniline Dyes† Coal-tar derivatives grouped according to general properties, basic, acid, direct, etc. Basics have extreme brightness, but are not fast to light. Acids are less brilliant, but faster. Direct are much faster than basic colors, and in some cases than acids.

Animal Sizet Gelatin used for sur face-sizing rag-content grades of bond, ledger, and index papers. 

Aniline Sulphate A chemical used to detect presence of ground-wood fibers. 

Antique† A term descriptive of lowfinished paper possessing tooth and bulk. It is akin to "eggshell."

Anti-Tarnish Paper which is acidfree, alkali-free, and sulphur-free, as tissues for silverware, needle and cutlery paper, bristol for jewelry mounts.

Announcements Papers made for advertising or social purposes (weddings and cardboards), often having fancy finishes, deckle edges, or other distinctive features, various tints, usually stocked in writing paper sizes with envelopes to match. 

Ash† The term applied to the mineral residue resulting from burning a sample of paper to determine the percentage of filler it contained.

Basic Colors See Aniline.

Basis Weight The standard weights accepted by trade customs, based upon a standard size for the given class of paper. The weights of all other standard sizes are made proportionate to the size and weights established for the given class of paper. These are known as "basic substance weights."

Beater The engine in which paper pulp is macerated and mixed with the other components of paper. 

Beater-Sized† Paper for which the pulp has been sized in the beater previous to fabrication into paper. 

Bible Paper† A thin, opaque book paper of relatively good strength, weighing basis 25x38-17 to 33 lbs. 

500 sheets. 

Binders' Board A thick board generally composed of mixed papers, and used for making into book covers. 

Standard thicknesses vary from .020 to .060 in.

Blackening A term descriptive of darkening of the intended shade of paper by calendering improperly. 

Sometimes this is called "burning." 

Blanc Fixe† A bright, white minera filler of extremely small particle size, used in combination with china clay as a coating for high grade enamel book paper.

Blanks A class of card board made either on a cylinder machine, or built up by pasting two or more plies together. If uncoated, called "plain mill blanks," if coated, "Coated blanks" or if coated one side only, "car card." 

Bleach† The chemical used to whiten paper pulp, a solution of chlorine.

Blotting Unsized, absorbent paper, generally used for absorbing ink. It is made both plain and coated one-side. 

It may be obtained with embossed finishes and in colors. 

Boards Thick paper made for a variety of purposes on multi-cylinder paper machines, as box-board, wall-board, etc. 

Bogus Bristol A cheap grade of bristol with a news center, groundwood outside liners, and if colored is usually only surface-stained.

Body Stock The term used to denote the foundation stock for any coated ✓ paper or cardboard. 

Bond Paper The name "bond" was #originally given to a paper made wholly from rags, animal-sized, which was used for printing bonds and stock certificates. It is now used generally in referring to hard-sized paper made wholly or partially from rags, or wholly or partially from bleached sulphite and rags, for letterheads and miscellaneous commercial purposes. 13 

Book Jacket A term applied to paper used by publishers to fold over bound books for protective and advertising purposes. 

Book Paper The name "book" was originally given to kinds of paper used in books, but today it does not have this significance exclusively as various so-called book papers are used also for magazines and miscellaneous job printing. 

Box Board Cardboard having suitable thickness and folding properties for the manufacture of boxes. Made on multi-cylinder machines. 

Box Covering Paper which is generally coated, glazed, or decorated on one side only, although plain papers are also used. 

Box Enamels Coated one side boxcoverings paper with high finishes, such as brush, friction, or flint-glazed. Made in white and colors. 

Breaks Term used to denote a fracture in a web of roll paper. Breaks are expected to be spliced, and marked by a protruding "flag." 

Breaking Length The length of a strip of paper sufficient if suspended to break of its own weight. Occasionally used by English technologists, but rarely by American. 

Bristol Board† A term alleged to have first been applied to cardboard made in Bristol, England. It was originally made in the United States as a matter of economy on multi-cylinder machines to avoid the process of pasting. It may be made on a Fourdrinier machine, although bristols so made are usually used as index cards rather than printing bristols. Cylinder-made bristols are designated as "mill" bristols, to distinguish them from Fourdriniermade bristols or index bristol. Extra heavy weights both of mill and index bristol, may be pasted, as are the better grades of wedding bristol. 

Broad Fold A term to denote that the grain runs in the shorter dimension of a sheet of paper. See Long Fold. 

Broad Leaf A tree such as poplar which is used for making soda pulp. 

Broke The term applied by papermakers to damaged paper unfit for sale. Not to be confused with the 

English term "broken" paper which is equivalent to our job lot.

Brush Coating The method of apply ing the coating by means of a rotary brush directly to the paper. It is then spread by flat brushes working back and forth with an eccentric motion. 

Brush Finish A process of brushing paper which has been coated preliminary to supercalendering in order to produce superior finish.

Bulk† The term ordinarily used to denote sheet thickness, but also used relatively to indicate a sheet which is thick in relation to its substance weight, as "high bulk" book paper.

Bursting Strength† Resistance of paper to pressure as indicated in pounds per square inch on a Mullen or "pop" tester.

Chemical Pulp The term often used to denote wood pulp produced by the soda process from the wood of deciduous trees; also called soda pulp.

Chemical Wood Fiber A generic term for any cellulose fiber isolated and purified from any kind of pulp wood by a chemical process. 

China Clay A fine grade of clay used as a filler for paper and as a component of coating mixtures.

Expansion As applied to a sheet of paper, indicates that owing to absorption of moisture, the dimensions mainly crosswise of the grain have expanded. 

Extra A prefix indicating more than normal sizing, strength, or quality, etc.

Facing Paper A paper used for lining or for pasting to enhance the appearance of some other paper or paper board. 

Feather Edge A thinned edge of irregular outline, as a deckle edge on paper. Sometimes artificially produced by the impingement of a stream of water on paper in its wet state, or on dry paper or bristol by mechanical means. 

Featherweight Paper which is light weight for its bulk, as "high bulk" book paper; also certain thin writing paper, and extra light weight coated book paper. See High Bulk. 

Feel A reflection of the sense of touch in judging the finish of a paper. 

Felts In hand making of paper, the woolen cloths upon which the sheet is couched, and between which the sheets are pressed. On a paper machine, the endless woven woolen blankets used to convey the moist web between press rolls. Felts of peculiar design for imparting their pattern to the paper are known as "marking felts." Cotton duck felts woven endless are used as conveyors of the paper web through the battery of dryers, while keeping it in close contact with them.

Felt Side That side of a web of paper opposite to the wire side of the sheet; in other words the top side during the wet state in paper making.

Felt Finish A finish applied on the paper machine through the use of a special marking felt.

Felt Mark An imperfection in paper caused by some coarse or grainy character of the felt after it has become worn, or filled with extracted material disrupting its porosity, so as to disturb the uniform expulsion of water from the web of paper. 

Festoon Dryer A type of mechanism which gathers a web of paper into loops, carrying them in festoons thus formed through the heated drying section. Used both for air-drying surfacesized paper and surface-coated paper. 

Fiber Filament of cellulose isolated from plant or tree for paper-making. 

Fill The maximum width of paper that can be made on any given paper machine.

Fillert A synonym for loading; the minerals used to improve the printing properties of paper. 

Filter Paper Unsized, chemically pure rag paper made entirely from fibers, for use in filtration. 

Fine Papers A term generally used to differentiate between industrial papers such as wrapping, and printing or writing papers.

Finish The surface state of any grade of paper, as "high finish" meaning glossy, "low finish" meaning lustreless, etc. Used as a verb, to finish paper, relates to the trimming, cutting, rewinding, or other preparations including packing paper. 

Finisher One who cuts, trims, counts, or packs paper in the final processes at a paper mill. Not to be confused with finish, or with one who controls finish, as for example, a supercalender tender.

Flock Paper A paper, one side of which has attached a deposit of finely chopped woolen waste to give it a velour-like surface.

Flong The substance of which stereotype moulds are composed; a specially prepared pulp-like board.

The same effects may occur on paper which was properly dried, but which has become affected by local atmospheric conditions after shipment.

Stationery An inclusive term which as related to paper products includes papeteries, typewriter paper, bill-heads, package paper, etc. such as are for sale by stationers. 

Stencil Board A high finished, strong board for cutting into stencils. 

Stencil Paper An impregnated, strong tissue used for duplicating machines.

Stereo Flong The specially prepared paper used for making stereo matrix. 

Stiffener Any stiff cardboard or blank, etc. enclosed for protecting mail matter in transit.

Stiffness Tester An instrument by which the stiffness, or resistance to bending may be measured (such as the Gurley and Schopper).

Stock Sizes The sizes recognized as regular in the Trade Customs of the 

National Paper Trade Assn., and the 

American Paper & Pulp Assn.

Strawboard A grade of board composed of incompletely cooked straw pulp, sometimes made as a combination board with liners of superior material.

Stretch† The elongation of a strip of paper undergoing test for tensile strength. The term is colloquially but incorrectly used to indicate expansion caused by moisture absorption of a sheet of paper. 

Stuff The papermakers' term for his beaten, refined, and mixed materials in watery suspension just prior to admission to the making process. Broken, washed, but unrefined pulps are called half-stock or half-stuff.

Stuff-chest+ The receptacle into which paper stuff is temporarily stored and agitated prior to admission to a paper machine. Also called machine chest.

A similar chest where stuff is held before refining is called the Jordan chest. 

On changing grades these chests must be rinsed, especially in making colored papers.

Substance The basic weights adopted as standard numbers for regular sizes of all kinds of papers. Formerly the substance represented only one ream's weight. In certain papers it now represents the weight per thousand sheets. 

Suction Box† A narrow box with perforated cover, connected with a vacuum pump, used for withdrawing water from the paper web, or from the wet felts of a paper machine. 

Suction Couch Roll† A rotary type of suction box which performs by means of suction the functions originally effected by the pressure of a roll covered with a felt jacket, in expelling excess water and consolidating the web of

Sulphate Paper A paper made of 

100 per cent sulphate or "kraft" pulp. 

It may either be bleached to a white shade, or unbleached and coffeecolored. 

Sulphate Pulp† See Kraft Pulp.

Sulphite Bond A hard-sized (beatersized or beater- and tub-sized) bond paper, which is made in four grades (U.S. standards). The two higher grades only may be watermarked according to Writing Paper Manufacturers' trade customs.

Sulphite Manila A manila-colored wrapping paper made of sulphite pulp, wholly or largely.

Stack of Supercalenders 

Sulphite Pulpt Pulp made from the chips of spruce and similar woods by the sulphite process. (Invented 1866, by Tilghman in U.S.)

Supercalenders† A separate machine consisting of metal and hard paper rolls, (or for use in finishing coated papers, cotton rolls). The metal rolls at top and bottom are the largest in diameter, the intermediate metal rolls the smallest, the paper or the cotton rolls all of the same diameter but different from the metal rolls, create a slight slippage which has a burnishing effect, favorable to producing a high finish.

The paper to be supercalendered is

run off from a roll, and threaded through the stack of rolls which are run at high speed, the pressure being augmented as desired by application of mechanical leverage. Uncoated papers are sprayed or moistened by steam before entering the first nip of the rolls. The degree of finish may be varied at will.

Supercalendered Finish† The fin ish applied by means of supercalenders to book, cover, or other papers to impart a high or glossy surface.

Superfines A superior grade of flat writing paper of more or less high finish to meet the requirements.

Surface Coated† Either side of a paper which has received a mineral coating.

Surface Sized† Often synonymous with tub-sized. Certain papers, however, receive a surface size not after immersion in a tub of size (animal or starch), but by passing between rubbercovered squeeze-rolls, the lower roll licking up the size from a shallow pan into the nip between the rolls. 

Sweat Roll A hollow roll, cooled inside by a stream of water which causes slight condensation upon its periphery, used to restore moisture to the dried web of paper near the end of the paper machine.

Tab Card A die-cut index card with a protruding tab for index purposes. 

Tablet Blotting The blotter affixed within tablet covers; sometimes ruled with printed lines for boxed stationery. 

Tablet News A grade of newsprint used in making tablets for pencil work. 

Tablet Paper A hard-sized book paper of chemical wood fiber, with English or high machine finish for use in making tablets.

Tablet Writings A cheap grade of smooth-finish writing paper. 

Tack The adhesive properties of the enamel surface of coated paper. A term taken from printing vernacular as applied to the stickiness of ink.

Tag Board A strong grade of manilacolored cardboard, sized for writing and suitable for shipping tags. Cheaper grades containing ground-wood pulp are also made.

Tag Envelope An envelope eyeletted so as to serve also as a tag. 

Telegraph Manila A ground-wood grade of yellow-colored manila writing paper.

Tensile Strength† The resistance to longitudinal strain. Paper is tested for tensile strength in both the machine and cross direction from accurately cut strips.

Tensile Stretch† The extent of elon gation before breaking of a strip of paper undergoing test for tensile strength. This is automatically indicated on the testing machine.

Text† A good grade of rag-content or of bleached chemical fiber book paper of medium finish, and bulking properties intermediate to M.F. and antique book. Special attention is given to appearance; often deckle edges and a choice of tints is optional. 

Text Finish† A finish intermediate between antique and machine finish. It is closely akin to vellum finish. 

Thick China A coated two-sides blank made in various colors in 2-, 4-, and 6-ply. 

Ticket Board A bogus bristol supplied in surface-tinted colors.

Schopper Tensile Strength Tester

Ticket Stock A coated board with news center available in various colors.

Tinted Bristols Stock grades of col ored bristols made in various light tints. The No. 1 grade is free from ground-wood. Made in 2and 3-ply, 2212x2812. (Substances 200 and 240M.) 

Tissue Manila Manila-colored tissue made of strong fiber of various sorts. 

Tissue Paper A general descriptive term for various grades of very thin paper, made sometimes on a Yankee machine glazed one side.

Tooth A term implying a slightly rough finish suitable for pencil or crayon drawing.

Top-sizing Synonymous with surfaceand tub-sizing. 

Tough Check A strong grade of cylinder-made cardboard, coated two sides in a variety of bright colors. 

Transfer Paper A specially finished paper of tissue substance, for the making of lithographic transfers, etc.

Translucents A coated cardboard with high, glossy finish. 

Transparent Manifold A very thin, transparent manifold paper. 

Trim of a paper machine, indicates the maximum width of finished paper which can be made thereon, allowing for trimming off the two deckle edges. 

Trimmed Paper which is trimmed on a guillotine cutter on two or more sides.

Tub-sizing The method of surfacesizing whereby the web of paper is immersed in the size vat. See Surfacesized.

Turmeric Paper A test paper treated with extract of turmeric, used to indicate the presence of alkali.

Two Sided A term applied to sheets of paper dissimilar in texture or color, the wire side being more conspicuous than normal, or the color being of lighter shade upon the wire side.

Two Sides Coated Litho A litho graph paper coated on both sides. 

Tympan Paper A specially oiled, or glycerine-treated paper of durable quality used for top sheets on cylinder and platen printing presses; it is supplied in rolls of standard widths. 

Typewriter Paper Bond and writing paper cut and boxed in sizes suitable for typewriter uses.

Unbleached Paper A paper made with unbleached pulp. 

Unbleached Pulp Sulphite, sulphate, or soda pulp which is not bleached and hence is a light brown or coffee color.

Uncoated Blanks Pasted or multicylinder-made blanks free from artificial coating. The latter are sometimes called mill blanks. 

Underlay Paper Any paper used for cutting underlays for press makeready. 

Usually a hard book paper or a thin cardboard.

Under-run An incompletely filled order delivered or made short of the specified quantity. Trade customs allow a certain tolerance for under- or over-runs. 

Unglazed Finish A term used in connection with grades of paper which are also made with a glazed finish, such as Onion Skin. It is a non-glossy finish. 

Union-made Paper made in a mill which is a "closed shop," i.e. one in which full recognition of a labor union has been granted.

Unsized Paper Paper to which no sizing has been added; sometimes called waterleaf because of its absorbency.

Varnish Label Paper A paper hard sized to withstand being varnished, or the use of high gloss inks containing varnish. The latter are popularly used on E.F. and S.C. book papers, which must be sized accordingly.

Vat The tank for containing the stuff used in making paper by hand, or in which it is made by formation upon a semi-submerged cylinder mould. 

Vat-lined Any board to which liners are applied upon a multi-cylinder machine. 

Vat Machine An English synonym for board machine, and cylinder machine. 

Vat-sizing Equivalent for tub-sizing. 

Vegetable Parchment A high grade bleached sulphite paper, parchmentized by treating with dilute sulphuric acid, and then washed and dried. It is thus rendered grease- and bloodproof. It is used frequently for greeting cards as well as for wrapping perishables.

Vellum Finish A finish simulating vel lum, a dull, smooth finish, often applied to bristol board and text paper. 

Vellum Paper A high grade, wellhydrated cream-tinted ledger paper, made to imitate parchment or vellum. 

Velour A trade name for a flock Paper. 

See Flock Paper.

Velvet Finish A trade name for Eng lish finish.

Visiting Card A high grade bristol for engraving social calling cards. 

Made both in thin substances and in thicker pasted plies.

Washer The engine in which rags or pulp are broken, bleached and washed.

Waste Leaves (a) "The outer leaves of tight-joint letterpress books, which are removed before the joint material is pasted to the board. (b) The outer leaves of account books which are made into hinges." (Graphic Arts Terms.) 

Waste Paper† Paper salvaged from printing plants and binderies in the form of sheets or trimmings which may be used as paper stock after cleansing and re-pulping. A bi-product valued according to its origin and condition as raw material for paper making. 

Waste Sheets Sheets of paper used in make-ready, or the spoiled sheets in printing and binding.

Water Finisht A finish produced by the application of water from one or more calender rolls, which are fitted with "water boxes" to keep the rolls moistened. This results in giving the paper a high, glossy surface.

Waterleaf Paper Unsized paper, consequently permeable to moisture and absorbent. 

Watermarkt A translucent marking produced in paper by means of raised designs or lettering, either on the surface of a mould or on the dandy roll. 

Genuine watermarks are also produced by countersunk and embossed designs giving artistic, shaded effects.

Imitation watermarks may be produced by means of impressions made in a web of paper while moist, notably in sulphite bond and ledger papers. 

Waterproof Any paper or board

made water-resistant by sizing, prolonged beating, or other treatment. 

Waviness† A formation occurring along the edges, particularly across the grain of paper exposed to an excess, or a deficiency of normal atmospheric moisture.

Web† The continuous sheet of paper forming, or having been formed and finished on a paper machine. In the finished state a web is not necessarily the full, trimmed width of paper made, but may be any portion of the original web wound into a roll for coating, embossing, or printing, etc.

Web-calendered A paper calendered from a roll of paper, as distinguished from paper calendered in the sheet.

Wedding Bristol A pasted bristol made up from two or more plies of high grade paper, hard sized for writing. The finish may be kid, plate, or linen. 

Weddings A general term covering high grade social announcement and correspondence papers which may have a kid finish or plate finish. 

Weight A term generally applied to the unit weight of a ream, or of 1,000 

sheets of paper. Otherwise the weight of a specified quantity of paper, or indicative of the substance number of any given sheet.

Well-closed A term indicative of a closely formed, or uniform sheet of paper. Opposite of a "wild," or "cloudy" formation.

Wet A papermaker's term for gelatinized, or well-hydrated paper stuff. 

Wet End† The part of a paper machine where the paper stuff is introduced and extending as far as the first dryer. 

White A relative description of uncolored paper, which, however is thrown off the natural shade by small additions of blue or pink dyes. 

Wild A descriptive term denoting irregular formation of a sheet of paper, giving a cloudy look-through. Opposite of "close formation," or "well closed."

Wiret In papermaking parlance, the woven-wire mesh of a Fourdrinier machine. 

Wire-mark The impression left in a web of paper by the wire of a Fourdrinier machine or the covering of a mould of a cylinder machine.

Wire Sidet That side of a web of paper which was originally in contact with the wire of a Fourdrinier, or the mould of a cylinder paper machine. 

The reverse of the "felt" side.

Wood Pulp Pulp of any kind composed of wood fibers.

Wove Paper Any paper showing no laid or chain lines, such as antique wove. 

Wove Mould A mould with a woven wire cover. The first wove hand mould was invented by John Baskerville in about the year 1750 to eliminate laid marks and produce a smoother surface. 

Wrinkles† Creases in paper caused during manufacture. A crease occurring at the presses is called a "wet wrinkle," if at the calenders, a "dry wrinkle."

Writings† A generic term inclusive of hard sized papers suitable for pen and ink writing.

Wrong Side The wire side of a sheet of paper, so-called because it displays the markings of the wire more prominently than the "right," or "felt side."

Yankee Machinet A type of Four drinier paper machine employing a

single dryer of large circumference with highly polished surface. The side of the paper coming in contact with this surface takes a glazed finish. 

Yellow Waybill Copying A sulphite tissue of yellow hue, made in various grades, the cheapest of which contains mechanical pulp. 

Yellowing A defect which develops gradually in paper as the result of the use of ground wood or unbleached pulps, the presence of impurities, etc., and the action of gases, light, etc.

Zone Test A test for the absorbent property of blotting paper, performed by a drop of ink which spreads itself by capillary action, showing zones of fainter and fainter coloration.
