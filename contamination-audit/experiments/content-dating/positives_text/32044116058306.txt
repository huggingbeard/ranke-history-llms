NEED FOR COMMUNITY PLANNING 

Throughout Pennsylvania, long before plans for the 1961 White House Conference were crystallized, a vast amount of energy had already gone into programs for older people. There had been wide experimentation and experience in all kinds of programs - employment opportunities, health services, housing, education, social service, social security and private pensions, leisure time activities, etc.

It is an unfortunate fact, however, that no community in the State has begun to provide these services in the quantity necessary to meet the need. The programs have been spotty, frequently experimental, and usually financed on a temporary and short term basis. 

From Erie to Philadelphia and Wayne to Greene counties most of our programs for older people have grown like Topsy. Some group has become enthusiastic about Golden Age Clubs. Another has built a home for the aged. Another has sponsored friendly visiting. Still another has tried "meals on wheels". have prompted "preparation for retirement" programs. Others

Such activities have usually met with eager response and their sponsors have had much satisfaction from their efforts. They have usually, however, failed to consider two prime questions: 1. 

Is this service the one most needed? 2. 

Does it fit into a network of services which comprise a total community plan?

It is a well established fact that community programs have gained stability and have taken on additional worth and dignity for both sponsore and recipients when they have been part of a unified whole. This is our "gap" in Pennsylvania. 

Community-wide mobilization and over-all planning constitute the next major step in meeting the basic needs of older people. 

The community organization structure or machinery will differ from place to place because each community is different from every other. Each must seek its own method for carrying out the responsibilities peculiar to it. 

Yet every community has these common basic responsibilities: 1. 2. 

To assess the needs of its older people To determine the resources currently available for meeting their needs 3. To institute the action necessary to bridge the gap between the needs and the resources.

Experience demonstrates that such community organization is soundest if carried on through a community or area welfare council which includes in its membership the public and voluntary social agencies; educational, health and religious groups; labor organizations; service clubs; and the general public. 

Where a formally organized welfare council does not exist, the initiative for 

-2-

planning may be taken by a governmental unit, a service club, a church group, even a single interested individual. 

The important point here is that in any community regardless of size effective planning must include representatives of certain interests: health, welfare, education, religion, employer, worker, government, lay-citizen. 

Another important fact demonstrated by experience is that planning in any special area - such as "aging" - should not be divorced from over-all welfare planning. With a problem as serious and unsolved as that of "The Aging" in order to create community awareness and urge to act it is necessary to highlight the problem by giving it special emphasis. This may require special temporary organization such as a County Committee on the Aging. The danger to be avoided, however, is creation of a permanent organization which would be competitive with and unrelated to the over-all planning organization. confusion and lack of coordination can result from such duality.

If the Committee on The Aging should be the community's first experience in coordinated planning this Committee could well form the nucleus of a permanent welfare council.

II PREVIOUS EXPERIENCE IN COMMUNITY PLANNING 

Terms such as "community organization" and "Social planning" are not precise. and often carry differing shades of meaning to different individuals. The idea of assessing needs, determining resources and instituting action on the basis of soundly conceived priorities is well understood, however, and has been practiced with some success in Pennsylvania, especially during the past decade. 

As early as World War I, social agencies were holding joint fund-raising campaigns. The development of Community Chests during the 1920's furthered the trend toward community-wide efforts to meet health and welfare needs. Even the economic and social catastrophe of the Great Depression underscored the values of orderly, community-wide and state-wide social planning. It was at that stage that government in Pennsylvania began to take a greater interest in the process. There was increased financial participation in community welfare programs and more evidence of conviction as to the mutual responsibility of government and voluntary enterprise for meeting total community needs. When the Social Security Act introduced the Federal Government to the partnership, the contemporary concept of community organization for social welfare began to take shape.

World War II slowed, but did not completely interrupt, progress in Pennsylvania in community planning for social welfare. It was during the immediate post-war period, however, that the vital need for such planning began to be recognized.

The expanding population of the 1950's, the movement of mass urban populations to the suburbs, the sharp rise in living standards for most of a/ The Administrative Code of 1929 (section 2306) authorized the Department of Welfare to promote the organization of "Councils of Social Agencies." -3-

Pennsylvania's citizens and the socio-economic effects of transportation and communication improvements were paralleled with chronic depresssion in certain sections of the state, changing patterns of family life and a rise in delinquency and other indices of social disorder. These inter-related developments burst with overwhelming effect on governmental and voluntary health and welfare planning concepts and machinery which were geared to a more leisurely, less. critical period.

When the announcement of the plan for a 1961 hite House Conference on the Aging was ma de (overlapping the impact of a hite House Conference on Children and Youth) the need for adequate community and state-wide social planning took on added importance. What are Pennsylvania's plans for an exploding population of older people? Who will be charged with responsibility for providing essential services? How will priorities be determined? What kind of government-voluntary community and statewide planning is feasible?

The curve of demand for community and statewide social planning from World War I to 1961 would show a rather steady but unspectacular rise through 1946. Thereafter, it would rise more sharply than almost any other social welfare index. On the eve of the 1961 White House Conference on the Aging, it can fairly be said that if the findings and recommendations of that meeting are to be of maximum value to the citizens of Pennsylvania, a way must be found to put them promptly into effect through adequate community organization procedures. 

THE ROLE OF STATEWIDE VOLUNTARY PLANNING 

A "community" in Pennsylvania can no longer be accurately identified by borough, township, city or county lines. Despite the residual importance of these boundaries to the operation of government, the problems of the aging and other social welfare issues must be studied and ultimately solved on the basis of larger areas and groupings of people. The emergence of metropolitan areas which cross county lines, encompass rural areas and draw people into new economic and social relationships is typical of the contemporary scene. 

It has been estimated that almost 78 per cent of Pennsylvania's population resides in metropolitan areas and that almost 98 per cent of the growth between 

1950 and 1960 came there.

The role of the state government in social welfare has become increasingly important. To some, this development is alarming and represents a retreat from sound governmental concepts. To others, it is the inevitable result of the obsolescence of local governmental jurisdictions and provides a more effective and efficient method of meeting the people's needs. 

There is considerable evidence that citizens of Pennsylvania have responded more slowly to changing social welfare needs than they have to other realities of the "space age." There is an observable reluctance to let go of the old and adjust to the new in dealing with health and welfare problems. Nevertheless, in 1960-61 there are potentials both within government and in the field of voluntary enterprise for substantial forward movement. An increasingly large number of citizens recognize the need for a state-regional-local system of voluntary planning for social welfare which will bring cohesion to the health and

welfare programs of governmental and voluntary agencies and guarantee the protection and expansion of human rights and opportunities. 

The number of civic, religious, fraternal, professional and citizen organizations concerning themselves with problems of the aging is growing; the intensity of involvement is increasing. From the women's groups such as the Soroptimists and the American Association of University Women to civic and professional organizations such as the Pennsylvania Economy League and the Pennsylvania Medical Society, the needs of the aging are recognized and the demand for comprehensive statewide planning is increasing. 

The Pennsylvania elfare Forum has placed its spotlight on the subject. The 

Pennsylvania Health Council, with its wide variety of member organizations and agencies, has stimulated attention. Community and regional social planning councils have attempted to find resources for a comprehensive attack. The activities of county committees appointed by the Governor to develop the Pennsylvania report for the 1961 White House Conference on the Aging have had the effect of instilling enthusiasm and enlarging the understanding of many people. 

Various units of the State Government have assumed whatever degree of responsibility is authorized by Pennsylvania's sometimes antiquated law. The Departments of Health, Labor and Industry and Public Welfare administer direct services for the aging. Housing authorities and rehabilitation units have assumed a share of responsibility.

A kaleidoscopic miniature of this scene would unfortunately highlight uncertainty, lack of coordination, inadequate funds, scarcity of trained personnel and general public apathy toward or misunderstanding of the nature of the problem and its possible cure.

For some years the Pennsylvania Citizens Association for Health and Welfare (PCA) has functioned as a voluntary statewide social planning organization. Its membership consists mostly of laymen although both its own staff and many volunteers with professional training provide technical advice to its committees and 125-member statewide board of directors. 

The Association has announced the formation of a FCA Council on the Aging which hopes to offer a means for interested organization, agencies and individuals to participate in a statewide program of community organization for the aging. The 

Council has invited a wide variety of groups and organizations to participate in the enterprise, including governmental departments. It is the avowed purpose of 

PCA to provide a "voluntary" structure for social planning for the aging. 

An organization of this nature should not expect to provide direct services for older people (such as operating recreation facilities or giving medical care or economic assistance) but it can gather facts and issue recommendations and suggest priorities for the guidance of those who will be responsible for these services. The extent to which recommendations may be accepted will probably be in direct proportion to the effectiveness of the organization in developing a broad base of public understanding of them. 

An important function of such a unit might be to serve as a clearing-house for organizations which do accept responsibility for direct services for the aging. 

In order for "community planning" on a statewide basis to be effective, the wholehearted support and participation of all such groups, including governmental units, will be needed. -5-

III POTENTIAL RESOURCES FOR LOCAL PLANNING FOR THE AGING 

It

Planning is a continuous process which is inherent in our daily life. is almost never an isolated experience but rather one which directly affects the people around us, and the conditions under which we live. The unit of society most deeply concerned with social planning is the family and plans wisely made, and carried out reflect themselves in sound family life and in turn in a strong nation. The "Aging", therefore, should be thought of not as a category to be "planned for" but as individual members of a family group who as long as they live want to carry their share of work and responsibility and to maintain their independence. Even those who have no immediate family, have their roots in a community, interests in church or social groups and convictions about 

a way of life. It any constructive plan is to be made they should be an integral part from the beginning.

Plans may be developed informally with little or no information, or based on simple facts, or a deeper survey may be made. Each community should decide for itself. 'n Pennsylvania where our geographical formation influences our transportation, communication, agriculture, industry, etc. - no one plan could apply satisfactorily. 

Cities or counties in which there are Councils of Social and Health Agencies or Councils of Community Services are prepared to give leadership in planning the the type inquiry best suited to your area. Many cities of course have had 

Committees or Study Groups on this subject for some time. 

Villages and rural areas may start such inquiries under the auspices of any organization which is experienced in dealing with people in trouble or who are ill or handicapped. The Office for the Aging, Department of Public Welfare, Harrisburg, the local Visiting Nurse Service, the Red Cross, the Church, the local Health Officer - all know something about the needs of older people, and frequently can offer not only skilled advice but help in getting out questionnaires and compiling answers. 

The motive which starts such an inquiry is usually strong enough to get something done about it, and once the facts are in. Each County in Pennsylvania now has a Governor's Committee on the White House Conference on Aging. If this group has not taken the initiative already, consult with them and plan together to find out: how many people over sixty-five live in your county, where do they live, and what services do they want and need?

Parallel with this inquiry should go another one estimating the resources of your area (available jobs, housing, leisure time activities, care of the sick, and special services such as counseling, homemaker services, etc.) 

Match them up list the laps and gaps, exchange experiences with other communities, both like and unlike your own, and establish priorities. 

Use the same technique that you used during the ware, the depression or an epidemic or disaster to mobilize your community for action. While age may be less spectacular than a disaster - make no mistake it has gained great headway and the old age you plan for others will shortly, in most instances, be your own! -6

IV-A ESTABLISHING SERVICES AND PROGRAMS FOR THE AGING IN SMALL COMMUNITIES 

The establishment of programs and Services for the Aging in small communities requires a much different approach and planning technique than that employed in large or metropolitan areas. 

In small communities 

villages, towns, boroughs, and rural areas - there are few, if any, private agencies and not many public agencies, institutions, or sources of funds that can be called upon to assist in establishing and maintaining meaningful programs for older persons.

Since small communities do not have the advantages of large communities for Community Organization thereis a tendency to avoid or neglect establishing needed programs with the excuse that the proper kindsof resources and help are unavailable. This is not the answer - small communities need and deserve as much assistance in local Community Organization as can be given. 

Organizing in the small community usually starts from "scratch." At the offset, a study should be made of available resources, what needs exist, and whether or not enough persons and groups are sufficiently interested to plan, initiate, and maintain activities that will help elderly citizens. 

The process and procedure used in organizing in a small community brings into sharp focus the wide variety of approach technique employed as compared to that used in large areas where basic facilities, trained personnel and funds are usually available.

In order to illustrate how a program of services for the aging was stimulated and launched in one of our smaller counties, the following resume of activity in Monroe County is presented, to demonstrate the approach and methods used. 

It is not intended as a model or stereotyped procedure that would guarantee success in all small communities.

PLANNING STRUCTURE IN A LA GEETROPOLITAN COU!ITY 

In the Philadelphia area the Health and Welfare Council is the successful central social planning agency for this County and those surrounding it. 

Traditionally, the Council operated with functional divisions in the major Services areas, whereby a Division on the Aging was one component, all under the Board of Directors. It had become obvious that problems affecting the aging in many areas cut across along the lines of other service divisions. For example: health was a concern of the Division on the Aging but was a prime responsibility of the Health Division, to say nothing of the Family Division and the Children's Division. Even the Recreation Division was involved in health situations. Hence there was necessarily overlapping and needed cooperation between the several Divisions, and occasionally question as to prime responsibility.

It was therefore determined that the entire Health and Welfare field would be best served if those involved in planning were familiar with and involved in all concerns, rather than divided into special interest groups separately active, necessarily cooperating, but often varying of satisfactory coordination, acceptance of responsibility, and hence accomplishment. As of September 1960, our Health and Welfare Council will have a single Services Division under its Board of Directors, which will concern itself with al problems on a coordinated basis.

The professional staff of the Council will still be specialized but the consideration and planning of projects and activity will be unified under a single jurisdiction. In order not to lose every forum for special interest groups and to highlight special problem areas in separate Health and Welfare fields, there will exist advisory units to the Services Division. Such will have no working responsibility nor project activity, but will serve to consider, highlight and advise the Services Division in the narrower areas of specific fields - like Aging. It is believed that the public must still be made aware of the special concerns we have for the aging population, and hence there must be a special forum devoted to problems of the aging, at least until we have successfully met the most pressing needs in this area, when perhaps thereafter there will be less focus thereon. 

It is, of course, much too soon for any knowledge of the success of the proposed new format for our Health and Welfare Council. We predict, however, that the identity of a problem centered approach for services to the aging can be maintained and achievement of solution will occur through a coordinated Services Division prodded by a specialized advisory unit. Then when such 

Services Division recommends handling a problem, the entire resources of a coordinated Health and Welfare Councill will most effectively work toward its goal. -10

EXCERPTS FROM THE NATIONAL BACKGROUND PAPER ON LOCAL COM UNITY ORGANIZATION Advantages of Establishing a Community Committee on Aging 

The apparent advantage of a community committee on aging lies in the fact that it permits a focus on all needs of the aging and can interrelate the various areas, such as income maintenance, housing, health, and recreation. 

The major disadvantage is one of isolation. The very existance of a committee on aging suggests that arbitrarily there are problems which arise at some arbitrary age, and therefore emphasis should be placed upon establishment of geriatric services and institutions. This is a dangerous assumption. If such programs are developed at the expense of other health and welfare services in the community, or if their establishment isolates the older person from general community life, we would judge their development to be undesirable. The statement has frequently been made, however, that programs for the aged, or general community health and welfare prograns for which the aged are eligible, have been in scarce supply, and that special emphasis needs to be given at the present time to bring into balance programs available to older people. 

Perhaps the issue implied by this observation could be resolved were we to say it is desirable to establish a community committee on aging if it functions as an integral part of, or in close cooperation with, the over all health and welfare planning structure of the community, and if it maintains its focus on keeping older persons in the life stream of the community. 

Sponsorship of a Community Committee on Aging

Who should sponsor such a committee: Government, private agency, or independent citizens? Should it be lodged in an existing agency or a new organization? We refer you to earlier parts of this report where several principles or guides for local organization are enumerated. The sponsor which can operate most effectively in light of these principles will probably be the most appropriate for successful action. 

The questions are raised, however, because experience has shown that sponsorship under various types of auspices has presented both difficulties and advantages.

Where there is a strong, effective community welfare council, it is wise planning to have the committee on aging be part of this council so that: (a) it is representative of the total community including the general citizenry and the various agencies providing service, (b) it has authority and is able to be effective in over-all community planning and (c) it does not isolate planning for the aging from planning for other population groups. 

Leadership and Community Participation 

How can top leadership of a community be involved? Who should be involved in order to cover the total aging field? These are broad questions, subject to many conditions. Space permits only a few generalizations. 

1. The impact of aging on every individual, the severity and complexity of many problems in aging, including the economic significance of problems of old age on the American scene, mean that top leadership can be readily interested if challenged and mobilized, -11-

2. Top leadership is attracted by other top leadership and by the status or potential status of the sponsoring organization. 3 Generally, the top leadership expects to do a superior job and must, therefore, have an assignment which is both challenging and encompassable, and in which there is an even chance of successful accomplishment. 

4. Leadership must have the tools with which to work, including appropriate structure, staffing, and access to resource materials. 

The job to be done generally determines who should be involved. It is difficult to include everyone who has a contribution to make in the total aging field. Some persons, by virtue of particular expertness, can make the best contribution on a subcommittee or special project. We suggest, however, that the key principle to be followed is to be certain that major community groupings and fields of interest are represented in the over-all committee together with citizen leadership, experts in major fields, and representatives from significant organizations and agencies which have a contribution to make. 

Perhaps the key to deciding who should be involved depends on the answer to these questions: who knows most about this subject; who can best help do the job and get community support; who would present a major obstacle if he were not included; who has the vision, the objectivity and the push that is needed?

The increasing interest and activity on the part of business and industry, labor unions, churches, civic and fraternal groups, libraries and educational institutions as well as health and welfare organizations, offers a rich reservoir of services and of community interest and support. 

Focus and Timing

It is probable today that the majority of communities face unresolved problems with respect to their aged population, and these problems will not automatically resolve themselves. However, it is true that, with the development of programs on the State and Federal level, some problems which have been considered essentially local problems in the past may be resolved at a higher level. To a certain extent this means abnegation of local responsibility. 

Ilevertheless, there is an important role for local community organizations in maintaining a continuing review of the State and Federal programs. 

In this way, policies and administration can be improved and the programs can more nearly fulfill the purposes contained in the laws creating the programs. Many significant problems remain for local community action. There is the danger, however, that with the current national impetus given to planning for the aging, communities may "jump on the bandwagon" without taking the necessary preliminary steps of establishing adequate planning structure. Purpose

The purpose of a committee on aging has been implied throughout this discussion. Perhaps, however, it should be stated explicitly, lest the tendency be to focus on organization and activity to the neglect of people. Organization for itself has no merit. Organization to be meaningful must fulfill a purpose. The purpose of a committee on aging is to serve people, to help a community provide a climate in which life can flourish and to provide services which will aid, sustain, and enrich the later years.
