A CHEERFUL SHUT-IN

The Shadow 

Na hot summer morning, a year or so after beginning to visit the Rhode Island Hospital, I went there with a turn down collar and a light sack coat. Going along one side of Ward C and returning on the other I heard a voice say, "Come over here again.'

It was from an Irishman with a beaming countenance and grey hair. 

Drawing me close to him he said quietly, "I want to ask you something." "What is it?" I replied, thinking the "something" might be quite serious. "Where's your clerical collar?"

Abashed, I explained why I did not wear it that day. "Keep it on always," he replied; and pointing to another good looking Irishman of the same age next to him and, with a sweep of the hand along both sides of the ward, he continued, "We fellers like to know that a priest thinks enough of us to come here and see us. Even if he only looks at us and nods and says nothing it does us some good." 

There was a collar, ever afterwards, to cast a shadow.

A Promise Kept 

MONG the first persons in the hospital that I got AM intimate with was an Italian youth, eighteen years old, Vincenzo by name, with a face like that you see in some pictures of St. Sebastian, who, though pierced with arrows, had the look of love in his eyes.

He had been brought from Italy to this country two years before by a brother already here. 

He was in the hospital with a tubercular leg and was often in a great deal of pain. The doctors did all they could for him for nearly a year in trying to save the leg. Then came a time when the leg must come off. Vincenzo was greatly discouraged and thought he was going to die. To me who was trying to cheer him he said, "Will you promise me something?" He got a little better after the operation and for a while was quite hopeful, though he felt lonely because his brother, the only relative he had here, had gone back to Italy to marry there the girl of his heart.

After a summer away, going to the hospital I looked around for Vincenzo. Perhaps he had died and I hadn't gone to his funeral.

"What have they done with Vincenzo?" I asked, hoping he was still alive somewhere.

He had been sent to the Infirmary at Howard. 

I went there hunting him up and found him on the upper floor among a lot of silly old men. He wept when he saw me and grasping my hand tightly, said, "Do take me out of here, I can't stand it."

Through the doctor in charge he was moved downstairs where there were young men in the same dying condition he was and where he could see trees and hear the epileptic children playing in the yard.

The priest of the Italian church which he attended could find no record of his being a communicant or a contributor and said, "I can do nothing, I have not his name. He must be an imposter."

The Superior of a Sisterhood advised consulting the then auxiliary bishop and he suggested sending him to Hillsgrove.

I sent him there, paying for an automobile to take him, and the next day he was sent back to Howard at my expense.

The Sisters sent him a medal. That pleased him. 

The priest gave him the Sacrament. That strengthened him. I brought him fruit. That cheered him, coming from a friend, his only friend.

It was November when the message came to me, "Vincenzo is dead."

I wanted to know when and where his funeral was to be. The brother, who had just returned from Italy

with his bride, had come to see him, but had done nothing about the funeral. Earning only nine dollars a week, he could not afford it. Vincenzo was to be buried the next day. "Wait for me," I requested.

In a wagon with the coffin I was driven to the Potters Field, where two men were digging the was the officiant and the only mourner. grave. I

When the grave was filled I put on it a bunch of asters and said aloud, "Rest in Peace, dear Vincenzo."

The promise was kept.

Something Inside 

NE day in war time I noticed well down the ward a heavily built young man on whom rather talkative neighbors seemed to turn their backs. 

He was a Russian Jew and that may have accounted for the aloofness of his neighbors. He looked rather askance at me, perhaps because of the clerical collar, and was surprised that I should speak to him. Having been here hardly a year his English was broken. In the three or four weeks he got to be friendly. I felt that he was worth knowing.

I used to meet him frequently on North Main 

Street. His face would brighten and kindly greetings would follow. 

By and by I would ask him, "Aaron, have you got a girl?” 

At first the answer would be, "No," and then "Not yet," and later, "Yes, I have; and she is fine." 

After that the question came, “Are you married?" and the reply was "Yes. You must come and see us.'

He gave the name of a nearby street and the number. 

Returning to my rooms on a Thursday afternoon after a summer in Maine, I found the cleaners had broken some panes of glass and I said to myself that it was a good time to get in touch with Aaron again. 

He was a glazier. Going to his shop about four

o'clock on Friday, I said, "Aaron, I want you to come to my rooms tomorrow (Saturday) and put some glass in my window."

He put his hand to his head and looking at me steadily, said slowly but firmly, "I cannot come then, it is our Sabbath," and after a moment or two continued, "but I tell you what to do. Go to your rooms with this measure and telephone me the size of the panes and I will put them in right away."

I did this and he came. While he was working I said, "Now, Aaron, I know why I have liked you from the first. You are a man of principles and you stick to them. Give me your hand!" "That is funny," he said, "it is something like what

A Good Reputation 

ONE meets, especially in the Rhode Island Hospital, strangers it would seem from all parts of the world "Parthians and Medes and Elamites, dwellers in Mesopotamia and in Judea... strangers of 

Rome, Jews and proselytes, Cretes and Arabians.” 

One day the Visitor came across a man, evidently a 

Turk, and hardly knew how to approach him. "You are a Turk, aren't you?" One of the interesting puzzles is to guess a man's nationality and then his age. The Visitor becomes an adept at it. "Yes," he replied. 

On the spur of the moment the Visitor asked, "How many wives have you?" "None; I am not married." "If you were married, how many could you have?" "If the first one had no children, I could have another." "You see how little we Americans know about you

Turks. It is generally thought you can have as many wives as you can pay for." 

Strange as it may seem that introduction made the

Visitor and Ali, that was his name, close friends. He was cook for a small colony of Turks working in factories and had used washing soda instead of baking

soda and eaten a good deal of his product himself before offering it to others and was badly poisoned thereby. 

One Friday morning as the Visitor entered the hospital, a nurse in the lower corridor meeting him said, "Your friend the Turk has gone," and when he got to an upper corridor, another nurse said, "Your friend 

Ali has gone, and we are all sorry." "Why are you sorry?" "He was always polite, and when he left, he hunted up everybody who had done anything for him doctors, internes, nurses, orderlies and thanked them." 

Ali was a text for me for some time afterwards. 

Whenever I saw a Turk in a bed, with an Englishman or an American on either side of him I would say, "Well, there is one thing I know about you and that is that you are always polite."

Then I would tell him the story of Ali loud enough for his neighbors to hear.

Katie's Easter 

T the midday service on Easter Day, rather tired from three services held previously a message in lead pencil was handed me. "Katie wants to see you. 

Come as soon as possible."

Katie was a student nurse in the City Hospital, and had come there from some hospital in Massachusetts for the training in contagious diseases for which the hospital is noted. She had caught scarlet fever and pneumonia followed.

After the service a friend drove me to the hospital. 

The head nurse met me and said, "You must have something to eat first," and led me into the dining room. Just leaving the table was the mother who had been summoned from a far off corner of Nova Scotia, a woman with character written over her sorrowful face. "I am glad you have come," she said. "Katie is dying. I want you to tell her so and ask her if she is ready to meet her God." 

With the joy of Easter still in my heart I was not quite ready to do all that in the way the Scotch Presbyterian mother would have it, and said, "I like Katie and will do what I can for her."

Sinking into a chair I had a good lunch. I said to the nurse, "I can't tell that girl outright that she is

going to die. You have a little hope for her, haven't you?" "A little. While there is life, you know, we hope." "I am going into your study first and smoke."

I went and smoked and smoked, and thought and thought and prayed inwardly that I might do the right thing. After a half hour I was ready. The nurse put a sterilized gown on me and with a pot of bright tulips brought from the church I went into Katie's room and said, "A Happy Easter, Katie. See these flowers! They were right by the altar and we prayed there that if it was God's will you might get better." "Thank you," she said and her face brightened. "You remember what I asked you the other day to say all the while, don't you?" "Yes, and I have been saying it, "Thy will be done." " "That is it. The doctors are doing all they can, and the nurses are doing all they can and we all want you to get well. But we don't know. You may not get well. God is going to do what is best for you, one way or the other. You know that, don't you?" "Yes."

I took Katie's hand. We all knelt by her bed. We said together "Our Father" with stress on "Thy will be done," and I said two other short prayers and then committed her into God's gracious care and keeping, the mother trying to smother her sobs all the while.

I said "Good bye" and stood by the door a moment.

With a wave of the hand came softly from her the words, "Thy will be done! Thy will be done!" 

Katie died that night. 

From that mother, bereaved of her only child, every Easter for ten years past I have received a small gift and a card bearing the inscription, "In grateful remembrance of Katie's Easter."

A Pleasure Expected 

HELEN, a lovely Irish girl, thirteen years old, was in the hospital a long while with some heart trouble. She was always cheerful, though sometimes so weak that she had to be kept in a quiet room. She requested the nurses to be sure and let me know where she was. 

One day, her eyes brighter than usual, she said, "Do you know, Father, I send all those things you give me to my mother, and when I get home I am going to take them one by one and tell her the stories about them that you told me and I shall have all the pleasure they have given me over again."

The next Friday I looked all over the ward and the adjoining rooms for Helen and could not find her. I asked the nurse in charge, "Where is Helen?" "Don't you know? She died yesterday."

I stood still and wondered whether Helen was re-telling those stories and what pleasure she was having from the telling.

The Virtue of a Smile 

"I'M going to die! I am going to die!!" I heard a woman shout at the further end of a ward as I entered it. 

I went to her immediately. "Yes, I am going up and I am going to die," she shouted shriller than ever. "I have a husband and three children. I am going to die and I don't want to die."

She repeated this again and again with all the women in the ward staring at her. 

Her voice betokened she was Irish and I said, "You have seen a priest and you have had the Sacramenthave faith in God!" "Yes! Yes! but I am going to die!" still at the top of her voice. "This woman by the side of you went up last week and she is all right, and that woman on the other side went up the week before and she is going home." "Yes, I know, but I am going to die, and I don't want to die." "Yes," I said, "you are going to die sure if you 'go up' with that feeling. There is no doubt about it, you will surely die.” "I don't want to die! I don't want to die!" 

Then I remembered a funny story. I have forgotten what it was. I can laugh as heartily as any one

at a funny story but I cannot tell one with any effect -I either forget it or tell it twisted. But the emergency of the moment brought this one to mind and I told it straight and told it effectively. She smiled and the women all about her laughed, and she laughed as loudly as any of them. The smile on her face lasted a moment or two and I said quickly, "Keep that smile. Keep it! Go up smiling and you won't die."

The incident was in the back of my mind. I had forgotten it when I entered the next week the darkened, quiet room where I merely say "Good morning!" or "I hope to find you better next week."

A woman in a far corner beckoned to me with her forefinger. I recognized her and said, "You didn't die after all!" "No, I didn't-I went up smiling and the doctors smiled and the nurses smiled; and I thank God and I thank you."

When she got out into the larger ward she pointed to a woman in the corner and said, "Go tell her my story, how scared I was and how foolish. Don't spare me.'

Her operation must have been a severe one for she was there convalescing for five or six weeks, and every week I had to tell some woman the story of how she went up smiling.

Miss Muffet

OING into a women's ward one morning a dozen years ago I was struck by the stillness there. No one was speaking, faces were glum, and the nurses were walking, it would seem, on the tips of their toes. 

I could not tell what the matter was. The ward felt like a tomb. I thought there had been a death or two. 

On a table by the third bed was a leaflet entitled, "Are you prepared to meet your God?" and on the next table a leaflet headed "Have you repented of your Sins?" 

Tear gas had been let loose.

From every table I took the little bombs, crumpled them, that they might do no more damage, and threw them on the floor. What to do to counteract the effect of the gas was for a moment the problem. 

Suddenly I was conscious of a box under my arm and taking it in my hands I stood in the middle of the ward, held it up and said, "Can any one of you women tell me the story of an old maid, very pretty I think she was, very small, rather squeamish about her diet and loving fresh air?”

Father and Son

AT the City Hospital a half dozen years ago, in the corridor leading to the syphilitic ward for men, I stopped a moment and had a word with the nurse in charge. When I got inside the ward I was surprised to be greeted immediately by a familiar voice, "What brings you here?"

It came from a man approaching fifty, who had been a boy in the choir of a church in another state. 

Bright and attractive he was in a large degree-so much so that he won the affection of one of the nicest girls in the town. I married them. The wife came to me later when I was on a visit to the place and said that she had to get a divorce for the sake of her children and herself, because of the habits of the husband. 

Here after a score of years was a man whom I had admired and hoped for as a boy. Sorrow filled my heart to the brim. I had heard other sad stories in that ward, but this was the saddest, because I had had a part in it.

The talk, however, was as cheerful as possible, turned as it was to the glad days of old times. 

Affected deeply by this unexpected meeting, I hunted up the orderly and asked him whether the man's trouble was of recent origin. 1

"He is a puzzle," was the reply. "The doctors think the trouble may have been from long ago." 

Going there again in a few days, intending to have a longer and more serious talk, he had gone, the orderly said, apparently in an improved condition. 

Five or six months later I had word from the hospital that the man I was interested in was there again, in a very bad way, not likely to live, and wanted to see me.

The longer and more serious talk came then and some praying. Before I left he took my hand in his and said with tears in his eyes, "There is one thing I want you to do for me. I want you to see my boy, whom I have not seen for fifteen years and tell him all about me. I don't want him to be as I am.' 

He died the next morning. دو The father's request has not yet been carried out. 

In answer to inquiries made the report has come, "The boy has joined a circus!"

A Christening Party 

WHEN a man is sick the good in him generally comes to the front; if there is any bad it stays in the background. The first time I saw Henry the good in him was apparent. Though he wasn't prepossessing in appearance, there was sincerity in his eyes and a heartiness in his grip. 

He was of English extraction and not thirty years old. He had married a French Canadian and had two children. What troubled him was that the children had not been baptized. "Never mind now," I told him. "When you get well we will have a christening party and a good time."

He was sent home and in a few days had to be taken to the hospital again, so his wife telephoned, and wanted to see me. I went at once and found him much worse. He held my hand tightly through the rather long, friendly talk and before he let it go said in a questioning voice, "You are going to baptize the children?" "Yes," was the assuring answer. 

He died the next day.

At his home, in a slummy part of South Providence, his mother, having Henry's bright eyes, rather flabby in shape and untidy in dress, expressed gladness

at seeing Henry's dear friend and said, "You are to have Henry's funeral." "Yes," said a sister in a raucous high voice, "for 

Henry loved you."

The wife was pretty and neat and superior in intelligence to the rest of the family. It was easy to see why Henry married her.

At the close of the funeral service I said to the widow, "I am going to baptize the children as soon as possible," and she replied, "Henry wanted it.”

The next Sunday a friend invited me to dinner. 

After dinner I told him and his wife and his wife's sister the story of Henry, and said, “You must take me to see Henry's family." The two ladies went with us.

It was dusk when we reached the miserable tenement and so dark within that we could not tell who were there until a smoky lamp revealed Henry's mother, his sister, the pretty widow and the two children.

After some talk about Henry I said, "I am going to fulfil Henry's wish." 

The thought pleased them greatly.

The two ladies, who later were a great help to the family, served as sponsors along with the raucous voiced sister of Henry. Having brought vestments along we had the service with as much solemnity as the smoky lamp and the dingy furniture allowed.

Such was the christening party Henry had looked forward to. Perhaps he was conscious in some way of the happy event!

Where Happiness Prevails 

'HE minor heartstrings are seldom touched in maternity wards or in the Lying-In Hospital. 

For a short while there may have been travail and a good deal of anxiety, but a Visitor seldom sees anything of it. The mothers' faces are wreathed in smiles. They are glad, as of old, that a child is born. 

Congratulations are in order. Compassion is not called forth, except in one or two cases where the baby has died.

It is not often that any unusual event occurs. One goes away with the fever of happiness.

The Visitor was attracted one day by the Murillolike beauty of an Italian woman hardly thirty years old, and asked her, because of her youthful appearance, "Is it your first baby?" "Oh! no, it's the eighth," she replied with an operalike voice in very good English. "The first was a boy and is now thirteen years old, a very bright boy. I want him to go through the high school. My husband is a laboring man, and I fear we can't afford it. I don't want any more babies.”

In a sympathetic tone the reply was, "See a doctor!"

Another place where joy abounds is the Crawford 

Allen Home on Potowomut Neck, Narragansett Bay, one of the most beautiful spots in Rhode Island, where it is an inspiration to be. Here fifty or more orthopedic children are scientifically treated and lovingly cared for. The original gift of the house and some two hundred acres which was munificent as well as beneficent, has been made more efficient by another building, perfectly equipped and with an endowment for its upkeep, erected by Stephen Metcalf and some of his relatives. It must reward the donors in some measure to feel the happiness of the helpless children it shelters, and to note from time to time the improvement in their physical condition and to know that many of them are fitted there for some usefulness out in the world.

The Visitor always has a cheering welcome and goes away with a large degree of the happiness that prevails, as was lately indicated in that of a little boy who for three years had been strapped to a frame, coming to him, almost dancing with joy, and saying, "See me now! I can walk!"

I was puzzled. "Don't you remember? I am the man from West

Warwick that was in the hospital and we had a lot of talk about the people there and old times." "It was three or four years ago, I recollect. We got to be pretty good friends because I lived out there once." "I don't forget," he interjected. "Well," I continued, "I am going to be buried out there some day in St. Philip's Churchyard." "I'll remember," he replied, "and I'll put flowers on your grave."
