​THE DARE STONES BY H. J. PEARCE, SENIOR 

Brenau College has already published two Bulletins, March '39 and 

January '40, relative to the Dare stones.*

The bulletin of March '39 related the discovery of the first Dare 

Stone on the Chowan River (N. C.) in September 1937.

The bulletin of January 1940 related the discovery of thirteen stones near the Saluda River (S.C.); one in Hall County (Ga.) and nine in Habersham County (Ga.) in 1939-both near the Chattahoochee River.

Since these publications twenty-three stones have been found in three different places in Fulton County near the Chattahoochee by five different people-two of them being concerned in previous discoveries.

The names and addresses of the five persons, who delivered the stones, are as follows: Wm. Eberhardt-Northside Drive, Atlanta; I. 

A. Turner-Lindbergh Drive, Atlanta; William Bruce-Powers Ferry 

R. F. D. Atlanta; Henry Campbell-R. F. D. 1, Roswell, Ga.; T. R. Jett- 

Ellenwood, Ga., R. F. D.

White's Colonists, backed by Sir Walter Raleigh, arrived at Roanoke Island on July 

22, 1587. Virginia Dare, the Governor's granddaughter, was born August 18 foliowing.

One week later White returned to England for supplies. Arrived there in November 

1587 he found all England astir with preparations to meet the menace of imminent Spanish invasion. Queen Elizabeth stopped all shipping in English harbors. Despite efforts on the part of White and his patron Raleigh, it was not until March 1590 that White was able to return to his Roanoke colonists. He arrived off Hatteras August 

15, 1590/91. On first coming to anchor he was cheered by seeing smoke rising from the island. The effort to effect a landing resulted in the loss of seven men in the treacherous waters. However, the survivors eventually reached land and sought out the site of the colony on the northern end of the island. 

Disappointed at not finding his colonists White, was cheered at the evidence which seemed to indicate that they had removed to Croatoan. On the next day (August 19), the ship Captain of the expedition, who was not interested in the search, reluctantly agreed to go to Croatoan; but various difficulties ensued, including a heavy storm, and the captain insisted on sailing away from the dangerous coast. White never saw his colonists again. In fact, there is no evidence that they were ever seen again by white men.

if Twenty-four stones now in the museum of Brenau College, authentic, reveal at least in part the fate of the long "Lost Colony of 

Roanoke" Island, N. C.

According to this revelation it would appear that the colonists did move from Roanoke Island to Croatoan-an island south of Roanokeas tradition relates. This island cannot be definitely located. - 

But within two years or less, either from lack of food or hostility of the Indians, they removed to the mainland and traveled southwest to what is now Greenville County, S. C., and located near the Saluda river in 1589.

There sixty-four of the colonists died or were murdered by the Indians. After the massacre of seventeen colonists, including Virginia and her father Ananias Dare in 1591 the remaining seven, accompanied by "four goodli men" (presumably, friendly Indians), travelled southwest.

Thirteen stones record the death and burial of these sixty-four colonists. These stones were found by Wm. Eberhardt of Atlanta, Ga., in 

Greenville County, S. C.

The next evidence of the movement of the colonists-now only seven in number-is a stone found by I. A. Turner of Atlanta, Ga., on the Chattahoochee River about five or six miles north of Gainesville, Ga. This spot would be about 75 miles southwest of Greenville County, S. C.

The first of the Dare stones was found by a tourist, Mr. L. E. Hammond of Alameda, California, in October, 1937, on the banks of the 

Chowan River, some five or six miles from Edenton, N. C.

This stone clearly states that Ananias and Virginia and fifteen others were murdered by savages (1591) and buried four miles east of this river-"names all written there on rock." The first assumption naturally was that this second stone would be found near the Chowan and extensive search was made in that area without result.

In May, 1939, Wm. Eberhardt of Atlanta, delivered thirteen stones * which he claimed to have found on a hillside near the Saluda River about twelve miles south of Greenville, S. C.-nearly four hundred miles southwest of Roanoke Island. One of these recorded the murder of 

Ananias and Virginia and fifteen others.

It would therefore appear that the stone found on the banks of the Chowan River in North Carolina must have been carved by Eleanor Dare on the banks of the Saluda River, S. C., and the most tenable theory seems to be that Eleanor sent this stone by a friendly Indian to North Carolina in the hope that it might be delivered to her father whom she constantly expected to return to his colony. 

Upon our suggestion that other stones should be found on the Chattahoochee River, ** Mr. Eberhardt made a search of the banks of the Chattahoochee River in July, 1939, and found nine stones, about eighteen to twenty miles north of Gainesville-dated 1591, 1592, 1593.

When announcement was made of the discovery of these stones, Mr. I. A. Turner of Atlanta, delivered to us a stone dated 1591, which he claims to have found on the banks of the Chattahoochee about six miles from Gainesville in the month of March, 1939. 

The story of the "Lost Colony" thus revealed seems to be as follows: 

The colony left "Croatoan" soon after their removal from Roanoke Island and travelled southwest through North Carolina and South Carolina.

As our record only accounts for seventy-one members and there were originally 119 members, manifestly 48 were lost or killed in Roanoke or in the journey from Roanoke to Greenville County. We believe other stones will be found between Roanoke Island and Greenville County recording the death of other members of the colony. 

The record does not show just how long they remained in Greenville County, but they certainly arrived on the banks of the Chattahoochee in 1591 and remained there certainly until 1593 under the protection of the King (presumably of the Cherokee nation or their predecessors), who shewed them "moche mercye."

After the publication of the foregoing excerpt from the Bulletin of January 1, 1940, the story seemed again to end. 

We were greatly impressed by the large number of stones being piled upon us and, I think naturally, somewhat skeptical. Some of our friends were even more so. Of these, the former head of a very large organization with branches in all sections of the nation offered to have the three men, who thus far had delivered the stones, investigated. We of course were glad to avail ourselves of this offer. 

After several months of careful investigation, our friend's agents reported that they had been able to find no evidence of collusion. 

For several months there were no further developments of importance.

We made several trips to Nacoochee valley, where the University of Georgia was making a very successful excavation of an Indian Mound. We even went to the crest of the Blue Ridge mountains where we observed some very well known pictographs, which are manifestly of Indian origin. But no trace of the lost colony. k Mr. Eberhardt stated that one of these stones was found 8 or 9 years ago and had been in his possesion ever since. 

This prediction was based on statement on stone found near Greenville, S. C., viz: 'Father wee goe sw." See page 5.

And then, about mid-summer (1940), our lagging interest was again aroused by the announcement by Wm. Eberhardt that more stones had been found on the Chattahoochee, about ten miles from Atlanta.

Again the stones had been removed and cleaned with a steel brush, but we were shown the places where three of them had lain. One of them, about 22 feet long twelve inches wide and three inches thick had been standing fifteen inches deep in the ground. The stone fitted exactly into the hole which had been left. 

Several stones were found in the same vicinity, by the same man, but we were not shown the exact spots where they were found. 

The frequent use of "We" refers to me and my wife and son who accompanied me on trips and helped in deciphering the stones and discussions with all of the participants in the story. 

After announcement was made in the Atlanta papers concerning the finding of these stones, Mr. Wm. Bruce, who lives on Powers Ferry road, about a half mile on the Atlanta side of Powers Ferry bridge, informed us that he had just found a stone, with curious marking on it, a short distance north of Powers Ferry bridge (about fifteen miles north of Atlanta) We finally succeeded in deciphering the carvings on this stone and it proved to be a "Dare Stone." Later Mr. Bruce found another stone in the same vicinity. 

About a year ago when I. A. Turner delivered to us the stone which he had found, about six miles north of Gainesville, he told us a story about seeing a stone when he was a boy in the old Jett mill, about four or five miles from Roswell, on the Atlanta side of the river. There were curious marks on the stone which no one could, decipher but it was thought to be "Indian writing." Turner remembered then that three letters on it appeared to be the numeral 8. After finding the other stones below Gainesville we became more interested in Turner's story and visited the old mill site-the old mill long since having disappeared.

We looked around the vicinity, with little hope, and found nothing, but Wm. Eberhardt and Turner, both of whom had accompanied us on our first trip, returned the next week and made a more thorough search, and found the stone in a ditch, not more than fifty yards from the old mill site. They immediately took the stone to Mr. A. I. Jett who had moved from the old Jett place to a farm fifteen miles south of 

Atlanta about fifteen years ago.

Mr. Jett immediately identified the stone as the same one which had lain around his fathers mill, by what he thought were three figure 8's in the carving. We later succeeded in reading the carving and the 

8's proved to be g's. We also visited Mr. Jett and heard him tell his story of the mill stone.

He also remembered another stone which his brother had brought up from the river with other stones to use for pillars in a barn which his father was then building. This stone had curious markings on it which could not be read. His father broke it in half and put one half in a pillar (built without mortar) under the barn. 

Upon our invitation Mr. Jett went with us to the old home place, which he had not visited since moving, 15 years ago. The place is now owned and occupied by his nephew Mr. Henry Campbell who knew nothing about the stones. The old barn was torn down some years ago. 

Upon his return home, Mr. Jett told his wife about our search. 

His wife remembered that when they were moving, fifteen years ago,

that she threw a rock with curious markings and some Indian relics in an old tool chest; that they stored this chest at her fathers home near Jonesboro and had had no occasion to move it since (15 years) They went now to her father's home found the chest, and the half stone. 

In the meantime Turner went back to the old Jett place to talk to Mr. Campbell further about the matter. Turner sat on the bank of the river for two hours while Campbell was fishing and discussed the matter. Finally Turner decided to leave and went back to the house to get his car and go home. Campbell sent two of his boys along to see that Turner got off all right.

Turner, instead of leaving, walked up the road about 75 feet looking in the grass and weeds and found the half stone lying almost covered by grass and weeds.

Campbell then remembered that when he was cultivating his corn across the road early last summer, that he used a stone as a weight, to hold down the cultivator, and when he finished plowing, he threw the stone across the road in the grass and weeds. He did not at the time, notice any markings on the stone and did not remember just where he picked it up, but it must have been somewhere near the crib, where he must have started his plowing. 

Later, these two pieces of stone, found fifty miles or more apart, fitted together perfectly.

We have stated as nearly as possible the bare facts. We are as yet committed to no final conclusion. We have been as amazed as any one could be that so many stones should be discovered within three years after having lain for three hundred and fifty years where they must have been seen by innumerable people. We have at present no thesis to establish. We are still considering several hypotheses, trying to establish a variety of facts, and-looking for more stones! 

There are among the 47 stones, sixteen recording the burial of 64 people in South Carolina and six people in Georgia. It is manifest that the burial stones, before they were found by any of our searchers, had been removed from their original resting places. Whether the actual burials were near or remote, we do not know. We shall make such efforts as we can to solve this as well as other problems. 

MEETING OF HISTORIANS 

Feeling the need of advice and assistance in the solution of the problems presented, we invited a number of historians and scientists to attend a meeting at Brenau College October 21-22 for the purpose of studying and discussing the 47 stones. 

At a meeting at noon October 21, Dr. H. J. Pearce, Jr., Vice President and Professor of History at Brenau told the story and discussed at some length the problems involved. In the afternoon the visitors were free to examine the stones; and in the evening at 8 o'clock, with 

Dr. Morrison of Harvard presiding, a closed conference for two hours or more was held. At the close of this conference a motion was passed authorizing Dr. Morrison to appoint a committee of five with himself as chairman, to express the conclusions, if any, of the conference. 

On October 22 the entire group visited the cave and other points where stones were found in Fulton County near Atlanta. A picnic luncheon was served on a high hill, where Eleanor is supposed to have been buried, and from which a view of Atlanta, ten miles in the distance, was enjoyed.

At the conclusion of the luncheon, the folowing statement was presented, and unanimously adopted by the assembled group: 

At a meeting of 34 scholars invited to study the Dare Stones at Brenau College, and to study the problems connected with them, a committee was appointed to make a public statement. This statement represents the opinions of the committee only. The report is under two heads: (1) The committee believes that the preponderance of evidence points to the authenticity of the stones. (2) Suggestions for further study of the stones are being made by the committee to Dr. Pearce. Until this investigation is concluded, no final conclusion can be reached.

Secy. North Carolina Historical Commission PROBLEMS 

We have been constantly cognizant of the many problems presented, some of which were discussed at the meeting of scholars. On the negative side we may mention the following— (1) Too many stones, apparently obvious and yet undiscovered for 350 years. (2) Lack of corroborative evidence of the discovery in majority of instances. (3) How could the carvings have been made in the wilderness 350 years ago.

On the affirmative side however much can be said: (1) Here are the stones, 47 of them. (2) The story told by the stones is in perfect harmony with facts related by history. (3) One of the stones has three letters covered by lichens which must have required many years to grow. (4) One of the stones is in two parts, which fit together perfectly. 

One witness remembers his father broke the stone and used a part in a pillar for a barn nearly thirty years ago. 

When he moved from the old home place, about 15 years ago, his wife threw the other half in a tool chest and they stored the chest at her father's home, twenty miles south of Atlanta, and left it there undisturbed until this matter came up. 

The other half, according to the present occupant of the old home, has been "throwing about" the place for years the old barn having been torn down. Last summer he used the stone as a weight on a cultivator when he was cultivating his corn. He threw it across the road in the weeds, where it was found in September after our search had been started.

It is obvious that the facts hitherto presented afford at least some bases for argument, but as we have made no claims, we do not propose

now to enter into any argument. Professor Paul Green of North Carolina, says there may be thousands of those stones. 

While we hardly dare hope to find so many, we do believe that others may be found between Roanoke Island, N. C., and Greenville or Anderson, S. C., and along the Chattahoochee River from Nacoochee valley to Atlanta in Georgia.

We cordially invite the cooperation of fishermen, woodsmen and others in these areas in the search. 

High school teachers might render a real service by telling the story to their students and enlisting their interest in the search-to the benefit of their minds as well as their bodies. 

SKELETONS 

The burial stones found in South Carolina had undoubtedly been removed from their original resting places. We excavated where the stones were found and were not greatly disappointed when we found no skeletons, nor any evidence of burials. 

These stones were found about half way down a rather steep hill. 

The top of the hill, several acres in extent, and thickly covered with underbrush and many trees, might have been the burial ground. It will require a good deal of time and money to determine. 

We do not know as yet the exact location near Atlanta where the burial stones were found, except in the case of the burial stone of Eleanor Dare. This was found on a rocky ledge near the foot of a rather large hill. Another stone found ten miles or more up the river states that Eleanor was buried on a "greate hil." The hill near the foot of which the stone was found might be the "greate hil." From its top, a beautiful view of Atlanta may be enjoyed. But it again is ten acres or more in extent. Moreover many evidences have been found that this hill played its part in the "battle of Atlanta" during the '60s and there were doubtless many burials there. 

And so, it is very doubtful if the grave of Eleanor Dare can ever be identified.

Some of the others may be identified. If not, then the mystery of the lost colony may remain a mystery, but we still have the stones. 

ADDENDUM 

One of the main objects of the visit of the 34 historians and scientists to the site near Atlanta where so many stones had been found, was to see the cave in which Eleanor Dare was supposed to have lived from 1593 to 1599, near the site of what had manifestly been an Indian village. Near the rear of this cave on an inconspicuous ledge was an obscure carving "Eleanor Dare heyr sithence 1593." A number of pictures which have appeared in newspapers throughout the nation have shown Dr. Morrison and others peering at this rock in the cave.

We had secured permission from the owner of the property to close the cave, but, before we could do so, we were informed that the rock had been cut out by a more or less irresponsible youth. Fortunately, however, through mediation of some friends we were able to recover this rock; although broken in two pieces, the carving is still legible, and the rock has been deposited in the Museum.

CONCLUSION 

While no final conclusions have been reached, it might be interesting to observe that, if the story be true, we have evidence that seven white English settlers were in this section some years before the English founded Jamestown and before the Puritans landed at Plymouth Rockand about 150 years before Oglethorpe founded Georgia.

Brenau students will be assisted by a large number of talented young men from the community, and it is hoped by native Indians from North Carolina.

This drama includes scenes from the Court of Queen Elizabeth in England when permission was granted to Raleigh to send the colony under the leadership of John White and also scenes on Roanoke Island, the perilous journey across the Carolinas, the massacre near Greenville and finally the more peaceful but still stirring scenes on the banks of the Chattahoochee.

BRENAU MAY FESTIVAL 

May 2-5 This has become an annual event at Brenau. 

The Gainesville Festival as usual will consist of several Operas. 

The Brenau Faculty which contains several artists who have had extended and successful experience as opera singers, and some of our most talented students, will be supplemented by several visiting artists from New York, Atlanta and elsewhere who have national reputation. 

The choruses will consist of Brenau students assisted by N. G. A. C. at Dahlonega and Riverside Academy and also young men from Gainesville and talented singers from other neighboring towns.

The Festival will also include a Shakespearian play under direction of the Brenau School of Speech, a recital by Franciszek Zachara, our noted Polish pianist, and also a concert by the College Orchestra under the direction of the eminent Director, Enrico Leide.

These occasions it is hoped will bring to Brenau many parents of our students and many alumnae and other friends of the college. 

After the operas receptions will be held on the stage or on the campus at which visitors will have an opportunity to meet members of the casts and also the faculty and student body of the college, and to see the Dare Stones.

HOUSE PARTY 

A house party will be held at Brenau College May 2. 

Students in any high school who are recommended by the Principal are invited. Write to the President of Brenau, Gainesville, Ga., and indicate your desire to come and arrangements will be made for you.

Opportunity will be afforded the visitors to share in two days of actual College life. Dances, games, chapel services and other activities will be arranged for their benefit.

The Brenau Ideal 

To find satisfaction in being rather than in seeming; to find joy in doing rather than in dreaming; to be prepared for service thereby earning the right to be served; to be pure in heart, vigorous in mind, discreet in action; to love deeply, fear nothing, hate never; to enjoy freedom which comes from knowledge of the "Truth"; to be modestly conscious of the limitations of human knowledge and serenely confident of the limitless reaches of human endeavor-this is the ideal of Brenau. -H. J. Pearce.
