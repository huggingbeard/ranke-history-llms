THE GIFT OF PHILIP HOFER CLASS OF 1921

Sumner Mead Roberts

Med 23 8. 1 

HARVARD COLLEGE 

Feb 23,1940

LIBRARY 

Philip Hofer

AⓇ 

S WE MEMBERS of the Class of 1921 grow older, and our paths branch far apart, there are fewer and fewer events which bring us together again-in the comradeship which college offered in an almost unclouded form. Only something which stirs each individual deeply can do that. Reunions become difficult to attend and are infrequent. 

The times have made us critical of, or indifferent to, success. 

There is only one thing which can surely affect everyone: the loss of a leader. Recognized, or unrecognized as such, the sudden death of Sumner Mead Roberts, in an automobile collision near Hyannis, Massachusetts, November 19th, 1939, means just that. We should pause, and take stock. We cannot adequately assess what we have lost; for his life was cut off in mid-stream. But perhaps a short account of his career to this point will show us our gain from the inspiration of his example. 

The mere mention of events and the suggestion of characteristics with which so many of us were familiar will kindle other memories. Our common heritage in these can bring us close again.

The facts of his early life need only be mentioned briefly. 

Sumner was born on January 25, 1898, at Dedham, Massachusetts, the son of Odin and Ada Mead Roberts. He attended public schools, and later prepared for Harvard at the Country Day School in Newton, where he was a member of the student council, and of the football, baseball, and track teams. Before entering college in 1917, he spent one year at the Evans School in Arizona, together with Paul Cabot, Dome Lowell, and Bunny 

Brooks '22. After Freshman year, the World War caused him, as it did most of us, to volunteer. He entered the Navy, saw limited service on a cargo vessel to South America, and was finally commissioned an Ensign. But by then peace had come, and he resumed his studies at Harvard. 

It was mainly in the last two and a half years there that those of us who had not known Sumner "always" realized his presence, and gradually-for he was very shy-became attached [3]

to him. With many it was "just luck"-a chance meal beside him, a geology field trip, or an escapade. He was not a conventional college "success"-he did not shine at athletics, on committees, or even in his studies. He did well in zoology and biology---but he shone as a companion and a friend. Few who remember Sumner on our Senior Picnic will ever forget him there: the wittiest, kindliest, but not entirely most dignified performer, of the occasion. Sumner despised snobbishness and pomposity. He was unaffected by conventions and his membership in social clubs.

After graduation he elected to go to the Harvard Medical School, where he spent four happy, hard working years. Yet he also managed to take time off to play. Those who traveled with him in the summer of 1922 in Europe found him apparently as carefree as when he ran down Linden Street after a football game the year before, popping roman candle stars into his friends' windows. But evidences of a serious purpose and unusual talents were not lacking. Sumner could do, as we say, "anything" with his hands. He assisted in a slight operation on himself, without the help of even a local anaesthetic. He could tell you any biological fact about an animal, fish or bird. 

Even to this day some of us remember, thanks to Sumner, that an elephant has no gall bladder, and that a sea gull has 125,000 pancreatic islands!

Rapidly he achieved an unexcelled reputation as an "after dinner" speaker. If a vote were held now, who that knew him would not declare him the best "raconteur" in our class? While relating one of his stories his handsome face usually wore a surprised look. Or else he began and finished his story without expression. The most he would ever condescend was to look even more surprised when his audience roared its applause.

Yet no embellishment was too small to be included, no nuance so subtle but it found expression in his voice, or by its position in the tale. Many of us will see him standing now-straight as a ramrod-with a starched shirt that would never quite stay [4]

down. But there was always an awkward dignity, a detached composure about him which made one think of an eagle surveying his domains. He wore a single tie for a year till it fell apart, and a brown hat all the way from the crater of Kilauea, in Hawaii, in 1919, to Buckingham Palace, in 1922. And yet somehow better dressed men looked a little superficial and ill at ease beside him.

Sumner graduated well from the medical school. As far as conventional book learning was concerned, he sometimes rose above it. But he made his mark in practical cases, especially whenever a living being was concerned, be it man, or animal. 

It is not surprising that his talents developed noticeably during his internship under Dr. Eugene H. Pool at the New York 

Hospital, and after he began to practice orthopaedic surgery at the Boston Children's and Massachusetts General Hospitals. 

His ability was at once recognized and he was asked to join the medical and surgical partnership headed by Dr. Robert B. 

Osgood, in Boston.

Despite this success Sumner remained simple, unaffected and shy. He still found time for play: duck shooting trips in the fall with Eddie Pierson and Arthur Rotch, fishing with 

Fuller Albright, "Down-East" cruises with Cab Storrow and

Paul Cabot. Somehow the simplest fisherman and huntsman liked him. He loved a trip where one went back, or almost back, to nature. There was a fish tank in his room, and all sorts of strange biological relics in his closet. Almost anything might appear out of the pocket of a suit. 

To the great honor of his fiancée and wife, these were never taken away from him! In 1927 he married Elizabeth Converse of Dedham, and was as devoted a husband and father as he was a friend. They had three children, Diana, born in 1928, Daniel (whom he called "Daniel Boone"), born 1931, and Lloyd, born 1934. His wife and children survive. 

His wedding was a gala occasion. Never had his stories sparkled more brightly, never was his warm friendliness more [5]

keenly felt. Knowing his love of animals, someone brought a live goose to his bachelor dinner. In spite of grave dangers, it is believed that this goose survived to a ripe old age. Sumner would permit no overt cruelty, though it was attempted. The wedding day, December 27th, was overcast. Wet snow fell intermittently. But within the Converse house cheer and hospitality reigned, such as those who attended will never forget. 

This was not so much impelled by the famous wine made on the "estate", as by the natural high spirits of the family and of those friends who realized already that a finer match had never been made! 

From his wedding trip in Bermuda, Sumner brought back a large pail full of tropical fish and a pickled octopus. The fish passed the customs easily, but grave doubts arose as to the alcohol in which the octopus was embalmed. These were prohibition times and there was doubt whether alcohol could be imported at all, even if it contained so unsavory an object! Then 

Sumner returned with his bride to Boston and settled down to work harder than ever. Some people say he grew "serious." 

Others will know that the same marvelous comradeship was there, only he had less time to demonstrate it to his old friends. 

A higher purpose called-he began to share more and more of himself with his patients—to reach out to a wider, more needy circle. He wanted to save time for his family. He became deeply interested in teaching. 

These last few years his name and fame as surgeon, lecturer, and teacher, spread far and wide. Stories came in of his amazing resourcefulness in a difficult situation, of his uncanny intuition when he was brought a case which other doctors had been unable to diagnose. His patients often spoke of his "kindly calm," of his thoughtfulness, of his ever observant comings and goings, which to many seemed "priest-like." They all felt he was their friend as well as their doctor. He was far above professional jealousy. Gratefully, people have told of his visits to other doctors' patients in the late hours of the night, wherever [6]

he sensed suffering. He was frank and open in consultation, did not keep one in suspense, nor deceive one. He wagged a long finger at the writer of these lines, and said “Oh, you'll be coming to me to be chopped up some day all right-only not yet.” Another patient's mind was completely relieved when 

Sumner pretended to find a bit of bone he had overlooked during the operation in the open wound. Afterwards he said "What difference does it make if I had to take the blame for a mistake I never made, so long as she feels better?" This complete unselfishness was as characteristic of him as his unworldliness. He held himself as nothing-his profession, his chance for service were everything.

Recognition was ever increasing. He became a fellow of the 

American College of Surgeons, a member of the American Medical Association, the Massachusetts Medical Society, the American Orthopaedic Association, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, and still other medical groups. At the time of his death he was President of the Boston Orthopaedic Club, and was Assistant in Orthopaedic Surgery at the Harvard Medical School. He also held numerous hospital appointments: most important of all, at the Massachusetts General Hospital, also at the Huntington Memorial, Robert Breck Brigham Hospital, and Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary. He was on the staff of the Faulkner Hospital and Dedham Clinic, and a Lieutenant Commander in the Naval Reserve. He wrote widely for medical journals and textbooks. 

How could he find the time? His friends all wonder, as testimonials pour in from the highways and the byways after his death. How could he continue to be a fine amateur ornithologist, a skilled fisherman and hunter? This side of his genius is far too little known. He was far more interested in wild life than in the shooting or the fishing. He caught bass mainly to "tag" them, which difficult feat, Eddie Pierson reports, he accomplished with maximum efficiency and minimum hurt to the bass! How could he play touch football with his boys, [7]

and go on cruises in the summer? Only a few weeks before his death he injured his shoulder, and laughlingly acknowledged it was his rightful punishment for being such a "clumsy surgeon." 99 Sumner died "in the line of duty." He had just visited a patient at the Hyannis hospital-and he was alone in his car when the collision occurred. He was struck and instantly killed by another car. Sumner was always a careful driver. There was nothing to indicate that he was at fault. 

Fate has played few more ironic, inexplicable rôles. Those with weaker faith in the wisdom of the infinite will ask why 

Sumner was killed when the world needs him so badly? Not even those of strong faith can answer. But this much we all know: the fullness of his life did not consist in the number of his days, but in what he accomplished, how he grew, and what he always, and increasingly, represented. To every single being who knew him well, Sumner's life and memory shine like a beacon to guide us on our own weary, plodding paths.
