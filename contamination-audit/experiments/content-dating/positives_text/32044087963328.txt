ENVIRONMENTAL FOUNDATIONS OF EUROPEAN HISTORY "Europe" a Historical Designation 

The scene of European history has not always been the continent of Europe as marked on atlases today. Occidental civilization has traced an unbroken history from African Egypt and Asiatic Babylonia, Assyria, Phoenicia, and Palestine. All these lands faced or lay not far from the 

Mediterranean Sea. 

Europe forms the north coast of the Mediterranean and projects into it in three considerable peninsulas. The easternmost of these makes intimate contact with Asia Minor, by way of the island-studded Aegean Sea. 

Thus abetted by nature, the Mediterranean and all its shores African, Asiatic, and European 

-1 have constituted throughout much of recorded history, a principal theater of the pageant which we call European civilization and of the society which we call Occidental. (1) The migration of this civilization from the southeast corner of the Mediterranean World to the Europe of today is crudely suggested by the maps in Shepherd's Atlas, nos. 4, 5, 34-35, 58-59, 179-182. 

These maps represent arbitrary points in the course of history, expressed as convenient round numbers about a thousand years apart, dating back from approximately our own time. They show that only a tiny corner of the physical continent of Europe figured in the civilized world three thousand years ago Greek land of broken peninsulas and offshore islands reaching toward Asia.

A thousand years later the entire Mediterranean Basin, but very little of the land north of it, had been brought into the orbit of civilization. 

Expansion northward and eastward was slow throughout the next millennium and a half. The Ural Mountains have been taken as the eastern boundary c. of Europe only during the present era. 

The boundary accepted today has no more reality or fixity than those of the earlier periods shown on the maps, because politically Europe has come to be thought of as a continent only through the operation of history. 

Physically Europe remains but one of the four gigantic peninsulas that project from the Asiatic continental block (Shepherd, Atlas, 2-3). Although the life of all four is occasionally disturbed by the intrusions of peoples from the central land mass, each division has developed its society largely in the peculiar frame of its own natural environment. 

Europe, the westernmost of the peninsulas, is almost completely cut off from the eastern half of the Asiatic continent by a broad belt of desert. This parched zone of meager resources and sparse population, like all dry lands, is subject to sharp variation in rainfall, from year to year and from epoch to epoch. Many believe that prolonged periods of drouth periodically forced denizens of this unhappy area to push outward in search of food, and so gave rise to recurrent folk wanderings that affected peninsular Europe.

Whatever their cause, such migrations have been noteworthy features of European history. The invaders have invariably followed a belt of grassland that runs between the desert and the forested north, all the way from central Asia to the Ural Mountains and beyond, as far as the open plain of Hungary in the Middle Danube Basin. Narrow in Central Asia, this belt widens invitingly toward the west, beckoning the stricken nomadic horsemen of the Asiatic interior to follow it to the still more favorable lands of

Central and Western Europe, and even to the shores of the Mediterranean. 

Repeatedly the peoples of the North European Plain, of the central mountain blocks, and of the Mediterranean coastlands have been uprooted by intrusions of peoples from eastward. Thus the thrust and recoil of folk-migrations originating in inner Asia have been chief among the forces determining the boundary of Europe at decisive moments of history.

PART I. EUROPE ON THE THRESHOLD OF THE MIDDLE AGES A Tripartite Entity 

The Europe of the present day thrusts its roots deep into the period of Classical Antiquity. Indeed, some of its most acute problems have their origin in part in the territorial pattern created by the Romans to serve the world of their era. Conditions laid down by nature were seized upon, and to some extent modified by Roman society, and the medieval world adjusted its life to an environment impressed in different degrees by Roman technology. In the Mediterranean, heart of their world, the Roman impress was strong, and has never disappeared. Beyond the immediate Roman political boundaries the land was subjected to Romanization only at second hand, through trade during Roman times and by penetration of Roman ideas and institutions throughout the Middle Ages. In the areas lying between the 

Mediterranean world and the northern and eastern confines of the Empire, the Roman impress was either effaced or blurred when these lands were overrun by non-Roman people in the fourth and subsequent centuries. 

The European 

Mountain Barrier

The Roman Empire reached its maximum extent with the conquest of the 

Lower Danube Basin (Dacia) in 117 A.D. (Shepherd, Atlas, 34-35), (Map of the 

Roman Empire at Maximum extent). The core of this empire was the Mediter ranean coastland, with its intricate pattern of peninsulas and islands. On the south and east, Roman authority was limited by an effective barrier -the wide and unbroken series of deserts that runs like a bend sinister across Asia and Africa. On the north its bounds crossed the barrier zone of mountains that runs disjointedly along the northern margins of the three

Mediterranean peninsulas. 

These mountains, unlike the deserts, interpose no unbroken bar to the movement of people and goods, but rather constitute an intermittent barrier, Pyrenees, Alps, Carpathians, Balkans, and a series of ranges and masses others. 

Passes are deeply notched in the crest lines of all these ranges. 

Many are famous in history. In the eastern Pyrenees is the Perthus, through which Hannibal led his troups on the way to Rome. In the Alps are the lofty St Bernard, known for its deep snows and its ministering dogs; the St. Gotthard, an important influence in the creation of the Swiss nation; the low Brenner, bone of contention between Germanic and Italian peoples from time immemorial to the present moment. In a few places there are narrow lowland breaches between adjacent mountain groups. The most notable of them, the Rhône Valley and its connections, provides a route of navigable streams and low watersheds. 

Despite numerous passways, the ranges that mark the northern confines of the Mediterranean World hinder the movement of people and goods and are of decisive military importance. Important as they have always been in these connections, they have served even more significantly to mark and accentuate a climatic boundary of the first order of magnitude between the Mediterranean coastlands and North Europe.

The Mediterranean 

Enviroment

Throughout the Mediterranean region the summers are rainless, and during the winters the land receives only moderate precipitation. Fortunately this moist season, although cool, is warm enough to permit the growth of crops.

The summers are hot; hence crops thrive then too, wherever water to irrigate them is available from stores in the hills and mountains.

Because the highlands lie nowhere more than a few miles back from the seashore, small supplies of irrigation water are widely available. 

Under such conditions the arable land, originally lightly wooded or covered with grass, could be turned into farms without preparation beyond the powers of primitive people. The soils, too, have played a helpful part. Thanks to a high limestone content, nearly all were fertile to begin with and respond well to careful management. Every winter rivulets from the wooded mountains spread a thin coating of silt on the gentle alluvial slopes built fanwise along the base of the ranges. This material, originating nearly everywhere in limestones, weathers into fresh, productive soil, retentive of moisture. In consequence, a small acreage of arable land can provide a living for a family in a climate in which plant growth is never halted by prolonged low temperatures. High yields have always permitted the farmer to concentrate on a small holding, and by careful, intensive tillage (including deep plowing, frequent weeding, and rotation of crops), to maintain soil fertility. 

As time passed and man's life became more civilized, each generation felled a little more of the mountain forests and denuded the lower slopes of brush to make charcoal. Then the rains of winter swept the exposed soil and subsoil off the steep slopes, and swollen torrents, sorting it, dumped the coarser part on the alluvial lowlands in the form of gravelly sand, and carried the finer material out to sea. Thus in a single operation of outraged nature, the uplands lost their earth mantle by erosion and the lowlands lost their fertile soil by burial. With vegetation and soil removed or diminished, the water-storage capacity of the mountains was greatly reduced, to the detriment of irrigation.

Throughout the Mediterranean world the resulting curtailment of the amount of arable land and diminished productivity of that which remained

were twin evils that increased as the period of Classical Antiquity gave place to the Middle Ages. They did not fundamentally alter the mode of life in the Mediterranean World, but they diminished its soope. 

The Enviroment of Northwest Europe 

In the part of the Roman Empire to the north of the mountains, natural conditions differed from those in the Mediterranean core of the Roman world. The Romans, accustomed to a habitat in which water is the critical, and therefore the dominant, natural element, found Northwest Europe a strange world, to which they were irresistibly drawn by the opportunities for trade along the expanding frontier -- -- an exchange enhanced by its contrast with the Mediterranean environment. 

To begin with, the scale of the region is larger. The plains, instead of being habitable patches of lowland facing the sea, are broad. 

Many of them lie well inland and are surrounded by belts of hill land. 

Most parts of the lowland plains could make contact with the sea only by long, winding riverways. Like the Mediterranean lowland basin, the plains of Northwest Europe were generally favored with soils derived from limestone. Such of these soils as were naturally well-drained, had been denuded of their light cover of deciduous trees in Neolithic times, and were already available for growing wheat, the staple food crop of Mediterranean peoples, when the Romans came in. Lowlands with heavier soils and clothed with forests could be converted into farmland as fast as the preferred light soils were preempted. Since the heavy soils were generally unsuitable for wheat, they had to be planted to oats, buckwheat, or forage 11 crops strange to the newcomers from the Mediterranean climate. 

To the differences of landforms and soil were added unfamiliar

climate and resultant vegetation. 

Instead of a rainless and brown summer landscape, the countryside was green and lush at that season. The summer On was not a great deal warmer on the average than winter in the Mediterranean, and was the only season when crops throve. Roman immigrants found the winter uncomfortably chilly and unpleasantly rainy, but not unbearable. the land this was the dull season, but it was not cold enough to kill fallsown wheat, or to deaden the grass, upon which stock could graze throughout the year.

The contrast in natural environment was conducive to trade. For 

Mediterranean wine, olive oil, woolen cloths, parchment, and other luxury goods, the Northwest could exchange forest products and beeswax 

timber, pelts, hair, besides hides, flax, and wheat from the farms. In the cool, moist climate, iron ore was found in scattered bogs, set amid woods which yielded fuel to smelt it. Far away in the northwest, Cornwall was the only important European source of tin a necessity in making bronze, upon which 11 the civilized world still relied, in Classic Times, for many metallic implements and utensils.

The progress of the Romans to the northwest was smoothed by the broad gap in the mountain barrier between the Pyrenees and the Alps. The 

Massif Central of France stands in this breach, but it leaves open a lowland pathway on either side. The Mediterranean shore of this double opening is almost identical in climate with other Mediterranean coasts of Europe, although it is more subject to storms from the north. 

Lowlands more than thirty or forty miles from the coast, both in the Rhône and Garonne basins, have a climate transitional between Mediterranean and North European. In keeping with the climatic transition, the soils and the natural vegetation alter by gradual degrees. In the south of France the Romans were provided with a natural laboratory in which they could learn

the lessons necessary for successful settlement of lands farther to the north and west.

The northernmost boundary of the Empire was drawn near the margin of the crystalline uplands of Britain, known today as Scotland and Wales. 

There rugged terrain, infertile soils derived from crystalline rocks, and an eternally cloudy and rainy climate combined to make an enviroment inhospitable to Mediterranean folk, even after a century of successful domicile in north France and in England.

The Environment of Central Europe 

Between the Mediterranean region and Central Europe the mountain barrier is higher, wider, and less completely breached than in the west. Even more important, the Central European habitat is a world apart from that of the Mediterranean coastlands. This the Romans recognized, as is evident from their failure to colonize it effectively. 

Eastward of the ranges of low mountains that run from the westernmost Alps almost to the North Sea (Shepherd, Atlas, 2-3), continental Europe begins. The change is not abrupt, but it is observable in every aspect of the natural environment. The even-tempered climate of Northwest Europe gives way to the prolonged cold and snow of continental winters, and the briefer, although often intense, warmth of continental summers words, the characteristic climate of inland areas in these rather high latitudes. 

in other

Eastward through Germany, Poland, and Russia, the plains increase in size and the hills diminish in elevation and in numbers. Fertile soils on limestones become rare. Nearly everywhere north of the Alps and the 

Carpathians sandy dumps left from the last continental ice-sheet cover wide

expanses of the lowlands with a disordered array of heaps and depressions left by the retreating ice itself and with broad sandflats spread out by water from the melting ice-front.

Because of colder climate and sandier soil, deciduous woodland gives way to dense and somber forests of spruce, pine, and other coniferous trees. 

Natural openings in this forest occur mainly in the marshy, impassible 

valleys of streams and on sandy heaths exposed to bitter winter winds. 

Only in the southeast in the Middle and Lower Danube basins of Hungary and Rumania -- does forest give way to grassland, and copious rains at every season to drier weather reminiscent of the Mediterranean climate. 

Yet even there the differences are considerable. 

The temperature is more extreme and uncertain, the cold winters are drier than the long, hot summers, and there is no dependably recurrent season of drouth. 

Although Northwest Europe was for the Romans a novel world, they did succeed in establishing themselves over most of it. North Central 

Europe posed problems they never solved, except partially, and for a short period, in the drier Danubian lowlands.

Their failure to conquer and occupy the lands beyond the Rhine and (except for Dacia, its lower valley) the Danube accentuated and reinforced the natural differentiation between Northwest and North Central Europe and overlaid it with a pronounced cultural contrast of the first magnitude and of decisive historical importance. The cultural distinction was sharply drawn along the political frontier of the Empire. To be sure, this frontier was a fluctuating line, but for a long time it corresponded roughly to the 

Rhine and Danube rivers (Shepherd, Atlas, 38-39).

After "barbarians" from beyond the frontier succeeded in breaking through the Rhine-Danube boundary, Roman culture lost ground. It was nearly effaced in the exposed border zone north and east of the protective barriers

of mountain which separate interior Europe from its seaward margins. This zone became and has remained a disputed land between Latin and Germanic culture in the west, and between Greek and Slavic culture in the east with historical consequences of profound importance from that day to this. 

North Central and Northeastern Europe remained dominantly Germanic and Slavic in blood, and until well into the Middle Ages these regions maintained their primordial life. In the coniferous forest the scattered tribesmen lived by the chase. On the grassy plains nomadic bands roved with the cattle which provided their food, clothing, and habitations. 

Only step by step from south and west to north and east, did these modes of life give way to sedentary farming. In significant contrast, Northwest 

Europe became Romanized in language, customs, technology, and to some extent in blood. Later, the incursion of tribesmen from the east altered this provincial Roman society, but never wholly effaced the imprint of the Mediterranean world. For some centuries Northwest Europe was patterned as closely after Mediterranean Europe as its somewhat different natural environment would allow.

The Landscape 

Pattern of

Mediterranean Europe The basic Mediterranean design of living has always been small in scale. Each fertile lowland basin, such as Attica in Greece, Latium in 

Italy, and Carthagena in Spain, more or less cut off from its landward neighbors by rugged and unproductive hills or mountains, furnishes a compact agricultural base for a small society. In periods such as the pre-classical and medieval, when subsistence agriculture was the prime basis of life, each district could and did live mainly on its farm produce, supplemented

along the coast by fish from the adjacent sea. At most seasons this inland sea is calm, and Mediterranean folk early took to fishing, in order to supplement the slender food supply of the fields, orchards, and gardens. 

From fishing to water borne commerce is a natural step, and the farmers' and fishermen's market town, if it was situated at the focus of natural water routes and on a productive lowland, was likely to find itself a mart for foreign goods. Towns less favored by nature were likely to engage in piracy, seizing goods that did not naturally flow to them. 

Opportunities for both legitimate and illicit trade proved less restricted than the resources of field and fishery, and thriving mercantile towns early dominated the life of the whole Mediterranean world, giving it an urban complexion which it has never lost. Even in periods most inimical to trade, when constant warfare reduced transportation to the minimum, and urban centers found difficulty in providing themselves with food, shrunken populations remained within town walls and urban life survived. Vestiges of the urban society of Classical Antiquity persisted everywhere in Mediterranean Europe throughout the Middle Ages, a period which depended chiefly upon local food production for immediate home consumption, and subjected the merchant to heavy taxes at home, and to peril of life if he ventured abroad.

In the course of history, small Mediterranean territorial units, each dominated by a town, have often been soldered together to form larger political units, their foreign trade converted into domestic exchange, and their parallel lives ermeshed in a common net. Such political structures, after periods of expansion, tend to break down because of the competitive economic nature of their constituent units which are based on similar natural resources.

Even Rome, the most successful of Mediterranean empires, lost its cohesive power at last.

These basic characteristics of Mediterranean life have changed little throughout the course of history, apart from alternation between small states coterminous with local units of subsistence, and larger empires based on consolidation of local units. 

For the student of European history, one profound change has occurred since the early Middle Ages. In the seventh century the African shore of the Mediterranean Sea, and later the Asiatic shore, were overrun by Arabic civilization, and so lost to European culture. As part of Islam the se large areas have never subsequently entered fully into European life, although gradually since the seventeenth century, section after section has been politically reconquered by European states and here tended to return, in some degree, to the European orbit. Throughout the Middle Ages they were known to Europeans only as thirsty infidel lands bordering the desert. 

Occasionally chivalric crusading armies penetrated them, to be decimated by the combined force of starvation, hot-weather, diseases, and human enemies. 

The Landscape Pattern of Northwest 

Europe In the Mediterranean lands human life has fitted itself into a clearcut environmental frame with remarkably little variation from age to age. 

Social customs and material technology that have been introduced from outside the region have likewise been molded to conform to the allpervading pattern.

In Northwest Europe the natural environment is less decisive. It has permitted its inhabitants a wide latitude of social structure. The land was used rather differently in the classical and medieval periods, and between these earlier ages and the modern era the difference is even greater. Instead of shaping its inhabitants of whatever origin to a

uniform pattern, it has permitted variety in use of the land and in social structures conditioned upon land use.

The lowland units of Northwest Europe are larger, on the averago, than those of the Mediterranean World. Commonly they are separated from each other by broad gentle hills, instead of being sharply cut off by rugged mountains.

For example, if minor subdivisions are disregarded, all of lowland France falls into five major basins, in contrast with peninsular Greece, where there are a dozen in an area less than one quarter as large. 

Moreover, the long coastline of the Mediterranean lands gives nearly every tiny lowland a sea front of its own, whereas in blockier Northwest Europe much of the land must gain access to the sea, if at all, by way of rivers or overland routes. Only in the Po Plain and on the Iberian Plateau are size and access similar to conditions in the north. (It is significant that both these areas are also exceptions to the climate typical of the Mediterranean Basin, owing to the fact that they are somewhat cut off from the equable sea.)

Taking Northwest Europe as a whole, variety in both temperature and rainfall is greater than on the Mediterranean Sea borders. The range of latitude is greater. Finally, the region extends inland some hundreds of miles, rather than a few dozen, and so partakes of the climatic conditions typical of continental interiors as well as of seacoasts. 

In an area of such natural diversity, economic pursuits tend to be correspondingly varied and social groups are stamped with different character from place to place, even if they share a common origin. For example, coastal people devoted to fishing and sea borne trade tend to interest themselves in the larger world, whereas inlanders whose trade must be carried via costly land routes, or at best by rivers subject to interruption alternately by floods and low water, are likely to be parochial in

outlook. 

Roman Impress on Northwest

Europe The Romans had been led to the naturally well-endowed northwestern part of the European continent by a desire to dominate the western basin of the Mediterranean Sea, their immediate goal being the Iberian Peninsula (Shepherd, Atlas, 38-39). The land route to Spain lay along the coast of southern France, a district of unalloyed Mediterranean character in climate, soils, and vegetation, and therefore easily assimilated by people from other Mediterranean shores. It was no accident that this coast became Rome's first non-Italian province; hence its name Provincia, modern 

Frovence. Once established there the Romans found it possible to control the Pyrenean passes, from a base in the broad gap between the Pyrenees and the Massif Central

This Massif is formed by a block of low mountains. It occupies most of the Mediterranean hinterland between the Pyrenees and the Alps, but on each flank it leaves an open breach to the lowlands beyond. On the 

Pyrenean side an easy saddle leads to Aquitaine, the rich lowland of southwestern France, possessing a climate moister than that of the Mediterranean region, but not too different to be utilized by Roman settlers after some experimentation. Between the Massif Central and the Alps, flows the Rhône 

River. This stream, with its tributary the Saône, leads by a water-level route to low passes connecting with both the Rhine and the Seine basins. 

This breach in the mountain wall is the sole direct and naturally-formed lowland line of access between the Mediterranean Sea and the heart of Northwest Europe. The Greeks had shown the way to it, planting a trading colony at Marseilles, the natural harbor nearest the mouth of the Rhône.

People bent on trade between the contrasting enviroments of north and south Europe have invariably followed this pathway in preference to any other. Whenever the conduct of the trade has involved political control, the same route has proved to be the easiest line of march for armies. 

The Romans took full advantage of the situation. They readily established themselves on the coastal lowland, since it was so like their homeland in natural environment. They profited more slowly by its exceptional advantage 17 ready access to the north and west. First into

Aquitaine, later up the Rhône, they thrust wedges of colonization so gradually that only by degrees did the settlers find themselves in a non- 

Mediterranean environment. 

In their slow progress inland they adapted their lives to novel elements in their new environment colder winters, moist summers, thick deciduous woods, and large units of land. At the same time they brought into the northwest a technology that modified this environment. Wheat was adapted to the better-drained soils and to the less rainy lowlands in the lee of hills and mountains. The plow, rotation of crops, and the use of 

Mediterranean goods, including luxuries, were fertilizer were introduced. brought in, to be exchanged for forest products of the new lands, and later for farm products as well. 

Towns grew up at route crossings to care for the trade Lyons, Rheims, and London among many others. Along the frontier combined trading and military posts were founded, such as Mainz and Cologne. Quickened economic life resulted from the application to novel environmental conditions of the Roman mode of statecraft. Roman legions succeeded in conquering the extensive plains and broad, intervening wooded heights of Northwest Europe, just as earlier they had conquered tiny Mediterranean lowlands surrounded by steep and narrow mountain ranges. Roman proconsuls

then installed, with little modification, an administrative system which had operated successfully to unify the Mediterranean World. Perhaps the chief geographic contribution to Roman success was their system of paved roads which held the conquered terrain in a web, knotted the economic and political life of each section of lowland plain into an urban focus, and integrated the whole of Northwest Europe with the Mediterranean region by way of the Rhône breach and its less important alternate, the gap between the Massif Central and the Pyrenees.

Northwest Europe was susceptible to Romanization, not only because it was accessible from Rome's front door, the Western Basin of the Mediterranean, but also because its natural enviroment was similar enough to permit easy adaptation of Mediterranean modes of life. Increasing distance from Rome and the corresponding contrast in environmental conditions inevitably left the farther regions less Romanized than the nearer areas. As a whole the Northwest appears to have lagged behind the 

Mediterranean region in effective utilization of its natural resources even during the epoch when Mediterranean technology was imposed upon it. 

Medieval Modifications of the Landscape

Pattern When the Roman military defense weakened, Germanic tribesmen from beyond the Rhine-Danube boundary broke into the Empire along its whole length. As these incursions grew to the proportions of folk migrations, they broke down the Roman mode of life, at first in remote and insular Britain, later on the northern fringe of the continent, and finally in southern France. It is true that the stream of migrating folk which poured across the disrupted frontier became increasingly diluted with local blood as it penetrated ever more deeply into the Romanized world. Nevertheless,

nearly the whole area lapsed into an economy largely rural and able to produce little beyond the minimum requirements of subsistence for the population.

This backward condition lasted throughout the centuries when the anarchy of the early Middle Ages replaced the ordered, centralized government of Rome. Northwest Europe suffered more than the Mediterranean region from the incursions of these hordes of forest dwellers because it lacked the protection of lofty barrier mountains. 

There also it proved difficult to reconstitute stable goverment after the folk-migrations came to a halt. 

This may have been due in part to the relatively short period of Roman rule in the region. It seems to have been related also to the character of the country.

Much of its large, productive, lowland area either lay inland or faced a turbulent ocean. Few, if any, of the small political units were set off from envious neighbors by any natural barriers more effective than wooded hills or belts of marsh, and all of them were exposed to enemies at several points. It was therefore difficult for the inhabitants to protect themselves against invasion, with accompanying destruction of capital goods 

of everything, in fact, except the land itself. 

In a warlike age the most reliable source of livelihood is farming, because armies rarely put the land permanently out of use. Nevertheless, they consume or destroy the crops and stock. Medieval farming, subjected as it was to repeated depredations, and lacking the benefit of exchanging products among diverse regions, long romained at a low level that barely sufficed to yield subsistence. The rather sparse population lived mainly on the soil and operated a farming system surprisingly uniform for so varied an area.

Everywhere pastures, while most productive in summer, remained green

were considered throughout the cool winter, thus providing feed for cattle all year. 

Staple crops invariably included wheat, an immigrant from the Mediterranean region, and prized since Roman times as the staff of life. In the wetter climates, on heavy soils, and in highlands, it yielded very poor returns. 

The other staples oats, barley, buckwheat, and rye inferior, but were widely grown, especially on the less fertile lands, because of their greater hardiness in comparison to wheat, and correspondingly higher yields. Very slowly, as population increased and government became effective in ever-larger units, subsistence agriculture gave place to specialization in crops and livestock adapted to the rich and diverse lowlands of Northwest Europe, and to exchange-economy based on such specialization.

Both trade and crafts are highly vulnerable to war and the threat of war, and consequently commercial and craft towns came into existence only toward the end of the medieval period. When they did take their rise, they appeared first along the seacoast and at principal junctions of inland trade routes, such as confluences, the outlets of passes, and crossings of navigable rivers by overland routes. The Baltic and North Sea coasts, the 

Rhône-Champagne and the Rhône-Rhine furrows, and the foreland of the Alps, saw the gradual growth of towns. 

These towns lived in part by exchange of local surpluses which grew as the population expanded, and to some extent by trade between the Mediterranean World and the receding frontier of eastern Europe. Sometimes they derived a supplementary income from handcrafts. Not a few of them grew up on the sites of Roman towns, partly because vestiges of the earlier centers had persisted and partly because a location suitable for urban life in one age is likely to be equally vital in any other urban age. 

After centuries of turmoil, during the Middle Ages, the commingled

20 populations of Northwest Europe still bore the unmistakable stamp of Mediterranean civilization. Even in Britain the Roman roads, at least, survived, as did numerous mainland towns and bridges. 

Likewise crops, such as wheat and vines, remained as evidences of Roman culture in the Mediterranean area.

The landscape pattern or design to be found in any region is the product of cultural forces and the natural environment in which they operate. A primitive society must adhere closely to the character impressed by nature on the region. People equipped with a less primitive technology are able to modify the natural landscape to a commensurate degree. The Romans brought to Northwest Europe a landscape design that had been worked out in the Mediterranean world. Some elements of it, such as straight roads, they added permanently to the northern landscape; others, such as life lived in the open, they failed to establish; still others, such as urban life, rose and fell with the fortunes of Rome. 

In general, the Mediterranean imprint upon the local pattern of landscape became much blurred during the Middle Ages, although it did not disappear. Germanization (or perhaps more properly de-Romanization) led the way to a mode of life less closely modelled on Mediterranean practices, and more neatly conformed to the local natural environment. 

Forest crept back on infertile land no longer used for fields. Many towns on the open plain were deserted for safer sites on hills or islands, and detours from the straight Roman roads led to these new defense points. 

Streams once again had to bear the bulk of commerce, and towns and castles became oriented to the lines of waterways. In short, the natural character of Northwest Europe reappeared as the Mediterranean veneer cracked and chipped, and was partially replaced by Germanic inlay. The resultant landscape was neither Roman nor German, but a composite in which the usages

and the technology of both were adapted to the local natural environment.

The Landscape Pattern of Central Europe Under Roman dominion the Mediterranean World and Northwest Europe worked out a reasonably harmonious, unified life, despite differences of natural environment. In sharp contrast, no attempt was ever made to apply the landscape design normal to Mediterranean coastlands upon the country beyond the boundary of the Roman Empire. As a consequence, the differences springing from natural contrasts were accentuated by modes of living at variance with each other. This was probably inevitable where the broad and lofty mountains of Central Europe have set the tiny, warm, dry and fertile coastlands of the south irrevocably apart from the large, rainy, infertile, inland plains of the north. As soon as Roman power began to wane, Mediterranean civilization began to be effaced in the zone between the Balkan-Alpine mountains and the political boundary. In the end the most obvious survival of it in all that belt was the language of Rumania. 

On the frontier of the Rhine below its Great Elbow (at Basel), cultural fusion succeeded Roman domination. Within the Rhine Basin no striking contrast occurs in natural conditions, and the barrier of low mountains west of the stream is breached at numerous points. The river lies in a zone of transition in respect to land relief, climate, soil, and natural vegetation. Yet neither the stream itself, its valley walls, nor its watershed, constitute an environmental barrier between Northwest and Central Europe. Throughout most of the basin favored districts yield satisfactory harvests of Mediterranean wheat and grapes. In accessibility also, the Rhineland is similar to Northwest rather than to Central Europe. 

Connections with the sea are alike in Western Germany and Eastern France.

From the vicinity of the Great Elbow, a broad gate between the Jura and the Vosges mountains (almost a water-level route), links the Rhine Valley to the Mediterranean Sea by way of the Rhône.

Despite the transitional character of the Rhineland, the nature of Central Europe as a whole differs considerably from that of Northwest Europe, and it is the antithesis of the Mediterranean region. East of a line connecting the Danish Peninsula with the west end of the Bohemian Forest (Shepherd, Atlas, 2-3) the landscape flattens and broadens. 

Mountain ranges are few and low. Lowland units are large and monotonously uniform. There is no contact with the ocean except by way of the Baltic 

Sea, and its shore line is short in ratio to the land area. 

Moreover, its harbors are few and shallow, and its entrance is subject to control by the people who occupy its narrow outlet. Rivers lie far apart, several of them flow in parallel courses instead of diverging from a common center as in Northwest Europe, and all of them empty into enclosed seas. They are further reduced in utility by being ice-blocked half the year. 

The cold and snowy winters that lock the streams confine the growth of crops to a short season. The summers, in spite of brief, hot spells, are so cool and rainy everywhere north of Hungary that staples are restricted to inferior crops 

barley, oats, rye, and buckwheat.

Much of the land can not support even these, because it is sterile sand, fit at best to grow pine forests, and in the natural state covered with forests or with useless heath. Other extensive areas are dotted with lakes and marshes, or crisscrossed with wet swales and river floodplains. 

These features, like the sandy soils, are earmarks of the glacial epoch.

As long as this area lay beyond the Roman frontier, it remained a land sparsely peopled with tribesmen of the forest who lived mainly by the chase. Tillage of the soil was secondary to a people moving about in pursuit of game. Towns did not exist. So long as the Rhine River was maintained as a military and cultural boundary, it marked the separation between two sharply contrasted types of landscape and two equally distinct modes of settlement.

Nevertheless, its weight must not be overestimated. long as the political boundary of the Roman Empire was maintained at a fixed line, such as the Rhine or the Danube rivers, outpost trading towns flourished on frontier trade with the Germanic and Slavic peoples beyond the boundary. When the political frontier was retracted, transition zones were left as districts once Roman but now again outside the Empire. Many of their inhabitants fled to take refuge behind the retreating frontier. 

Others must have stayed and intermarried with the incoming barbarians, creating a new society of combined Roman and German elements. During the period of Roman decline, increasing numbers of barbarians obtained permission to settle within the confines of the Empire. 

Ultimately the Empire, by infinitely slow degrees, disintegrated as a political entity, and lay wide open to immigrants from Central Europe. 

At the same time, the people who remained in Central Europe were identifying their civilization with that of the Roman world. The lands both within and without the former Empire were reconstituted into political units of varying

size and fluctuating boundaries, diverse in their political character from place to place, and from generation to generation. 

Most of them fell into a simple landscape design in which cultural features were intimately related to nature a phenomenon typical of all disintegrating economic and political societies. As might be expected, the design differed in detail from place to place with differing combinations of natural environment, but in essentials it was remarkably uniform, not only throughout the Roman world but in Central, non-Romanized Europe as well. 

With the relaxation of Rome's political sway in the fourth and fifth centuries, the ordered economic world of the Empire gave way to confusion. 

Trade dwindled, and Europeans were increasingly thrown back upon local resources for their livelihood. This inevitably meant a reduction in the variety of goods available for people of every class, because no small area can produce as varied products as the whole earth, or even such a considerable part of the earth as the Romans had incorporated in their Empire.

Many goods formerly abundant could no longer be obtained at any price. Only the very wealthy could pay the high cost of bringing goods from far away, and thereby benefit from using the varied resources of different regions. Barring a very few necessities, such as iron and copper for utensils and implements, and salt, the common man had to be content with what could be raised on land within a day's walk of his home. Towns shrank or even disappeared, as townsfolk lost their chief businesses--trade, and such crafts as contributed to trade. Food was readily available only on the land; consequently the European populace during the Middle Ages was organized primarily as a rural society. The few surviving urban centers were chiefly market towns, engaged in weekly exchange of the products of a

small district, or holding occasional fairs to which itinerant merchants brought wares from afar, for a day, a week, or a month. 

Geography of the Manor

The basic unit in rural medieval society was the manor. The manor should not be thought of as narrowly administrative in character, for it also had a geographic aspect as a unit of land. 

Both before and after the political disintegration of the western Roman Empire, say from the third to the tenth centuries (the so-called Dark Ages), population decreased. The total amount of land devoted to farming diminished to correspond with the reduced demand for food. The fields selected to be kept under the plow were those that would yield a fair crop with the least labor, because the shrinkage of population reduced available field hands as well as consuming mouths. The land not needed for cultivation was allowed to revert to a state of nature. This varied in character with climate, landforms, and even with soils.

Layout of the Manor

The manor was the basic unit of land, and evolved from Roman practice and custom, especially in those parts of Europe north of the mountains, but also in many Mediterranean areas (Shepherd, Atlas, 104). It consisted of a nucleus of arable land, surrounded by pasture and woodland. Its boundaries ran mostly through forests in the north, and across scrub-covered hills in the south. 

The settlement stood convenient to water a spring or brook, or a place where a shallow well could tap an underground supply. On a choice site rose the manor house, residence of the family which held title to the property. Where the terrain permitted, the site of the house took advantage of natural defense, such as a hill or cliff, a stream or lake. 

In especially troublous times or in exposed locations, the manor house was fortified by a wall, constructed so as to aid the men of the community in Close by was the church with the rectory. its defense.

A little farther off, the farm village straggled along both sides of a road, perhaps the only connection the manor had with the outside world. Each farm family occupied a cottage, frequently under the same roof as the stable and barn. The farmyard, haystacks, and kitchen garden were close to the house. Beyond, and scattered from one end to the other of the manorial plowland, lay the many plots which together comprised the fields of each farmer.

The number of families in the village was determined by the amount of arable land available. The theoretical upper limit was an acreage so large that to reach the more remote fields and pastures entailed a walk of a couple of hours. Generally the limit was more narrowly fixed by nature -- a valley lowland sharply bounded by hills, a plain between marshy streams, a fertile stretch of land surrounded by relatively unproductive soils.

Whatever the limitation, the arable tract had to be large relative to the population of the manor, because the system of land-holding and crop production held even the most fertile soils down to low output. 

In a region of such diversity of landforms and climate as Europe, soils are certain to vary greatly. Those of sandy areas, common in North 

Central Europe, contained little plant food to begin with, and were soon depleted. Their one virtue was ready drainage, a real asset in the rainy, cool climate of this region. Heavy silts and clays, found on river floodplains and deltas, and overlying shales and some limestones, were cultivable in the dry Mediterranean region, but in the rainier north were generally left in pasture. The soils most highly prized everywhere were found on limestone plains. These soils retained their productivity longer than the light soils and were, at the same time, adequately drained and retentive of sufficient moisture to guard against rainless periods. 

Even the best of soils must be well treated if it is to maintain its

fertility. After the decline of the relatively advanced Roman farming practices, there was scarcely any manure available on the manor for fertilizing the fields, because the livestock grazed at large, and of course chemical fertilizers were unknown. Therefore, it was necessary for the land to rest frequently, so that nature might have an opportunity to rid it of toxic substances and to replenish a measure of the natural plant foods withdrawn by the crops.

The Farming 

System In the early Middle Ages only half the arable land was tilled each year, the other half being left fallow. Later on, farm practice improved, and it became the rule to plant the land for two consecutive years, leaving it fallow during the third. This is the so-called "three-field system" of agriculture, finally abandoned in France only at the time of the 18th century Revolution, in Russia with the Bolshevik Revolution two decades ago, and not even yet in out-of-the way corners of East Central Europe. 

In order to give each farmer a fair share of all available types of soil, drainage, and exposure to sun and wind, the arable land was divided into many strips, the number being determined by the local diversity of landforms and soils.

The shape of each piece of land was a long, narrow rectangle. It had to be long to facilitate plowing. The crude, wooden or iron-shod plow was practically the only piece of farm machinery in common use. The suitable length for a furrow (a furrow long, or furlong), was generally accepted as 1/8 of a mile or 40 rods. Each farmer was compelled to plant or fallow his strips as they fell into one or the other of the three fields. 

Thus in any one year, one-third of the land was planted to bread food for the inhabitants of the manor, and one-third to stock feed, grain for the local alcoholic drink, and minor human foods. The remainder, while lying fallow, could be used for grazing stock, and it thus got a little manuring, but not enough to do much good.

Crops requiring less land, such as garden vegetables, olives, wine, nuts, and fruits, were grown in the manorial garden and orchard for the benefit of the lord and his family, and in the yards of the farmers. 

The manorial field crops produced only grain feed for livestock, and none too much of that. Poultry scratched about the village and in the fields to pick up what living it could. Stubblefields and fallow lands provided poor pickings at some seasons for grazing animals, but the stock had to live chiefly on pasturage. 

In the south, sheep and goats nibbled the brush of the hills, and a few cattle could subsist on the scattered marshy lowlands. In summer the forage ran very short. In the north cattle predominated over sheep, and both grazed the manorial pasturage adjacent to the fields. Generally pasturage was relegated to hill land, wet valley floor, heavy soil, thin soil on porous ridges, or infertile heath. In

summer both cattle and sheep fared well on these lands, but in winter, especially in the more extreme climate of Central Europe, the animals lost weight.

At the end of the lean season the scrawny stock was far from fit. 

This was serious in the case of draft animals. Half-starved, the oxen or cows commonly used were too weak to pull the plow if it was thrust deep into the soil. The inevitable shallow plowing left the land ill-prepared, and its yield was light, even in favorable seasons. Fortunately, wheat was fall-sown, and preparation of the wheat field found the animals in better condition. But at best the ox-yoke and the medieval plow were inefficient contrivances. Output improved when the collar for horse or ass was substituted for the ox-yoke. The farmers who could afford to adopt the improvement could plow more deeply and in normal years accumulated a surplus of agricultural products. Yokes of oxen continued in general use, however, because these beasts are cheaper than horses or even mules. 

The pastureland was used in common by all the farmers, and was not fenced. Boys, men, or women were delegated to keep the stock off the fields, and to prevent their straying into the woodland. The Growth of Land Units 

The manor was primarily a unit of farmland. It was too uniform to provide economic diversity, and too small to support a political organization of any consequence. It may be thought of as the cell-unit of medieval agricultural society. The combining of several cells resulted in the creation of organs of that society 

entities which retained their rural character while taking on political functions. Many an agglomeration of manors and their inhabitants provided a broad enough economic base to sustain a political structure. Thus the manor constituted an incipient state. If it was sufficiently favored by nature and by vigorous and successful policy on the part of its rulers, it grew by accretion. more successful of these political entities were kernels of political units of the modern European world. The During all the earlier Middle Ages, however, territorial disintegration prevailed over integration, until finally the ancient tradition of the Roman Empire became an empty form, without power beyond the force which the current emperor derived from his personal landholdings. The territory which slipped from the imperial grasp fell, in blocks of varying size, into the hands of rulers who inherited corresponding political functions, ranging from king ship down to presiding over petty neighborhood courts. 

The least of the administrators owned, operated, and gained his living from a single manor. Others had several. More often than not the manors belonging to any one lord were scattered about among holdings of

other and often rival landowners. The larger landlords came to have two sorts of holdings: the demesne, consisting of the manors held directly and relied upon for the livelihood of the owner and his administrative officers; and lands granted in some sort of fee to other lords, as a rule in return for military service.

Any number of elements entered into the successful construction of a state out of the manorial building stones. The vigor and shrewdness of the ruler played a part, and no doubt luck figured in many cases. Nevertheless, some holdings possessed environmental advantages. These, being continuously in force from century to century, worked for political superiority in the long run.

In a rural age the chief value of the land was inevitably its fitness for agriculture. Fertile and well-drained soil was the only sort that could be used by the sparse, poor population of the earlier Middle Ages. Suitable land was likely to be found on lowland plains facing the sea, segments of river valleys, areas lying on well drained plains between streams, abutting marshy floodplains, and fertile spots amid forested sand plains. In such localities people could make an assured living and gradually win something more than subsistence, thus fitting themselves to take part in the complex life of farms, trades, and crafts that developed during the later Middle Ages. 

In this era of perennial fighting, when any petty ruler might be ranged against his neighbor, a lord who could push the boundaries of his holdings to some natural barrier had a military advantage over one whose arbitrary bounds were marked only by agreement. Barrier boundaries commonly found in medieval Europe were broad marshes, impassible gorges in rivers, wastes of heath or scrub, and forbidding, dense forests.

The successive concentric ranges of hills which surround Paris, particularly well-developed on the east almost to the Rhine River, appear to have been an important factor in the establishment of Paris and its surrounding territory as the core of the French state. On a smaller scale, the Dutch nation owes its distinctiveness in part to barrier marshes among which it took shape. Dozens of other small regions have had their political heyday, only to disappear when their protective barriers were destroyed, as by felling forests or draining marshes, or when they were reduced in value through improved technology, as by building engineered roads across ranges of hills.

Protecting barriers were important to a budding state, but no more so than an easily defended site so located as to serve readily as the focus of life for the whole unit. Perhaps the most prized site for defense was the crest of an isolated, cliff-sided hill where drinking water could be had from a spring or shallow well. A flat-top was useful, but not necessary, and further protection might be lent by a stream washing the base of the cliff.

Lacking an isolated eminence, the end of a spur might be utilized by walling it off. In flat country, islands in streams or lakes were commonly utilized. Sometimes an island was created by an artificial moat, particularly if this could be done merely by diverting part of a stream. 

However well defended by nature, a political unit lacked an essential quality if it was isolated from the life-giving currents of human movement. 

Trade never ceases, even in the most backward society, and in medieval Europe it waxed as time passed. Some of the most strongly fortified political centers of the period were on trade routes, in order to protect the traffic or to levy toll upon it. A narrows on a navigable river, across which a chain might be stretched as a toll-gate, was a favored site. 

Another was a focus of routes, particularly if the spot controlled

access from several directions, or if no alternate competitive route could be found, as at a confluence of navigable streams. A point where an overland route crossed a navigable stream was another likely location. Except in flat country the exact site of such a crossing is usually determined by the convergence of tributary valleys from opposite sides of the stream. 

Wherever it was necessary or desirable to change from one mode of transportation to another, a center might grow up. Such locations are several. The head of an estuary on a coastal lowland requires change from ocean ships to river craft, and is usually the farthest down-stream crossing place for overland trade as well. Along the base of every mountain range are centers which have thriven on the change in mode of transportation between plains, roads and mountain trails. 

In many instances the unrivalled value of a site for trade caused it to be chosen as a political capital, even if it had no natural aids for its defense. The lord's chief manor house was the center of life for the people of his territory, whether its protection was based on nature or created by man. Thither the farmer folk came to hold their weekly market, to settle their grievances under the lord's arbitration, and to make carnival. handcrafts.

The prosperity of such a center kept pace with the expansion of the lord's agricultural lands and his opportunities to tap trade and to foster One of the principal achievements of the Middle Ages turned out to be the amalgamation of manorial cell-units into ever larger and richer, and at the same time more compact and more interdependent entities.

PART II. EUROPE ON THE THRESHOLD OF THE DISCOVERIES The Territorial and Commercial Expansion of the Middle Ages

In terms of the earth and its utilization, the business of the Middle Ages was fourfold: (1) to regain for agriculture some or all of the land lost to use in the break-up of classical civilization, and to add to cultivation a vast acreage north and east of the old Roman Empire; (2) to discover new sources of mineral wealth, and to amplify both the number of minerals used and the ways of using them; (3) to reestablish intercourse between isolated regions, and to increase the exchange of products and ideas between areas reciprocally related by contrasting natural endowment; and (4) to consolidate society into larger blocks than the self-subsistent manorial cell-units of the early Middle Ages.

Territorial Expansion 

Eastward (Drang nach Osten)

The first movements of expansion were tentative, and took the form of sporadic border fighting along the eastern frontier. This thrust

The inhabitants of eastern Europe were either nomadic horsemen of the grassy plains or somewhat less mobile migrant tribes of the forest. They were heathen in an age aflame with the missionary spirit of Christianity, and they made repeated incursions across the boundary and devastated sedentary Christian settlements that lived by growing crops in the European tradition. Such a contrast in mode of life and in ideals, laid the foundation for an interminable life-and-death struggle, for which depredations all along the frontier furnished ample provocation.

During the earlier Middle Ages the eastern boundary of Europe lay for centuries in what is now central Germany. It took advantage of natural barriers 11 marsh and heath in the north forested ridges of hills between the flat coastal plain and the Eastern Alps. Thence it followed the

The Discoveries and Overseas

Expansion 

The goal of all the seagoing explorations of the later Middle Ages was the Orient, for the sake of obtaining its goods, now rapidly rising in price under the combined pressure of increasing demand and diminishing supply. At the very time Europe wanted to buy more oriental goods, the nomadic conquerors of the trade routes levied heavy taxes upon the traffic. 

Three alternative routes invited investigation (1) north around Europe, (2) south around Africa, and (3) west around the earth. All of them were seaways, but that to the north proved impracticable because of ice blockade, while that to the south was made difficult by the long, arid and harborless coast of the Sahara and the disease-ridden, rainy forest-lands beyond. 

This southern route nevertheless turned out to be the most direct. 

Explorations in both of these directions could perhaps have been made simply by hugging the coast, but they were greatly facilitated by the recent introduction of the mariner's compass and the sextant. These instruments permitted point-to-point sailing, and even open-sea navigation,

as in Da Gama's famous voyage across the Indian Ocean, where he took advantage, both going and returning, of the seasonal or monsoon winds. 

Without the new instruments, exploration westward across the Atlantic could not have been attempted. Of different character but equal importance in the later Middle Ages was the general revival of belief that the earth was spherical. Owing to the interposition of the long doublecontinent of the Americas, the westward route did not lead directly to the Orient, as was assumed, but the essential principle involved was sound. 

Clearly both the devising of new instruments and the revival of geographical concepts were prerequisites to the voyages of Columbus. Finally, the shift of interest from otherworldly concerns, the rebirth of classical learning, and the new practice of the scientific observation of nature, all contributed to the Discoveries. Such dynamic combinations of ideas are typical of the Modern Age, and in a real sense distinguish it from the learned, but otherworldly medieval period.

The most immediate result of the explorations was the meteoric rise

Less obvious and more gradual, but no less drastic, came a change from the simple agricultural system of the Middle Ages to a variety of systems, inherently more complex, and varying from region to region in conformity with differences in soil, terrain, and climate --the so-called Agricultural Revolution. Increasingly farming in Europe became affected by the competition of crops grown in the world outside Europe. The Agricultural Revolution 

No development differentiates the Medieval from the Modern period more decisively than the new mode of using farm land. 

From the eleventh century onwards, population and the standard of living in Europe persistently increased in spite of drastic setbacks due to wars, famines, floods, and pestilences.

This expanding population required more and better food and at the same time provided the labor to obtain it.

Bit by bit farm land was reclaimed. 

Brush and forest was cleared for crops or pasture, until most of the gentler lands were open, leaving only rough terrain and infertile soils under tree growth. An exception to this tendency was the demesne forests of the more powerful landlords, including kings, who kept blocks of woodland for hunting, often arbitrarily selected for personal convenience regardless of their utility for other purposes. 

Many of these preserves survived into recent times, notably in Britain. 

Wet lands were drained wherever possible. In this manner floodplains along streams, and parts of coastal plains bordering the sea, were converted into rich pastures or exceptionally fertile fields. Irrigation ditches were reopened throughout the Mediterranean world, and in the Iberian Peninsula Christian states fell heir to Moorish irrigation districts, some of which appear to have been more cunningly designed than any the Romans had operated.

Farmland, (Drang nach Osten)

As the arable land in western Europe available for reclamation dwindled, the mounting demand for tillable soil led to expansion into country hitherto occupied only by wandering tribes. By the twelfth century the coniferous forests of the North Central European plain were already being cut, and the marshes left by the continental ice-sheet were being drained.

These activities took place immediately in the rear of the armed Germans who were pushing back, exterminating, or incorporating the aboriginal Slavic inhabitants, as the Drang nach Osten swept on. Professional woodsmen found their way to the promising new colonizations from older communities throughout Northern Europe, where they and their ancestors had relentlessly felled forests for generations. The more specialized work

As new lands were laid under the plow or converted to pasture, the familiar farm system of Western Europe was established, and the frontier progressed eastward precisely as did the west-moving frontier of North America centuries later.

While Europe east of the line along the Elbe, the Bohemian Forest, and the Eastern Alps was being conquered and stamped with the medieval agricultural pattern of the older Europe west of that line, new and revolutionary modes of using the farm land were beginning to appear here and there, especially in the central Mediterranean World and in the lowlands that focus upon the Strait of Dover.

As the Middle Ages passed their apogee, conditions in western Europe conspired to make farming less and less attractive as a vocation. 

In district after district, reclamation of all the readily arable land and its tillage under the three-field system closed local outlets for the increasing population. This heightened two evils already prevalent: (1) Chronic undernourishment became more widespread, and this made the people more than ever susceptible to famine. These occurred whenever floods, drouth, or blight suddenly and drastically reduced the local food supply, because society lacked organized means of storing a surplus, distributing the risk of loss, and repairing the devastation which then as now is universally called an "act of God." (2) Pestilences, like famines, were all too familiar. They often ran their course through the population of one district and another at irregular, but frequent intervals, and more rarely ravaged the whole Continent. Nobody understood their source, few suspected the communicability of diseases, or understood the virtues of sanitation.

New Opportunities: 

crusaders and pioneers Driven by adversity to seek other means of subsistence than the traditional medieval agriculture, people found their horizon extended both literally and figuratively. Small surpluses of certain commodities tended to accumulate in districts especially fitted to produce them. Examples were wool in the hill land of Italy and the frontier country of England, salt from mines or springs at various spots in Central Europe, salt fish from the North Sea, and nuts and fruits in the Mediterranean Basin. Some of these commodities, even in their natural state, were ready to join the stream of trade from the untamed northeast and from the exotic Orient. 

Others could be turned into trade goods by relatively simple processes of manufacture. As traders increased in number, tales they told spread rapidly among farm laborers - 10 now becoming eager to escape heavy and disheartening toil, until even stagnant backwaters were increasingly aware of a great world beyond the hills, the woods, the marshes, or the sea that had heretofore isolated them.

At the close of the eleventh century new outlets for pent up energy appeared. People hitherto chained to the soil, by ignorance and lack of opportunity even more than by law, learned that Crusades against the infidel were afoot. Some took the Cross, others merely fell in with the rabble that hung on the fringes of these military thrusts to the Levant. 

Others heard that new farm lands were being seized from heathen nearer home, in central and eastern Europe, whither land-hunger enticed many sober men who were unwilling to join the rash venture of an overseas Crusade.

Still nearer home towns devoted to trade and handcrafts were growing up, where an industrious man might earn a livelihood, and perhaps amass a competence. Freemen could leave the land without legal hindrance, once

they had the vision of a world outside and funds to reach it. Serfs who could avoid detection "for a year and a day" in a free town were by custom free of further servile bonds. The growing towns, needing labor, generally welcomed recruits without close investigation. Lay and church lords, eager to absorb raw frontier lands, una bashedly advertised for hands, and offered a tacit liberation from outraged masters to any who were willing to undergo the risks of living on a fluctuating frontier of conquest. Such calls must have been heeded chiefly by the more vigorous and venturesome young pioneers, just as in North America during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

A New Agri cultural System The effect of all these factors was to produce a net decrease of farm labor in older Europe, to the detriment of the labor-consuming threefield system. At the same time the rising flow of commerce encouraged exchange of products, so that many districts could partially abandon subsistence agriculture by producing trade goods for exchange. Wool and hides were commodities in demand in the craft towns. 

Livestock to produce 

It was only natural, wool and hides required less care than tilled fields. therefore, that landlords encroached upon the common pasture, and even upon plow land, for a new purpose, namely to provide grazing for cattle, sheep, or goats. Thus they could enlarge their income and solve their labor problem at the same time.

Goats were generally raised in the driest parts of the Mediterranean World, because they can subsist on less and poorer food than other domestic animals. Elsewhere about the Mediterranean sheep were preferred, and they were also common on the heaths and downs and other hill lands of Britain and France -- lands where porosity of the soil or steep slopes keep the

ground fairly dry even after abundant rains. On moister lands cattle were the rule.

Everywhere the effect was to take over for the landlord's benefit, land formerly used by free farmers, many of whom therefore found themselves without means of subsistence. Few hands were needed to watch the stock, and often the pastures were fenced. More and more as the Middle Ages wore on, did enclosure of the land destroy the livelihood of an important fraction of the rural population, forcing whole families to find work elsewhere. 

These landless folk took an important part in the colonization along the eastern frontier, in the rise of towns, and, later, in overseas settlement.

Enclosure worked temporary hardship upon great numbers of people, but it laid the foundation for an improved agricultural system. Under the new system, as soon as livestock was kept in large numbers, the amount of available manure was sufficient to benefit the soil. It came to be common practice to turn pastures thus fertilized into plow land every few years. 

During the winter, animals were likely to be confined to barnyards, and the manure accumulated could be spread on the fields just before planting when it would yield maximum benefit.

The soil of most parts of Western Europe responds quickly and well to fertilization, and manuring made it no longer necessary to leave any of the land fallow. This increased the productive acreage by half, and brought to an end the regime of three fields. Much of the acreage gained could be used for hay or other feed crops to tide the livestock over the lean season. Draft beasts could now be kept in good condition throughout the year, and so could work the land more effectively, especially in the spring.

A rotation practice was evolved in which pasture for a number of

years was followed by wheat or other staple food grain for one or two seasons, then by a planting of varied and less important crops for food and feed, and finally by a few years of hay, before turning in the stock to pasture the deteriorating hayfield. Such a scheme was flexible enough to permit adaptation to suit climate, slope, drainage, and soil. Farmers learned that even on the same holding different pieces of land demanded different treatment. The thrifty and intelligent man could now improve his land at will within rather wide limits set by soil, slope, and climate. 

Consequently, individual management of the arable land was substituted for uniform handling. 

The ancient, medieval subdivision of the holdings into narrow strips that had been adopted to serve the three-field system, proved awkward and inefficient under the new system, in which each farmer decided what crops he could best raise. The narrow untilled strips (balks) required by medieval practice between the holdings, wasted a large fraction of land, and no one holding was large enough by itself to fence for pasture. In the course of time, exchange, purchase, and reallotment by law have eliminated most of the strip holdings, although even today they are to be seen in parts of East Central Europe.

The Agricultural Revolution began in Western Europe and slowly worked its way eastward. Much of it has taken place within the Modern 

Period. European society first became acutely aware of the problem in 

England and France after the Black Death in the mid-fourteenth century. 

This plague reduced the population by one-third in a single year, and subsequently still more. So diminished was the laboring class that much land unravaged by war lay idle, enclosure for sheep pasture was accelerated, and movement of farm labor to the frontier and to the towns was stimulated. 

The ultimate consequence was a new mode of agricultural life for all of

Europe.

This change in farming accompanied and stimulated improvements in transportation. So long as transport was slow and costly only the least perishable and most valuable products could be sent beyond the market town of the producing district. Lack of a wide market restricted the list of crops a farmer could profitably raise, forced him to produce nearly everything used by his family, and limited his purchasing power proportionally. 

The shift from medieval to modern farming was essentially the replacement of subsistence agriculture by commercial production. This could occur only with enhanced opportunity for exchange. The farm system which emerged during the Modern Age took advantage of the wide variety of natural enviroment in Europe, to grow crops adapted to different regions and to exchange surpluses. Lacking this environmental differentiation, both rural and urban Europe would be far poorer, as indeed it was during the medieval era of subsistence agriculture, when its natural diversity counted for little.

The Commercial Revolution 

In its usually accepted sense the Commercial Revolution occurred after the Discoveries, rather than before them. 

Obviously overseas trade could not exist until the great ocean ways had been discovered. Nevertheless, changes of real import had begun well before the fifteenth century, affecting the radius, the variety, and the volume of trade. As farm surpluses began to increase with the tendency to specialize in the production of crops according to the regions best suited for them, the volume of agricultural commodities in commerce increased, and even today remains large. But Europe pulled itself up from its old local self-sufficiency to a modern exchange economy very slowly. During the early Middle Ages the 

Roman roads had largely ceased to function as parts of a Europe-wide

primarily transportation system. Their pavements disappeared, many segments became grass-grown or incorporated into private properties. Such long-haul business as there was had to take to routes provided by nature rivers and coastal waters. The headwaters of navigable streams were linked by arduous portage routes, some of them over mountain passes. As always in a period of backward technology, medieval man remained closely dependent upon his natural environment. 

During the century or two before the Discoveries, transportation routes closely conformed to the major riverways and to coasting routes in the protected inland seas. Local traffic, moving only a few miles, doubtless constituted the bulk of the goods and persons carried. Trunkline business, confined to the less bulky and more valuable luxury goods and to the occasional movement of persons, was geographically more significant, because it determined the primary pattern of routes along which towns grew. 

The North

Two exotic areas acted as magnetic fields to attract traders and create trade routes across the heart of Europe. To the north lay the frontier. Its products were not notably valuable, but they could be had for very little in exchange, as is always true of frontier regions. 

Wool from pastoral Britain, and beeswax, hair, pelts, and amber from the forested continent were characteristic frontier products. With 

Britain and Scandinavia intercourse was maintained by small boats plying across the narrow straits and bays. Goods were landed in all the estuaries from the Seine to the Vistula. Ports on both sides could be established in the many deep harbors provided by a coastline on which the river mouths have been drowned, their valleys filled by broad inlets

penetrating inland dozens of miles and kept free from silt by tidal scour. 

Nearly every ancient port of North Europe stands at the head of tidewater, where a trading town could profit from three advantages: maximum penetration inland by seagoing craft with relatively low carrying charges; location far enough inland to be relatively safe from the pirates of the high seas; the overland crossing farthest downstream (in effect, the coast road). Such of the rivers as were navigable provided a fourth advantage: easy connection with the interior. Rouen, Ghent, Cologne, Bremen, Hamburg, Lubeck, Stettin, Danzig, Stockholm, Oslo, and London have characteristic locations (Shepherd, Atlas, 98-99).

The East 

The second magnetic field of medieval European commerce was the Levant (the eastern end of the Mediterranean Sea), to which Oriental goods found their way by overland routes. Until the Turks took possession of the Byzantine Empire a short time before the Discoveries, Constantinople was the major western terminus of the Oriental trade, with Antioch and Tripoli as minor ports. All these places lie at or near the end of the age-old trade route that runs in a great crescent between the Arabian Desert and the mountains at the base of the Asia Minor Peninsula. Of the

ports serving this trade, only Constantinople was favored with a commodious and protected harbor.

The direct route from Constantinople to Central and Western Europe involved either much overland travel through rugged country or use of the Danube River. This swift stream traversed open plains, where fighting was almost constant between the Austro-Hungarians and nomads from Eastern Europe. The Mediterranean sea route was preferred on every count, and the traffic fell more and more largely into the hands of Italian port cities. 

Venice and Genoa, at the Mediterranean outlets of principal Alpine pass routes, came to be the dominant trading states. Each had a harbor satisfactory for ships of the period, and each grew into a wealthy and powerful country by serving as middleman to the trade. 

Not all the business of the Byzantine and Italian cities was in goods. At intervals they profited greatly by transporting Crusaders to 

Palestine, and in the periods between Crusades those in Italy did a humbler but larger business in taking pilgrims to and from Rome.

Above all, these two parts of Europe are distinguished from each other by climates so different that their agricultural trade is permanently reciprocal. Legal Status of Commercial Towns 

Not all Europeans who lived by trade fostered it. Many lords, bred in the agricultural tradition, saw in rising trade either a threat to their economic and social world, or else a goose laying golden eggs. Such of these men as controlled strategic bits of trade routes were in a position to tap the traffic by taxing it. Ownership of a castle that had been erected for defense on a cliff overlooking a navigable stream, of a narrow mountain pass, or of a natural cross-roads, made it possible to levy on all goods and persons using the route. Toll gates on roads and trails, chains across rivers, and tolls at bridges, were devices used to take a profit from the traffic. Wise rulers kept the taxes low enough to insure continuance of the flow of business. Others laid such heavy taxes that alternative routes were used. 

If new routes could not be found in such cases, the traffic languished. In general the larger landholders were least inclined to over tax trade. They had other sources of income, and they were in a position to see the damage excessive taxes might cause. The enlighterment of the Counts of Champagne has already been mentioned. A minor lord, whose fields and pastures could not keep him in comfort, was more likely to snatch greedily at the wealth passing his door. In districts where holdings were generally small, the aggregate levies tended to strangle trade even when individual takings were not excessive. Central 

Europe presented the paradox of having the most numerous and arbitrary imposts, while at the same time having the most powerful and independent trading towns. As soon as coasting routes were established, the seaboard towns of this region abandoned the tax-fettered overland routes.

The 

The manorial aristocracy looked down upon trade, as an upstart means of livelihood. Most of the townsmen were therefore people lacking gentle blood, and as in all new commercial societies, success was as likely to crown ruthlessness and deception as to reward industry and shrewdness. landlords felt themselves to be victims of the merchants and money-lenders with whom they did business. Consequently trading towns usually had an uphill fight to obtain the grants of privilege from hereditary rulers necessary for their legal protection.

Where the rulers were relatively strong, as in the extreme west of Europe, cities were only slowly given privileges, and often under strict supervision of the king. In Central Europe the burden of the Empire drained the surplus wealth of the successive wearers of the Imperial Crown. 

To get funds for the pursuit of the title of Emperor and for the imperial expeditions to Italy, many a wealthy noble was impoverished and was forced to call upon his urban subjects for loans of money. Such money was rarely repaid, and so was usually forthcoming only in return for a grant of privilege. By obtaining successive privileges many towns in Germany as in 

Italy came to be independent in all but name. 

Incipient "National States"

The interweaving of the economic, social, and political life of a people never ceases, but the earth-pattern produced, varies from age to age and from region to region. The centuries that saw the beginnings of profound alterations in agricultural and commercial pursuits, witnessed also a territorial rearrangement of political units no less far-reaching. Territorial 

Patterns 

The Middle Ages inherited from Rome the ideal concept of a World

State. The ancient Roman Empire had embraced roughly the civilized world of its day and at Constantinople in the Greek-speaking Eastern Roman Empire, the imperial succession continued unbroken until 1453, when that capital fell to the advancing Turks. 

In the Latin West the ideal of political unity survived the Teutonic invasions and the temporary abeyance of the imperial title for some three centuries (476-800). This ideal unity found a dual expression in the West: (1) in the spiritual realm it was expressed in the imperial pretensions and the expanding moral sway of the See of Peter embodied in the person of the Pope, whose prestige derived from his status as Bishop of Rome, the ancient capital of the west. (2) In the secular sphere the imperial tradition was revived (800) in the person of the ruler of the 

Frankish people.

The Franks, a Germanic folk whose original homeland lay in the Rhine Valley between the Delta and the confluence of the Main, founded their imperial claims on the conquest of most of the Rhineland and the Seine Basin. This conquest of the Seine region reversed the Roman progress from northern Gaul to the Rhine frontier, following the same natural routes, south and westward, and anticipating the course of invasion from that day to this (including 1940). 

Expansion and conquest, based on the Rhine and the Paris Basin, gave the Franks a brief continental mastery, including virtually all of western Christian (Latin) civilization, except Britain, south Italy, and the bulk of the Iberian Peninsula. Charlemagne's empire barely survived its creator, but the restoration of 800 had endowed the imperial title with a prestige that preserved the title and lent it a potent influence on the political thought of western mankind for a thousand years.

Shortly after Charlemagne's death, the indisputably Latin lands to

the west of the Rhine, and the equally Germanic lands to the east of that valley, began to go their own ways. This dual development left a central corridor with fluctuating frontiers, nominally under the imperial sway, but in fact the continuous battleground of the future France and Germany. 

Here germinated the great struggle that still goes on. 

This narrow strip of disputed territory may be regarded as extending in practice from the mouth of the Rhine to Rome. The corridor was cut in two by the barrier of the Alps, a serious obstacle to free movement even in summer, and a little-traversed waste in winter. Lacking barriers on any of its frontiers, it never attained permanent racial or cultural unity, and its chief value lay in the fact that it was a great artery of trade between the south and the north. This trade the early emperors made a vigorous effort to control. But the parallel weakening of world economy and political unity throughout western Europe doomed this local effort to failure a striking example of the deterioration of European unity resulting from the dissipation of imperial energies in 

pursuit of empty imperial pretensions. 

The real power of the emperors was derived from their personal holdings, which lay chiefly in the German part of Europe. Two centuries before the Discoveries the imperial title migrated southward and eastward and became traditionally associated with Austria, the Danubian frontier march of the Germanic world and with the House of Hapsburg. At one time or another Austrian holdings included the more valuable central and eastern Alpine passes, the Moravian Gate between the Middle Danube Basin and the North European Plain, and the east-west route along the Danube itself. 

Elsewhere in the world traditionally associated with the Western Roman Empire, both north and south of the Alps, the ideal of a unified world state was lost in a complex of minute territories.

Meanwhile less grandiose political ambitions were being achieved. 

In an age that counted wealth almost exclusively in land, landowners strove mightily to obtain as much territory as possible. In general the man who possessed the most land could marshall the largest fighting force, and so make himself the leader of his district. But landowners great and small were confronted with the stubborn fact that optimum use of farmland under European conditions of soil and climate, requires that it be managed in rather small tracts. It was this fact that brought the small districts, later known as manors, into existence as the basic unit of agrarian economic life, even before the end (476) of the Empire of the West. 

The lord of numerous manors, especially if they were scattered, usually found it necessary to turn some of them over to vassals who could personally supervise their share of the holdings. For use of the land they rendered specified returns, especially military service, to their liege lord. While clinging tenaciously to the traditional ideal of an emperor paramount throughout the civilized world, the various regions of Western Europe took a more realistic attitude toward the immediate problems of local goverment. The The need was simple --the creation of political units large enough, and provided with sufficient natural resources to defend themselves. process was overlaid with complicated legal conditions, but territorially it entailed the assembling of manors and their market towns into units large and varied enough to be self-sustaining and self-governing. As centuries passed, a few of these political units outstripped the rest by the simple but effective method of incorporating their neighbors. procedure has never become obsolete, as is evident in the moves of Germany into neighboring states at the present moment.

The The elements of success in the long sweep of territorial consolidation

were social and economic, and sometimes personal, as well as environmental and political.

Assimilation was swifter and less difficult during the earlier era of slight cultural differentiation and fluctuating political boundaries, than later, when linguistic groups had crystallized in semi-isolation, and had acquired material and cultural resources with which to resist incorporation into a more powerful state. Three examples will illustrate this point. Fusion of the Orléanais with the Île de France in the early years of the French monarchy, amounted to little more than an extension of a uniform administration over adjacent tracts of about equal size and value, occupied by peoples who dwelt in much the same sort of country and spoke virtually the same dialect. Some centuries later a French state that had demonstrated its vigor by forcibly eliminating English and Burgundian authority from large tracts of French-speaking territory, and had already amalgamated most of the area known today as France, conquered Brittany. 

This was a small district handicapped by rugged and rocky terrain, infertile soil, and a wet, cool climate; but it derived coherence from the distinctiveness and unity of its peninsula, and the reliance of most of its inhabitants upon fishing. Today, after nearly 500 years as part of France, the Bretons conserve their language and their costume, and are not altogether French. This fact the German invader has sought to capitalize (1940) by supporting Breton separatism.

Until Holland was overrun (in 1940) by military force, the Dutch kept themselves independent of Germany. For two centuries they had lived under the shadow of a far larger Teutonic state, and for twice that period they had been middlemen in the trade of the whole German area. 

But they had early evolved a distinct language in the isolation of their marshy delta, they had continued to live a highly individualized life in ceaseless

struggle with the waters of their amphibious habitat, and an important part of their national energy was devoted to overseas commerce and colonial developments activities carried on in an environment which Germany could not reach, so long as it remained a disunited group of land-states. 

Many small states persisted, especially in the part of Central Europe where the Roman imperial legend survived longest, because central authority had been too weak to consolidate them. As powerful and jealous neighbors grew up to west and to east, they made it their policy to prevent union within the intermediate zone. This was very apparent in northern and central Italy. There for ages the Emperors and the Popes had struggled for supremacy. After the imperial power was finally broken (1250) on the hot and malarial Po Plain, France and Austria waged war after war for the dominance of Italy. This struggle was later transformed into rivalry between France and Germany for Italian support, which has survived in the

National 

States 

France grew from the Île de France and had Paris as a focus. The immediately surrounding country was fertile, and the Seine and other navigable river systems brought moderate trade to the capital city. outstanding advantage of this small, original holding of the Capetians The was its position at the center of half a dozen concentric lines of hills, each rising outward imperceptibly and presenting a steep and rugged face to the invader.

This multiple military barrier of encircling hills enabled the rulers of France to ward off enemies century after century, while steadily pushing their borders outward. In the course of this expansion they annexed the profitable Champagne trade route, as well as much farmland.

England likewise expanded from an urban center partly shielded by similar concentric lines of hills. This was a minor item in its defense and territorial expansion compared to the tremendous advantage derived from insularity. The North Sea and the English Channel kept the country inviolate from invasion so long as British seapower went unchallenged. At the same time, these "narrow seas" and the wider oceans gave to England opportunities for colonization and trade which, in the course of time, produced the British Empire. Scandinavia and Holland are smaller states that fall into the general pattern of England, with local variations, due chiefly to incomplete physical separation from the continent. 

Another group of vigorous states of the early modern epoch gained their position more by fighting than through natural defences, and include those of the Iberian Peninsula and eastern Germany.

Portugal and the small units that finally coalesced to form Spain, emerged from fortress-like habitats in the Pyrenees and Cantabrian mountains, to grow into relentless and warlike states on the bleak plateaus

and barren hills of the northern half of Iberia. Fired by Christian zeal to oust the Moslem conquerors from their homeland, these small states pushed slowly southward in an intermittent crusade of seven centuries, until the Peninsula was finally cleared in the very year that Columbus discovered America. In the process all of their people intermingled on the open plateaus of the Meseta, except the Portuguese of the Atlantic lowland and the Catalonians of the Ebro Basin. A major distinction between these two districts and the bulk of the Iberian Peninsula is their lowland character and the ease with which they make contact with the sea for fishing and for trading. In contrast, the other coastal lowlands of the peninsula are very narrow, and are cut off from the interior. This "Meseta" is a high, dry, barren, and mountain-crossed plateau, the chief natural resource of which is its minerals. Such a contrast in lowland and upland enviroments could be a source of strength to a unified state if autonomy were conceded to component environmental units. Nowhere else in Europe is the delicate balance between separatism and reciprocal union so neatly illustrated. The Meseta countries, as they expanded southward on the upland, coalesced into a political unity that has never been shattered in spite of regional variations that persist. This is true even where the population is distinct in origin and language, as in the Basque country. 

Catalonia early attached itself to this group, in part because it lay in the path of territorial expansion. After five centuries, however, it retains its language and its national feeling, and in 1936 was the backbone of the losing side in a civil war which from the viewpoint of political geography was a struggle between centralized authority and demands for local autonomy. Portugal, outside the line of expansion, carried on its own thrust against the Moors on the broad Atlantic coastal plain. Its efforts were successful more than two hundred years before the larger task of Spain

was accomplished. When Spain finally annexed the smaller country by force, in the century following the Discoveries (1580) its distinctive language was already set, its traditions at home and abroad constituted a rallying point for the assertion of Portuguese national feeling, and the union was soon dismembered (1640).

In east central Europe coherent states of moderate size grew up along the line of contact between Germanic and Slavic societies. Two of these states were of German origin, and stood on the open routes between the stable west and the fluid east. Both were established during the 

Middle Ages as outposts of a sedentary society against the threat of raids by nomadic migrants from the east. Austria occupied the gap made by the 

Danube between the Alps and the lesser, but effective barrier of wooded ranges known as the Bohemian Forest and the Erzgebirge (Ore Mountains).

Saxony and Prussia are the modern heirs of numerous border "march" states on the Plain between these ranges and the Baltic Sea. In this discussion 

Saxony may be disregarded, because the Prussians became dominant in this whole area. Both Austria and Prussia were condemned by circumstances to a constant state of readiness to fight in defense of Germany. natural that such states should use their military machine to carry on aggression against their enemies.

Germanic culture upon its neighbors without suppressing their individuality. 

This was natural because they occupied definite and defensible territories, which could be penetrated by cultural influences more readily than conquered by military force. The Bohemian and Moravian Slavs, lodged behind the Bohemian Forest and the Ore Mountains, were semi-Germanized before the end of the Middle Ages. The Hungarians of the grassy plain of the Middle

but they had also became sedentary enough to build a Gothic cathedral in their capital city in the fourteenth century. 

In contrast to Austria, Prussia pursued a policy of ingesting small territorial units. In each such district the Slavic natives were either killed or completely Germanized. No vestige of Slavic culture remains on the north European Plain east of Poland except in two districts. 

In the sandlands and marshes of the upper Spree Valley (a very inaccessible and poverty-stricken region during the period of conquest), a few Wends still speak their Slavic tongue, a cultural island in a Prussian sea. In the rugged and infertile hills of Pamerelia a similar, small group speaks a dialect closely resembling Polish. But this district lies close to the 

Polish frontier and at times has been incorporated in a Polish state. 

A little prior to the Discoveries, the Germanic expansion eastward came to a halt. In the Danube Basin the Hungarians maintained quasi-independence until their sovereignty was snuffed out, not by Austrians pushing downstream, but by Turks moving upstream. On the Northern Plain the Polish state, at the time entrenched against Prussian aggression behind a double barrier of marshes, established its independence, but accepted Roman Catholicism and many other aspects of Western European culture. Farther east, Russia was throwing off the yoke of Asiatic nomads, and laying the foundations of a national state on general European lines. Meanwhile overseas exploration was progressing under the aegis of Iberian and 

Scandinavian states. 

Thus, in the early fifteenth century the broad territorial pattern of Europe was not unlike that of the two decades following 1919. Most of the national states of modern Europe had taken form, leaving only bits of territory to be filled in. The notable exception was Central Europe, where from the North Sea to Sicily, the territory lay broken into political units

of every size from the single manor to a vast, inchoate, sprawling organism such as that headed by Austria. Few of these entities had barrier boundaries, and none of the larger ones consisted of a single, uninterrupted block of territory. In this section the map continued to look like that of the Middle Ages.

Elsewhere, as has been said, both east and west, the map already began to take on the aspect of the modern world. States were generally of moderate or even large size, many of them possessed barrier boundaries, at least in part, and all but two or three of them comprised uninterrupted blocks of territory. There remained enclaves of foreign sovereignty here and there, but they constituted no threat to the integrity of the states that surrounded them. Most of these states were internally coherent. 

Each had sprung from an organizing nucleus around which territorial accretion had begun, and where it continued to occur. In such states the bulk of the territory tacitly recognized the dialect of its nucleus as the national vernacular, because it was spoken by the ruler. However, until the invention of printing (in the mid-fifteenth century) variations in spelling and pronunciation were of little consequence among people who read little and travelled hardly at all. 

In the course of the Middle Ages, under the spell of a common heritage, and particularly of a common struggle against foreign enemies, the intangible but powerful force of nationalism, had been born in most, if not all, these larger states. Nationality has so far turned out to be the most powerful integrating force in modern society. 

Indeed, it might not be far wrong to consider the birth of nationality in any given state as the moment of its transmutation from a medieval to a modern institution.

Territorial 

Advantages of the

National State 

The national state had certain territorial advantages over its medieval predecessor.

It was obviously larger than any of its constituent units. Even in the poorest regions increase in area meant some increase in resources. This was sure to be an advantage, but it might be especially valuable because of incorporation of resources reciprocal with those in the original core of the state. For example, the original nucleus of France was agricultural, and trade was a minor matter. The addition of Champagne, a poor agricultural area, brought France athwart a major European trade route. Later the accretion of the Mediterranean littoral gave the growing nation a contrasting climate and increased its trade potential, especially in agricultural products.

Another advantage of territorial enlargement and rearrangement was the reduction of the length of boundaries. Typical medieval states comprised scattered units which were costly to defend at best, and at worst were virtually held in pawn by the rulers of intervening territory. 

The assemblage of scattered units into a single uninterrupted block reduced the task of boundary defense and gave the strength of unity to the whole aggregation of the nation's natural resources. 

The energy released by this process was incalculable. Most national states had no sooner perfected their internal structure than they launched a program of expansion. The direction in which this energy moved was determined by opportunity. The states of East Central Europe began waging wars upon each other in order to obtain maximum territory on the extensive and indeterminate plains of that part of the continent. Austria was soon embroiled in struggles with the advancing Turks on the east, and with western European states along the Franco-German border, where imperial

claims demanded or permitted intervention.

The national states fronting the Atlantic Ocean generally devoted their surplus energy to overseas exploration, and later to colonization of the lands which were discovered. 

Inevitably they were more or less constantly at war with one another over these colonial spoils.

The General 

Picture 

Europe of the Fifteenth Century 

The map of Europe in the epoch of the Discoveries reveals a continent in which most of the states of subsequent centuries were adumbrated, but where considerable areas still bore close resemblance to the minuter subdivision of the preceding period. It was a continent which had just begun to envisage the distant Ural Mountains as its eastern boundary. Its southern margin was not yet fully cleared of Moslem invaders in the southwest, and their co-religionists had not begun to be driven back in the southeast. The African and Asiatic shores of the Mediterranean Sea were wholly independent of European control, and cut off from European culture. 

Already intellectual, economic and political ferments of profound significance had begun to alter the ways in which people occupied land, and these changes went hand-in-hand with territorial rearrangements in the political world. Some of the changes were made possible or accelerated by the revival of classical learning, and by inventions which lifted material technology to a new level. Improved methods of farming utilized the land more efficiently in general, took account of regional differentiation in climate and of local variation in soil and in exposure. Surpluses, accumulated by wiser land utilization, stimulated exchange and were in turn redoubled because of the

opportunity for trade among areas reciprocal in products. Trading towns grew up at route junctions and crossings, and at points where goods and people had to shift from one form of transport to another. The people who lived in the towns and engaged in trade provided an additional market for farm produce. Handcrafts sprang up under the magic of improved transportation for raw materials and finished goods, and the artisans swelled urban populations. The axis of trade and of mercantile towns reached from the 

Mediterranean between Venice and Marseilles, to the vicinity of the English

Channel. To the eastward lay the expanding frontier, and along it the nuclei of several states destined to become larger than most of those to the westward. This was in keeping with the larger scale of the lands in East 

Central and Eastern Europe. Apart from mere size, the character of most of these states was medieval, rather than modern. Few of them had been stirred by the ferments that were at work in Western Europe. West of the line of trading towns the states had achieved a marked degree of territorial unity and coherence, although their outlines were not yet filled out. They stood at different stages on the way to modern utilization of the land, but all had set their feet upon the road. The energy they conserved by modernization found no outlet on an expanding frontier, except in southern Spain where the work was all but finished. Instead it was available for exploration and colonization overseas, and to those objectives much of it was directed. 

States of the

Atlantic Periphery 

All of the colonizing states on the Atlantic periphery of Europe had considerable populations devoted to fishing. The waters on the shallow banks off Western Europe are cool and therefore teem with firm-fleshed fish, suitable for curing to supply a substitute for meat on fast days, and a

cheap protein food during the season when farm products are scarce. Crews for merchant ships could be recruited from fishing boats, and as navies grew up, the fishermen made hardy sailors for warships. Fishing fleets on the banks of Iceland, Newfoundland, and Morocco pointed the way to the routes later followed in the Discoveries.

The Atlantic coast of Europe is well supplied with harbors of every size and suited to every use. Fishing fleets used small coves close to the fishing grounds. Merchant ships generally preferred harbors as far inland as possible, in order to load and unload near the populous core of the country. Naval vessels could be segregated in commodious but secluded harbors more useful for them than for commercial ventures. 

The several European coastal states from Norway to Portugal were unequally matched for the race to colonial power. Probably no one living in 1450 could have correctly forecast the actual course of modern imperial development.

Norway and Dermark had been recently embraced in political union, but their equipment for overseas exploration and colonization was unlike. 

Norway was the most exclusively maritime region in Europe. Its climate is cold, its relief mountainous, its soil infertile, and much of the interior has been scraped by glaciers to bare bedrock. Its hundreds of little harbors and the abundant fish offshore have turned every generation of Norwegians to the sea for a livelihood. They early undertook exploration, but were balked by the drifting ice which perpetually locks the coast of north Europe east of the White Sea. They moved across the foggy north 

Atlantic by way of the Faeroes and Iceland. If they reached the North 

American continent long before the era of the recognized discoveries, they left no clear record of such a find.

In contrast to Norway, Denmark has no satisfactory harbors on its

west (Atlantic) side. It is a moderately productive lowland that faces east at the gateway to the Baltic Sea, and profits from the trade which passes its door.

It has no incentive to become an important maritime state beyond this limited radius. 

The two states of the Iberian Peninsula are likewise similar in location and very different in other respects. They lie closer than the rest of Europe to the African coasting route which proved to be the shortest nature-made route to the Orient, but this advantage is reduced by the fact that they are cut off from the rest of Europe by distance and by the Pyrenees mountains.

Portugal, however, is a small country of infertile soil. Like 

Norway it has been turned to the sea by the presence of richer resources there than on land. Its interests therefore have always been maritime -a great advantage to a trading and colonizing state. 

Spain also possesses a poor section, the mountainous country on the Bay of Biscay. This remote and insignificant area is devoted to fishing, and the focus of Spanish economic interest is traditionally in the interior plateau, a dry country of grazing and metal mining, with a certain amount of dry-farming of wheat and barley. The great advantage of both Spain and 

Portugal for overseas ventures was their unified government. 

Centuries of fighting the Moors had forced each Iberian Christian state to subordinate all individual freedom to the Church and the Army. The resources of the succession countries when they entered the contest for overseas trade and empire were therefore completely under the control of their rulers. They made up in unified effort a part of what they lacked through the poverty of their homelands.

PART III. EUROPE ON THE THRESHOLD OF THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION A Major Development in Modern European History and Culture 

By tradition and common consent, the history of the occidental world has been divided for convenience into three periods: 

Ancient (ending c. 476 A.D.); Medieval (ending c. 1453); and Modern. Parts I and II of the 

Environmental Foundations of European History have dealt with the significant differences in geography and culture between these eras. Part III will treat of modern Europe, and particularly Europe on the eve of the Industrial Revolution. 

From the beginning of the modern period, the earth's resources have been used more comprehensively and more intensively than ever before in history, and the process maintains its intensification today. 

In their time, Ancient and Medieval Europe have each constituted the known world, geographic and cultural, of the day. Modern Europe, by contrast, is merely one of several continents, each of which contributes its natural and human resources to a society that encompasses the entire world. In the course of history, curiosity and ingenuity have exploited a steadily augmented body of natural resources, and have created an interdependent world, today thirteen times more extensive in territory, and vastly more complex, than Europe at the dawn of the Modern Age. This process has involved momentous changes in European political, economic, social, and intellectual life, all of them intimately related to the environmental conditions in which they evolved. For this reason it is important to take careful stock of the geographic influences at work when expansion from the limited base of ancient and medieval tradition to the familiar enlarged environment of the modern world, took place. The most decisive changes of this great transition occupied two generations (1760-1825), and began the transmutation of society which is generally called the Industrial Revolution.

Renaissance and

Reformation Precursors of the Industrial Revolution 

The genesis of this great change can be traced at least to the end of the Middle Ages when the Revival of Learning marked a shift in man's thoughts from medieval otherworldliness, and from his traditional dependence on clerical direction, to humanism, individualism, and mundane ideas. This refocusing of interest is reflected in the Age of Discoveries when rapid expansion of the known world and increasing mastery of their environment engrossed the attention of Europeans. The Discoveries (supra, pp. 39 sq.) and European enterprise, linked the seacoasts of the globe into a great thoroughfare, the highway of the sailing ship. In time the Continent of 

Europe lost its ancient self-sufficiency, and became but one of six continents and--next to Australia--the smallest. 

Despite this change in status, Europe, by further exploration of continental mainlands, by occupation of vast territory, and by establishment of overseas trade, maintained its initial advantage. The world was gradually transformed into an economic whole, if not into an economic unit, as commerce and colonization drew the natural resources of all continents into a single system of exploitation. Europe led in this unparalleled integration of scattered mineral deposits, varied resources, contrasting climates, and divergent technological skills, and profited by the unheard-of advance in human productivity that it produced.

Trade was the first activity to prosper, as the original trickle of luxury goods from the ancient civilization of the Orient swelled steadily in volume and variety, and frontier goods from Northeast Europe mingled in European marts with the unfamiliar products of new frontier continents--Africa and the Americas. Soon the European trade sphere included the whole range of climates, and the distinctive products of every population group of the globe.

Commercial Revolution 

The 

To profit by seaborne world-trade, Europe was ideally placed. land surface of the earth from which all but a negligible fraction of the earth's production is derived, is unequally distributed over the globe. If a Great Circle be drawn to include the maximum land area, the resultant hemisphere will be found to have its pole in the southern North Sea. Except for the obstacle offered to navigation by the Arctic ice directly north of this center, the ocean stretches uninterrupted in every direction to all the lands of the earth. The initiative of western Europeans in exploiting the possibilities of this great water highway, gave the original impetus to the Commercial Revolution (supra, pp. 48 sq.), and, partly by reason of its central location within the land continent, fixed the center of gravity of this development within Atlantic Europe.

Agricultural Revolution Practically all the arable and pastoral land of the earth, likewise lies within the land hemisphere just described, or adjacent to it, on extensions of the ocean trade routes that run in every direction from its pole near the mouths of the Thames and the Rhine. Naturally increased trade led to increased agricultural production, and livestock and crop plants were transplanted from one continent to another. In this process new plants were introduced into Europe, and such as could be acclimatized became useful additions to the regional economy of their new homes. A larger number were unsuited to the European climate, and are grown nearby on other continents, producing food, clothing, and goods useful to Europeans. Such was the beginning of the Agricultural Revolution (supra, pp. 41 sq.). Today, as a result of this Revolution, every staple crop and domestic beast has a range

essentially coterminous with the habitat in which it can thrive. Europe has known how to profit by the progress of this development to exploit the vast acreages of the newly won continents, eventually on a mass production basis. 

But until the full tide of the Industrial Revolution, the complete potential of the colonial larder could not be tapped.

Reformation 

Notwithstanding the range and influence of this Revolution, a far larger number of men found their outlook completely altered by the great religious revolution which goes by the name of the Reformation. Completing the inner logic of the development of individualism that marks the Renaissance, this movement--particularly Calvinism and its derivatives--placed responsibility for personal acts directly upon the individual. This extended the groundwork of a new society, destined ultimately to reject completely the medieval ideal of a material universe ordained and dominated by divine forces, and reflected on earth in two world states, the Holy Roman Church and the 

Holy Roman Empire, National States 

As the inspiration of the Renaissance and Reformation flagged, the traditional idea of an amorphous world-state receded, especially in Protestant Europe, before a new conception, the national state (supra, pp. 57 sq.). 

Within this new political frame, the individual began to function in a new environment; a new class, the bourgeoisie, began its rise to power; and eventually a new socio-economic doctrine, laissez-faire, appeared. The logio of these developments is clear and it is no accident that the Treaty of Westphalia (1648) which closed the bloodiest of religious wars, established the implicit "right" of the individual to worship as national opinion

(expressed by the national state) rather than the decree of the universal church, decided. Under this treaty there also emerged two, essentially democratic, new states, Holland and Switzerland. In subsequent centuries industrial expansion was to be increasingly sponsored by national states. 

Their governments, feeling no preoccupation with the other world and unhampered by tradition, were free to concentrate on exploiting the wealth of the segments of the earth which they severally controlled. 

Henceforth individuals, freed of religious pressure as perhaps never before since the Golden Age of Hellas, were increasingly free to investigate the material world. A new interest in natural phenomena, growing freedom to experiment, and great advances in mathematics, led to the birth of modern science and invention in post-Reformation Europe. Practical solutions of the problems set by nature, advances in theoretical knowledge of the material world, gave men increasing control over their environment, and led to discoveries and inventions that brought the Industrial Revolution (more accurately designated the Industrial and Intellectual Revolution) to its full tide. This development preceded the French Revolution by a generation or two at most, and still continues toward an unknown destiny. 

THE GEOGRAPHIC SETTING OF THE INDUSTRIAL REVOLUTION 

It is no part of the geographer's task to analyze in detail the pattern of the historical forces at work in early modern Europe. For him it is enough to point out that the Age of Discoveries, the Renaissance and Reformation, the Commercial and Agricultural Revolutions, and the Enlightenment, all contributed to the development of the Industrial and Intellectual The immediate task of the geographer is to picture the geographic setting of this new movement, and to describe the environmental foundation from which it rose.

Revolution.

Europe in the Mid-Eighteenth Century (the Enlightenment) The ferment of the Renaissance and Reformation affected the entire cultural development of "traditional Europe" (i.e. Europe as so far defined by history and habit) and even spread into Russia, which in the eighteenth century began to turn its face sharply westward, and even assumed a slight 

European cultural veneer. 

Russia in

Europe In making its territorial growth, the original Muscovite State (the future Russia) expanded from a center at Moscow, near the water parting of the streams flowing to the Baltic, Black, Caspian, and White Seas. During the early centuries of the Modern Era, Russia made territorial accretions by pushing downstream toward all these seas. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, territory along the Baltic was annexed, opening Russia's first great "window to the West." At the end of the eighteenth century, the lion's share of Poland was seized, and the Turks were thrust back from the northern shore of the Black Sea (Treaty of Kuchuk Kainarji, 1774), opening a second "window." These acquisitions pushed Russia into lands traditionally considered a part of Europe since the Middle Ages. Territorial conquest in these areas was followed by active participation in European politics, and eventually the Russian state began to make itself a part of Europe. This development has now been so generally accepted that the eastern boundary of Europe is today commonly considered to have marched eastward approximately to the line of the Ural Mountains and the Ural River. Indeed, it is a striking fact that the Urals, today regarded as the eastern frontier of Europe, were thought of as lying within the very matrix of Russian lands, rather than as situated on their fringe, from the earliest beginning (c. 1580) of Russia's swift push toward the Pacific Ocean (first reached, 1637).

By contrast with this rapid march, Russian absorption of her western provinces was a slow, piecemeal operation, steadfastly contested to the present moment. The crucial boundary in this quarter appears to lie somewhere along Russia's fluctuating western frontier zone, rather than on the narrow, low, and easily traversed Ural Range. The conclusion is obvious, namely that the claim of Russia to admission into the family of western nations rests on insecure geographic foundations, and is subject to revision. 

Beginning with the reign of Peter the Great (1689-1725), Russian influence in European politics rested chiefly on her overwhelming size and on the lack of natural barriers between her and the lesser states of East Central Europe. In the eultural and political movements that profoundly altered the, character of the "traditional Europe" defined by the Revival of Learning, the Discoveries, etc., Russia took no effective part, for the accidents of geography and history had alike combined to insulate her. 

Reflection of their impact came late and at second-hand.

At the time of the oceanic discoveries, the Russians were a land-

Geographically, if Russia is to be treated as part of Europe, it must be considered as a region culturally distinct from the rest of Europe, even during the early years of the Industrial Revolution, when Russian participation in European affairs was at its apogee. 

A Divided Mediterranean World 

Mediterranean Europe retained its ineradicable distinctiveness, but lost both area and influence during the period between the Discoveries and the Industrial Revolution. In this quarter, as the inroads of the Turks from the east by way of Asia Minor (reaching at one time (1683) to the gates of Vienna) extended into the Balkan Peninsula and the Lower Danube region, the boundary of Europe was actually retracted as compared to previous ages, until the nineteenth century. Turkish conquests hastened, if they did not produce, the Age of Discoveries, which brought the Iberian Peninsula to the leadership of Europe. 

This Peninsula, situated at the western end of the Mediterranean, was by tradition and environment a part of the Mediterranean world. On the other hand, the great Iberian ports opened on the Atlantic; this gave the Spaniards and the Portuguese opportunity to lead the great overseas expansion that opens modern history, and a chance to profit largely from colonial enterprise. As decades passed into centuries, other Atlantic states entered the competition, and wrested larger and larger shares of overseas commerce from the Iberian

peoples. Nevertheless, until industrialization profoundly altered the relative values of natural resources, the Iberian states were rightly regarded as great powers. It should be noted that aside from the strategic advantages of their location, these nations derived no part of their political weight from their Mediterranean habitat. Their power was based on control of overseas trade and colonies, as was clear when they relapsed into minor Mediterranean states upon losing their empires. 

At the moment when the Balkan Peninsula was subjected to conquest from Asia, and the Iberian Peninsula turned its back on the Mediterranean world to face the overseas continents, the intervening Mediterranean coast of Europe entered its eclipse. Restricted to little more than the peninsula of Italy, this coast was cut off from commerce of the first magnitude, the primary basis of its former prosperity. Trans-Alpine trade likewise had dwindled to an exchange of local products, but Italian trade was still valuable enough to keep every section of the Peninsula a pawn on the chess board of the European powers--Spain, France, and Austria. In this period of political fragmentation and impotence, the Italian core of the Mediterranean world resembled north central Europe more than any other part of the continent.

Central Europe Between Russia and Turkey on the east, and the countries facing the Atlantic and the North Sea on the west, stretched a large area that had come to a partial share of the social and spiritual heritage of traditional Europe, but which lay too far from the sea to profit immediately from the Commercial Revolution. 

Two loose complexes, large for Europe, occupied the extensive lowlands of the eastern two-thirds of this area: (1) Poland, a state in the

open north European plain, lacked barrier boundaries, except for the Carpathian Mountains and a few short reaches of hills and marshes. (2) 

Austria and Hungary, under the Hapsburgs, had spread out to fill the mountain-girt basins associated with the middle course of the Danube River. 

Each of these included several language groups, resembling in this respect the Atlantic states at a comparable stage of their development. Both appeared to be achieving national unity after the pattern established by France, Great Britain, and Spain. No one could have foreseen the twentieth century failure of these two states to complete the task of integrating their peoples with the land units to which they laid claim. 

The western third of North Central Europe (i.e. Germany) was subdivided into more than 300 small political units of varied size, relics of German medieval political structure. Like the small states of Italy, these were pawns of the larger European powers: Spain, France, and Britain on the west, Austria and Hungary, and sometimes Russia, on the east. 

Static politically, this section had made slight economic advance since the Middle Ages. Its commerce had actually diminished, when, like 

Italy, it had lost its share of the Oriental trade to the countries on the Atlantic seaboard. Local and trans-Alpine commerce languished under the levies exacted by petty rulers of the many tiny states through which goods had to pass.

The Atlantic 

Seaboard

What Italy and Germany had lost by the Discoveries was gained many fold by the seaboard states on the Atlantic periphery of the continent. The stimulus of overseas trade was first felt in Portugal, whence it made swift headway along the coast northward to Norway and Sweden. 

Each of the states in this group had already been welded into a nation

by centuries of joint endeavor, and had substituted internal unity for the minute subdivision of feudal tradition. Freed from internal strife, they were ready to embark upon enterprises beyond their own borders. Wellestablished central authority took prompt advantage of the commercial opportunities that were opened by explorations of other continents. After nearly three centuries of trafficking and colonizing, Portugal, Spain, France, the Netherlands, and Britain held rich lands overseas, and Dermark and Sweden had small possessions and trading stations. 

These Atlantic seaboard countries sent to the overseas continents cloth, pottery, hardware, ornaments, and other products of handicraft. They received in return foodstuffs, beverages, raw materials for the craftsman, and (from East and South Asia) silks, lacquers, jades, and other luxury goods. 

The larger part of the profits went into the pockets of European merchants, who were the middlemen and the owners of the ships in which the goods were carried.

The Commercial Pattern. Transportation. 

Ships were not large, and could put into shallow harbors. The whole 

Atlantic coastline of Europe has been drowned in recent geological times. 

In the process the sea has run up all the river valleys and other depressions facing the ocean, converting them into estuaries and bays, some of which penetrate the shoreline sixty miles or more. 

Because it is cheaper to carry goods by water than overland, the heads of long estuaries were especially favored as sites for ports. Provided the approach was wide enough for easy sailing, ocean-going ships could make these ports their terminals. Amsterdam, London, Bristol, Bordeaux, and Lisbon are conspicuous examples of such ports.

There are many similar sites, only a little less desirable. These gave rise

to ports serving hinterlands local in scope. By the mid-eighteenth century, the whole coast from Cadiz to Bergen teemed with busy ports. 

Rivers were the chief highways of the Middle Ages, and most of the large waterways flow into broad estuaries where the port towns grew up. 

These great harbors were alive with shipping--inland, coastal, and colonial--and the collection and distribution of goods for overseas trade was enormously facilitated by the use of inland waters. 

Europe is highly favored in the number of separate navigable rivers which flow directly into the sea, rather than into a single system, like that of the Mississippi. Even so, the rivers are not close enough together to provide a complete natural network of waterways, and had to be supplemented by canals wherever possible. On the flat but narrow coastal plain, and particularly on the broader Rhine-Scheldt delta, canal building without locks was possible. Here, single locks were used as early as the fourteenth century, chiefly to move from tidal streams to fresh-water canals. Navigation canals in sloping country required a series of locks, and a constant supply of water to operate them. This more intricate form of canal was in use before the end of the seventeenth century. Where streams were lacking or engineering knowledge was inadequate, roads might be run at public expense.

By these means, established craft towns along the Atlantic margin of the continent were put in touch with the sea, and the larger market towns and provincial capitals of the same region were able to satisfy their evergrowing wants without excessive charges for haulage. Thus the same seaboard nations which had had a favorable situation for building up colonial empires along with their colonial trade, were favored by nature with means of spreading the benefits of their economic and political expansion throughout their homelands.

Improvements in transportation were undertaken in Central Europe as well as in the seaboard countries, but they often lagged for want of money. 

Unless canals and roads passed through a number of countries they could not be used. It proved difficult or impossible to get the numerous small and mutually jealous political units to plan and construct a comprehensive route system, and even where funds were available, few canals or roads were built. 

First Steps 

Toward 

In the lucky coastal countries, there was room for further expansion of commerce, but it could come about only with reduction in the cost of export articles. Cheaper goods would widen the market among the numerous but very poor people of the overseas lands--especially Asia, Africa, and lowlatitude America. Cheaper goods might be produced if the cost of transportation could be further lowered, or if the cost of fabrication could be reduced. 

The Canal du Midi (opened in 1681) carried barges across south France from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic. This canal, equipped with the first great flight of locks, was an innovation of the first magnitude in inland navigation, and was soon followed by others in France, the Low Countries, and Great Britain. Eighty years later, a canal was first carried across a stream in an aqueduct, and this began the partial emancipation of navigation from local streams. Further major improvement of internal waterways had to await the application of power through machinery (e.g. steam towage, 1802). 

Roads were somewhat less costly to construct than canals. Nevertheless in humid, western Europe, where streams are numerous and rains are frequent, the combined expense of bridge and road building and of maintenance was high. A more serious handicap was high cost of operation, which limited

their use to passengers, mails, and the most valuable freight. Areas that could be tapped only by roads remained out of touch with the world until the advent of the "rail-road" (steam road, 1825), at the very end of the first phase of the Industrial Revolution. 

The most direct means of lowering the cost of fabricating goods, was to substitute machines for hand operations. Even the simplest tools are machines, but until the modern era few changes in tools had been made since classical times. Of the major commodities made for the export trade, textiles lent themselves most readily to mechanical fabrication. In a few instances, simple machines had long since replaced hand labor, and reduction in cost of production had been effected. For example, the loom operated by hands and feet was faster, and its output was more uniform than material woven on a single hand loom. Machines continued to be invented, but they soon outran the feeble physical power of human muscle, and so were worthless. 

Want of mechanical power was clearly the decisive bar to effective progress. 

THE INDUSTRIAL AND INTELLECTUAL REVOLUTION New Applications of Power 

In none of its many aspects did the Industrial Revolution make so conspicuous a mark on human society as by broadening the base and increasing the scope of mechanical power.

Europeans of the early eighteenth century were familiar with small jobs of harnessing fragments of the tremendous power residing in natural forces. Small brooks tumbling down hillsides or dropping over rock bars were dammed to divert a part of their flow to huge water wheels, which turned millstones for grinding grist or ran saws for making lumber. Along the deeply indented coasts of north Europe, where the ebb and flow of the tide

ranges between 15 and 30 feet, small tide mills were run by salt water. In districts lacking water power, people built wide-armed windmills on exposed hills everywhere, and on flat ground along the windy seacoast of North Europe. 

In most places these were grist, or saw-mills, but on the wet delta of the Rhine, and in lesser wet flats in other parts of rainy North Europe, windmills pumped surplus water off pastures and fields, and so added lands high in value and not inconsiderable in quantity to the continent's usable acreage.

Each of these small-scale applications of power suffered an inherent handicap. Low water forced mills to cease operations; spring freshets carried unwelcome sand and gravel into the mill pond or burst the dam. Tidemills could operate only while the tide was strongly ebbing (i.e. somewhat less than half the time), and often at inconvenient hours. Windmills stopped when the wind fell off, as it frequently and unpredictably does in European latitudes.

Besides these limitations, windmills and tidemills could not be enlarged to increase their individual output of power which was invariably small. In contrast, many large streams were broken by falls and rapids, and were capable of furnishing much power. Unluckily, such power sites were generally in mountainous or hilly country with sparse populations poor in resources and simple in wants.

Water Power 

Harnessing of the larger streams nevertheless marked one starting point of the Industrial Revolution. Such streams furnished concentrated blocks of power far larger than the brooks hitherto used. Their flow was reliable throughout the driest season, because they were fed by a large watershed.

In the

The streams of Northwest Europe were more readily usable at this stage of technology than those of other sections of the continent. 

The long, rainless summer of the Mediterranean Basin reduces even the large streams to a small fraction of their winter flow. Rivers fed by melting Alpine snowfields and glaciers are no steadier because they run full in summer, and diminish greatly during the snowbound winter. In Central Europe, cold winters regularly seal the streams with ice, and so reduce their flow; unpredictable, and not infrequent rains or sudden thaws cause floods. 

Northwest where snow and ice are moderate, summer evaporation at a minimum, and rainfall evenly distributed, the stream flow is reasonably even all the year round, with slight risk of violent stoppage or damage to waterworks. 

Within each of the three climatic regions, the streams most amenable to the inexperienced techniques of the middle eighteenth century flow through hill lands, rather than mountains. The great power inherent in the long leaps of torrents in glaciated mountains such as the Alps, Pyrenees, or Scandinavian ranges can be utilized only by expensive, large-scale engineering which lay beyond the capacity of the pioneers in industrialization. 

On this basis, the most favored areas for advances in waterpower exploitation were the hilly core of the Island of Britain, the Ardennes, with its associated hills of the lower Rhine-Scheldt country, and the northwest slopes of the Massif Central of France. Among these regions, France and 

Britain had the further advantage of being politically unified. Both had been leaders in overseas commerce and colonial expansion. This cultural superiority was due in part to their location on the margin of Europe, which brought their long coastal frontages close to the center of the land hemisphere.

For a long time France and Britain made approximately equal strides toward more effective utilization of waterpower from their larger streams.

Then Britain drew ahead.

Insular security permitted Britain uninterrupted experimentation with machinery and expansion of colonial business, whereas France was repeatedly distracted by military struggles on its landward frontier.

The harnessing of waterpower on a moderately large scale after centuries of waste, reflects the impact of two separate streams of influence: (1) the progress of science and invention dating from the Revival of Learning and tending toward increased mastery of environment; (2) the expansion of environment begun during the Discoveries and accentuated by commerce. The upshot was that the rapid development of overseas markets set inventors to devising means of supplying the growing demands by increasing the productivity of human labor through mechanical contrivances. Textile Manufacture

One of the commonest commodities in trade has always been cloth, and the textile trade was one of the first to respond to the new forces. 

Florence, the cities of Flanders, and ports the world over had prospered for centuries by cloth manufacture or by the cloth trade. The extent and antiquity of this commerce is suggested by the fact that some fifty towns have given their names to various kinds of cloth. To the long-established trade in woolens and linens fabricated in Europe, the Commercial Revolution had added silks from the Far East and cottons from India. Decreasing costs of transportation, made possible by the new ocean trade routes, lowered prices, and so increased the demand for all sorts of textiles. Invention of machines to spin yarn and weave fabrics, promised still further reduction in costs of production, but power to run these machines was not available on the lowlands where most of Northwest Europe's population lived, though it could be readily developed in the hills.

Shift of Population 

Sylvan streams throughout Northwest Europe saw the rapid establishment of chains of towns on their banks, wherever there were suitable sites for dams. Small factories soon absorbed the small labor supply available from sparse local populations, and began to draw upon more remote districts as families migrated to distant towns, attracted by year-round jobs provided by perennial waterpower. This development accentuated one of the most striking aspects of the Industrial Revolution, the rearrangement of the pattern of population, marked by: (1) a pronounced, perhaps many fold, increase of population; (2) a shift from rural to preponderantly urban character. Towns grew up where none previously existed, old centers languished as traditional market business was absorbed by nearby manufacturing cities. 

Coal and Steam

The manufacture of textiles saw the earliest (c. 1770) of many major applications of large-scale mechanical power. This entailed the fabrication of new machines, and, during succeeding decades, led to an incredible multiplication of factory made goods of every sort. 

Some of the less bulky raw materials followed cotton, woolen and the other fibers to the water-power sites. Bulky or heavy materials did not repay the cost of transportation. For them, and subsequently for all sorts of factory output, a novel source of power was made available. water in the form of its vapor, steam.

Steam power is more mobile. It can be generated by burning any combustible. Wood was the most obvious fuel in Northwest Europe, but it was already growing scarce, with increasing population, and consumption for heat, cooking, and smelting iron. The voracious boiler of a stationary steam engine will soon deplete the woodland within hauling radius, and necessity rather than chance dictated that the first successful steam engine should be fired by coal.

The coal was drawn from the very mine it kept dry, and owing to the high concentration of heat in coal, the steam engine consumed only a tiny fraction of the coal mined. Consequently, with improvement in steam engines, all sorts of machines could be run, and factories could develop over coal fields as readily as beside waterpower. It has repeatedly been proved that a small area underlain by thick coal seams can support a steam-powered industrial development many times more intensive than that of an ordinary waterpower site, and without threat of the imminent exhaustion of fuel. 

Where coal lay close to the seacoast or on a navigable stream, it could be cheaply transported to other districts, and so added to the mobility of power produced from it. The proximity of a few coal fields to navigable water made them centers of export. Most of the coal sent out was used in steamships, rather than in factories. More commonly, the fields producing coal especially suitable for making steam, became the sites of mill towns. 

Factories on the coal fields vied with factories on waterpower sites in the race for preeminence in machine production.

With the rise of coal as a motive power, Britain further outdistanced France, because France had very little coal, and all but a tiny fraction of

it lay along the Belgian border, where wars raged during the formative period of the Industrial Revolution. In peaceful Britain, coal was abundant, of high grade, and widely distributed. 

Coal stepped the dawning machine age up to a new level of production, and today, after a century and a half, and despite technical advances, it still remains by all odds the chief source of power. No combination of power sources at present available can compete with coal. Despite this preponderant position as a source of power, it is another use of coal that has turned out to be its greatest contribution to the modern industrial world. New applications of power lowered prices and transformed rural landscapes into cities, but it was new uses for minerals and new combinations of minerals that completed the foundations of a new cultural epoch, the age of iron and steel. 

New Uses of Minerals

Minerals constitute one of the five great groups of natural resources.

Some of them have always been used by human societies, even the most primitive. The mineral dominantly employed for tools and weapons by any society commonly gives its name to its civilization. 

The "stone age, the "bronze age," the "steel age" are familiar terms that bespeak the position of minerals and implements at the core of human progress throughout history.

On this basis, the later decades of the modern era may properly be called the "multi-mineral age," for during the last two centuries, mankind has learned to use tools, machines, and weapons on a scale beyond the imagination of earlier ages. In geographic terms this development means discovery of hundreds of new uses for old minerals, utilization of new minerals hitherto regarded as valuel ss or unknown, and an acceleration in the shift of population. Minerals were the earliest attraction which drew

people into unsettled districts as new uses and new values sent prospectors and miners all over the earth. Many finds were followed by the mushroom growth of mining camps, sometimes by permanent changes in the population. 

The Marriage of Iron with Coal 

The industrial age did not spring full-fledged into being, but grew slowly and painfully out of the searching minds and patient experiments of many men.

At the outset no new minerals were brought into use, and only a few of the well-known ones were given new applications. Yet these changes were so basic that they revolutionized economic society. The leaders in this development were iron and coal. 

Iron had already given humankind a boost some two thousand years earlier, when men learned to substitute iron tools and weapons for bronze or stone implements. Steel had been widely used for swords, cutlery, and numerous other items for many hundred years. Yet, until the latter part of the eighteenth century, the world output of both iron and steel remained small.

During all this time the forges were tiny and scattered. The simple metallurgy of those days could cope only with ores containing few or no refractory substances. Primitive mining was confined to the deposits that happened to lie close to the surface. Many such deposits exist, but they are small. The only known fuel for smelting the ore was charcoal, made by slow combustion of wood. To be assured a long life, an iron furnace had to stand in a heavily wooded district, and keep its output small. Otherwise it would before long exhaust the fuel supply within the profitable radius for hauling charcoal, a light but bulky commodity. Iron and steel goods were therefore made in small furnaces, scattered over districts where wood and ore were plentiful.

The Mediterranean world, famous from Damascus to Toledo for fine steel, was hopelessly at a disadvantage in the matter of large-scale output. 

Its climate produced only scrub woodland, and centuries of cutting had long since reduced available wood to patches of brush in the remote mountains. 

Dense forests still covered much ground in Central and Eastern Europe, but these parts had hardly begun to feel the urge to develop manufacturing beyond crude and simple handicraft. Northwest Europe was the true home of iron and steel production.

By 1700 wood for charcoal smelting was already beginning to run short in Northwest Europe, and some ironmasters experimented with coal. Most of these innovators lived in Great Britain, where there were numerous deposits of coal. 

By charring coal, coke is produced, and this could be used in place of charcoal for smelting. Coal from same fields made a type of coke that proved unsatisfactory in the smelter; at other mines coke could be directly substituted for charcoal, with excellent results. At first no notable advance in technique was made, but before long, the substitution of coal as the smelting fuel completely metamorphosed both the geographic and the economic status of iron and steel production. 

The Geographic Pattern of Iron and Steel Making 

At once it became possible to increase the scale of production because a few square miles underlain by coking coal promised to support a large furnace for decades, perhaps centuries, Steam pumps, run by coal, permitted deep mining of both coal and iron, and further extended the life of mines. Still, only a few coal fields could be used at all, however suitable their product for coking, because iron ore, heavy and bulky, could

be economically transported only where navigable water was available to bear the burden. About Birmingham, England, coking coal and easily worked iron were found together. Here and there coal and iron could be mined on the coast or a navigable stream. Such favored districts had initially a monopoly of large-scale iron and steel production. 

The new and underdeveloped continents lacked both markets and labor, and this disadvantage postponed their industrialization, even in the rare districts possessing both ore and coking coal, such as Birmingham, Alabama. 

Asia had not benefited from the stimuli which were stirring inventive minds in Europe, and made no advances in the industrial arts. Europe alone was intellectually and economically prepared to experiment. All parts of the continent west of Russia and Turkey stood at about the same stage of technology. Hence the districts where the two raw materials were found together, or could be cheaply brought together, were able to take the lead over their rivals.

A glance at a map of iron ore and coking coal will show that the island of Britain was the only part of Europe in which the two minerals lay side by side, or within a short and cheap haul by water, involving no crossing of political boundaries. It is not beside the point to recall that 

England's position at the focus of the major ocean routes and her insular security from foreign wars, had already enabled her to take the lead in the Commercial Revolution, and in the concomitant acquisition of overseas colonies. She also possessed the most favored district for factories based on waterpower, and was beginning to feel the need for cheap production of textile machinery. It would have been surprising indeed, if England, thus prepared for economic progress, had not taken the cue from nature and begun to pioneer in the smelting of iron ore with coked coal. 

Subsequently, ore and coal, somewhat separated, but lying adjacent

to different parts of a river system, or on the ocean, were brought together by water transport. Steel districts have since grown up on coal fields lacking ore, on iron ore bodies lacking coal, and on intervening points where transportation routes make it easy for essential commodities to move toward each other.

The use of water and coal for power, and of large supplies of cheap iron and steel, was the foundation on which the Industrial Revolution was built. From iron and steel were fabricated: (1) machines that made possible the factory system; (2) railroads and ships that reduced the time and the cost of transportation to a fraction of their former levels, and so permitted world-wide marketing of the products of the European factories; (3) agricultural machinery that took the place of labor in the new continents and produced a steady outflow of raw materials for European factories and of food for European laborers.

This sequence of events was a complicated one, involving new applications of power, invention, large-scale output of many raw materials, old and new, amplification of the Agricultural Revolution at home, its extension into the new continents, and an intricate interaction of all these elements upon each other and upon social and political life. 

Supplementary 

Minerals

The fundamental character of coal and iron should blind no one to the fact that other minerals have played smaller but often decisive parts from the outset of the Industrial Revolution. With every advance in the art of metallurgy, and with successive discoveries of new uses for minerals, the base of industrialized society of the European type has broadened. 

Within Europe, countries and regions possessing these lesser minerals have entered into the fellowship of industrial society. It should be

emphasized, however, that the drawing power of coal, especially in conjunction with iron ore, has sucked most of the secondary minerals from their scattered mines into the coal fields, or their vicinity. The same tendency has drawn minerals to Europe from the other continents. This has been most notable where Europe is poor in a key resource, such as tin. 

Europe and the World at Large

With every discovery of mineral wealth, both within and without Europe, the economic balance between that continent and its potential rivals has trembled. So far, only two non-European areas have been able to set themselves up in industrial housekeeping. Both lie in the humid middle latitudes of the northern hemisphere, both possess coal and iron. Of the two, the Northeastern United States and adjacent Canada constitute Europe's only potential rival. Japan, even with the resources of the Asiatic mainland, is a minor third in the industrial triad. Moreover, Japan has entered the field of competition with Europe only during the present generation. 

The United States, by contrast, began to take active part in the Industrial Revolution early in the nineteenth century, and during almost the entire period since the beginning of the Industrial Revolution, the political autonomy of North America has been increasingly buttressed by its unfolding wealth of minerals.

Any serious threat to European world-supremacy lay beyond the horizon of the mid-eighteenth century. At that time, thanks to its early start, fortunate location, and varied resources, the Continent of Europe as a whole, the western seaboard states in particular, and Britain above all, were on the threshold of the most prosperous era of their history. No menace to this prosperity and this supremacy became clear until the twentieth century had dawned.
