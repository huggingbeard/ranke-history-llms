The prospects for a substantial increase in exports of American merchandise to the five counries of Middle Africa, visited by the Trade Mission, are limited for the present. However, these narkets should not be overlooked and trade with hem can be developed, even now, on a long range pasis. American goods are highly regarded in hese countries and there is room for more U.S. products. Of course the criticism of lack of sales effort, lack of proper attention to the requirenents of the respective markets, and especially, ack of literature and instructions in French preails here as in many other markets.

The imports of American products into these reas amount to approximately 6% of the total mports and a sizable proportion of the value is ccounted for by petroleum products. The Mission as carefully analyzed a number of factors which Imit U.S. trade opportunities in the Middle Afican countries.

Although these five new emerging Republics re politically independent, economically they still ave very close ties with France. Because of the 'rench language, culture, and education and long nd well established French trading companies, lese areas are oriented principally toward France. nports of French origin goods approximate 60% f the total with the balance shared by West ermany, other Common Market Countries, United ingdom and the United States. The French 1 introduction principally, and the British trading companies handle about 90% of all imports, including imports of American goods. There are very few independent African firms or firms of other nationalities who are able to import substantially. The 

French trading companies, for instance, are branches of French concerns, managed in Paris where buying is done for overseas outlets.

France supports the principal export products of these countries with a guaranteed market, usually at prices well above world prices. France also provides substantial financial and technical assistance, including budget support and capital development. In exchange, France enjoys trade free of restrictions with these areas. 

Negotiations to join the five countries into a 

Customs Union are almost complete. Of more importance, however, because of its probable impact on U.S. exports to these countries, will be their association with the Common Market, which will result in a tariff discrimination against American products.

The great distances to major seaports, coupled with an extremely poor road system, the low purchasing power of more than 90% of the population, and the low density of the population-about 8% live in cities-present difficult marketing problems.

Another important factor restricting U.S. trade opportunities is the limited allocation of foreign exchange for American goods.

The plus factors which should encourage American businessmen to develop trade with the five Middle African countries on a long range basis are many.

They are all anxious to achieve their economic independence and to diversify their present trading pattern.

All five countries have or will soon have an economic development plan. Under this plan agriculture will be diversified, methods of production. improved, the cooperative movement expanded, thereby increasing agricultural output. The plan also calls for industrialization on a selective basis. 

This desire for progress and change will result in an ever-rising standard of living, creating an increasing demand for products and opportunities for U.S. suppliers of goods and services. 

The U.S. aid program, under negotiation for four countries, may make possible some increases in purchases of American products. In Cameroon, where in 1961 direct American aid amounted to $1.8 million, $1.4 million was allocated to finance commercial imports, thus making it easier for local businessmen to obtain licenses for imports from the United States.

There is a strong trend in all countries to "Africanize" commerce. This move will open new channels of distribution, who will seek new sources of supply. Cooperatives of small native business firms are being organized to facilitate importations. Financial assistance is given these firms in the form of credit from the Development Bank, especially in the Cameroon.

There is a definite interest in American goods and a desire on the part of African trading firms to obtain them. The interest of the American businessmen to supply these markets could eventually influence the governments to ease the restrictions which now limit American imports. 

CONSUMER GOODS 

In evaluating the markets of the five countries visited by the Mission, one must divide the population of slightly under 10 million into two markets: (1) a so-called "European Market" for the small

European population and the city-dwelling 

Africans, and (2) a "Mass Market" for more than 90% of the

Africans whose purchasing powers are extremely limited.

On the "European Market" as in any average size American town, one will find packaged foodstuffs, including frozen meats and vegatables, clothing, automobiles, electric appliances and air conditioners, soaps and toiletries, and all the necessities and luxuries of modern civilization. 

The large trading companies operate, in the principal cities, general stores, and in many instances self-service. There are also small specialized independent stores such as baker shops, butcher shops, men's stores, ladies' stores, pharmacies, and so forth. Because the bulk of the consumer goods is imported from distant areas and frequently by air, the prices are exorbitant for the area. 

On the African market or the "Mass Market" one will find, above all, textiles of many varieties, especially cotton prints, canned goods, used clothing, lanterns, flashlights, plastic footwear, cheap handtools, inexpensive toiletries, and in primitive market places, livestock. INDUSTRY 

Industry in these undeveloped Middle African Countries is extremely limited, and, in many instances, primitive. It centers mostly on the processing of agricultural and forest products. The 

Mission visited cotton gins, small cotton seed oil press and soap factories, palm oil factories, cigarette factories, small metal fabricators, bicycle assembling plant, sawmills, breweries, cocoa butter factory, and other small manufacturing plants. Practically all were using primitive processes and old machinery. However, in several countries there was at least one modern installation. Chad, for instance, has a modern slaughterhouse in Fort Lamy; Cameroon has an extremely modern aluminum plant in Edea which turns out. annually 45,000 metric tons of aluminum ingots; 

Congo has an efficient sugar refinery which supplies some of the Equatorial African Countries; and Gabon has one of the largest plywood factories in the world. at Port Gentil and is a major exporter on the world plywood market. In 1961,

Congo's production of crude oil actained 102,900 metric tons; Gabon's oil production in 1960 approximated 800,000 metric tons. Gabon has rich mineral deposits of manganese ore, iron ore, and uranium.

The industrialization plans of the Cameroon, Congo, and Gabon are probably more advanced and call for small industries on a selective basis, realizing the lack of capital, small income, and qualified technicians. Cameroon's Five-Year De velopment Plan calls for the installation of several new industries, including a cement plant at Douala; Congo is planning small and medium sized industries in the area of Pointe Noire, which will include fishing and fish processing industries, brewing plant, food industries, lumber industries, such as plywood and match factories, clothing, plastic, metal fabrications and bicycle assembly plant; in Gabon, studies are under way for the introduction of new industries such as the manufacturing of cellulose and packing materials, petroleum refining and a petrochemical industry, cement works and food industries.

Located in Central Africa, between 8 and 23 degrees north latitude, near the Equator, Chad covers an area of about 496,000 square miles or roughly three times the size of California. It is a predominantly agricultural country, with livestock raising in the central regions and some fishing along the main rivers of Chari and Logone. 

The principal cities include Fort Lamy, the capital, with a population of approximately 93,000, followed by Moundou (25,000) and Fort Archambault (18,000).

Chad's population is estimated at 2.8 million, with very few non-Africans. The density is only 

6 persons per square mile. The population may be divided into fifteen different groups, of which twelve important groups are basically Moslem (45% of the population) and three pagan and Christian (accounting for 55% of the population). In 

1960/61 the school enrollment was 76,839.

Chad's economic growth and future development face considerable difficulties in view of the inaccessibility of its vast, landlocked territory, extreme transportation difficulties and the great distance to major sea ports. In addition, the climate is not suitable for the cultivation of tropical crops such as cocoa or coffee, which could be easily exported. Because of these limitations the country's exports (which in 1961 amounted to $21.6 million) consist principally of cotton fiber (80%), meat, cattle on the hoof, reptile skins and hides and dried fish in descending order of importance. Chad's most important imports ($25.5 million in 1961) are tea, sugar and flour. Taken as a group with tobacco, wine and beer imports they represent 24% of all imports. Petroleum products, vehicles, cotton textiles, wood, cement and iron and steel products of all types are also important imports. Over one-half of all imported products ($13.5 million) came from France; in 

1961 only an estimated $980,000 was imported from the United States, or 3.8 percent of total 

Chadian imports. Chadian industry, which is extremely limited, includes 24 cotton gins in the south of the country, one small cotton seed oil press and soap factory, one peanut oil factory, 3 small metal fabricators, one bottled water factory and two brick kilns. 

There is a modern abattoir in Fort Lamy and a smaller one in Fort Archambault.

Although Chad has nearly 18,600 miles of roads, of which about 500 miles are all-weather, because of heavy rains and floods most of these roads are closed to heavy traffic during 3 to 6 months of the year. The same uncertainties apply to waterways, with the Chari River navigable only during the three or four month rainy season. Since the country has no railroad, air traffic represents an indispensable link with foreign countries and many consumer products are imported by air freight. In view of the uncertainties both of the road and river traffic, the Government is interested in an extension of the Transcameroonian Railroad to Fort Archambault which would connect the Chad with the Atlantic Ocean. This project is considered to have more economic justification than the Bangui-Chad railroad project which has been dropped.

GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS 

Chad is a vast and undeveloped country, with 96 percent of the population engaged in subsistence agriculture, stockraising and river fishing. Sorghum, millet, paddy rice and wheat are grown as food crops.

Production of cotton is the most important economic asset (just over 100,000 tons in 1960/1961). In the central part of the country and in the north there are estimated to be at least 

4 million head of cattle and 4.5 million sheep and goats. Cattle are exported on the hoof, largely to neighboring Nigeria, while meat exports, although increasing, are limited by the high cost of shipment by air, made necessary by the great distances and primitive overland transportation. Exports of reptile skins are increasing. 

Chad's only mineral currently exploited is natron, a carbonate and bicarbonate of soda, which is found in the shallow pits around Lake Chad. It is used locally for human and animal consumption and small quantities are also exported.

The following is a list of principal American products currently imported into Chad: gasoline, trucks under ten metric tons, lubricating oils and greases, diesel oil, lamp kerosene, passenger vehicles, cast iron manufactures, air conditioners, refrigerators and freezers, nuts and bolts, and second hand and reconditioned clothing. These eleven exports totaled $692,000 or 71% of all U.S. exports to Chad in 1961. Asphalt is a traditional U.S. export to Chad but fluctuates with street paving projects in Fort Lamy. CONDITIONS OF TRADE 

For the present Chad is a very limited market for American goods. Imports from countries other than France and members of the Equatorial Customs Union are subject to import quotas which tend to limit them. The global quotas for all countries except the European Economic Community, are subdivided among leading importers on the basis of their imports during previous years. 

Chad is a member of the Franc Zone, with strong economic ties with France. Its Government desires to diversify its trade and develop wider trade relations with a number of Western countries. However, its own financial resources are very limited and it continues to require substantial external aid, provided primarily by France through FAC (Fonds d'Aide et de Coopération) and by the Common Market, Funds extended by France are normally used to procure French products. 

Development of tourism is receiving considerable and serious attention in official Chadian circles. The chief tourist resources which could be exploited are areas with game and wild life, and scenic attractions. An effort is being made to attract foreign capital to this potentially promising sector. In connection with foreign investments in general, Chadian Government officials stated that there are plans to enact an investment code which would offer potential foreign investors adequate guarantees for the repatriation of capital and profits.

Cameroon 

The Federal Republic of Cameroon was formed in October 1961 by the union of the Republic of Cameroon (which became independent in January 

1960) and the United Nations Trust Territory of

Southern Cameroon. The country, located on the 

Gulf of Guinea in the heart of tropical Africa, occupies an area of 175,300 square miles or slightly larger than California. The most important cities include the capital Yaounde, with a population estimated at about 80,000 people including 2,000 Europeans; Douala, the country's largest city and principal port with an estimated population of 

150,000 and 5,000 Europeans; Garoua, with about 

20,000 people; Edea; and Buea.

Cameroon's population, according to 1961 estimates, is 4.1 million, with about 16,000 non-Africans. Located in the racial crossroad of Africa,

Cameroon has a complex of some 140 ethnic groups, including in the south, the Bantų, mainly farmers, and frequently Christian; in the north the aboriginal pagan Kirdis and Moslem stock breeders such as the Foulbes; in the west the industrious Bamilekes; and in the east some 

6,500 pygmies.

The country is predominantly agricultural, with very little industrialization and with an average per capita income of about $90 per year. However, the income is very unevenly distributed. 

In 1961 school enrollment reached 382,000. 

The economy of Cameroon is based almost entirely on agriculture, with cattle raising around Garoua. Athough subsistence farming remains predominant, there has been a considerable increase in the output of exportable crops in recent years. Cassava, millet, and palm oil are the principal crops produced almost exclusively for local consumption. While peanuts and bananas (116,000 metric tons) are also produced for export, the country's major export crops consist of cocoa (about 76,000 m.t. a year) and coffee (1960/ 

1961 production 45,000 m.t.), which together account for about 65 percent of Cameroon's exports. 

Wood, rubber, cotton and livestock are also exported in significant quantities. Production of crops is increasing due to the improvement of the transportation system and the opening up of new areas for marketing. Unlike the traditional money crop of cocoa which is still largely in the hands of Africans who organize production as a family enterprise, the export crops such as coffee and cotton are largely organized on a plantation basis. Like most tropical countries Cameroon's exports are concentrated on a few agricultural prod ucts, several of which are now in world surplus. 

Cameroon has special arrangements with France and the United Kingdom, which provide guaranteed outlets at satisfactory prices for a large part of exportable production. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS 

Income and purchasing power in the Cameroon are dependent on the sale of agricultural products. 

Most of the people live by farming, essentially subsistence, and herding. Principal crops include cassava, millet, sorghum, corn, peanuts, cocoa, coffee and rubber. The livestock population is estimated at 1.25 million cattle, 1.5 million sheep and goats, also pigs, horses, and donkeys. Forestry offers great possibilities. Currently forest exploitation is far below its potential, due to the high cost of labor and transportation difficulties. 

Cameroon has modest deposits of gold, cassiterite (1960 production: 99,250 m.t.), and bauxite. 

Iron ore deposits in the Kribi coastal area are estimated at 100 million tons.

Import restrictions placed on the dollar area, the United States in particular, have adversely affected a number of American products such as earthmoving and refrigerating equipment, automotive equipment, etc., of which the U.S. had enjoyed a competitive advantage. While these American exports suffered somewhat, there has been a growing interest in other products where recent improvements have given the United States a technological advantage. Material handling equip ment, small motors and compressors are ex- . amples of successful American sales. It is becoming more and more evident that to maintain successful sales on the local market American firms supplying electrical or mechanical equipment must have on hand a supply of spare parts and offer on-the-spot servicing.

SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS AFFECTING MARKET 

While the licensing system plays an important role in channelling imports from various sources other factors such as special market agreements and foreign aid also influence the level of imports. 

As indicated above, under Cameroon's special marketing arrangements with France, coffee and bananas are purchased by France at prices substantially above world price levels, thus contributing to Cameroon's purchasing power. Partly as a result of these advantages France is able to supply more than 60 percent of Cameroon's imports.

Quantitatively even more important are various French aid programs, which include budget support, capital development and technical assistance, all of which amount to approximately $50 million a year. Benefits are now beginning to flow to the Cameroonian economy from the European Economic Community which has approved expenditures of $33 million for developmental projects. In addition, the United States, France and the EEC have agreed, subject to certain considerations, to provide $33 million to finance the first section of the 

Transcameroonian Railway extension. The Amer ican share of this contribution will be between $8 and $10 million.

In 1961 direct American aid to Cameroon amounted to $1.8 million of which $1.4 million was allocated to finance commercial imports, thus making it easier for local businessmen to obtain licenses for imports from the United States. While it is anticipated that Cameroon will maintain a rather tight control on imports from outside the franc zone, the U.S. aid program may make possible some increases in purchases of American products. 

There are also anticipated tariff developments to be considered. Cameroon is interested in an association with the Common Market, which would grant preferential treatment for agricultural tropical products in exchange for which Cameroon is ready to grant tariff reductions to Common Market goods. In addition, negotiations are under way for the creation of a customs union between the five Middle African countries of Chad, Cameroon, Cen tral African Republic, Congo (Brazzaville) and

Congo (Brazzaville) The Republic of Congo (Brazzaville), southern most of the four territories that once comprised 

French Equatorial Africa, has an area of 131,538 square miles, comparable in size to Iowa and Nebraska. The country is almost evenly divided the Equator and has a coastline of about a hundred miles on the Atlantic, with a good harbor at Pointe

The Congolese population, entirely Bantu, is estimated at about 800,000, unevenly distributed between the more densely populated south and the sparsely populated north with its large areas of virtually uninhabited forest and swamp. There are over 50 tribes, which are generally grouped into four ethnic groups: the Kongo, the M'Bochi, the Bateke and the M'Vili. A little over 10 percent of the adult population is literate and there are 133,145 students enrolled in schools.

The Congo has few natural resources and generally infertile soil. The country's agriculture is still in the primitive stage and almost entirely oriented toward the production of foodstuffs for local consumption. Manioc, in particular, but also corn, potatoes, carrots, tomatoes, green peppers, bananas, mangoes, pineapples, oranges are grown by the Africans in small patches for barter among themselves and for family consumption. These crops actually contribute more to the national income than cash crops produced for the limited urban market or for small exports. By and large the organized production of cash crops is still in the most preliminary stages of development. Among the most important cash and export crops are palm kernels (January-September 1961 production 

4,617 metric tons), peanuts (1,444 tons), cocoa beans (497 tons), bananas (659 tons), coffee beans (267 tons), palm oil (2,841 tons), peanut oil (425 tons) and rubber (74 tons). 

Stockraising is almost unknown to the local population. In recent years efforts have been made to overcome some of the tremendous obstacles such as the inhospitable climate, unfavorable forage possibilities, endemic infections (tse-tse fly, trypanosomes, etc.) and the lack of know-how on the part of the population. Currently the cattle herds consist of 22,400 head, and there are about 39,000 pigs and 75,600 chickens. However, most of the meat consumed in the country is imported at high cost from Chad, Europe, or South Africa. The Congo's most important natural resource is its forest wealth, which has been barely tapped. 

Forests cover roughly half the area of the country and account for more than two-thirds the value of export earnings. CONDITIONS OF TRADE 

The Republic of Congo is a member of the franc zone, with the trade free of restrictions for franc zone countries. All other trade, however, is strictly controlled through the import and export licensing system. The allocation of foreign exchange is made through the Exchange Stabilization Fund in Paris, but these allocations are not directly tied to the amount earned by each member. They are established on the basis of negotiations by representatives of the member states. 

In the process of allocation of foreign exchange to Congolese importers the local Chamber of Commerce plays an important role, as it ranks all business establishments on the basis of their commercial activity and their importance to the country's economy. It is on the basis of this rankorder that the Ministry of Economic Affairs makes allotments of foreign exchange. The division of foreign exchange is usually based upon the importer's purchases the previous year, with some preferences given to importers of capital equipment over consumption goods. United States trade opportunities in the Congo (Brazzaville) are restricted by a number of factors including the difficulty of obtaining foreign exchange for American goods. In addition, the very low level of per capita income, the absence of industrial development and the low density of the population tend to restrict trade expansion. 

Most of the large business enterprises operating in the Congo are branches of French concerns, managed in Paris where buying is done for overseas outlets. Some less important handicaps lie in the differences in language and systems of measurement, which would require American business to accommodate its operating practices to local conditions.

MISSION'S OBJECTIVES 

Through its numerous contacts with government officials, consultations with local businessmen, general meetings at chambers of commerce and extensive use of publicity media (newspapers and particularly radio) the Mission has effectively aroused interest in American products, created a friendly atmosphere of better understanding, emphasized the need for these countries to diversify their sources of supply and provided extensive in formation on the U.S. Market. The Mission made efforts to obtain as much information as possible regarding Government economic development plans in the countries visited, investment codes, the anticipated economic growth and the short and long-term trade potential of particular interest to the American industry. The Mission stressed the desire of the American people to participate in the development of these countries and to contribute to the well-being of their people by raising the standard of living. In addition, commercial serv ices of our Embassies, through Mission's activ ities, have further strengthened and extended their relations with the local business communities. 

The Mission laid good foundations for the possible future development of trade relations be tween the United States and countries of Middle 

Africa. While this process may take time, the basis of better understanding through many direc personal contacts, carried out in the most friendly manner undoubtedly represents a positive step No less significant have been the Mission's ac complishments in the public relations field, where associations formed by members of the Mission the pleasant impression left by them on all wit whom they came into contact, represents a new and real advantage to the United States not only in commercial and business circles but among the public at large. The Mission's performance wa an effective and positive projection of an America presence. 20

Planning for the Mission's visit to the five Middle African countries was done jointly by the De partment of Commerce and the respective Em bassies in these countries. In arranging the programs particular emphasis was placed on the fol lowing objectives: 

(1) To meet with Presidents of Republics an other high Government officials in each countr and discuss economic conditions, government plan ning in the agricultural and industrial sectors, at titude toward private foreign investment and pos
