THE PLACE OF PUBLIC OPINION POLLS IN A DEMOCRACY GEORGE GALLUP Director of the American Institute of Public Opinion 

"PUBLIC OPINION," wrote the eminent French political philosopher, Montaigne, "is a powerful, bold, and unmeasurable party.'

Despite Montaigne's caustic judgment, the need for devising ways of making the mass of people articulate on public issues has presented a continuous challenge to every student of the democratic process. The observation of James Bryce, the English observer of the "American Commonwealth" and its institutions, penetrated beyond Montaigne's categorical conclusion. Bryce agreed that "the obvious weakness of government by public opinion is the difficulty of ascertaining it". But at least it was a forward step to suggest that the measurement of public opinion had advanced from being impossible, to being, as Bryce declared, merely difficult.

Bryce realized that public opinion could be roughly gauged through its organs and agencies of expression-through the vote at elections, the newspapers, and through associations, political parties, and pressure-groups. But none of these indices was sufficiently direct for his critical taste. He was well aware that elections often confused men and issues, and that political and social conflicts needing decision occurred at intervals not pre-arranged by the electoral clauses of the American Constitution. He knew too that newspapers acted not only as "weathercocks", but also as "advocates" in interpreting public opinion. And finally, he saw the dangers of identifying the noisy claims of small pressuregroups with a truly "public opinion". Perhaps he had heard the story of the three tailors of Tooley Street, who had written to His Majesty, the King, signing themselves, "We, the People of England".

Bryce was aware that these organs and channels often distorted public opinion in the process of expressing it. In search of a better index, he was deeply impressed by the possibilities of the Swiss referendum, as a direct method of reaching people and obtaining their views.

"The referendum, or plan, of submitting a specific question to the popular vote, is the logical resource," Bryce wrote, “but it is troublesome and costly to take the votes of millions of people over an area so large as that of one of the greater states; much more then is the method difficult to apply to Federal matters." (¹) 

Thus in order to reach what he believed to be the next stage of democracy "in which the will of the majority of citizens could be known at all times", Bryce looked forward to the emergence of some future method of ascertaining public opinion—a method which would have two main characteristics: 

1. That it go directly to the people themselves. 2. 

That it be based on some unit or segment of the population smaller than the entire electorate. 

With the emergence of the sampling referendum in our own day, the solution to the problem of measuring public opinionthe problem which Montaigne had thought insoluble, and Bryce thought difficult, appears to be in sight. 

Of all contemporary methods for assessing public opinion, the modern sampling referendum has perhaps aroused greatest attention on the part of students of politics. In the short space of four years, a considerable literature of description, analysis, and criticism has sprung up. 

I: Criticism which comes from individuals or groups who dislike the particular results which a given poll indicates because it fails to agree with their own political interests. 

At various intervals, Republicans have charged that polls were supporting the Democrats, and Democrats have suggested that polls were in the pay of the Republicans. Socialists have hinted that the polls are "reactionary", while extreme Conservatives have accused the polls of being too democratic. When the attack comes intermittently from each of the political groups in turn, and when polls are alternately praised and blamed by rival partisans, it may fairly be observed that such groups are less interested in the poll method, than in the concurrence of particular results with their own political predilections.

Reform groups, political parties, and all who are trying to mould and influence public opinion, have seemed at times to proceed on the assumption that the polls should do their propaganda for them. When a particular result is described, contradicting their claims and slogans, they have, quite naturally perhaps, tended to exhibit annoyance. The Communist party organ, (1) Bryce, James: "The American Commonwealth”, p. 346.

the Daily Worker, inveighed against the polls when recent figures indicated that a slight majority of American voters-some 52%believe that the policy of the American Communist party is determined after close consultation with Soviet Russia. But who is to blame for the existence of this belief or stereotype in the American public mind-the public opinion polls, or the Communist party? Is it not better for any minority political group to realize that certain beliefs and prejudices exist, which, if the movement is to succeed, must be moulded and modified? The polls showed here that fully 39% of the voting public was undecided or without opinions on this question-another indication that the educational program of the American Communist party has not been carried far enough. What is essential to realize is that the public opinion polls aim at providing measurements of the effect of propaganda. They try to chart public attitudes and reactions. 

They have no other purpose. Criticism of the polls on the basis of specific results often reveals more about the critics than it does about the polls themselves. 

II. A second type of criticism is constructive and valuable. 

It comes from individuals and groups who assume the desirability of measuring public opinion, but point to possible weaknesses and defects in present methods:

Analysis of points of method, and suggestions for improving the conduct of modern surveys, have centered on various problems, ranging from the conditions of sponsorship, to problems of question-wording, the nature and size of the sample, the character of the opinions obtained through the use of questionnaire and interview. Here it is true to say that outside criticism, coupled with the continuous type of experimental research which marks modern survey organizations, has led the way to steady improvements in poll technique, and to a constant increase in the accuracy of the measurements. Studenski's recent article on the importance of careful wording,(¹) underlines the care taken by the Institute in pre-testing and comparing alternate wordings by the use of the "split-ballot" technique. Such recent notes as those of Rogers of 

Columbia (2) on the problem of the intensity of opinions furnish the problems which meet with constant experiment and testing in the survey organizations themselves. Studies of the "No 

Opinion" group, of the theory of the "bandwagon" vote, the constant use of primary sources to check on the accuracy of the cross-

(2) Rogers, Lindsay: "Dr. Gallup's Statistics"-The New Republic, No vember, 1939. 582868

section, the modern emphasis on the importance of "comment analysis" as a way of discovering the background of the opinions of different groups in the general public-such methodological studies are the earmarks of a young and developing science. Above all, the constant efforts to check sampling methods and poll technique in the realistic situations provided by elections and state referenda, have resulted in a steady increase in the competence of this new technique to measure the divisions and the changes in public opinion. In a very real sense, the early mistakes and pitfalls have provided the stimulus to criticism and close study. 

The results of this critical study may be observed in the increased accuracy and reliability of modern sampling surveys. 

In this paper today, I should like to examine some of the arguments and some of the consequences of the polls from a third point of view:

III. From the point of view of the analysis and criticism of public opinion polls which comes from political theorists and students of the democratic process who view such research as menacing democracy, and as destructive of the representative system of government: In the modern day, it is above all the implications of the survey for democratic government which are the focus of attention and discussion.

Immediately after the 1936 campaign, when modern public opinion surveys first tested their methods against an actual election vote on a national scale it was argued that pre-election surveys were dangerous to existing democratic procedures. Congressman Pierce of Oregon has for many years argued in the House of Representatives that pre-election polls are dangerous on two main grounds: 

1. That they tend to destroy the democratic process by reducing the number of people who vote. Winning voters, he urged, would feel that they were unnecessary for victory, while voters on the losing side would remain away from the polls because they thought it useless to go, since their candidate appeared doomed to hopeless defeat. 

2. In the second place, Pierce has argued that polls tend to handicap the losing side because, in his opinion the publication of pre-election standings tends to swing voters to the "bandwagon", and encourages them to cast their vote for the winning candidate. In the present day, Senator Robert Taft of Ohio has criticized recent polls which indicate the standing of various Republican candidates

for the nomination. The basis of Senator Taft's condemnation bears a general similarity to the charges made by 

Congressman Pierce.

To these arguments, certain facts must be opposed. In the first place, a careful examination of all the evidence reveals no evidence that polls decrease the popular vote. On the contrary, since 1933, the period when election polls have risen to national prominence, the popular vote has shown a steady increase. It would be just as plausible to argue from this fact that the polls have stimulated widespread interest in political questions and have contributed to the increase in popular participation. Merely keeping to the facts indicates that 1936, the peak-year of poll interest, saw more than 45,646,000 Americans cast their votes, an increase of 6,000,000 votes over 1932, and the highest degree of voting participation in the history of the nation. 

Secondly, research has suggested that the basis of the bandwagon theory is more imaginary than real. Actual study of poll returns made at successive time-intervals indicates that, in the course of a campaign, the curve of sentiment of support for each candidate is often flat, or in the direction of the losing candidate. 

In 1938, to cite but one example, the Institute conducted a series of studies in Kentucky on the Barkley-Chandler campaign for the Senate. The first survey made in April, showed Senator Barkley far in the lead with 67% of the Democratic vote. This survey was given wide publicity in Kentucky. According to the bandwagon theory, Senator Barkley from this point on should have gained in popularity. Subsequent surveys, reported at monthly intervals until the election, showed the margin of his lead to be dwindling. 

A final survey found him with 59% of the total vote. The Institute has conducted almost fifty surveys of elections and spaced issues, where the movement of votes to candidates in successive surveys was charted, and little or no evidence of the existence of such a bandwagon vote has ever been detected.

But beyond such problems, and at the root of many objections to the polls of public opinion lies a fundamental conflict between two opposed views of the democratic process and what it means. 

This conflict is not new-it is as old as American political theory itself. It concerns the nature of the relationship between representative government and direct democracy, between the judgments of small exclusive groups, and the opinions of the great mass of people. Many theorists who criticize the polls do so because they are fearful of giving too much power to the people. A mod-

ern re-statement of this attitude may be found in an article written by Col. O. R. McGuire in the November issue of the U. S. Law 

Review. (1) 

Col. McGuire quotes James Madison: that ". pure de- mocracies . . . have ever been spectacles of turbulence and contention"; have ever been found incompatible with personal security or the rights of property; and have in general been as short in their lives as they have been violent in their deaths". In his arguments against government by public opinion, McGuire leans heavily on eighteenth century Conservative theorists like James Madison and Edmund Burke, and on the anti-democratic psychological generalizations of Ross, Tarde, and Le Bon. McGuire follows 

Burke in upholding the conception of representative government under which a body of carefully chosen, disinterested public representatives "whose wisdom may best discern the true interest of the country, and whose patriotism and love of justice will be least likely to sacrifice it to temporarily or partial considerations" govern their mute peoples. Under such conditions, it is argued, “it may well happen that the public voice, pronounced by the representatives of the people, will be more consonant to the public good than if pronounced by the people themselves convened for that purpose".

Certain specific dangers attendant on popular government are set forth by many critics including Col. McGuire: 

1. That the level of public intelligence is low; 

2. That political and economic problems are too complex to submit to voters; 

3. That representatives will be transformed into demagogues by pressure of public opinion; and 

4. That public opinion polls will destroy the representative system and substitute direct democracy. 

This case against government by public opinion, reveals suspicion not so much of public opinion surveys, as of the mass of the people and their intentions and capabilities. By and large, the thesis that the common people are unfit to rule, and that consequently they must be led by their natural superiors-the legislators and the experts-is different only in degree and not in essence from the view urged by Hitler and Mussolini and other contemporary autocrats that the "people are mere ballot cattle", and that because of their numbers and stupidity, because of their lack of training and their gullibility, they must be kept as far away as 

(1) McGuire, Col. O. R. The United States Law Review, November, 1939.

possible from the expert's job of formulating laws for the people blindly to obey.

Many previous statements and charges of just this kind can be found throughout history. Every autocrat and despot has claimed that the people were incapable of ruling themselves, and by implication decided that only certain privileged leaders were fit for the legislative task. They have urged that "The Best" should dominate, although at different times and in different places the ideas of which group should constitute "The Best" have been completely contradictory. In Burke's England, or Madison's 

America it was the peerage or the stable wealthier classes "the good, the wise, and the rich". In Soviet Russia, it is the representatives of the proletariat who constitutes "The Best". 

But the history of autocracy has paid eloquent testimony to the truth of Lord Acton's conclusion that "Power corrupts-Absolute power corrupts absolutely". The danger of what has been called "the never-ending audacity of elected persons", emphasizes the need for modifying executive power by the contribution of the needs and aspirations of the common people. This is the essence of the democratic conception, which has always urged that political societies were most secure when deeply rooted in the political activity and interest of the mass of the people, and least secure when social judgment was the prerogative of the chosen few.

The American tradition of political thought has known and has tried to reconcile these two viewpoints. Since the beginning of the country's history, political theorists have disagreed on the extent to which the people and their opinions could play a part in political decision. "Men by their constitutions", wrote Jefferson, "are naturally divided into two parties: 1.-Those who fear and distrust the people and wish to draw all powers from them into the hands of the higher classes; 2.-Those who identify themselves with the people, have confidence in them, cherish and consider them as the most wise depository of the public interests."(1) Jefferson himself believed that the people were less likely to misgovern themselves than any small exclusive group, and for this reason urged that public opinion should be the decisive and ultimate force in American politics.

His opponents have followed Alexander Hamilton, whose anti-democratic ideas represent an armory for present day Con- (1) Agar, Herbert: "Pursuit of Happiness", p. 42.

servative thought. They have chiefly been concerned with the liberties and property of the minority and have repeatedly urged the necessity of building checks against people's power. * * It is essential to realize that those who favor rule of the Best, of the gifted representative, and the expert, and those who desire to give the common people more power are at loggerheads because their arguments do not meet each other. The need exists to find the right balance between the kind of mass-judgments and comments obtained by the public opinion polls, and the opinions of legislators and experts. In both extreme views, there is a significant kernal of truth. No one would deny that we need the best and the wisest in the key-positions of political life. But the democrat is right in demanding that these leaders be subject to check by the opinions of the mass of the people. He is right in refusing to let these persons rule irresponsibly. For in its most extreme form, this criticism which opposes any effort, like the modern polls, to make the people more articulate, which inveighs against the perils of a "direct democracy", leads directly to a view of government which is in itself anti-democratic. If it is argued that legislators understand better than the people what the people want, it is but a short step to give legislators the power to decree what the people ought to want. Few tendencies could be more dangerous. When a special group is entrusted with the task of making the value-judgments for a whole community of people with diverse attitudes, interests, and experiences, then we have gone a long way from democracy, representative or any other kind. 

The important fact which Colonel McGuire, and others who plead for a representative from the government tend to forget is that the legislator's task in a democracy is to "represent". Unless he is to be the easy prey of special interests and antisocial pressures, he must have access to the expression of a truly "public" opinion, containing the views of all the groups in our complex society. For it is important to remember that the free expression of public opinion is not merely a right which the mass of the people are fortunate to possess—it is as vital for the leaders as for the people. It is the only way the legislators can know the wishes and prejudices and values of the people, to know whether certain kinds of legislation are possible, what the people think about the present laws, how serious is the opposition to a particular political proposal. A rigid dictatorship or any organization of political society which forbids the people to express their own attitudes, is dangerous not only to the people, it is equally a danger to the

leaders who never know whether they are sitting in an easy chair, or on top of a volcano. People who live differently-think differently. In order that their experience be incorporated into political rules under which they are to live, their thinking must be included in the main body of ideas involved in the process of final decisions. That is why the surveys take care to include those on relief as well as those who draw their income from investments, young as well as old, men and women of all sorts from every section of the country, in their sample "public". 

Another form which the case against the people takes is the argument that we are living today in a society so complex and so technical that its problems cannot be trusted to the people or their representatives for solution, but must lie in the hands of experts. 

It has been urged that only those who know how to legislate should have the power of decreeing what type of legislation ought to exist. The Technocracy movement put this view squarely before the American public. If it is true, it means that the kind of mass value-judgments secured by the polls and surveys is quite useless in political life. It means that the people and their representatives must abdicate before the trained economist, the social worker, the expert in public finance, in tariffs, in rural problems. 

These learned persons, the argument runs, are the only ones who know the facts, and therefore they are the only ones competent to decide on policy. 

There is something tempting about the view which insists that the people should be led by an aristocracy of specialists. But 

Americans have learned something from the experience of the last few years. They have learned, in the first place, that experts do not always agree about the solutions for the ills of our times. "Ask six economists their opinion on unemployment", an English wag has suggested, "and you will get seven different answerstwo from Mr. Keynes".

The point can doubtless be exaggerated. Certainly in the modern day a vast body of useful, applicable knowledge has been built up by economists and other specialists-knowledge which is sorely needed to remedy the ills of our time. But all that experts can do, even assuming we can get them to agree about what need be done, is to tell how we can act. 

The objectives, the ends, the basic values of policy must still be decided. The economist can suggest what action is to be taken if a certain goal is to be reached. He cannot qua economist say what final goal should be reached. The lawyer can administer and interpret the country's laws. He cannot say what those laws should

be. The social worker can suggest ways of aiding the aged, he cannot say that aiding aged persons is desirable. The expert's function is invaluable, but its value lies in judging the meansnot the ends of public policy. 

Thus the expert and his technique are sorely needed. Perhaps 

Great Britain has gone farther than the United States in relating expert opinion to democratic government. The technique of the 

Royal Commission, and the other methods of organizing special knowledge are extremely valuable ways of focusing the attention of the general public on evils and on solutions. In these Commissions, expert opinion is brought to bear, and opportunities for collective deliberation are created for those with special knowledge of political and economic questions. But even these Royal 

Commissions can do nothing until the public has passed judgment on whether or not their recommendations should be implemented into legislation.

As a corollary of this view that expert opinion can bear only on specific questions of means, on the technical methods by which solutions are to be achieved, we must agree that the mass of the people do not, and in the nature of things, cannot have the necessary knowledge to judge the intimate details of policy. Repeated testing by means of the poll-technique reveals that they cannot be expected to have opinions or intelligent judgments about details of monetary policy, of treaty-making, or on other questions involving highly specialized knowledge. There are things which cannot be done by public opinion, just as there are things which can only be done by public opinion. "The people", said Lord Bryce, "who are the power entitled to say what they want, are less qualified to say how, and in what form, they are to obtain it; or in other words, public opinion can determine ends, but is less fit to examine and select means to those ends."(1) 

All this may be granted to the critics. But having urged the need for representatives and experts, we are still faced with the need for keeping these legislators and experts in touch with the public and its opinions. We will still have need of declarations of attitude from those who experience and who live under the laws and regulations. For only the man on relief can tell the legislator how it feels to be on relief. Only the small business man can express his attitude on the economic questions which complicate his existence. Only women voters can explain their views on marriage and divorce. Only all these groups, taken together, can formulate the general objectives and tendencies which their experience (1) Bryce, James: "The American Commonwealth”, p. 347.

makes them feel would be best for the common welfare. For the ultimate values of politics and economics, the judgments on which public policy is based, do not come from special knowledge, or from intelligence alone. They are compounded from the day-today experience of the men and women who together make up the society we live in.

That is why public opinion polls are important today. Instead of viewing the polls, as some critics do, as attempts to sabotage rrepresentative government, kidnap the members of Congress and substitute the taxi-driver for the expert in politics, public opinion research may be seen to be a necessary and valuable aid to truly representative government. The continuous studies of public opinion will merely supplement, they will not destroy the work of representatives. What is evident here is that representatives will be better able to represent if they have an accurate measure of the wishes, aspirations, and needs of different groups within the general public, rather than the kind of distorted picture sent them by telegram enthuiasts, and overzealous pressure groups who claim to speak for all the people, but actually only speak for themselves. Public opinion surveys will provide legislators with a new instrument for estimating trends of opinion, and minimize the chances of their being fooled by clamoring minorities. For the alternative to these surveys, it must be remembered, is not a perfect and still silence in which the Ideal Legislator and the Perfect Expert can commune on desirablee policies. It is the real world of competing pressures, vociferous demonstrations, and the stale cries of party politics.

Does this mean that the polls, and constant soundings of public sentiment will substitute demagogery for statesmanship? The contrary is more likely. The demagogue is no unfamiliar object. 

He was not created by the modern opinion surveys. He thrives, not when the people have power, facts, information; but when the people are insecure, gullible, see and hear only one-side of the case. The demagogue, like the propagandist of untruths, finds his natural habitat in a situation where there is no method of checking on the truth of falsity of the case. To distinguish demagogues from democratic leaders, the people must know the facts. They must be politically aware.

Is this element secured by having no measurements of public opinion, or by having frequent, accurate measurement? When local Caesars rise to claim a large popular support for their plans and schemes--is it not better to be able to refer to some more tangible index of their true status than their own claims and

speeches? The poll measurements have served in the past to expose the claims of seekers after utopia. 

As the public opinion survey develops in accuracy, and as its returns become more widely accepted, public officials and the people themselves will probably become more critical towards pressure groups that parade under the banner of "public opinion". 

The story of the Townsend movement forms a good example of this. Who will forget the clamour that was raised by the proposal to give to all persons over sixty 200 dollars a month? When the Townsend Clubs began to spread the gospel of plenty throughout the land, "Congressmen shivered in their boots", Professor

Smith tells us, "fearful of the strength of this incorporated octopus in pursuit of the milennium". (1) But when the American Institute of Public Opinion polled the public on this question, it was revealed that only 3.8% of the voters thought there should be $200 a month pensions. The Institute's investigation of the movement threw a spotlight on this pursuit of the chimerical. * * Finally there has always been a fear of the majority in the minds of some critics of the polls. Ever since the time of De 

Tocqueville, the phrase "tyranny of the majority" has been widely used by the critics of democratic procedures, fearful lest the sheer weight of numbers should crush intelligent minorities, and suppress the criticism which comes from small associations which refuse to conform to the minority view. It has been asserted that the same tendencies which exist in a despotism may also exist in a society where the will of the majority is the supreme sovereign power, and grim pictures of a majority placing unfair economic burdens on a small minority have been conjured up. A recent 

Institute survey indicates how empty some political speculations about the "tyranny of the majority" can be. The question was recently asked: "If Congress decides to increase the Army and

Navy, should this increase be paid for by extra taxes next year, or by borrowing more money?" Majority public opinion here voted in favor of more taxes, and the degree of support for this increased taxing measure rose with the amount of taxes paid by the various income levels in the populations. History is not always the record of the errors and abuses of majorities. An intelligent and wide-awake democracy can impose voluntary burdens on itself.

The "tyranny of the majority" does not seem to have been America's biggest problem. It is as great a danger to contemplate 

(1) Smith, C. W.: "Public Opinion in a Democracy", New York, 1939.

the "tyranny of the minority", who operate under cover of the Bill of Rights to secure ends in the interests of a small group. 

The real tyranny in America today will not come from a better knowledge of how majorities feel about the questions of the day which press for solution. Tyranny comes when ignorance exists—an ignorance of other views, and ignorance of the power and wants of the opposition. Tyranny triumphs when the media of information are closed, not when they are open for all to use. 

The best guarantee for the maintenance of a vigorous democratic life lies not in concealing what people think, but in trying to find out what their ultimate purposes are, and in seeking to incorporate these purposes in legislation. It demands exposing the weaknesses of democracy as well as its values. Above all, it is posited on the belief that political institutions are not perfect, that they must be modified to meet changing conditions, and that a new age demands new techniques. 

It is in relation to these long-term needs and objectives that modern surveys of public opinion must finally be evaluated.
