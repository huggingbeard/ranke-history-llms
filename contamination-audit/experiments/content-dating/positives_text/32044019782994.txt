ON WIT AND HUMOR 

Every age and generation, as well as every class of human, has its unique form of engendering laughter.

A jest is a lightning-quick manifestation of the spirit of man, ordinarily overcast by the gloom of civilized life. It reflects the true inwardness of the individual or the group. And since the interests and strivings of one generation differs from those of any other, many of its humorous trends and expressions are not comprehensible by the others. So too among various human classes. 

The jest that will bring loud laughter from the coarse may cause revulsion in the genteel.

In reverse, the refined humor that brings chuckles to the intellectual will be met blankly by ruder minds.

This book endeavers to express the emotions and ideas ascendant today among the young people assembled into the services from all parts of the United States, comprising intellectuals, professors, business and professional men, as well as backwoodsmen of primitive culture. This position must inevitably raise peculiar and comical situations such as have been depicted in the book.

Humor is the only branch of literature that requires to apology, for the smile of good cheer

is always understandable and welcome.

The sparkling bon-mot and the clever anecdote transcend time and place; they give joy and cheer to all men in all places - for they reach those basic regions of the soul that all mankind have in common.

There are many physical and cerebral traits common to man and beast. But only man has the gift of laughter. The reason for this is that even the most highly developed animals and those most fit in life's struggle, can see the phenomena of existence only as they appear at the moment. But man sees in every occurrence all of his life's past, all outer relationships, and all possible changes for the future when these seem wry, contorted, and ridiculous, he laughs.

On the basis of the general moral standard, there have been omitted all jokes concerning sex, religion, and race, such as may perturb men's minds or create class prejudice. Humor must leave hearts clean and not with an after-effect of unpleasantness.

Readers will recognize themselves or their acquaintances in these pages. After this war is forgotten, this little volume may well serve as a source of folk humor in the second-and lastworld conflict. 

Nahum Ben-Horim

IF MEN COULD SEE 

If a man could look ahead into certain movements, and see how their founders and leaders so often are foolish, combative, little men, operating behind a mask of high-sounding terms and phrases, instead of supporting them he would laugh at their pretensions.

If he could preview the end of certain idealistic strivings, and see the emptiness they are transmuted into, he would laugh at the naive founders who devoted their souls to those presumed ideals. 

Whenever a person looks about him and discovers that those nearest and dearest to him are mere egotists, who love him only to the extent that he gives them support and satisfaction, and will turn upon him when he can no longer do so, he need but laugh at their displays of affection.

This book uncovers some of such secrets of life read it and laugh.

LAUGH, BROTHER, LAUGH 

Life is like a rainbow, variegated, richly colored and radiant. He who can see life thus, finds enjoyment in it and laughs.

When words are too brief and limited to give full expression to such inner emotions of joy, laughter serves to express the most noble, profound, and heartfelt emotions of men.

When God for the first time looked upon the world He had created, and He was well satisfied, he laughed with joy and that laughter gave birth to the sun. 

The mother seeing her newborn child, smiles happily. And the first gift of the babe to the parent is also a smile.

Those by God's grace skilled in the arts, when their spirits fill with creative thought, do not express their joy of creation in words- they too show their emotion in a smile.

When God saw, however, that the world was filled with evil, cruelty, and ill-will, and sought to keep mankind from ruination, he gave them the saving gift of laughter.

Laughter is integrally part of justice and righteousness. Wickedness knows naught of laughing. 

Laugh, brother, laugh, and bring joy to all about you, to all who feel sorrow and misery and require a gift of happiness. 

HE BEGAN IT 

An A. M. G. officer entered a local school, in time to see a pupil slap his teacher. "How is it," he demanded, indignantly, "that you dare to lift up your hand against the teacher?"

The tearful pupil stood up defiantly. "Believe me, sir," he cried; "he started it!"

NAZI FAIRNESS 

From a letter found on a German soldier: 

My dearest Gretchen: We were in Kiev last month. Nothing happened there. We just killed off all the Jews. As soon as we got rid

of the Jews, the general delivered a talk to the division about German fairness. He said the weakest element among our enemies is the Jew-and we're through with him. But this isn't all. We must carry out our mission of extermination with due German punctiliousness.

The destruction must be threefold. First, the children. Every soldier must shoot any child he sees, and leave it there. Second, there must be sufficient work camps for healthy men and women; they will be systematically transported to Germany, so that we shall have plenty of free labor, while their numbers stop growing. 

Third, the intellectual status of these people must be lowered through killing out all the intellegentzia; they must be held as hostages, and then shot at first provocation. And, he added, we must make no distinction between one enemy and another. Our German justice must be equally expressed to all.

THE SPARK 

Private: I can't explain it. I've read this jokebook all the way through, and I never laughed once. 

Sergeant: Well, here in the South we make fire with charcoal; then we use a bellows to make it flame up. But when there isn't a spark of fire in the first place, all the bellows in the world will not make a flame.

TWO LOCKS 

An engineer, coming to a strange town to arrange for building an army camp, found lodgings in a private home. On his first departure he set a lock on his suitcase. When he returned he found it replaced by another lock! "What is the idea?" he inquired angrily. 

The head of the house was calm. "Why did you put a lock on in the first place?" he demanded. "Why," was the answer, "I'm a stranger here and don't know anyone, and have valuable papers. Someone might take them out." 

That doesn't justify it," returned the proprietor. "After all, I don't know you, and I have valuable things in the house. Someore might put them in the suitcase!"

EAST AND WEST 

Lieutenant A: I was in the Chicago station, and heard a private inquire when the next train would go out. The agent asked him whether he was going east or west. And the dumb cluck said he was returning to camp, and it made no difference whether he went east or west, just so he would get there all right. 

Lieutenant B: Why be surprised that a private should be confused? He is no worse than the politicians and the rest who say let's make peace right now, good or bad, just so it's peace.

THE WHOLE TRUTH 

Drinker: Say, barkeep, what are you doing with a Bible in your pocket? Do you read a chapter a day? 

Barkeeper: I don't read it, and I'm not interested in reading it. 

Drinker: Then what are you keeping it for? 

Barkeeper: This Bible saved my life five years ago. There was a murder right here in the saloon,

and I was suspected. The evidence wasn't conclusive, but I had no witnesses in my defense.

Finally the judge said he would believe me innocent if I swore I didn't do it on a Bible. I did, and he released me, and since then I carry the Bible around for good luck!

PLENTY OF TIME 

Sergeant: Stop crawling like a snail, Bilks. 

Get a move on! 

Bilks: I've got time. What's the hurry? 

Sergeant: What's the hurry? The whole regiment is rushing ahead, and you've got to keep up with them, you numbskull! 

Bilks: Well, tell them to go slower, and we'll all be together. 

Sergeant: You idiot, the enemy is going fast, and we've got to reach the position before they do, if we want to take it. 

Bilks: Oh! Why didn't you tell me that before?

SHARPSHOOTER 

Lieutenant: Private Brown, I hear you've been saying you're the best shot in the division. 

Brown: I am, sir. I can shoot a hundred times straight in the same circle. 

Lieutenant: Marvelous, if true. How do you do it? 

Brown: Very simple, sir. First I shoot, and then I make the circle.

BIRDS OF A FEATHER 

Captain: Can you tell me why we keep Private Johnson in the army? He's a real idiot, and is doing the army no good. 

Lieutenant: It is like this, sir. We are beginning the invasion, and we're bound to make contact with the civilian population. You know an idiot understands another idiot, and Johnson will be the best man to get along with civilian idiots!

FROM A CHUM TO A WAC 

Dear Elinor:

I want you to be the first to learn the great news. Last week I was married to-guess who! -Willie Hatoff, whom you used to go around with for years. 

A month ago he came and asked me if it

were true that you had become a Wac. He said her mother had told him you joined the army, but he didn't believe her, because you would have preferred to marry him rather than do that. Well, then he repeated some old saying about when there aren't any fish you have to eat oysters; so, since you were away, would I marry him? 

If I weren't anxious for a proposal I would have got mad and sent him packing—but what can you do when there are twenty girls for every man? So I said, "See here, Mr. Hatoff, do you compare me with an oyster, while Miss 

Lyons is a fish? Does that make me worse than she is?" And he just laughed and said, “A girl should have a sense of humor, and anyways you're a lot older than Miss Lyons."

Really I should have slapped him for that, but in these days even a decrepit old boy is desirable, so I remained quiet. 

Then I asked him if he could make a living for a wife and family. He said that in wartime only workingmen and manufacturers make a living, but really worthwhile people don't. 

They eat what they can, or else they borrow from friends till the end of the war. 

So I asked how he would earn a living after the war. And he answered, "Didn't you ever hear of the wonderful plans of President Roosevelt? The manufacturers and the Democratic

party are making plans too. You can be sure there will never be any worry about an intellectual giant like myself earning enough to live on." 

Then I asked him why he didn't join the army. He said he was a 4F. That means, I said, that you are sick. "Oh, no!" he cried. And he told me about the secret policy of the government, which was afraid of what would happen in the future if all the young men of the nation were taken away. To avoid that danger the local boards were told confidentially that when they found an exceptionally healthy and able young man they should mark him 4F-then sufficient fine young men would be on hand. 

That sounded right and reasonable. And besides it is better to be a bride than an old maidso I said yes, and last week we were married. 

Don't be angry, and think I stole your sweetie. He wouldn't have married you anyway, because he is angry at you. You didn't tell him or say goodby before you joined the Wacs. 

But I almost forgot the most important thing. 

You remember Panzer, my big bulldog? I gave him away to my aunt. You really can't have two friends to love at the same time. So I changed my dog for Willie. 

Your own Susie

COMMON SENSE 

Private: Colonel, sir, thank you for letting me talk to you. You see, nobody here understands me.

Colonel: What is the trouble? 

Private: It's this way, sir, The officers insist on my marching all day. In the village I come from, everything is quiet, and no one runs around all the time. I really can't march like the other fellows. And I never in my life smelled powder before. I get frightened when I hear a shot. But they insist I must learn to use a rifle. 

I don't like it, sir. 

Colonel: I don't know what to do with you.

You're in the army now, and we have to use you in one way or another. What do you think you can do? 

Private: You could suggest to the general, sir, that I serve on his staff. I could advise him on strategy, Colonel, because one thing I have, sir, is common sense!

WOMEN'S ARMY CORPS 

Middle-aged Woman:

I want to enlist as a Wac. 

Officer: Madam, we take only younger women. You see, the Wacs have to go where the soldiers go, and do work which might be difficult for older women.

Woman: Then take me in as a cook. I've been cooking for the Hotel Statler for fifteen years, and everybody likes my cooking. 

Officer: But people need cooks today in civil life. You won't have any trouble finding work. 

Woman: To tell you the truth, I really want to work among soldiers so that I can meet some nice man and get married. 

Officer: I'm surpriseed you haven't found one before this. 

Woman: They're a terrible lot around here.

When a man takes you out a few times, and you mention marriage, he right away asks how old you are. And I answer that a woman is not a chicken, which goes into the pot only when it is young. A woman, I say, should be able to cook a good meal, look nice, and bear children. 

But they won't listen to me. For a woman they want a spring chicken! 

Officer: How do you know the soldiers won't talk the same way?

Woman: Soldiers are too serious to talk so foolishly!

NO HEARING 

Captain: I was surprised, chaplain, to hear your eulogy of Sergeant Sweeney. It's all right to say nothing evil of the dead, but you ascribed virtues to him that he never possessed. 

Chaplain: Well, let's say that we use strong field glasses on the battlefield, and I was looking at Sweeney through the glasses. 

Captain: But you have the reputation of never giving anyone unnecessary flattery. 

Chaplain: Quite right, captain. A living soldier might be flustered and hurt if I tried to praise him that way, but a dead man has the great virtue of being deaf.

M. P.

ON REDUCING 

Medical Officer: You'll have to reduce somewhat. You're too fat for your height. 

Private: Please, sir, if I take off weight, will my mind be reduced too?

But when I worked myself up in business, and by good living managed to reach two hundred pounds, everybody was respectful and even asked my advice about things.

NO HORSES IN POLITICS 

The captain is riding with a farmer to catch a train. The horses move very slowly. 

Captain: Please touch up those horses. I have to get that train. 

Farmer: My horses don't like to be beaten. 

Captain: Then holler at them. I must get to my superior officer in a hurry and give him an important message. 

Farmer: My horses don't like to be yelled at. 

Captain: Speak softly then! I can't miss it! 

Farmer: My horses don't hear anything. 

Captain: You mean they are deaf?

Farmer: No, but since the war began they refuse to mix in politics, and they don't listen to soldiers or politicians, lest they later blurt out some military secret!

FURNISHED ROOM TO LET 

A woman entered a camp and started distributing leaflets announcing furnished rooms to let. 

Brought before the commandant, she was informed that the government supplied lodging for all the encampment. "I know," was the reply. "But the soldiers on furlough can come to stay in my house!"

NO TEARS

Sergeant: Sorry, sir, but I can't cry this afternoon. I've just received a telegram that my wife died.

PRESERVING THE JOKE 

Private Smith kept talking and yelling at

Private Jones, who seemed completely deaf to his repeated exclamations. Finally he looked closely,

and saw that Jones's ears were stuffed with cotton. In pantomime he inquired the reason for the stuffing. "I just heard a good joke," murmured Jones, "which I want to repeat to a friend. But I'm afraid that if I hear anything else in the mean while, the joke will fade out of my mind!"

REMEMBRANCE 

A new camp in hilly country purchased a number of packmules for transportation where jeeps could not go. A dealer, hearing of this, approached the commandant in an effort to sell some of his own stock. "But we have all the mules we need," answered the general. "I'll let you know if we need any more later." "Fine!" smiled the dealer. "And, general, whenever you think of mules in the future, by all means think of me!" 

"LADIES WEAR" 

A lieutenant about to go on leave was looking for some gifts for his wife and daughter. Seeing a large sign, "Ladies' Wear," over a store, he entered and asked for some dresses. "I can't sell you dresses," returned the proprietor. "My business is used furniture, and things like that."

"Then what's the meaning of that sign?" inquired the bewildered lieutenant. "Oh, that!" exclaimed the storekeeper. "That's a sign I bought second-hand, and it's on display outside. I want to sell it!"

LEND-LEASE 

A lend-lease official was arranging to turn over some goods to the representative of a small nation. 

The latter wanted to know and discuss the price of every article. The official became impatient. "You don't have to worry about costs," he explained. "You won't have to repay right away. 

It will take many years till we dun you.' "To tell the truth," replied the foreign agent, "I'm arguing about everything so that your government should think we really intend to pay!"

NO ANIMAL

PREFACE 

Hundreds of books have been published concerning the present war. They cover many topics, including the huge number of armed forces involved, the tremendous costs of the conflict, victories on many fronts, and post-war problems.

But not sufficient has been written concerning the actual life of the fighters, their superhuman strivings, their moments of rest and privacy, their intimate emotions and aspirations. 

These life details are fully presented only when they appear not as a scholarly study, but are told incidentally through flashes of humor and unposed conversation. "Soldiers' Wit and Humor" is a spontaneous representation of military life and thought.

In this collection one meets many humorous, naive, and simple characters with which soldiers come into contact. There are many additional odd characters, including the worse and cynical types, who laugh at everyone, who for money or glory injure even their closest associates. Thus the book is richly variegated. It provides a closer gaze into the human spirit as reflected in its various guises.

We find these characters comic, and laugh heartily at their words and antics. They are not puppets in a doll house or visitors from another planet, but the kind of men we are closely acquainted with.. Though ordinarily we consciously control our words and deeds, there are moments when our subconscious comes completely to the fore. Then do we laugh. If we wish, we discover how far the actual man is from the civilization he professes. 

The mirror shows our physical configuration; humor, our inner essence. 

Nahum Ben-Horim.

FIRST CLIENT 

The lieutenant, walking through a railroad junction, saw a business sign reading, "Successful

Matchmaker." He entered the office, where the following colloquy took place. 

Lieutenant: I'm almost forty, single, and a lawyer by profession. Do you think you can make a match for me? 

Matchmaker: Certainly, sir. Tell me just what kind of woman you want. 120, 130, or 140 pounds? I would suggest that you don't take a heavier one, because men like slender women today. 

Lieutenant: I am astonished that you speak of brides, of one with whom I shall have to spend

my days, as though she were a steer or hog, to be judged by her weight! When one chooses a wife, it must be on the basis of beauty, character, and culture. 

Matchmaker: Sir, you are in error. Beauty doesn't mean anything, for the beauty parlors can make any homely woman pretty. As for character who ever heard of a woman having any character? And as to culture the less education his wife has the better for the man; an educated woman always wants to boss her husband. 

Lieutenant: I am surprised to find in these days, and in democratic America, opinions like yours about the other sex. It sounds like the beliefs of primitive men thousands of years ago. How long have you been in this business, and how many couples have you brought together? 

Matchmaker: I've been in this business a whole year, and I haven't managed to make a match yet. 

That's why I'm so anxious to make one with you. 

If you will be good enough to be the first, that will really save me. Don't turn down a patriotic

American who is trying to earn an honest living. 

Won't you please be my first?

NAZI SPY 

A sergeant on guard at a prison camp saw three German soldiers arguing violently. A tall captive was holding forth; suddenly a smaller comrade made angry gestures and began beating up the

larger man, threw him down, and kept on belaboring him unmercifully. The sergeant, after breaking up the fight, demanded an explanation from the third prisoner. And this was the story.

The tall German had been enthusiastically lauding the American way of life and democracy, praising President Roosevelt and bitterly cursing Hitler as a Nazi mániac and wholesale murderer. 

Upon this the smaller man had accused him of treason against Germany and against Hitler, whom he declared a great leader above criticism; and further, if all Nazi prisoners spoke in this manner they would not want to return home and prove Germany's ultimate invincibility. Then the hostilities began.

But the sergeant wanted to know why the narrator was laughing so heartily all through his recital. "Because," explained the third man, "I know the whole incident is only camouflage. The tall one is actually a Nazi spy, and he brought on the spectacle you saw to convince the prison authorities of his love for America. He figured that after the war he would be freed and be able to travel all over the country as a German secret agent!" 

"ACROSS THE WAY" 

A Nazi prisoner managed to elude his guards, waylay a wayfarer, and having stolen the victim's clothes, enter a New York bus undetected. He

had the address of a Nazi sympathizer, at number 

1400 of a Bronx street. However, he looked on the odd numbered side and in perplexity asked a passerby where the address on his paper might be. The answer was a curt, "Across the way!"

The escaped prisoner, believing this meant a specific street, looked up at all the signs expecting to find one reading "Across the Way." Not finding any such, he asked another person where "across the way" might be. This person pointed to the opposite side. The now indignant Nazi, convinced once more that all Americans are bluffers, appealed to a policeman.

He explained in his broken English that the citizens thereabouts were intent on making him a laughing stock, shunting him from one side of the street to another to find some mythical "across the way." The policeman recognized the accent, and soon had the fugitive in custody. 

TAKE YOUR TIME 

Captain: Now, James, tell me you've been in the army two months. How do you like the life, the marching, the shooting, and all the rest? 

James: I like the life fine, sir, except for one thing. When we're on the route, we run. I asked the sergeant why we couldn't walk slowly, and he said we have to run so the enemy won't catch us, and if we happen to be chasing the enemy

we mustn't let him get away. So I asked him why we couldn't arrange with the Red Cross that neither we nor the enemy should hurry up, but both of us take it easy. And he answered that he wasn't in politics, and only knew the job he was doing. 

You know, captain, the philosophy of taking it easy runs in my family. I had a grandfather who lived that way. He was a watchmaker by trade, and after he fixed a watch the hands stood at the same minute as when it had been brought to him. His father ( a slaughterer, used to cut the neck of a steer so deliberately that the animal would look up, see what was going on, and run off unharmed. He was not even in a hurry to die; this he did when he was ninety-eight. 

Before he passed away, he called me in and said, "James, if you want to live your life happily, never hurry. It isn't necessary to hasten to the other world. Both those who hustle and those who crawl get to heaven or hell together. "There are no priorities in the world to come, nor discrimination. Everyone is treated alike, regardless of religion, race, or color. When a new batch arrives, a train is provided for those going to heaven and another for those being sent to hell. 

But no one knows just what is his destination when he boards either of the trains. Theirs is a blind course. 99

Then I asked him how he knew all this, and whether he'd ever been there to see; and he answered, "Son, no one living ever was there. It's all imagination or vision. One man's vision is as good as another's."

THE LEADER 

By special arrangement a troupe came to entertain at a camp. The commandant was assured that no payment need be given the regular actors and actresses, but the singers in the chorus were to receive ten dollars each.

After the performance, the commandant complained to the head of the troupe that the latter's chauffeur had stood all the while within the choral group but had not sung a note-obviously for the purpose of collecting an easy ten dollars. 

But the trouper quickly interposed, "That man is the leader of the band!"
