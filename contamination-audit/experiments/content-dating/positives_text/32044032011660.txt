CHAPTER I. 

INTRODUCTION. 

Historical. The title Agency as referring to a distinct branch of substantive law is first met with in comparatively recent times. Blackstone dismisses a discussion of this subject in the following words: (1 Blackstone's Commentaries 427.) 

"There is yet a fourth species of servants, if they may be so called, being rather in a superior, a ministerial capacity; such as stewards, factors and bailiffs, whom, however the law considers as servants pro tempore, with regard to such of their acts as affects their master's or employer's property."

The following is from the Preface to Paley on Agency (1811), and is hardly in accordance with the present attitude of the profession toward this phase of the law: "The law of principal and agent appears, at first view, to be founded upon principles so few and simple, and in general so easy of application, that a treatise upon such a subject may seem altogether superfluous. And indeed the decisions upon this branch of the law, which are met with in older reports, are neither numerous nor important." 

It is unfortunate that the term "Agency" is generally applied to all cases of representative action. In this broad sense, it embraces the Law of Master and Servant, as well as that of Principal and Agent. In this work, only the principles of law applicable to the latter relationship will be considered although the two relations are so similar in many respects that illustrations will be freely drawn from either field. 

Definitions. "Agency is founded upon contract, either express or implied, by which one of the parties confides. to the other, the management of some business to be transacted in his name or on his account, and by which the other assumes to do the business and to render an account of it." Kent's Comm. II, page 784.

“Agency is a legal relation created by contract, whereby one party, called the agent, is authorized to represent the other party, called the principal, in business dealings with third persons, and is usually empowered to bring the principal into contractual relations with such persons." 

Sternaman vs. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 170 N. Y. 13; 

57 L. R. A. 318. 

"The relation of principal and agent, or the relation of agency in the narrower sense, is the legal relation which exists where one person, called the agent, is authorizedusually by act of the parties, but occasionally perhaps by operation of law to represent and act for another, called the principal, in the contractual dealings of the latter with third person." Mecham on Agency, Volume I, page 13. "An agent is a representative vested with authority, real or ostensible, to create voluntary primary obligations for his principal, by making contracts with third persons, or by making promises or representations to third persons calculated to induce them to change their legal relations." Hufcutt on Agency, 2nd Edition, page 17. See also Kratovic vs. Van Buren, 235 N. Y. 96.

From the above definitions, it is apparent that the relation of Principal and Agent is, in the main, contractual. 

Strictly speaking, an agent's authority must be conferred upon him by or derived from another before authority exists in him to act in his representative capacity. However, there appears to be exceptions to this general rule-instances where authority to act for another is vested in one by operation of law, viz: cases where a married woman has authority to buy necessities on her husband's credit when he has failed to supply her, and where a child is permitted to buy necessaries on his parent's credit illustrate this form of "compulsory agency.' 

See C. A. Brandt Co. vs. Englehardt, 182 N. Y. Supp. 618; 

Wanamaker vs. Weaver, 176 N. Y. 75, 65 L. R. A. 529. The relation so created is not, strictly speaking, a true agency rela- 

---

tion. It is often inaccurately said that an unpaid seller of goods, who makes a sale of goods for the purpose of foreclosing his lien, acts as agent of the buyer in making the sale, but, as was said. in Moore vs. Potter, 155 N. Y. 481: "It is to be observed that in many cases cited it has been said that in thus selling the property the vendor acts as the agent of the vendee for that purpose. Clearly, the use of the words 'as agent for the vendee' was not intended as a determination that the relation between the parties was that which ordinarily exists between a principal who owns property, and an agent who may be authorized to manage or sell it. But it is a general expression which has been somewhat inaccurately used to define the right of a vendor to make a re-sale and hold the vendee responsible for his loss."

The contract upon which the relationship of principal and agent is founded may in general result from the express or implied agreement of the parties, or may result from the ratification of the transactions of one who assumed to act as agent by the one for whom he assumed to act; or the actions of one with reference to the conduct of another may be such as to estop him from denying the existence of the relation. 

Merritt vs. Bissell, 155 N. Y. 396; Hoyt vs. Thompson's Executor, 19 N. Y. 207, at page 219. For a case illustrating a limitation on the power of parties to create relationship of principal and agent by contract, see Sternaman vs. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 170 N. Y. 13. Contrast this case with 

Allen vs. Insurance Co., 123 N. Y. 6. See also Drilling vs. 

N. Y. Life Ins. Co., 234 N. Y. 234.

After the enactment of Section 58 of the Insurance Law (1906) Sternaman vs. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. ceased to be authority on the question of the liability of an insurance company on a policy issued under similar circumstances. 

Minster vs. Insurance Company, 254 N. Y. 333.

Distinction Between Agent and Servant. The essential distinction between an agent and a servant is in the nature of their respective duties. An agent is a business representative. 

His employment contemplates bringing the principal into contractual relationship with third person; while the function of a servant is to render services under the direction and control of his master which do not result in contractual obligations between his master and others. It has frequently been advanced as an element in distinguishing an agent from a servant, that an agent is vested with more or less discretion while a servant usually acts under express directions from his master. Wharton on Evidence (sec. 1182) states that: "An agent is authorized to exercise discretion; when a servant is authorized to exercise discretion then he ceases to be a servant and becomes an agent." 

However, I may direct my stock broker to buy a certain. share of stock at a certain stipulated price, and he is unquestionably my agent, although he can exercise no discretion in the premises. Also, I may permit my chauffeur to use the utmost discretion in the operation of my automobile, and he is still my servant, as far as the operation of the car is concerned. A person may be a servant as to one branch of a transaction, and an agent as to another. Kingan vs. Silver, 13 Ind. App. 80. If I direct my chauffeur to purchase automobile accessories, he becomes, in that transaction, my agent, for he is bringing me into a contractual relation with a third party. 

Mr. Justice Holmes in his edition of Kent's Commen taries (12th Edition, Vol. II, page 269) says: 

"The distinction between a servant and an agent is the distinction between serving and acting for." 

Dwight on Persons and Personal Property, page 323: 

"The great and fundamental distinction between a servant and an agent is that the former is principally employed to do an act for the employer, not resulting in a

contract between the master and a third person, while the main office of an agent is to make such a contract. 

Servants may make contracts incidentally while agents may in the same way render acts of service. The principal distinction between them is as above stated." 

It is frequently important to distinguish between these two relations. In Wakefield vs. Fargo, 90 N. Y. 213, an act was construed making stockholders in a corporation liable for debts owing to their "laborers, servants and apprentices." A person who was bookkeeper and general manager of a corporation was held not to be within that class. 

Distinction Between Agency and Trust. In Agency, the legal title and use of the property involved is in the principal, while in the relationship of Trust, the legal title is in the trustee and the use in the cestui. Simon vs. Burgess, 71 

Misc. 300. See also Taylor vs. Davis' Administrators, 110

U. S. 330. An agent is frequently spoken of as a quasi trustee, and is required to account in equity. Marvin vs. Brooks, 94 N. Y. 71; Uhlman vs. N. Y. Life Ins. Co., 109 N. Y. 421.

Distinction Between Agency and Partnership. While every partner is an agent of the partnership within the scope of the partnership business, not every agent is a partner of his principal even though he may share the profits of his principal's business by way of compensation for services rendered. 

See Partnership Law-Laws of 1919, Chap. 408, Art. 2, Sec. 

11, and Art. 3. 

An agent does not act for himself, but for his principal alone, whereas a partner, in partnership affairs, acts as principal for himself and as agent for the other partners. See 

Meehan vs. Valentine, 145 U. S. 611.

As to whether or not a certain contract entered into between A and B constitutes them partners, or merely makes one the agent of the other, is a question of construction. 

Every doubtful case will be solved with reference to the intent of the parties. Post vs. Kimberly, 9 Johns (N. Y.) 470.

Distinction Between Agency and Sale. Although at first blush the distinction between these two relations may appear obvious, yet situations of the following types may arise. A may direct B to accumulate certain goods and to deliver them at a designated place on a certain day. In this transaction is 

B the agent of A in purchasing the goods, or is it the intention of the parties that B purchase the goods on his own account and then re-sell them to A? See Keswick vs. Rafter, 35 A. D. 508; aff'd., 165 N. Y. 653.

The following tests if applied to a situation of this type are helpful in solving the problem: 

1. Is B to supply the goods at a fixed price, irrespective of market fluctuations? If so, a sale probably results. 

2. Whose credit is to be pledged in procuring the goods? 

3. Does A direct from whom, where, and upon what terms the goods are to be procured? If so, the relation savors of agency. Black vs. Webb, 20 Ohio 304; 55 Am. 

Dec. 456.

Again, A places certain goods in the possession of B to be disposed of, and B agrees to turn over at periodic intervals the agreed value of the goods, keeping to himself the difference between the agreed value and the price that he obtains for the goods. This relation may either be an agency to sell or a sale. To solve a situation of this type, the following tests may be helpful. 

1. Is B liable as debtor for the price to be paid, or is he liable for the proceeds of the sale, less commissions? 

2. Has A power to fix the price and terms of the sale, and to recall the goods if necessary? 

3. Has A passed title to the goods?

A great number of doubtful contracts of this general type have been the subject of judicial construction, and in the fol-

lowing cases, the relationship of Principal and Agent was held to exist: Cole vs. Mann, 62 N. Y., 1; Sturm vs. Broker, 150

U. S. 312; Colyler vs. Krakauer, 122 A. D. 797. 

It was held in Kaempfer vs. Tompkins-Kiel Marble Co., Inc., 242 N. Y. 376, that a contract appointing defendant "sole sales agent" for five years with renewal privilege, and specifying quantity to be taken yearly as required, was a contract of agency and not of sale. The decision was based upon the in-. tention of the parties as expressed in their written agreement. 

In a number of cases, of which the following are examples, the contract between the parties was held to constitute a sale; 

De Miles Medical Co. vs. Park, 164 Fed. 803, 220 U. S. 373; 

Conn vs. Chambers, 113 A. D. 298; aff'd 195 N. Y. 538; 

Baldwin vs. Feder, 135 A. D. 97; Snelling vs. Arbuckle Bros., 104 Ga. 362; 30 S. E. 863. For complete discussion see

L. R. A. 1917 B at page 626; L. R. A. 1915 B at p. 110. 

Distinction Between Agency and Bailment. A may de liver goods to B to keep for him or to sell. If the goods are delivered for safekeeping the relation is that of bailor and bailee and the bailee has no authority to sell as agent, and cannot consequently convey good title. Ordinarily possession alone gives no authority to sell (Utica Trust Co. vs. Decker, 244 N. Y. 340), but possession coupled with indicia of ownership will estop a true owner from attacking the title of an innocent purchaser for value. Smith vs. Clews, 114 N. Y. 190; 

Marine Banks vs. Fiske, 71 N. Y. 353. A more detailed discussion of this matter will be reserved for a later chapter. 

Distinction Between Agency and Lease. A lessor is not liable for the negligent acts of a lessee in the conduct of the leased premises, whereas a principal is liable for the torts of his agent committed in the furtherance of his principal's busi- 

Miller vs. N. Y. Cent. R. R. 125 N. Y. 118; Marsh vs. 

Hand, 120 N. Y. 315; Harrison vs. McClellan, 137 A. D. 508. ness.

The true nature of the contract determines whether the relation of Landlord and Tenant or Principal and Agent exists. In Petteway vs. McIntyre, 42 S. E. Rep. 851, a lumber company and B entered into agreement called a "lease" which recited in substance that the company was the owner of a mill and lumber rights and that B was desirous of manufacturing timber. The company leased the premises to B for a period of three years at a yearly rental of $1, and the net proceeds of the transaction. The company agreed to pay B a certain stipulated yearly salary. It was held that the company was liable for the expenses of the operation of the mill by B under his "lease" as he was an agent of the company. Had he been a lessee, B alone would have been liable for the operating expenses. See Hine vs. Cushing 53 Hun. (N. Y.) 519; 6 N. Y. 

Supp. 850.

Distinction Between Agent and Independent Contractor. 

The difference between these two relations is, does the employer retain any control or right of control over the means or method by which the work is done? Kings vs. N. Y. Cent. 

R. R. 66 N. Y. 181; Kirby vs. Steel Co., 109 A. D. 334.

If the employer retains the right of general supervision and management of the work, he makes the employe his agent or servant, but if he leaves choice of means and method to the employe, he makes him an independent contractor. 2 Corpus 

Juris 424, Kirby vs. Lackawanna Steel Co., 109 A. D. 334; 

95 N. Y. Supp. 833; Casement vs. Brown, 148 U. S. 615.

Creation of the Relation. Agency is a legal relation created by contract; therefore as a general rule one can be made neither a principal nor an agent except with his consent. Raney & Cheney v. Weed, 3 Sandf. 577 (N. Y. 1850).

But an express contract is not necessary to create the relationship. The consent to be agent or principal may be implied from certain conduct on the part of each. 

A principal is not bound by his agent's contracts unless he authorized him to make such contracts. This author-

ity may be either express or implied from usage, custom or other facts, or the so-called principal may have so represented that another was his agent that he is estopped to deny the authority as against an innocent third person. 

Where there was no original authority, express or implied, the lack of it may be cured by a subsequent ratification which has the effect of conferring power and authority ab initio. 

One may in legal effect be an agent although he is to receive no compensation for so acting. A gratuitous agent may bind his principal by contract to a third person if he was authorized by the principal to make such contract. So far as third persons are concerned, the important question is whether the agent was authorized to act, not whether he was promised a compensation for doing so. 

The following facts may show a principal-agent relationship between A and B: (1) B's right to require obedience from A, (2) B's right to give A instructions, (3) B's right to dismiss A, (4) A is paid by B, (5) A was appointed by B, (6) there was a contract between B and A under which A was to act for B. Stenaman vs. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 170 N. Y. 13; Raney & Cheney vs. Weed, 3 Sandf. 577 (N. Y. 1850).

Classification. With reference to the nature of their duties and the extent of the authority delegated to them, agents may be classified under the following three headings:

Universal 

1. 

2. General 3. Special 

A Universal Agent can at best have but a theoretical existence, as were such an agent possible, he would be authorized to do every conceivable act that his principal might do. 

No matter how broad a grant of power to an agent may be, it cannot possibly provide for every emergency that may arise.

Store on Agency §21.

"A general authority to do an indefinite number of acts of a particular kind by no means constitutes a universal agency." Farmers' and Traders' Bank vs. Butchers' Bank, 16 N. Y. 125.

A General Agent is one who represents his principal in a particular line of business--as the manager of a store; or he is one whose business or profession is to transact for any or all persons a particular kind of business, as for instance a lawyer, factor, etc. Jacques vs. Todd, 3 Wend. (N. Y.) 83.

Evidence that a mother had allowed her son to act generally for her in reference to the consummation of the enterprise in hand, or to a particular line of business such as purchasing or selling shares of stock for her or managing her store in her absence, was held to be sufficient to constitute him her general agent. Gibson vs. Snow Hardware Co., 94 Ala. 346; 

Small vs. Houseman, 208 N. Y. 115; New York City Car Advertising Co. vs. Greenberg, 142 N. Y. Supp. 226. It is not necessary, in order to constitute a general agent, that he should have before done an act the same in specie with that in question. If he has usually done things of the same general character and effect with the assent of his principals, that is enough. Commercial Bank of Lake Erie vs. Norton, 1 Hill 

501 (N. Y. 1841).

A Special Agent is one authorized to do some particular act or to act upon some particular occasion. 2 L. R. A., p. 808 

Note. He acts usually in accordance with specific instructions, or under limitations necessarily implied from the nature of the act to be done. (Mechem on Agency Vol 8, page 40.) 

2 Corpus Juris 428. See Dudley vs. Perkins, 235 N. Y. 448. 

In Corklite Co. vs. Rell Realty Corp., 249 N. Y. 1, the distinction between general and special agent is given as follows: "A person who has power to transact all the business of his principal of a particular kind is a general agent. A person who is authorized to act only in a speci-

fic transaction is a special agent. The mere fact that the authority of the agent is limited to a particular business does not make his agency special."

Many refinements as to the liability of a principal for the acts of his agent have been built on the distinction between General and Special agents. Thus it has been held that third persons do not need to inquire into the exact extent of a general agent's authority, but have the right to rely upon the usual or implied authority customarily conferred upon such general agents. Gibson vs. Snow Hardware Co., 94 Ala. 346.

However, where the third person knows at the time that he is dealing with a special agent, he must at his peril ascertain the extent of the special agent's authority. Martin vs. Farnsworth, 49 N. Y. 555; Molloy vs. Whitehall Portland Cement 

Co., 116 A. D. 839, 102 N. Y. Supp. 363 (App. Div. 2nd Dept., 1907); Metropolitan Aluminum Mfg. Co. vs. Lau, 61 Misc. 

105, 112 N. Y. Supp. 1059; Dudley vs. Perkins, 235 N. Y. 448.

But if the proper exercise of the agent's authority depended upon the existence of an extraneous fact, peculiarly within the knowledge of the agent, and not readily ascertainable by the third person, the latter has the right to rely upon the special agent's act as itself a representation that the fact upon which the proper exercise of his authority depended, really did exist, and the principal is bound by such representation even though it were false. In Farmers' & Mechanics' Bank vs. 

Butchers' Bank, 16 N. Y. 125, it was said: "It is, I think, a sound rule, that where the party dealing with an agent has ascertained that the act of the agent corresponds in every particular in regard to which such party has or is presumed to have any knowledge, with the terms of the power, he may take the representation of the agent as to any extrinsic fact which rests peculiarly within the knowledge of the agent, and which cannot be ascertained by a comparison of the power with the act done under it."

In accord see Mach vs. Bryant Park Bank, 197 App. Div. 

78, and N. Y. N. H. & H. R. Co. vs. Schuyler, 34 N. Y. 30, where the court said: "It is impossible to escape the conclusion that the law of this state is settled that where the authority of an agent depends upon some facts outside the terms of his power, and which from its nature rests particularly within his knowledge, the principal is bound by the representations of the agent, although false, as to the existence of such fact."

Another common classification of agents is into del credere agents and non del credere agents. 

A del credere agent is one who, in consideration of increased compensation, guarantees the payment of any debt arising out of his agency. An example of this type of agent is a commission merchant who guarantees the payment of goods he sells on commission. In England, a del credere agent was formerly considered a surety; that is, the legal effect of this relation was construed to be that he guaranteed the solvency of the vendee. In the United States, he is held primarily liable for the proceeds of the goods sold, as for goods sold to him. Sherwood vs. Stone, 14 N. Y. 267; Wolff vs. 

Koppel, 5 Hill (N. Y.) 458; Shoyer vs. Wright-Ginsberg Co., 240 N. Y. 223.

His obligation, being original, is not within the Statute of 

Frauds. Sherwood vs. Stone, 14 N. Y. 267. See Shoyer vs. 

Wright-Ginsberg Co., 240 N. Y. 223. 

It was held in Gihon vs. Stanton, 9 N. Y. 476, that if after a del credere agent has made advances to his principal, the principal becomes bankrupt, the agent cannot prove the advances against the bankrupt estate until he has exhausted the property in his hands.

A non del credere agent is, of course, the opposite of a del credere agent.

Particular Designations. Certain classes of agents are so well known and of such importance in the business world, that particular designations or titles have been applied to the members of the class, as for instance, attorneys, brokers, factors, auctioneers, etc. Around each of these various classes have grown up special rules of law which will be considered in detail at a later period. For the present, brief definitions will suffice. 

Attorney in Fact is an agent authorized by power of attorney to sign a deed or other formal document. 

Attorney at Law is an agent whose profession is to give advice and assistance in legal matters. (Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 43.)

In all that properly relates to the conduct of a trial, an attorney represents his client and is his authorized agent. 

Humphries vs. Shapiro, 175 N. Y. Supp. 426.

An attorney may in general control the management of the matter intrusted to him, but he may not compromise the rights of his client outside of his conduct of the action, or accept less than the full satisfaction sought, or release his client's rights or subject him to a new cause of action. Lewis vs. Duane, 141 N. Y. 302. Broker. "A broker is one, who, as middleman brings persons together to bargain or bargains for them in the private purchase or sale of property of any sort, not ordinarily in his possession." Bishop, Contracts, p. 1135. "A broker is a specialist employed as a middleman to negotiate between the parties, a sale or other business contract." Wharton on Agency, p. 695. "A broker is an agent employed to make bargains and contracts between other persons, in matters of trade, commerce or navigation, for a compensation, commonly called brokerage." Story on Agency, p. 28.

Factor. 

A factor is a commission merchant. He differs from a broker in that he has possession of the goods and usually sells them in his own name. 

Auctioneer. "An auctioneer is one whose business is to sell or dispose of property, rights or privileges at public competitive sale, to the person or persons offering or accepting the terms most favorable to the owner." Mechem on Agency, Vol. 

I, page 43. While primarily he is the agent of the seller in making the terms of sale, he becomes the agent of the buyer when he accepts the latter's bid and enters his name on the memorandum of sale.

Legal Recognition. While as a rule no presumptions are indulged in as to the nature and extent of an agent's authority, but such must be established by evidence, yet where the duties of a particular agent are well defined by custom, it will be assumed in absence of express restrictions that such an agent's authority extends to the performance of the acts which according to usages of business, he is ordinarily employed to do. 

Farmers' Bank vs. Butchers' Bank, 16 N.. Y. 125.

CHAPTER II. 

CAPACITY OF PARTIES. 

Who May Be Principal. The general rule is that anyone who is competent to contract is capable of acting through an agent.

Joint Principals. Persons whose interests are joint may unite in appointing one of their own number or a stranger to act on behalf of all. Existence of a common interest in several persons does not imply authority in one to represent the others, nor to appoint an agent in behalf of all. The assent of all is necessary. Corlies vs. Cumming, 6 Cowan (N. Y.) 

181; Noe vs. Christie, 51 N. Y. 270. Lawlor vs. 'Lowe, 235 

U. S. 522.

All however, may acquiesce in the act of one as their agent so as to make his act their act. Clute vs. Clute, 197 N. Y. 439.

The general principle herein announced appears to have its most conspicuous and only exception in the law of partnership. Within the scope of the partnership business, each partner is the agent of all the other partners for the transaction of the partnership affairs, and the appointment by one partner of an agent within these limits is an appointment by all. See Partnership Law (Laws of 1919, Chap. 408) Article 

3, Sec. 20.

Voluntary Associations, Societies, Clubs and Committees. 

Voluntary associations, societies, clubs and committees are not in their nature partnerships. No power arises merely from the existence of such bodies which will justify one or more of the members in pledging the credit of others. The express or implied assent of each member sought to be charged must be established before liability accrues. McCabe vs. 

Goodfellow, 133 N. Y. 89; Ostrom vs. Greene, 161 N. Y. 353; 

Lafond vs. Deems, 81 N. Y. 514; Hale vs. Hirsch, 205 App. 

Div. 308; Willcox vs. Arnold, 162 Mass 577; Fruchley vs. 

Eagleson, 15 Ind. App. 88; Cousins vs. Taylor, 115 Ore. 472, 239 Pac. 96.

This expressed or implied assent may be established in the following ways. The constitution or by-laws of the body may provide that certain obligations may be entailed upon a majority vote or at the discretion of the officers. By joining such a body, one impliedly consents in advance to be bound by obligations incurred as above set forth. In the absence of such a provision in the constitution or by-laws, one can only be charged with liability when he expressly or impliedly assents to the incurring of the obligation. This may be proved either by his previous consent or by his subsequent adoption, or by his acquiescence in an established course of dealing. 

See Ostrom vs. Greene, 161 N. Y. 353; Stikeman vs. Flack, 58 A. D. 277; Lawlor vs. Lowe, 235 U. S. 522. 

In Jones vs. Maher, 62 Misc. 388; 116 N. Y. Supp. 180, aff'd 141 A. D. 919, 125 N. Y. Supp. 1126, it was held that contributing money to a strike fund constituted an adoption, by the members of a union so contributing, of all acts theretofore done by the union officials in the prosecution of the strike in question.

Article 3 of the General Associations Law, formerly the Joint Stock Association Law as amended by Chapter 915 of 

Laws of 1920, outlines the proceeding to be followed in bringing suit against an unincorporated association of seven or more members.

Corporations. Corporations both public and private usually have provisions in their charters empowering them to appoint agents to transact the corporate functions. In the absence of express provisions, power to appoint necessary agents will be implied. Morawitz on Corporations, Vol. I, section 503; Thompson on Corporations, Vol. V, section 5832; 

Hull vs. Wisconsin, 103 U. S. 5.

WHO MAY NOT BE PRINCIPALS. 

Infants. Until recently, it was the law in New York 

State that the appointment by an infant of an agent for any purpose whatsoever was a void act. Fonda vs. Van Horn, 15

Wendell (N. Y.) 631; Robbins vs. Mount, 4 Robt. (N. Y.) 

533; Bennett vs. Davis, 6 Cowan (N. Y.) 393; Room vs. Stafford, 7 Cowan (N. Y.) 179; Bool vs. Mix, 17 Wendell (N. Y.) 

120; Wamsley vs. Darragh, 14 Misc. (N. Y.) 566; Casey vs. 

Kastel, 119 Misc. (N. Y.) 116. 

In an action of Casey vs. Kastel, et al, 237 N. Y. 305, the

Court of Appeals held that the appointment of an agent by an infant for the purpose of selling a chattel, was a voidable act, and the resulting sale merely voidable and not void. However, the court did not pass on the question as to whether or not the appointment by an infant of an agent to confess judgment, or to represent the infant in a judicial proceeding, was voidable or void. By quoting in its opinion the following from 

Williston on Contracts (Vol. I, page 444), the court indicated that such an attempted appointment would be void: "Probably courts would still hold an infant unable to authorize a confession of judgment or to appoint an attorney for judicial proceedings."

Insane Persons. The weight of authority supports the rule that the appointment of an agent by an insane person before office found and the acts of the agent before an adjudication are voidable and not void. Blinn vs. Schwarz, 177 

N. Y. 252; Merritt vs. Merritt, 43 A. D. 68; 59 N. Y. Supp. 

357; Wamsley vs. Darragh, 12 Misc. 199; 33 N. Y. Supp. 274.

In so far as the contract is fair and is entered into in good faith without knowledge of the insanity and has been acted upon it is binding upon the principal unless the principal can place the other party in statu quo. Mutual Life Ins. Co. vs. 

Hunt, 79 N. Y. 541.

After an adjudication of insanity, contracts entered into by the incompetent are void. Carter vs. Beckwith, 128 N. Y. 312.

Therefore, an appointment of an agent by a judicially declared incompetent is void, and all acts done by the alleged

agent under the power are of no effect. Wamsley vs. Darragh, 12 Misc., 199; 33 N. Y. S. 274; Marvin vs. Inglis, 39 How Pr. 

329; Van Dusen vs. Sweet, 51 N. Y. 378. 

In Finch vs. Goldstein, 245 N. Y. 300, it was held that the term "office found" refers to proceedings under the Civil Practice Act and not to a commitment under the Insanity Law. 

The effect upon an existing valid contract of agency of the subsequent insanity of the principal, will be discussed in a later chapter. 

Aliens. An alien friend, whether resident or non-resident of this country, may appoint an agent to act for him. An alien. enemy cannot make any contract which involves communication across the lines of hostilities, hence a non-resident alien enemy could not appoint an agent in this country. U. S. vs. 

Grossmayer, 76 U. S. 72; Hubbard vs. Matthews, 54 N. Y. 43; 

Panee vs. Soler, 101 Misc. 693, 167 N. Y. S. 901; Rothbard vs. 

Herzfeld, 179 A. D. 865, 167 N. Y. S. 199, aff'd 223 N. Y. 578. "The disability of alien enemies is not a prohibition against dealing with all of the enemy nationality, but is a prohibition against those who reside and do business in the enemy country." Schultz Jr. Co. vs. Raimes & Co., 100 Misc. 697, 166 N. Y. S. 567; Arndt-Ober vs. Metropolitan Opera Co., 102 Misc. 320, 169 N. Y. S. 304, modified in 182 A. D. 513, 169 N. Y. S. 944.

The effect of the outbreak of war between the country of the principal and the country of the agent on an existing contract of agency will be considered in a later chapter. 

Married Women. At Common Law a married woman was incapable of contracting, and hence could not appoint an agent or act through an agent. Modern statutes have removed practically all the disabilities of coverture, and now a married woman has power to appoint an agent to act in all those matters which she may lawfully do herself. She may even appoint

her husband to be her agent. Nash vs. Mitchell, 71 N. Y. 199; 

Bodine vs. Killeen, 53 N. Y. 93; Buckley vs. Wells, 33 N. Y. 517. 

Trust Estates. A trust estate cannot be a principal since a principal presupposes a person who can consent to being principal. But a trust estate cannot give consent because it has no will of its own nor separate existence as a legal entity. It follows, therefore, that a trustee cannot act as agent of the trust estate. Taylor vs. Davis' Administrators, 100 U. S. 

330.

WHO MAY BE AGENTS. 

Infants. An infant may act as an agent. This, however, is subject to the exception that the infant must possess sufficient mental capacity for the business intrusted to him. Tiffany on Agency, page 107; Huffcut on Agency (2nd Edition), page 34; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 110; Whitemarsh vs. Hall, 3 Denio (N. Y.) 375.

However as between the principal and the infant agent, the contract is voidable. Whitemarsh vs. Hall, 3 Denio (N. Y.) 375.

So also, as will be pointed out later, third parties cannot hold an infant agent to those liabilities that adult agents are often subjected to. 

Lunatics. Story on Agency, section 7, states: 

"That an idiot, lunatic or person otherwise non compos mentis cannot do any act, as an agent or attorney, binding upon the principal."

Yet, if a principal knowingly appoints an insane person as his agent, or permits such a one to act for him after knowledge of the insanity he will not be excused by reason of it. 

(21 Ruling Case Law, page 819). Central of Georgia R. Co. 

vs. Hall, 124 Ga. 322, 52 S. E. 679; 4 L. R. A. (N. S.) 898.

Married Women. Despite the legal fiction existing at 

Common Law that a husband and wife were one, a married woman could act as agent for her husband or a third party. 

Hopkins vs. Mollinieux, 4 Wend. (N. Y.) 465; Goodwin vs. 

Kelly, 42 Barb. (N. Y.) 194.

Modern statutes have removed any doubts that may have existed as to the soundness of this ruling. 

Corporations. Within the scope of their corporate powers, corporations may act as agents either for individuals or for other corporations. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 127; 

Jemison vs. Citizens Savings Bank, 122 N. Y. 135. 

Partnerships. Partnerships are competent agents within the scope of their partnership powers. When a power to act is delegated to a partnership, in absence of express restrictions, either partner may execute the authority. Mechem on 

Agency, Vol. I, page 128.

Persons Adversely Interested. An agent, as will be more fully discussed in a later chapter, owes the utmost fidelity and loyalty to his principal's interest. Therefore an agent generally cannot represent both parties to a transaction or serve as an agent in a matter in which his personal interest is diametrically opposed to that of his principal. Carr vs. National 

Bank, etc. Co., 167 N. Y. 375; Taussig vs. Hart, 58 N. Y. 425; 

U. S. vs. Carter, 217 U. S. 286; Gardner vs. Ogden, 22 N. Y. 

327; Girardi vs. Irving, 174 N. Y. S. 701. See Leathers vs. 

Canfield, 45 L. R. A. 33; Orthey vs. Bogan, 236 N. Y. 351.

However, an agent may act for two contracting parties, if his duties to each do not conflict, as where he acts as a mere intermediary to bring parties together to bargain. Pollatscheck vs. Goodwin, 17 Misc. 587.

Again, an agent may act for both parties to a transaction, if he does so with their full knowledge and consent. 

N. Y. Ins. Co. vs. National Ins. Co., 14 N. Y. 85; Rowe vs. 

Stevens, 53 N. Y. 621; Joslin vs. Cowee, 56 N. Y. 626; Whit-

ing vs. Saunders, 22 Misc. 529; Fox Co. vs. Wohl, 255 N. Y. 268. 

It was held in Loew vs. Gillespie, 153 N. Y. Supp. 830, that an attorney employed by the city could not recover for services rendered from a client in a city department, whom he represented in an action to obtain salary from the city. 

In re Moran, 130 N. Y. Supp. 432, held that a contract made by the trustees of a village with a firm of which one of the trustees was a member, was void. 

In Peo. ex rel. Schenectady Illuminating Co. vs. Cyprus, 151 N. Y. S. 1012, a member of the board of supervisors was secretary and treasurer of an illuminating company which sold certain lamps to the city. It was held that such sale was void, irrespective of financial interest. 

In Wendt vs. Fischer, 243 N. Y. 439, a contract of sale of real property was held voidable at the option of the seller where it appeared that one of the brokers was an officer and owned all of the stocks of the buyer corporation. The fact that the price was fair was immaterial. 

Unlicensed Agencies. Certain agents, for example, Attorneys at Law, Auctioneers, Brokers, etc., are required by law. to procure licenses or certificates as a condition precedent to their engaging in their vocation. Hall vs. Bishop, 3 Daly (N. 

Y.)) 109; Stout vs. Wm. Kennelly, Inc., 218 N. Y. Supp. 259.

The usual effect of a failure to secure a license is to preclude the agent from recovering compensation for his services. 

Tiffany on Agency, page 109; Roman vs. Lobe, 213 A. D. 162; affirmed 243 N. Y. 51; Bendell vs. De Dominices, 251 N. Y. 305.

In Calhoun vs. Banner, 254 N. Y. 325, it was held that although the plaintiff was not a licensed real estate broker at the time he was employed, he may maintain an action for commissions if his negotiations with the customer took place after he received his license.

However, as was held in Harland vs. Lilienthal, 53 N. Y. 

438, where an attorney had been admitted to the state courts, was entitled to be admitted to the U. S. District Court, and did in fact practice therein without question, he may, in the absence of statutory provision or rules of the court prohibiting it, recover for services rendered in such court, though never formally admitted to practice therein. 

Real Estate Brokers. Section 442-f. of the Real Property 

Law exempts, among others, attorneys at law from securing a real estate broker's license. The opinion given by the At-, torney-General is that the reason for excepting attorneys at law was that their activities in the sale of real estate was more or less part of their professional duties and that the exemption does not run to attorneys as citizens but rather to them in their capacities as attorneys. If, therefore, an attorney at law chooses to deal in real estate as a business apart from the practice of his profession he should be licensed as other dealers. 35 St. Dept. Rep. 1925, p. 194. 

It was held in Weingast vs. Rialto Pastry Shop, Inc., 243

N. Y. 113, that the statute requiring real estate brokers to be licensed was not applicable to one who negotiates the sale of a business, although in the sale there was included a lease. 

Joint Agents. Authority to act may be given to two or more persons jointly or severally. If the authority is of a private nature, unless a contrary intention appears, it will be presumed to be joint, and all must unite in acting. Salisbury vs. Brisbane, 61 N. Y. 617; Hawley vs. Keeler, 53 N. Y. 116; 

Brenan vs. Wilson, 71 N. Y. 502; Unterberg vs. Elder, 211 

N. Y. 499; Paige vs. Faure, 229 N. Y. 114.

A different rule, however, exists in the cast of partnerships and agencies of a public nature. A power conferred upon a partnership may be executed by one of the partners. 

Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 145.

In Striker vs. Daly, 175 A. D. 620, it was held that where power to sell is given to two or more executors or trustees, and one resigns or is removed before the power is fully executed, the remaining executors or trustees may validly execute the power.

Public Agency. In agencies of a public nature, usually all the agents or officers must be present to deliberate or be duly notified of the meeting and have an opportunity to be present. Williams vs. School District, 21 Pick. (Mass.) 75; 

Horton vs. Garrison, 23 Barb. (N. Y.) 176.

If this condition is complied with, the action of a majority, if the number is such as to admit of a majority, is ordinarily deemed the action of the body. Peo. vs. Nichols, 52 

N. Y. 478.

However, the law sometimes requires the joint action of all. Powell vs. Tuttle, 3 N. Y. 396. 

It was held in Downing vs. Rugar, 21 Wend. (N. Y.) 178, that where the number is such as not to admit of a majority, as where there are only two agents, the concurrence of both is indispensable, though if one should die or become disabled the other might act alone, except in case of officers exercising powers of a judicial nature.

CHAPTER III. 

FOR WHAT PURPOSES AGENCY MAY BE CREATED. 

In General. An agent may be appointed to do any act which the principal might lawfully do. Authority may not, however, be delegated to (a) Perform an act essentially personal in nature. (b) Perform an act which is required by statute to be personally performed. (c) Do an act which is illegal, immoral, or opposed to public policy.

Personal Acts. Some acts are so essentially personal in their nature, that it requires but little reasoning to arrive at the conclusion that authority to perform the acts cannot be delegated to another. For example, an elector must cast his vote in person at a public election, and cannot send an agent to vote for him, unless there is some express provision of law permitting such conduct. Mechem on Public Officers, section 187.

Also, where powers requiring the exercise of discretion are conferred upon one, as for instance, an executor, trustee, or guardian, such powers cannot ordinarily be delegated. 

Lyon vs. Jerome, 26 Wend. (N. Y.) 485; Newton vs. Bronson, 13 N. Y. 587; Coleman vs. Beach, 97 N. Y. 545.

Similarly, a person cannot authorize another to make his will, although he may delegate the clerical duty of preparing it according to his wishes, and even may appoint another to affix his signature to the instrument in his presence. Contracts to marry may doubtless be made through a messenger, gut general authority to make marriage engagements could not be delegated. As to the act of marriage, the substitution of an agent or representative while at times allowed to princes, would not be tolerated generally. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I,

pages 86, 87. See Comments on "Marriage through an Agent" in Brissand, History of French Private Law. See also U. S. ex. rel. Aznar vs. Commissioner of Immigration, 298 Fed. 

Rep. 103.

In the absence of authorization in the charter or by-laws of a corporation, a stockholder cannot vote by proxy. 

Acts Required by Statute to be Personally Performed. 

Ordinarily an act required by statute to be performed, may be performed by an agent. The exception to this rule arises in those cases where a fair construction of the statute in question shows the legislative intention that the act he personally performed.

Thus, where a commissioner to take acknowledgments in another state is required by statute to subscribe his signature in the book of a certain official, the implication is clear that the signature must be in the handwriting of the commissioner. 

Finnegan vs. Lucy, 157 Mass. 439.

Acts Which are Illegal, Immoral or Opposed to Public 

Policy. In general, authority cannot be delegated to do an act which is illegal, immoral, or contrary to public policy. Of course, if an agent acts under such an invalid appointment, the principal cannot escape liability on that account. However, the courts will not entertain action between the guilty parties but will leave them both in the situation in which their own cupidity has placed them. Leonard vs. Poole, 114 N. Y. 371; 

Strebel vs. Lissberger, 151 N. Y. Supp. 822.

If under an appointment to do an unlawful act the agent does the act, both the agent and his principal are liable to criminal prosecution and in addition must respond in damages to the party injured.

As the principles which determine the illegality of contracts of agency are in the main the same as those that apply to other contracts, a brief discussion of some illustrative examples will suffice for the purposes of this work.

Services in Influencing Legislation. Contracts to render services in securing the passage of legislation are scrutinized very carefully by the courts. Although the parties to an agreement of this type do not stipulate for illegal action, yet if the contract calls for the use of personal solicitation, influence or persuasion with the members of the legislative body, it is void. 

Carey vs. Western Union Tel. Co., 47 Hun 610; Mills vs. 

Mills, 40 N. Y. 543; Veazey vs. Allen, 173 N. Y. 359.

It has been held that it matters not that in the particular case nothing improper was done or was expected to be done. 

If the employment in question tends necessarily and directly to such results, the contract will be condemned. Atchenson vs. Mallon, 43 N. Y. 147.

However, not all contracts for services to be rendered in securing the passage or defeat of legislation are illegal. An agent may be appointed to draft a bill, prepare and submit arguments publicly, collect facts, attend hearings, etc. If the services rendered are public in their nature and are directed toward reaching the understandings of the legislators, they are legitimate. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 57; Sedgwick vs. Stanton, 14 N. Y. 289; Chesebrough vs. Conover, 140 

N. Y. 382; Ersenstein vs. Maiden Lane Safe Deposit Co., 113 

N. Y. Supp. 967.

Sometimes cases arise where the result to be obtained could equally be attained by proper as by improper means, where the employment does not necessarily and directly tend toward the exercise of improper solicitations. Some courts, in their zeal to defeat lobbying agreements, presume that the improper means were to be employed, although there is abundant authority for the rule that the presumption of innocence should be indulged. Drake vs. Lauer, 98 A. D. 86, aff'd 182 

N. Y. 533; Salinas vs. Stillman, 66 Fed. 677.

Procuring Government Contracts. Employments to secure government contracts rest on identically the same principles as outlined above. If no personal influence or improper

1 means are to be employed, the agency is valid. Lyon vs. Mitchell, 36 N. Y. 235; Pease vs. Walsh, 49 How. Pr. (N. Y.) 

269; Swift vs. Aspell, 40 Misc. 453.

There appears to be a conflict between the New York and Federal rule with reference to the effect of the fact that the agent's compensation was contingent on his success in securing the contract. In Lyon vs. Mitchell, 36 N. Y. 235, it was held by a divided court that a contract, fair upon its face to obtain government contracts on a commission, which employment could be carried out by legitimate means, and where it did not appear that objectionable methods were contemplated, was valid.

Swift vs. Aspell, 40 Misc. 453, re-affirms this doctrine and states that it is still the rule in New York State. Massachusetts has adopted the New York rule. Kerr vs. American 

Pneumatic Service Company, 188 Mass. 27. The Federal rule as announced in Tool Co. vs. Norris, 2 Wall (U. S.) 45 is that "Agreements for compensation contingent upon success suggest the use of sinister and corrupt means for the accomplishment of the end desired. The law meets the suggestion of evil, and strikes down the contract from its inception."

This rule was later applied in Oscanyan vs. Arms. Co., 103 U. S. 261.

Miscellaneous Cases. 

Contracts to secure an appointment to public or private office where personal solicitation or improper means are to be used, are invalid. Gray vs. Hook, 4 

N. Y. 449; Hagar vs. Catlin, 18 Hun (N. Y.) 448; In re Clark, 182 N. Y. 222.

Contracts for services to be rendered in improperly influencing elections are unenforceable. Murphy vs. English, 64 

How. Pr. (N. Y.) 362.

Contracts of agency the objects of which are to secure pardons are carefully scrutinized. If the employment contem-

plates the use of personal influence or other sinister or improper means, the contract is void. 'Bremsen vs. Engler, 49 

N. Y. Super. Ct. 172.

The employment of an agent to secure such evidence as actually exists in a given case and may be lawfully produced is proper. But a contract to furnish evidence sufficient to win a case, or to procure witnesses to testify in a certain manner is void. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 71; Lyon vs. Hussey, 82 Hun (N. Y.) 15. 

It was held in Loffin vs. Billington, 86 N. Y. Supp. 267, that a contract with a physician for expert testimony in a personal injury action, in which his fee was contingent on the amount of the verdict, was bad. Services to be rendered by an agent in illegal dealings in stock and merchandise for his principal are invalid. If the parties merely intend to speculate on the rise and fall of the price of the goods, and do not intend a delivery, the transaction is a gambling one and is illegal. 

However, the courts indulge the presumption of innocence. 

Irwin vs. Williar, 110 U. S. 499; Cameron vs. Durkheim, 55 

N. Y. 425; Bigelow vs. Benedict, 70 N. Y. 202; Hurd vs. Taylor, 181 N. Y. 231.

Marriage brokerage contracts are opposed to public policy and are void, even though no fraud was practised on either party. Duval vs. Wellman, 124 N. Y. 156; Place vs. Conklin, 34 A.D. 191; Crawford vs. Russell 62 Barb. (N. Y.) 92.

Delegation of Authority. In the absence of authority, either express or implied, an agent must personally execute the trust imposed upon him and cannot delegate its performance to another so as to affect the rights of his principal. 

Lewis vs. Ingersoll, 3 Abb. Dec. (N. Y.) 55; Lyon vs. Jerome, 26 Wend. (N. Y.) 485; Carroll vs. Tucker, 2 N. Y. Misc. 397; 

Callinan vs. Bowker, 180 N. Y. 93; People vs. Public Service 

Commission, 143 A. D. 769 (aff'd 202 N. Y. 547).

This rule applies also in general to servants. Eaton vs. 

Delaware, etc. R. Co., 57 N. Y. 382.

Thus in Commercial Bank vs. Norton, 1 Hill 501, it was held that a person authorized to accept bills of exchange or to make promissory notes could not delegate this authority to another. See also People vs. Public Service Commission, 143 A. D. 769, 128 N. Y. S. 384; McConnell vs. Macklin, 22 

A. D. 537, 48 N. Y. S. 18; Fellows vs. Northrup, 39 N. Y. 117.

As to the application of this rule to real estate agents, see Carroll vs. Tucker, 2 Misc. 397; Bonwell vs. Howes, 15 Daly (N. Y.) 43; and to agents of casualty and surety companies, see Cullinan vs. Bowker, 180 N. Y. 93.

Attorneys at law are also within this rule. Meaney vs. 

Rosenberg, 32 Misc. 96; Reese vs. Resburgh, 54 A. D. 378; as are factors, brokers, auctioneers, and executors. Laverty vs. 

Snethen, 68 N. Y. 522; Hicks vs. Doin, 42 N. Y. 51; Gates vs. 

Dudgeon, 173 N. Y. 426. See also Marks vs. Marasco, 150 

N. Y. S. 792; Peo. ex rel. Cohoes Ry. Co. vs. P. S. C., 128 

N. Y. S. 384.

The reason for the general rule is apparent. Ordinarily an agent is selected with a view to his personal fitness, and inasmuch as confidence is generally at the basis of the appointment, the agent, owes to his principal the duty of personal attention to the commission, unless the principal has expressly or impliedly consented to the appointment of a substitute. Of course, a principal may expressly authorize the appointment of sub-agents, and by so doing, he confers authority upon the latter in advance. Authority to appoint sub-agents will sometimes be implied from the nature of the act to be performed, and sometimes from the nature of the agency. The instances where authority to appoint sub-agents will be implied may be classified under the following headings:

I. Performance of Ministerial Acts. When an agent has determined upon the propriety of a certain course of action, he has implied authority to entrust the performance of any purely mechanical act entailed to another. Gibson vs. Nat. 

Park Bank, 98 N. Y. 87; People vs. Bank of N. A., 75 N. Y.

547. Thus an agent employed to execute commercial paper, or to bind his principal by an acceptance of commercial paper, may, after satisfying himself as to the propriety of the act, delegate the performance of the mechanical act of signing the note or acceptance to a sub agent. Commercial Bank vs. Norton, 1 Hill (N. Y.) 501.

Similarly, an agent employed to sell land may employ a sub-agent to exhibit it, and an insurance agent may employ clerks to collect premiums and deliver policies. 

II Implication from Nature of Business. The authority of an agent is usually construed to include the necessary and usual means to execute it properly. Mechem on Agency, Vol. 

I, page 232.

Thus, if one is commissioned to effect the collection of a claim, he would have implied authority to employ an attorney. In Egan vs. De Jonge, 113 N. Y. Supp. 737, it was held that an agent to buy land had implied power to employ an attorney "to close the title."

Similarly, if an agent is placed in general charge of a business which extends over a large territory, he is ordinarily deemed to have implied power to appoint necessary assistants. 

Bodine vs. Exchange Ins. Co., 51 N. Y. 117; First Nat. Bank vs. Comm. Trav. Assn., 108 A. D. 78, aff'd 185 N. Y. 575.

This rule also applies to the appointment of an agent to control a business which is so extensive that one man could not possibly manage it. National S. S. Co. vs. Sheehan, 122 

N. Y. 461.

If a well established custom exists in a certain trade or profession and the principal appoints an agent to act for him with reference to a matter embraced in such trade or profession, in the absence of express stipulation to the contrary, the principal will be deemed to have acquiesced to such custom. 

Gray vs. Murray, 3 John Ch. (N. Y.) 167; Arf vs. Star Fire 

Ins. Co., 125 N. Y. 57; Carpenter vs. Insurance Co., 135 N. Y. 

298; Lamson vs. Simms, 48 N. Y. Super. Ct. 281.

Thus, where by custom, a factor is authorized to employ another to dispose of the goods, such authority will be implied. 

Tiffany on Agency, page 121.

III Implication From Emergency. There is authority for the statement that in an unforeseen emergency, an agent has implied authority to appoint such sub-agents as are necessary under the circumstances. Guilliam vs. Twist, 2 Q. B. 

84; Sloan vs. Central Iowa Ry. Co., 62 Iowa 728; Fox vs. 

Chicago, etc., Ry. Co., 86 Iowa 368; Georgia Pac. Ry. Co. vs. 

Probst, 85 Ala. 203; Louisville, etc., Ry. Co. vs. Ginley, 100 

Tenn. 472; Hollidge vs. Duncan, 199 Mass. 121; Scarff vs. 

Metcalf, 107 N. Y. 211; for complete information see Hanscom vs. Minneapolis Street Ry. Co., 20 L. R. A. 695.

However, as was announced in the English case of Guilliam vs. Twist, 2 Q. B. 84, the impossibility of communicating with the principal is the foundation of the doctrine of an agency of necessity. If the agent has an opportunity to communicate with his principal, no implication of authority to appoint a sub-agent would arise. 

The application of the doctrine of the implication of authority in an agent to appoint other agents in cases of emergency has arisen in instances where conductors, or other railroad employees have employed, on the company's account, physicians or nurses to attend injured employees. Terre 

Haute & Indianapolis R. Co. vs. Stockwell, 118 Ind. 98. 

It was held in Scarff vs. Metcalf, 107 N. Y. 211, that where a seaman becomes ill in a distant port, the master of the vessel stands in the position of agent for the owner in procuring medical attention.

Relation of the Parties. A sub-agent, appointed by express or implied authority of a principal, is, as far as third parties are concerned, the agent of the principal, and the acts of the sub-agent within the scope of his agency are binding on the principal. Murphy vs. Emmigration Comm., 28 N. Y. 

134. But whether or not privity of contract exists between

the principal and the sub-agent-so that the principal may look to the sub-agent for redress, and the sub-agent may look to the principal for compensation-depends on whether the principal expressly or impliedly agreed to the appointment of the sub-agent as his agent or as his agent's agent. This distinction was made in the case of Allen vs. Merchant's Bank, 22

Wend. (N. Y:) 215, as follows: 

"There is a wide difference made as well by positive law as by the reason of the thing itself between a contract or undertaking to do a thing, and the delegation of an agent or attorney to procure the doing of the same thing; between a contract for building a house, for example, and the appointment of an overseer or superintendent, authorized and undertaking to act for the principal in having the house built. The contractor is bound to answer for any negligence or default in the performance of his contract, although such negligence or default be not his own, but that of some sub-contractor or under workman. 

Not so the mere representative agent who discharges his whole duty if he acts with good faith and ordinary diligence in the selection of his materials, the forming of his contracts and the choice of his workmen." See Exchange 

National Bank vs. Third National Bank, 112 U. S. 276.

The determination of whether the principal expressly or impliedly authorized his agent to create privity of contract between the sub-agent and himself, depends sometimes on the construction of the words creating the power (Wicks vs. 

Hatch, 62 N. Y. 535), and sometimes from the character of the business, and the customs and usages of the agency. National S. S. Co. vs. Sheehan, 122 N. Y. 461.

If it appears that the sub-agent was employed by the agent for the principal and by his authority, between the principal and sub-agent there exists the perfect relation of Principal and Agent with all its attendant rights and liabilities. 

Carpenter vs. Insurance Co., 135 N. Y. 298; National S. S. Co. vs. Sheehan, 122 N. Y. 461.

"If an agent employs a sub-agent for his principal, and, by his authority, express or implied; then the subagent is the agent of the principal, and is directly responsible to the principal for his conduct, and, so far as damages resulting from the conduct of the sub-agent, the agent is only responsible for a want of due care in selecting the sub-agent; but if the agent, having undertaken to do the business of his principal, employs a servant or agent on his own account, to assist him in what he has undertaken, such a sub-agent is an agent of the agent, and is responsible to the agent for his conduct, and the agent is responsible to the principal for the manner in which the business has been done, whether by himself or by his servant or agent." Zurich Ins. Co. vs. Watson El. 

Co., 253 N. Y. 404.

However, if the agent has undertaken to transact certain business for his principal, and appoints a sub-agent on his own account to assist him, even though the principal assents to this appointment, the relation of Principal and Agent, except as far as third parties are concerned, does not exist between the principal and sub-agent. Crowley vs. Fabien, 204 N. Y. 566. 

Instances where the principles above announced find frequent application are in cases where banks receive from customers, for collection, notes which are payable at distant points. In cases where the correspondent banks fail, Federal rule is that the original banks are liable to their customers, for their undertaking was to collect, and not to procure the collection of the notes in question. Exchange National Bank vs. Third National Bank, 112 U. S. 276. This was formerly the New York rule. Ayrault vs. Bank, 47 N. Y. 570; Montgomery County Bank vs. Albany City Bank, 7 N. Y. 459; Gilpen vs. Columbia National Bank, 220 N. Y. 406; Isler vs. National Park Bank, 239 N. Y. 462. In a majority of states the 

Massachusetts rule prevails, which is that the original bank merely undertook to procure the collection, and thus dis-

charged its duty by exercising due care in selecting a subagent. Dorchester, etc., Bank vs. New England Bank, 1 Cush. (Mass.) 177. For an exhaustive discussion of this question see 36 A. L. R., page 1308.

Article 19A of the Negotiable Instruments Law of New 

York added by the Laws of 1929 in effect April 12, 1929, evidently alters the rule formerly existing in New York and com mits this jurisdiction to the Massachusetts rule. Section 350a of this Act provides: (a) Bank as agent for collection. Except as otherwise provided by agreement and except as to subsequent holders of a negotiable instrument payable to bearer or indorsed specially or in blank, where an item is deposited or received for collection, the bank of deposit shall be agent of the depositor for its collection and each subsequent collecting bank shall be sub-agent of the depositor but shall be authorized to follow the instructions of its immediate forwarding bank and any credit given by any such agent or sub-agent bank therefor shall be revocable until such time as the proceeds are received in actual money or an unconditional credit given on the books of another bank, which such agent has requested or accepted. 

Where any such bank allows any revocable credit for an item to be withdrawn, such agency relation shall nevertheless continue except the bank shall have all the rights of an owner thereof against prior and subsequent parties to the extent of the amount withdrawn.

Section 350d provides: 

(b) Duty and responsibility of bank collecting agents. 

It shall be the duty of the initial or any subsequent agent collecting bank to exercise ordinary care in the collection of an item and when such duty is performed such agent. bank shall not be responsible if for any cause payment is not received in money or an unconditional credit given on the books of another bank, which such agent bank wwwww

has requested or accepted. An initial or subsequent agent collecting bank shall be liable for its own lack of exercise or ordinary care but shall not be liable for the neglect, misconduct, mistakes or defaults of any other agent bank or of the drawee or payor bank.

CHAPTER IV. 

APPOINTMENT OF AGENTS. 

Introduction. In general an agent may be appointed by oral or written authority, or his power may be conferred upon him by a formal instrument under seal. In some instances, an implication of authority to act will arise from the acts of the parties, or one may be estopped by his conduct from denying that the relationship exists. 

Authority to Execute Sealed Instrument. Power to exe cute a sealed instrument must be created by an instrument of equal solemnity. Peterson vs. New York, 194 N. Y. 437; 

Worrall vs. Munn, 5 N. Y. 229; Hanford vs. McNair, 9 Wend. (N. Y.) 54. But if an agent, authorized to execute a simple contract, unnecessarily executes a contract under seal, which could be enforced as a simple contract, the courts will treat the seal as mere surplusage and enforce the contract as a simple contract. Henry vs. Root, 33 N. Y. 526; Wood vs. Wise, 153 A. D. 223, 137 N. Y. S. 1017 (Aff'd 208 N. Y. 586).

In many jurisdictions where a conveyance of land is still required to be under seal, a deed, executed by an agent whose authority was not conferred by an instrument under seal, will be treated as a contract to convey enforceable in equity. 

Blacknall vs. Parish, 6 Jones Eq. (N. C.) 70; Morrow vs. Hig ging, 29 Ala. 448; Watson vs. Sherman, 84 Ill. 263; Lyon vs. 

Pollack, 99 U. S. 668. In New York, since the enactment of the Real Property Law of 1896, a conveyance of real estate does not require a seal. Leask vs. Horton, 39 Misc. 114; Hepburn vs. Reynolds, 73 Misc. 73, 132 N. Y. S. 460.

As the theory of the solemnity of a seal is unsuited to commercial transactions, it is held that one partner may be authorized by parol to execute specialties in the partnership name. Smith vs. Kerr, 3 N. Y. 144; Skinner vs. Dayton, 19 

Johns, 512; McGahan vs. Rondout Bank, 156 U. S. 233. Part nership Law (Laws of 1919, Chap. 408), Art. 3, sect. 21.

Authority to Fill in Blanks in Deed. It is the settled law in New York, although there is authority in other jurisdictions to the contrary, that parol authority is sufficient to authorize the filling of a blank in a sealed instrument, especially if the grantee is ignorant that such parol authority has been given and executed. Forster vs. Moors, 79 Hun 472, aff'd 156 

N. Y. 666; Chauncey vs. Arnold, 24 N. Y. 330.

The authority to an agent to fill in and complete a deed and deliver the same may be shown by parol, and, if proved, is sufficient to give force and validity to the deed in the hands of a party who has received the same for value and without fraud. 2409 Broadway Corp. vs. Lange, 217 N. Y. S. 666. 

However, as was held in March vs. Marasco, 150 N. Y. 

Supp. 792, this principle does not apply to a referee's deed, for a referee is an officer appointed by the court and cannot delegate his authority to another.

Instrument Executed in Presence of Principal and at His Direction. If an instrument is executed in the presence of a principal and at his direction, it is considered as executed by the principal in person, and not by the actual signer as agent for the principal. For if a person has determined upon the performance of an act, he may avail himself of any convenient mechanical or ministerial agency to give physical form to the act. A human being may be utilized as well as an inanimate object; for example, one may sign a deed through the instrumentality of the hand of a third person, as well as by pen. 

Mackay vs. Bloodgood, 6 Johns. (N. Y.) 285; Mallon vs. 

Story, 2 E. D. Smith (N. Y.) 331; Bank vs. Johnson, 17 L. R. 

A. (N. S.) 969.

Therefore, if an instrument necessarily under seal, be executed in the presence of a principal and at his direction, the execution is deemed the personal act of the principal. Shimer vs. Ronk, 123 N. Y. Supp. 479.

This rule also applies to the filling in of blanks in deeds when done in the presence of and at the direction of the prin-

cipal. Mackay vs. Bloodgood, 6 Johns. (N. Y.) 285; People vs. Smith, 20 Johns. 62; 21 R. C. L., page 878.

Authority to Execute Simple Contract Required to be in 

Writing. In the absence of statutory provision to the contrary, an agent may be orally appointed to execute a contract on behalf of his principal which is required by the Statute of 

Frauds to be in writing. 25 R. C. L., page 675; Newton vs. 

Bronson, 13 N. Y. 587.

The folowing sections of the Statute of Frauds do not require the agent's authority to be in writing: (1) special promise by executor or administrator (Decedent Estate Law, Sec. 113); (2) promise made in consideration of marriage 

(Personal Property Law, Sec. 31 [3]); (3) promise which by its terms is not to be performed within one year from the making thereof, or not to be completed before the end of a life time (Personal Property Law, Sec. 31 [1]), (4) promise to an swer for the debt or default of another person (Personal Property Law, Sec. 31 [2]), (5) contract to sell goods or choses in action of the value of fifty dollars or upwards (Personal Property Law, Sec. 85).

But by statute in New York State (Real Property Law, 

Sec. 242) the authority of an agent to create, grant or assign an interest in real property by a deed or conveyance in writing, must also be in writing. Authority even to contract for leasing for a longer period than one year or for the sale of any real property or interest therein must now be conferred in writing (Real Property Law, Sec. 259 as amended in 1934). 

Thus an agent may be verbally authorized to make a written contract for the sale of his principal's land, Moody vs. 

Smith, 70 N. Y. 598; Worrall vs. Munn, 5 N. Y. 229; or to sign or endorse a note. Negotiable Instruments Law, 38; Bank of 

N. A. vs. Embury, 21 How. Prac. 14; Noel vs. Kinney, 106 

N. Y. 74.

By statute in New York State (Real Property Law, sec tion 242) the authority of an agent to create, grant or assign

an interest in real property, other than a lease for a term not exceeding one year, or any trust or power, over or concerning real property, must be created by a writing. 

Oral Appointment: Where a written authorization is not required, no formal words are necessary to confer authority on an agent, although express words or formal procedure may at times be desirable. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 168; 

Story on Agency, section 47. 

Thus an agent may be appointed by parol to demand and collect rents (Ledwith vs. Merrit, 74 A. D. 64, aff'd 174 N. Y. 

512) and to fill in the blanks in certificates of stock (McNeil vs. Tenth Nat. Bank. 46 N. Y. 325), etc.

AUTHORITY BY IMPLICATION. 

(a) In General. In a great majority of cases the authority of an agent is not expressly conferred upon him, but is informally delegated, or is presumed from the acts and conduct of the principal. Nuttings vs. Elevated Ry. Co., 21

A. D. 72. 

"A word or look or gesture often suffices to give assent to great undertakings or to set in motion the complicated machinery of commerce." Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 174.

The relationship of principal and agent may be implied from the circumstances existing in a particular case, when the circumstances are such as to indicate an intention to create the relationship, or the relationship may be implied from the acts or conduct of the person sought to be bound. McGoldrick vs. Whillits, 52 N. Y. 612; Hermann vs. Niagara Falls Ins. Co., 100 N. Y. 411. See also, Handvill vs. Drayton, 253 N. Y. 270. "Where the circumstances, or a person's acts, words, or conduct are such as to reasonably raise an inference of an intention to appoint an agent and to confer particular authority on him, the law gives sanction and force to such inference in the same manner, as if it had been expressed

in written or spoken words." Farmers Bank vs. Butchers' Bank, 16 N. Y. 125.

In Herschenhart vs. Mehlman, 125 Misc. Rep. 887, it was held that upon proof that plaintiff left his automobile in the garage of defendant with a person unidentified, the presumption arose that the person was the agent of the defendant. 

An implied agency must be based upon facts and facts for which the principal is responsible and upon a natural and reasonable, and not a strained construction of those facts. 31 

Cyc. 1218, Associate Alumni Gen. Theo. Sem. vs. Gen. Theo. 

Seminary, 26 A. D. 144, 49 Supp. 745 (modified on other grounds 163 N. Y. 417). 

(b) Implication from Circumstances. A master of a ship has implied authority, in the face of sudden emergencies, to act as the circumstances of the case require. He may borrow money on the credit of the owner of the vessel or hypothecate the ship or cargo. McCready vs. Thorn, 51 N. Y. 454;

Story on Agency, section 118. 

So a physician attending an unconscious 'man at the request of a third person has a right, growing out of the agreement implied by law, to recover the reasonable value of his services from the patient. 21 R. C. L., page 411; Meyer vs. 

Supreme Lodge K. of P., 178 N. Y. 63. In a case of this type the third person referred to may be regarded as an agent whose authority to act arises from the circumstances existing. 

See also, N. Y. Cent. Ry. Co. vs. Davis, 86 Hun 86, 34 Supp. 

206; aff'd 158 N. Y. 674. (c) Implication from Acts or Conduct. Where one purporting to act as agent for another, repeatedly performs acts which have not been authorized, but the principal acquiesces in their performance, the latter's conduct naturally gives rise to an inference that he wishes the agent to perform other acts of the same kind. Steele on Agency, page 59; 31 Cyc., page 

1221; Anderson vs. Supreme Council, 135 N. Y. 107.

As was said by the Court in Dickinson vs. Salmon, 36 

Misc. 169, 73 N. Y. Supp. 196: "Authority is often implied from the recognition of the principal of his acquiescence in the acts of the agent as well as from circumstances. Thus, where one is accustomed to permit his wife, child or servant to purchase goods on his account all purchases so made will be deemed authorized, and he will be bound to pay for them," citing 

Story on Agency, sections 54, 56; 2 Greene Ev. (Redf. 

Ed.), Section 65. See also Olcott vs. Tioga Ry. Co., 27 

N. Y. 546. In re Zinke, 90 Hun 127, 35 N. Y. Supp. 645;

Sariol vs. MacDonald, 127 A. D. 648. 

In Hammond vs. Varian, 58 N. Y. 398, evidence that a son had repeatedly signed his father's name to notes that his father had afterwards recognized and paid, was held competent to prove the agency of the son to sign the notes in question. But see Duryea vs. Vosburg, 121 N. Y. 57. 

In Miller vs. Loncao, 127 N. Y. Supp. 70, the defendant being engaged to be married to one "F,” purchased a house and gave the latter the key. "F" engaged the plaintiff to paint and decorate the house. It was held that the plaintiff could look to the defendant for payment as "F" was the defendant's implied agent for the transaction. Other cases illustrating this principle are: Schanz vs. Sotscheck, 152 N. Y. Supp. 351; 

Weiss vs. Levy, 150 N. Y. Supp. 489; Guarantee Construction 

Co. vs. Rickert Finlay Realty Co., 150 N. Y. Supp. 551; Newberry Co. vs. G. & E. Fire Ins. Co., 257 N. Y. 184. 

Relation of Parties. Agency cannot be inferred from mere blood relationship or family ties, unattended by conditions, acts or conduct clearly implying the relation of principal and agent. 31 Cyc. 1218 note; Dineen vs. Martin, 178 

N. Y. Supp. 384; Bates vs. Brockport First Nat. Bank, 89 

N. Y. 286; Aarons vs. Klein, 29 Misc. 639; Cousins vs. Boyer, 114 A. D. 787, 100 N. Y. Supp. 290.

Thus a wife has no implied authority to draw her husband's money from a savings bank. Allen vs. Williamsburg 

Savings Bank, 69 N. Y. 314.

However, acts which might be insufficient to imply the relationship of principal and agent were the parties strangers, may do so in view of the close relationship of two individuals. 

Gates vs. Brower, 9 N. Y. 205.

Where a minor son making his home with his father sells intoxicating liquor not secretly or furtively as to the one in control of the premises, an agency in the transaction might be inferred. Com. of Mass. vs. Slauski, 140 N. Y. 465.

Agency Created by Estoppel. Although in general one cannot become the agent of another without the latter's consent, yet if a person by his conduct, acts, language or silence, knowingly or negligently leads third parties reasonably to believe that another has authority to represent him, and the third parties act on that belief, such person, as against them, will be estopped to deny the authority of the agent within the scope of the employment which was represented to exist. 

Steele on Agency; Poel vs. Brunswick-Balke Co., 159 A. D. 

365, 144 N. Y. Supp. 725; Talcott vs. Standard Oil Co., 149 

A. D. 694, 134 N. Y. Supp. 617. "Where one knowingly and without dissent permits another to act for him in a particular transaction, or in a general course of transactions of which the particular transaction is one, he will be estopped from denying the agency of such others as against one who in good faith, and in the exercise of reasonable prudence, has dealt with such apparent agent relying on such apparent authority." 

Bigelow on Estoppel.

One seeking to invoke the doctrine of estoppel against an alleged principal has the burden of proving that he was misled by the acts or conduct of the alleged principal. If he is misled by the representations of the alleged agent, the prin-

cipal is not estopped to deny the existence of the relationship of principal and agent. Rathburn vs. Snow, 123 N. Y. 343. 

To constitute an estoppel there must be: 

1. A representation by the principal. 

2. A reliance on the representation by the third party. 

3. A change of position by a third party in reliance on the representation.

Again in order to establish an estoppel the plaintiff must show that he personally was misled by the appearance of authority. It is not enough that the third parties relied on the appearance of authority. McGuire vs. Selden, 103 N. Y. 642.

Thus a husband who is present at the execution by his wife of a chattel mortgage covering his property, is estopped from denying the authority of his wife to act as his agent in this transaction. Edgerton vs. Thomas, 9 N. Y. 40.

Also, a mortgagee who permitted his attorney, who negotiated the loan, to retain possession of the bond and mortgage after the principal became due, is estopped from denying the authority of the attorney to collect the principal as against a mortgagor, to whom the bond and mortgage were exhibited by the attorney. Crane vs. Gruenewald, 120 N. Y. 274. Kessenger vs. Quirin, 206 A. D. 126. "A person who accepts the benefit of a contract cannot, upon the trial of an action brought to charge the other party thereto with liability thereunder, question the authority of the agent who made the contract in his behalf, so that where a person selling a milking machine took with him a representative of the manufacturer who made statements with regard to the efficiency of the machine which resulted in a sale, the seller cannot claim that the representative of the manufacturer was not his

agent for the purpose of making the sale and avoid responsibility for the warranties which brought about the sale." Finkle vs. Lasher, 178 App. Div. 471, 165 N. Y. 

Supp. 141. See also Small vs. Housman, 208 N. Y. 115. 

In Hannon vs. Siegel Cooper Company, 167 N. Y. 244, the defendant advertised itself as carrying on the practice of dentistry in one of its departments, and plaintiff employed a dentist working in the said department to treat her teeth. The work was unskillfully done and an action for malpractice was instituted. It was held the defendant was estopped from denying the agency of the dentist in question because of the representations above set forth. 

In Ferris vs. Kilmer, 48 N. Y. 300, it was held that one who authorizes another to use his name in the conduct of a business is liable for the debts incurred, although he had no beneficial interest therein. See also Durst vs. Burton, 47 N. Y. 

167; Sloss Iron Co. vs. Johnson Iron Works, 103 A. D. 316; 92 

N. Y. Supp. 1056.

Difference Between Agency by Implication and Agency by Estoppel. It is frequently difficult to determine whether an agency is created by implication or by virtue of the doctrine of estoppel. In either case, as far as third parties are concerned, the principal is bound, yet an agent by implied appointment is, as far as his principal is concerned, a real agent with all the rights and liabilities of an agent, while an apparent agent, an agent by estoppel, is, as against his principal, no agent at all, and has none of the rights of an agent. In re 

Zinke, 90 Hun 127, 35 N. Y. Supp. 645. "An implied agency is an actual agency and is a fact to be proved by deductions or inferences from other facts, while in a strict sense agency by estoppel should be restricted to cases in which the authority is not real but apparent." 2 Corpus Juris, p. 444. Columbia Mill Co. vs. National Bank of Commerce, 52 Min. 224; 53 N. W. 1061.

In case of agency created by implication, an inference can be drawn from the conduct of the principal of an intention to confer authority, while in agency created by estoppel, the apparent intention of the principal to confer authority is immaterial. It is sufficient that the principal's conduct is such as to render it inequitable for him to deny the existence of the agency. Agency by estoppel can be invoked only by third parties who knew and relied on the conduct of the alleged principal, while an implied agency may exist when third parties at the time of dealing with the assumed agent had no knowledge of the conduct of the principal which gave rise to the implication. Bickford vs. Menier, 107 N. Y. 490.

An implied agent has such authority as the principal, as evidenced by his conduct, intends the agent to have, while an apparent agent, an agent created by estoppel, has such authority as the principal permits the agent to appear to have. 

Steele on Agency.

Acceptance by Agent. As in all contract cases, the agent must accept the offer of the appointment before the contract of agency between himself and his principal can come into existence. The law does not require this acceptance to be evidenced by any particular formality. An agent may orally accept an appointment under seal to execute a sealed instrument. Again, the acceptance may be implied from the performance of the agency. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 180. 

However, a principal may incur obligations as to third parties based upon his assertion that he has appointed or will appoint a certain agent, even before the agent has accepted his commission. Barr vs. Lapsley, 1 Wheat (U. S.) 151; 

Rathburn vs. Snow, 123 N. Y. 343.

CHAPTER V. 

RATIFICATION. 

Definition. Ratification may be defined as the subsequent adoption and affirmance by one person of an act which another, without authority, has previously assumed to do for him while purporting to act as his agent. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 260; 31 Cyc. 1245; Stanton vs. Granger, 125 A. D. 174; 

109 N. Y. Supp. 109 (aff'd 193 N. Y. 656).

The doctrine of ratification when analyzed is, to say the least, unique. By the application of the principles arising thereunder, one who is not actually a party to a contract may by some subsequent act of his own without the assent of the other party, or new consideration passing, adopt the contract as his own. When applied to the law of Torts, one may incur responsibility for the wrongs of another in which he never actually participated but which were done in his behalf, by merely subsequently assenting to them. The doctrine of ratification applies as well to acts performed by a stranger who has assumed to act for another without receiving authority, as to acts of an agent who has acted in excess of his authority. 

What Acts May be Ratified. "It is, the general rule that one may ratify the previous unauthorized doing by another in his behalf, of any act which he might then and could still lawfully do himself, and which he might then and could still lawfully delegate to such other to be done." Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, section 354; Brady vs. Mayor, 16 How. Pr. (N. Y.) 

432; Goodhue vs. Cameron, 142 A. D. 470; 127 N. Y. Supp. 120.

It is well to bear in mind that the following conditions must exist as a condition precedent to a valid ratification. 

1. The act must have been done for and in behalf of the person seeking to ratify it. 

2. The person ratifying must have been capable of performing the act himself at the time it was performed in his behalf.

3. The person ratifying must at the time of ratification have the power to perform the act himself. 

4. The person ratifying must have had authority to delegate performance of the act at the time it was performed in his behalf. 

5. The person ratifying must at the time of ratification have authority to delegate the performance of the act. (a) Torts. Oftimes a principal may be held liable in tort for having adopted or knowingly appropriated the fruits of an unauthorized act of his agent or a stranger who assumed the act in his behalf. Griswold vs. Haven, 25 N. Y. 595; National Life Ins. Co. vs. Minch, 53 N. Y. 144; Vogel vs. New 

York, 92 N. Y. 10.

Thus one may become liable by ratification for fraud, wilful trespass or other torts. 31 Cyc. 1249. (b) Crimes. In general a principal can only be held criminally liable for his agent's acts by showing prior assent, as intent is the essence of most crimes. If a principal shares the fruits of another's crime, his liability arises from the commission of an independent criminal act. (c) Contracts. Contracts which are illegal because of being opposed to public policy and good morals, or because of being in contravention of law, cannot be ratified. This is because authority to enter into such contracts could not have been previously delegated. 21 R. C. L., page 925.

Thus a contract made in behalf of another with a nonresident alien enemy cannot be ratified. U. S. vs. Grossmayer, 76 U. S. 72.

So also contracts in restraint of trade, or to influence legislation, etc., cannot be ratified.

Contracts absolutely void when made cannot be subsequently ratified, although an act which is merely voidable can be rendered valid by ratification. La France Fire Engine Co.

vs. Syracuse, 33 Misc. 516; Smith vs. Newburgh, 77 N. Y. 130; 

Commercial Bank vs. Warren, 15 N. Y. 577. (d) Forgery. In New York a forged instrument may be ratified by the person whose name is forged so as to render him liable upon it. Howard vs. Duncan, 3 Lansing 174; 

See: Trustees vs. Bowman, 136 N. Y. 521; Commercial Bank vs. Warren, 15 N. Y. 577.

However, in a number of jurisdictions this rule does not meet with favor, notably in Pennsylvania, Ohio and Indiana. 

The argument to sustain the doctrine of ratification in this case is that although the forger does not act professedly on behalf of the quasi principal, yet from the very nature of the forgery the act purports to be the act of the person whose name is forged and not the act of the forger himself. This it seems is a sufficient basis to warrant a ratification. A ratification, however, does not relieve the forger from criminal liability.

CONDITIONS OF RATIFICATION. 

(a) Assumption of Agency. A person may ratify a contract only when made in his behalf by one who assumed to act as his agent. Hamlin vs. Sears, 82 N. Y. 327; Vanderbilt vs. 

Turnpike Co., 2 N. Y. 479; Brainerd vs. Dunning, 30 N. Y. 

211; Garvey vs. Jarvis, 46 N. Y. 110; Collins vs. Suau, 30 N. Y. 

Sup. Ct. 623; Travis vs. Scriba, 12 Hun 391; Stanton vs. 

Granger, 125 A. D. 174; Ramsay vs. Miller, 202 N. Y. 72.

The contract at its inception must purport to be the contract of the principal. 2 British Rul. Cas. 261.

There can be no ratification where it appears that the person who made the contract was not at the time, and did not profess to be, acting on behalf of the alleged principal. 21 

R. C. L., page 923; 31 Cyc. 1251; Condit vs. Baldwin, 21 N. Y. 

219; Garret vs. McComb, 58 A. D. 419; 68 N. Y. Supp. 966.

In Garvey vs. Jarvis, 46 N. Y. 310, one M held a judgment against G for $2,000. He offered to discharge it for $500, but G did not accept. Later one R (through whom Jarvis claims), a stranger to G, by falsely representing that he was a friend of G and was acting for him, induced M to assign the judgment to him, R, and then R proceeded to enforce the judgment for its full amount. G then sought to ratify the assignment to R as one made to him, but it was held he could not do so. "The essential element is wanting that the act must be done for another. Here it was not so done. The most that can be claimed is that the defendant said he was acting for the plaintiff, which was false. He paid his own money and, in fact, acted for himself. He was a stranger to the plaintiff and of course under no obligation to act for him, and he deprived the plaintiff of nothing to which he was entitled." (b) Existence. In order for there to be a valid ratification, the principal must have been in existence, either actually or in contemplation of law, at the time the act to be ratified was done. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 276, 31 Cyc. 1252; 

Huffcut on Agency, page 43; 2 Corpus Juris, p. 475. (1) Corporations. Strictly speaking, there can be no ratification by a corporation after organization of contracts entered into by the promoters prior to its organization, although the corporation may incur liability on such contracts by adopting them. Such liability does not relate back to the time the contract was made, but only to the time of adoption. 31 Cyc. 1253; 7 R. C. L., page 81; 

Elliott on Private Corporations (4th Ed.), section 59.

It is unfortunate that the courts, particularly in New York State, have frequently used the term "ratify" instead of "adopt" as applied to contracts of this type. Allen vs. 

Corn Exchange Bank, 87 A. D. 335. In the case of Oakes vs. Cattaraugas Water Co., 143 N. Y. 430, which is fre-

50. quently cited as an authority for the statement that there may be a ratification by a corporation after organization of the acts of its promoters, the court said that ratification or adoption in that case meant one and the same thing. A careful reading of the case shows that the contract in question was adopted and not technically ratified, and that the court in holding that the contract was ratified, really held that the contract was adopted. See also Mesinger vs. Mesinger Bicycle Saddle Co., 44 A. D. 26; Bommer vs. American Spiral 

Spring Manufacturing Co., 81 N. Y. 468.

A learned authority on the Law of Agency has said with reference to this point (Huffcut on Agency, page 44): "If after the incorporation the company is found in possession of property or benefits accepted under the terms of the contract, this may be equivalent to proof of a new contract on the terms of the original one or of a novation. This comes very near the line of ratification but is distinguishable from it in theory. Some courts treat the case as one of ratification, but this is not justified under the general doctrine, unless, indeed, it be upon the theory that the court looks beyond the corporate entity and fixes upon the stockholders as the real principals." See Morawetz on Corporations, sections 547-549. 

For complete annotation see 17 A. L. R., p. 441. 

(2) Executors and Administrators. The power of an executor or administrator relates back to the death of the decedent, and since before his appointment, he is capable of being identified by the appointment, he may ratify an act done since the death of decedent and before his appointment, 31 Cyc. 1253, Denton vs. Sanford, 103 N. Y. 

607; Smith vs. Robinson, 30 Hun 269. 

(c) Designation of Principal. The principal in whose behalf the agent assumes to act must be in some way designated. It is not essential that the agent actually know the

intended principal at the time he assumes to act for him, but it is necessary that the principal be a person who is then capable of being ascertained. Thus an agent may effect insurance on a vessel or on certain property "for the benefit of those concerned" and any one of the parties answering this description may ratify the act. See Stillwell vs. Staples, 19 N. Y. 401.

Who May Ratify. In general a person may ratify a lawful contract made in his behalf by one who assumed to act as his agent, provided at the time it was entered into he possessed legal capacity to make such a contract, or to authorize its making, and provided further that at the time of ratification he still possesses such capacity. Steele on Agency, section 56. 

(a) Insane Persons. The decisions in New York State. 

tend to hold that the appointment of an agent by a person who is insane in fact but not judicially declared so is voidable. 

Merritt vs. Merritt, 59 N. Y. Supp. 357. 

Hence after recovering his reason, the principal may ratify the unauthorized acts of his agent or a stranger who assumed to act for him. Blinn vs. Schwartz, 177 N. Y. 252. 

Of course, a contract made without authority on behalf of a person who subsequently becomes insane, could not be ratified by the latter while non compos mentis. Wilke vs. 

Wackerhauser, 143 Iowa 107; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 273. (b) Infants. An infant cannot, while under the disability of infancy, ratify the act of one who has assumed to act for him. Mechem, Vol. I, page 273; 22 Cyc. 539; Yates vs. 

Lyon, 61 N. Y. 244. However, upon attaining his majority, he may ratify acts done in his behalf during his minority, with certain possible exceptions. See Casey vs. Kastel, et al., 237 

N. Y. 305. (c) State. The state through its legislature may ratify the unauthorized acts of its agents. People vs. Denison, 80 

N. Y. 656.

(d) Municipal Corporations. A municipal corporation can ratify all those acts which it had authority in the first instance to authorize. Thus a municipal corporation which is without power to issue bonds, cannot validate an issue thereof by ratification, even though the power to issue the bonds has subsequently been granted. Calhoun vs. Millard, 121 N. Y. 69. (e) Private Corporations. A corporation cannot render valid by ratification an act it had not authority in the first instance to perform. Park Hotel Co. vs. Bank, 86 Fed. 742;

La France Fire Engine Co. vs. Syracuse, 33 Misc. 516. 

But if the contract be neither immoral nor illegal and is such as the corporation might lawfully have made under proper circumstances, the only defect being in the power of the agent who made it, it may lawfully be ratified as in the case of a private individual. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 265. 

MANNER OF RATIFICATION. 

Since ratification is equivalent to a prior authorization, it follows that when the adoption of any particular form is necessary to confer authority in the first instance, there can be no valid ratification except in the same manner. Long vs. Poth, 16 Misc. 85; 37 N. Y. Supp. 670.

I. Express Ratification. (a) Instruments Under Seal. 

If the precedent authority must have been under seal, the ratification must be under seal to be effective. Hamford vs. 

McNair, 9 Wend. 54.

Such a ratification may be effected by an instrument in terms ratifying the deed, or by a power of attorney prospective in terms, authorizing the deed, but dated back to a period anterior to the execution of the deed it is intended to ratify. 

Tiffany on Agency, page 63.

Of course, if the seal is a mere superfluity, it may be dis regarded and the instrument ratified by parol. Worrall vs. 

Munn, 5 N. Y. 229.

It appears that a parol ratification by one partner of a sealed instrument executed by another in behalf of the firm is sufficient. Skinner vs. Dayton, 19 Johns (N. Y.) 513.

In Massachusetts it is held that an unauthorized execution of a deed by either an individual or a partnership may be ratified by parol. Holbrook vs. Chamberlain, 116 Mass. 155. 

(b) Instruments Required to be in Writing. Unless a statute requires the original authority of an agent to be evidenced by a writing, an unauthorized contract required to be in writing to satisfy the Statute of Frauds may be ratified by parol, or by acts and conduct. Newton vs. Bronson, 13 N. Y. 

587; 31 Cyc. 1262; Moody vs. Smith, 70 N. Y. 598. 

Thus in Lestershire Lumber & Box Co. vs. Winter, 144 

N. Y. Supp. 269, a third party, without authority, ordered certain doors of the value of over $50 from the plaintiff to be shipped to the defendant. The defendant was later informed by invoices that the doors had been ordered, and, after requiring changes, received and used them. It was held that the defendant thereby ratified the unauthorized purchase.

I. Implied Ratification. The acts, words or silence of a principal may give rise to an inference of an intention to be bound by the unauthorized acts of an agent or a stranger assuming to act as an agent. "The methods by which an implied ratification may be effected are as numerous and as varied as the complex dealings of human life." Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 314.

A distinction is made between cases where a person assuming to act as agent did so without authority, and cases where an actual agency existed but the agent exceeded the authority conferred. Conduct which might constitute a rati-

fication in the latter instance may be insufficient in the former cases. Merritt vs. Bissell, 155 N. Y. 396. (a) By Affirmative Act. "Any conduct by the principal which would lead a reasonable man to conclude that the principal is manifesting an intent to be bound by the agent's conduct will be deemed a ratification." Huffcut on Agency, p. 48.

An act done by a principal in recognition of an unauthorized transaction, with knowledge of the facts surrounding such transaction, is evidence of an intention to ratify. See Benedict vs. Pell, 74 N. Y. Supp. 1085; 70 A. D. 40; Barnet vs. 

Daw, 55 A. D. 202.

The following cases illustrate this principle: In Graves vs. Miami S. S. Co., 61 N. Y. Supp. 115, 29 Misc. 645, a soliciting freight agent of the defendant steamship company made an agreement with the plaintiff to carry freight at a certain rate for a period of six months in settlement of a claim. The agent was authorized to adjust the claim but not to bind the defendant by time contracts. The defendant subsequently carried freight for the plaintiff for a period of one month at the agreed rate. Such act was held to be evidence of a ratification of the entire agreement made by the agent. 

In Schanz vs. Martin, 75 N. Y. Supp. 997, 37 Misc. 492, the plaintiff had instituted a former action against a customer for the value of two suits of clothes, but discovering that the customer had paid a salesman, the action was discontinued. 

The plaintiff then brought an action against the salesman, and it was held that such action constituted a ratification of the otherwise unauthorized act of the salesman in receiving the money.

So, also, a promise to pay an agent's commission with full knowledge of an unauthorized contract is evidence of an intention to ratify such contract. Gillett vs. Whiting, 141 

N. Y. 71.

An action brought on an unauthorized contract, whether it be against the agent for the proceeds or against the third party to enforce it, will be deemed an election to ratify such contract. Bank of Belort vs. Beale, 34 N. Y. 473. See also 

Rosenthal vs. Hasberg, 84 N. Y. Supp. 290; Hess vs. Barr. 

14 Misc. 286; Brown vs. Reiman, 48 A. D. 295; Canada vs. 

Casey, 14 Misc. 322; Manne vs. Siegel Cooper Co., 20 Misc. 

592; M. Ufland & Co. vs. McMahon, 215 A. D. 267; 213 N. Y. 

Supp 519.

If with full knowledge of all the material facts, a principal accepts and enjoys the benefits of an unauthorized act of an agent, he will be deemed to have ratified such act and to have assumed the burdens incident to the benefits derived therefrom. Kane vs. Cortesy, 100 N. Y. 132; Finch vs. Gillespie, 122 A. D. 858, 107 N. Y. Supp. 418; Hyatt vs. Clark, 118 N. Y. 563; Fairchild vs. McMahon, 139 N. Y. 290; Smith vs. Barnard, 148 N. Y. 420; Meyers vs. Knights of Pythias, 194 

A. D. 405, 185 N. Y. Supp. 436.

Ratification of a single act does not justify an inference that any further similar acts will likewise be ratified. Woods vs. Francklyn, 46 N. Y. State Rep. 396; Hammond vs. Varian, 54 N. Y. 398; Woods vs. Railroad Co., 8 N. Y. 160; Montreal vs. American Locomotive Co., 173 A. D. 387; 159 N. Y. 

Supp. 21.

Conduct of a principal, equally consistent with repudiation, will not be construed as evidencing an intention to ratify; as where the owner of a building was held not to become liable for unauthorized improvements, although used by him, where the improvements were of such a character that they could not be removed. Mills vs. Berla, 23 S. W. 910. (b) By Silence. Ordinarily an indication, by affirmative conduct of an intention to be bound by the unauthorized act of another is essential to constitute a ratification. In some instances, however, a failure to repudiate an unauthorized act done on one's behalf, upon being fully informed of all the sur-

rounding circumstances, is evidence from which, in connection with other facts, a ratification may be inferred. Hyatt vs. 

Clark, 118 N. Y. 563; Andrews vs. Ins. Co., 92 N. Y. 596; 48.

A. L. R. 927. This general rule is applicable to acts of an agent in excess of his authority and to acts of a mere stranger or volunteer, although of course the presumptions arising from mere acquiescence are much stronger in instances where the relationship of principal and agent already exists. 2 Corpus 

Juris 512. 

In Merritt vs. Bissell, 155 N. Y. 396, the court held: "While it is the duty of a principal to disavow the unauthorized act of his agent within a reasonable time after it comes to his knowledge, or, otherwise, in some cases, he makes the act his own, still, where one who has assumed to act as an agent for another has no authority to do so but is a mere volunteer, a failure to disavow his acts will not amount to a ratification, unless under such circumstances as indicate an intention to do so." 

Ordinarily if a stranger, in the name of another, performs an unauthorized act, the latter need take no notice of it and liability will only attach when an affirmative ratification can be shown. However, silence in some instances may constitute a ratification, as where one stands by knowingly and without any objection permits another to do acts in his name, which acts cause innocent third parties to change their status, relying on such semblance of authority. 21 R. C. L. page 854.

As a general rule, a principal must repudiate within a reasonable time after learning of an unauthorized act of his agent, or his silence will be construed as evidence of an intention to ratify. Ketchem vs. Marsland, 18 Misc. 450, 42 N. Y. Supp. 

7; Hamlin vs. Sears, 82 N. Y. 327; 31 Cyc. 1275; Seymour Imp. 

Co. vs. Viking Co., 161 N. E. 389.

The facts and circumstances of each particular case determine the question of the reasonableness of the time. 31 

Cyc. 1275; Hazard vs. Spears, 43 N. Y. 469.

Knowledge of Material Facts. In general, to charge a principal with the ratification of an unauthorized act, it must be shown that such ratification was made by him with full knowledge of all the facts material to said act, or that he deliberately ratified the act without investigation, in the face. of such facts as would have placed a reasonably prudent man upon inquiry. Craighead vs. Peterson, 72 N. Y. 279; Smith vs. Kidd, 68 N. Y. 130; Ramsay vs. Miller, 202 N. Y. 72; Bierman vs. City Mills Co., 151 N. Y. 482; Scott vs. Ry. Co., 86 

N. Y. 200. 

The Court in Ahern vs. Goodspeed, 72 N. Y. 115, said: "Such ratification does not take place unless the principal is fully informed at the time of all the circumstances of the transaction, or unless he means without inquiry to take upon himself the responsibility of what the other may have said or done and to adopt all of his acts." 

However, in cases where an agent was originally authorized to do an act, but in the doing thereof has departed from his instructions, there is a presumption that the principal knows all the facts. This presumption grows out of the principle of Agency that knowledge of the agent is knowledge of the principal. Meehan vs. Forrester, 52 N. Y. 277; Hyatt vs. Clark, 118 N. Y. 563.

However, this is merely a presumption of fact, and like all other presumptions may be overcome by evidence to the contrary. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 299; Long vs. 

Poth, 16 Misc. 85, 37 N. Y. Supp. 670.

The difficulty of establishing actual knowledge on the part of a principal of all the material facts surrounding an act which it is claimed the principal has ratified, increases or diminishes according to whether it is claimed the ratification arises by implication or express ratification; for if one expressly adopts an unauthorized act, there may be a presump-

tion that he had ascertained all the material facts. Glor vs. 

Kelly, 49 A. D. 617, aff'd 166 N. Y. 589; Meehan vs. Forrester, 52 N. Y. 277.

Ratification in Part. Ratification must extend to the entire unauthorized act. A principal cannot ratify that portion which is beneficial to himself and reject the remainder. Davis 

& Son vs. Russian Transport and Insurance Co.. 182 A. D. 

668, 169 N. Y. Supp. 960; Crans vs. Hunter, 28 N. Y. 389; 

Hyatt vs. Clark, 118 N. Y. 563; Shirk vs. Brookfield, 77 A. D. 

295, 79 N. Y. Supp. 225; Elwell vs. Chamberlain, 31 N. Y. 611.

Thus if one expressly ratifies an unauthorized act of another, he is ordinarily held responsible for the instrumentalities through which the act was accomplished, whether such instrumentalities are in the form of promises or warranties, or whether actual fraud was perpetrated. If a principal subsequently learns of the unauthorized acts of his agent in procuring a contract, he must offer to undo the wrong or he will be deemed to have ratified such contract. As was said in 

Elwell vs. Chamberlain, 31 N. Y. 611: "It is not material that the plaintiffs authorized or knew of the alleged fraud committed by their agent in negotiating the sale of the note. They cannot be permitted to enjoy the fruits of the bargain without adopting all the instrumentalities employed by the agent in bringing it to a consummation. They have ratified the sale by seeking to enforce payment of the check given for the thing sold. If an agent defraud the person with whom he is dealing, the principal, not having authorized or participated in the wrong, may no doubt rescind when he discovers the fraud, on the terms of making complete restitution. But so long as he retains the benefits of the dealing, he cannot claim immunity on the ground that the fraud was committed by his agent and not by himself."

To the same effect is Bennett vs. Judson, 21 N. Y. 238. 

See also Krumm vs. Beach, 96 N. Y. 398; Harris vs. Tams, 258 

N. Y. 229.

However, this rule is not extended to charge a principal with liability for collateral contracts made by an agent in excess of his actual or ostensible authority, even though such collateral contracts were the controlling factors in bringing about the principal unauthorized contract, provided, however, the principal had no knowledge of the collateral contracts at the time he received the proceeds of the unauthorized contract. No ratification of the collateral contracts can be predicated even though the principal refuse to return the proceeds after knowledge of all the facts. See Condit vs. Baldwin, 21 

N. Y. 219; Smith vs. Tracy, 36 N. Y. 79; St. John vs. Fowler, 229 N. Y. 270. 

In Harris vs. Tams, 258 N. Y. 229, Lehman, J., said: ""The remedy of rescission, if allowed at all, is allowed on broad principles of justice. The basis of the remedy is that the buyer has not received what he bargained for (2 Williston on Sales, §608). The price is the consideration paid for the bargain, and here the plaintiff has not received the full benefit of his bargain. He accepted the muniments of title without knowledge that what he received was other than what he had agreed to buy; but the seller accepted the price without knowledge that it was the consideration for something other than what he agreed to sell. So long as retention of the purchase price even after the seller has knowledge of the unauthorized warranty is not inconsistent with a repudiation of any obligation arising from the warranty, no ratification of the agent's acts can be inferred from such retention. At least until the buyer offers to return the goods and to rescind the sale or contract to sell, the seller is entitled to retain the proceeds of the sale he authorized without impliedly agreeing to accept additional obliga-

tions which he never authorized. The situation changes when the seller refuses to relinquish an advantage he gained through an unauthorized misrepresentation of his agent. "The morality of taking advantage afterward of false statements innocently made, by insisting on retaining the advantage of a sale induced thereby, is almost as questionable as that of making knowingly false statements to bring about the sale' (2 Williston on Sales, 

§608). In the old case of Elwell vs. Chamberlain (31 

N. Y. 611 at p. 619) this court has held that principals who after offer to rescind, retain or demand the fruits of a contract obtained by unauthorized representations of an agent stand in the same position as if they had made the representation or authorized it to be made. We have never departed from that rule and it has been accepted generally. (See 2 Williston on Sales, §445-a, and cases there cited.)"

EFFECT OF RATIFICATION. 

(a) In General. Ratification is equivalent to a prior authorization, is irrevocable, and binds the principal as effectively as though precedent authority had been given. Lawrence vs. Taylor, 5 Hill (N. Y.) 107; Hazleton vs. Batchelder, 44 N. Y. 40; Cowley vs. Fabien, 204 N. Y. 566. "The ratification operates upon the act ratified precisely as though the authority to do the act had been previously given, except where the rights of third parties have intervened between the act and the ratification." 

Cook vs. Tullis, 18 Wall (U. S.) 332. 

(b) Intervening Rights of Strangers. Rights of strangers which have accrued between the act of the agent, and the ratification, cannot be defeated. Thus, a principal could not defeat a valid sale of certain property by a ratification of a prior unauthorized sale of the same property made by his agent. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 357. See also People ex rel. Goldsmidt vs. Board of Education, 217 N. Y. 470.

EFFECT OF RATIFICATION AS BETWEEN PRINCIPAL AND THIRD PARTY." 

(a) As to Principal. The ratification by a principal of an unauthorized act binds the principal in the same manner as though the act had been previously authorized. National 

Life Ins. Co. vs. Minch, 53 N. Y. 144; Fairchild vs. McMahon, 139 N. Y. 290; Andrews vs. Life Ins. Co., 92 N. Y. 596. 

In Martin vs. Gotham Nat. Bank of N. Y., 211 N. Y. S. 

828, it was held that where agents of a bank, by false representations as to financial condition of the parties, whose note the bank held, induced plaintiff to lend money to such parties, from which the note due the bank was paid, and the bank failed to repudiate the fraudulent representations of its agents and refused to make restitution when the fraud was brought to its atention, the bank was held liable to plaintiff for loss from fraud of its agents. (b) As to Third Party. Prior to ratification by the principal, the agent and third party may consent to cancel any unauthorized agreement made between the agent and the third party. Stillwell vs. Staples, 19 N. Y. 401.

As to whether or not a third party may recede any time before ratification by the principal, from a contract entered into by an agent who had no authority to act, the decisions are in conflict. There appears to be three rules deducible from 'the adjudicated cases, namely: 

1. English rule, that a principal has a reasonable time within which to ratify, and during this time the third party may not withdraw. Bolton Partners vs. Lambert, 41 Ch. Div. 295. 

2. Wisconsin rule, that a simple ratification by the principal is not sufficient to bind the third party, unless the third party specifically signifies his intention to be bound. Dodge vs. Hopkins, 14 Wisc. 630; Cowan vs. Curran, 216 Ill. 598; 

Wilkinson vs. Heavenrich, 58 Mich. 574.

3. Pennsylvania rule, that a third party may withdraw prior to ratification, but if he has not signified his intention to withdraw, a ratification by the principal is binding on him, even though no new consideration pass between the parties. 

The latter rule seems the most logical, as prior to ratification there is no mutuality as between principal and third party. Huffcut on Agency (2nd edition) page 53; Mechem on 

Agency, Vol. I, page 380. See also Andrews vs. Aetna Life 

Ins. Co., 92 N. Y. 596.

Effect of Ratification Between Principal and Agent. In general, ratification absolves the agent from any personal liability both as to the principal and the third person, and he may look to the principal for compensation for services rendered. Towle vs. Stevenson, 1 Johns (N. Y.) 110; Hanks vs. 

Drake, 49 Barb. (N. Y.) 186.

However, a principal may, in an endeavor to save himself and all parties concerned from unnecessary loss, do certain acts, which as to third parties, might be construed as a ratification. In a case of this type, the principal may still hold the agent accountable to him. See Brown vs. Foster, 137 

Mich. 35. 

If the unauthorized act was a tort, ratification does not relieve the agent, as far as the third party is concerned, from joint responsibility with the principal. Thorp vs. Burling, 11 

Johns (N. Y.) 285; 31 Cyc. 1287.

CHAPTER VI. 

TERMINATION OF AGENCY. 

In General. The contract of agency may be terminated by force of the original agreement between the parties, or by subsequent act of the parties to the contract, or by operation of law.

By Force of Original Agreement. It is elementary that if a definite time is mentioned for the existence of the agency, or if the agency is terminable on the happening of a certain event, the expiration of the time mentioned or the occurrence of the particular event ends the relationship. Marbury vs. 

Barnet, 17 Misc. 386, 40 N. Y. Supp. 76.

So also where an agent is appointed to accomplish the performance of a particular act or acts, the accomplishment of the purpose of the agency terminates the relationship. Hermann vs. Niagara Falls. Ins. Co., 100 N. Y. 411.

By Subsequent Act of the Parties. As the agent's authority is ordinarily vested in him to subserve the purposes of the principal, this authority may, in general, be revoked at the will of the principal. Lord vs. Thomas, 64 N. Y. 107; Terwilliger vs. N. Y. Ontario & W. R. R., 149 N. Y. 86; 21 R. C. 

L. 887.

But the power to revoke must be distinguished from the right to revoke. If a principal employs an agent for a definite period of time or to do a certain act, the exercise of this power to revoke may subject the principal to an action for damages for breach of contract of employment. 2 Corpus Juris 529; 

Slattery vs. Cothran, 210 A. D. 581; Moses vs. Bierling, 31 

N. Y. 462.

The general rule, that a principal has the power to terminate at any time his agent's authority, is subject to important exceptions which will be discussed in the following paragraphs.

HOW TERMINATION IS AFFECTED. 

(a) As Against Agent. The agent must receive actual or constructive notice of the intention of the principal to terminate the relationship. Gilbert vs. Quinlan, 59 Hun 508, 13 

N. Y. Supp. 671; Drake vs. Sewing Machine Co., 133 A. D. 

446; aff'd 199 N. Y. 595.

No particular form of notice is necessary as long as it is clear that the principal has withdrawn from the agent his power. Authority conferred by deed may be revoked by word of mouth. 31 Cyc. page 1303; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 442.

So also a revocation of authority may be implied from acts or conduct on the part of the principal which are inconsistent with the continuance of the relationship, as where a principal personally effects the sale of an article which he had authorized his agent to sell. Mechem on Agency, Vol. 

1, page 445; 31 Cyc. 1303.

Ordinarily the dissolution of a firm or corporation constitutes a revocation of the authority of former agents to act, although such dissolution does not necessarily affect the contract of employment. Kinsman vs. Fisk, 37 A. D. 443, 56 

N. Y. Supp. 33; People vs. Ins. Co., 91 N. Y. 174. (b) As Against Third Persons. Whether or not a principal, in order to absolve himself from future liability, must give notice to third parties of the revocation of his agent's authority, depends considerably on whether the agency was an apparently continuing one, or whether it was confined to the performance of a particular act.

If the agency is general in nature and an apparently continuing one, third persons who deal with the agent in good faith and relying upon his former authority, are protected as against the principal, unless notice of revocation of the agent's authority is given. McNeilly vs. Ins. Co., 66 N. Y. 

23; Claflin vs. Lenheim, 66 N. Y. 301; Marshall vs. Reading 

Fire Ins. Co., 78 Hun 83, aff'd 149 N. Y. 617.

As an illustration, a debtor is justified in paying an agent until notified by the creditor that the authority of the agent to receive payments has been revoked. Looymans vs. Chatham & Phoenix Nat'l Bank, 200 N. Y. Supp. 898, 121 Misc. 152. 

As to the sufficiency of notice, the rules governing notice in case of a dissolution of a partnership are analogous. Claflin vs. Lenheim, 66 N. Y. 301; Lynch vs. Rabe, 28 Misc. 215, 59 

N. Y. Supp. 109; Stevens vs. Schroeder, 40 A. D. 590, 58 

N. Y. Supp. 52.

Actual notice must be given to those who have had actual dealings with the agent, or knowledge of facts sufficient to put ordinarily prudent men on notice must be brought home to them. Claflin vs. Lenheim, 66 N. Y. 301 (case cited above). "Though a customers' man is an employee and agent of a stockbroker by whom he is engaged to solicit clients, nevertheless, a client who gives a power of attorney to the customers' man authorizing him to buy and sell stock for the client's account and who deposits the power of attorney with the stockbroker invites the stockbroker to rely on the authority of the customers' man, and the power of attorney so deposited with the stockbroker remains effective until the client gives notice of revocation to the stockbroker. The client's notice of revocation sent to the customers' man is not notice to the stockbroker; if actual knowledge thereof is not brought to the stockbroker, the client remains liable on his account for purchases and sales made by customers' man as though no notice of revocation had been given." Bosak vs. Parrish, 252 N. Y. 212.

Publication of a notice in a newspaper, circulated in the place where the business is carried on, is deemed sufficient notice as to those who have had no actual prior dealings with the agent, but who knew of his prior authority. Mechem on 

Agency, Vol. I, page 453.

As to an agent appointed to perform a particular act, no duty is incumbent on the principal to give notice of the revocation of the agent's appointment, except to those with whom the agent has actually entered into negotiations. Mechem on 

Agency, Vol. I, page 450.

For an exhaustive note with reference to the necessity. and sufficiency of notice as between principals and third parties of the termination of the agency by act of the parties, see 

41 L. R. A. (N. S.) 663.

Revocable Agencies. A mere naked power, by which is meant an agency in the outcome of which the agent has no further interest than the earning of his commission, is revocable by the principal during his lifetime, provided it has not been so far executed by the agent as to have subjected the agent to a liability to the third person (Terwilliger vs. Ontario 

R. R., 149 N. Y. 86).

Irrevocable Agencies. In certain instances neither the power nor the right to revoke an agent's authority exists in the principal. Cases of irrevocable agencies are of three general types: (1) If the authority conferred constitutes part of the security for the payment of money to the agent, or the performance of an obligation on the principal's part, or is necessary to give effect to the security, it is irrevocable by act of the principal. Thus, if an agent is authorized to collect a debt and out of the proceeds reimburse himself for advances made to the principal, such authority may not be withdrawn by the principal. See Raymond vs. Squire, 11 Johns (N. Y.) 

47; Knapp vs. Alvord, 10 Paige (N. Y.) 205; Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. vs. Wilson, 139 N. Y. 284. See also Terwilliger vs. The O. C. & S. Ry. Co., 149 N. Y. 86. (2) If an agent is commissioned to perform an act which involves him in personal liability to a third party, and he has actually incurred such liability, his authority to act

may not be withdrawn by the principal. Hess vs. Rau, 95 

N. Y. 359; Gelpcke vs. Quentell, 74 N. Y. 599. This illustrates "a power coupled with an obligation." (3) If an agent possesses a power coupled with an "interest" in the subject matter of the agency, the principal may not revoke the power. To constitute such an “interest,” in 'addition to the power being conferred as security for the payment of money or the performance of an obligation, a property in the subject matter of the agency must be vested in the donee of the power so that he may deal with it in his own name and on his own behalf. Hunt vs. Rousmanier, 8 Wheat, 

"By the phrase 'coupled with an interest' is not meant an interest in the exercise of the power, but an interest in the property on which the power is to operate." Taylor vs. Burns, 203 U. S. 120. See also Hoffman vs. Union Bank, 109 A. D. 24, 95 N. Y. Supp. 1045. 

Renunciation by Agent. Ordinarily an agent's power to renounce his agency is co-extensive with his principal's power to terminate his authority to act, but his right to renounce is limited by the terms of the contract of agency. 31 Cyc. 1306. 

While in general a principal cannot compel an agent to specifically perform the contract of agency, but must content himself with an action for damages for breach of contract, yet in certain instances where the services to be rendered are unique and cannot be adequately replaced, and the actual damages suffered by the principal are incapable of being exactly ascertained, the courts will, by injunction, restrain the agent from serving another during the term he contracted to serve his former principal. Standard Fashion Co. vs. Siegel- 

Cooper, 157 N. Y. 60; Duff vs. Russell, 14 N. Y. Supp. 134, aff'd 133 N. Y. 678; Daly vs. Smith, 49 How. Pr. 150; Philadelphia Ball Club vs. Lajoie, 202 Pa. 210.

If an agent abandons his work, the principal is justified in regarding such act as a renunciation. Stoddard vs. Key, 62 How. Pr. (N. Y.) 137.

The principal's misconduct may justify an agent in abandoning his work, as, for instance, the use of physical violence by the principal toward the agent. Horn vs. Luntz, 125 N. Y. 

Supp. 786.

The principal, in general, is entitled to reasonable notice of the renunciation of the agency, so that he can notify third persons of the termination of the authority. 31 Cyc. 1307.

In some instances it is also advisable for the agent to notify third persons of his change of employment.

Termination by Operation of Law in General. The death, insanity, bankruptcy, or marriage of one of the parties to the relationship may operate to terminate the contract of agency.

The declaration of war may suspend commercial intercourse between the principal and agent, the subject matter of the agency may cease to exist, or subsequent legislation may brand the authority as unlawful to be performed.

Since a bank which receives a check for deposit is merely an agent for the purpose of collection, its agency is revoked by operation of law when the State Superintendent of Banks takes possession of its affairs. In re Vavoudis, 252 N. Y. 

Supp. 779.

Death of Principal. With the few exceptions to be noted later, the death of a principal immediately revokes the authority of his agent, even though the agent or third parties have no actual knowledge of such fact. Hoffman vs. Union Dime 

Sav. Inst., 109 A. D. 24; Lacy vs. Getman, 119 N. Y. 109; 

Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. vs. Wilson, 139 N. Y. 284. 

Thus in Weber vs. Bridgman, 113 N. Y. 600, it was held that a payment made to an agent after the death of his principal did not bind the estate of the principal, although the payor was ignorant at the time of payment of the death of

the principal. See also: In re Bensel, 68 Misc. 70; Oatman vs. Watrous, 120 A. D. 66; Lalof vs. Tooker, 130 A. D. 11; 

People vs. Bellando, 137 A. D. 777; aff'd 199 N. Y. 533. See 

45. L. R. A. (N. S.) 349; In re Ehler's Estate, 231 N. Y. Supp. 

16; 132 Misc. 910. 

However, in Glennan vs. Trust Co., 209 N. Y. 12, it was held that if a bank makes payment of a check in the usual course of its business and without knowledge of the death of the drawer, it will be protected.

In a preceding paragraph, agencies which were irrevocable during the lifetime of the principal were divided into three classes. In general, the death of the principal terminates the authority of the agent to act in cases falling under subdivision (1) of the classification, but does not terminate the agencies represented by subdivisions (2) and (3). In other words, unless the authority to act is accompanied by the grant of such an interest or estate in the subject matter of the power as to admit of its exercise by the donee in his own name and on his own behalf, the death of the donor terminates the authority of the donee to act, even though the donor in his own lifetime did not have the power to revoke the authority conferred. 

Hunt vs. Rousmanier, 8 Wheat. (U. S.) 174; Grapel vs. 

Hodges, 112 N. Y. 419; Glendenning vs. Telephone Co., 163

A. D. 489. 

Thus in Knapp vs. Alvord, 10 Paige (N. Y.) 205, a debtor delivered certain property to his surety, with power to sell the property to pay the debt or reimburse himself in case of default by the debtor. It was held that such pledge and authority were irrevocable by death of the debtor. So, also, in 

Stevens vs. Sessa, 50 A. D. 547, where leases were assigned as security with authority to collect the rents and apply the same in payment of the debt secured. 

Thus one who has been given certain shares of stock as security with power to transfer the stock on the books of the corporation, has a power coupled with an interest which is not

revoked by the death of the transferor before transfer on the books. Leavitt vs. Fisher, 4 Duer (N. Y.) 1.

A mortgagor's death is held not to revoke the power of sale conferred by the mortgagor on the mortgagee. Bergen vs. Bennett, 1 Caines Cas. (N. Y.) 1; Wilson vs. Troup, 2 

Cow. (N. Y.) 695. 

In Babrowsky vs. Grand Lodge, 129 A. D. 695, money was advanced under a formal power whereby the lender was to be entitled to collect a certain insurance money to reimburse himself. It was held that such power was a "virtual assignment of the fund" and was irrevocable by the death of the borrower.

Death of Agent. The death of an agent ordinarily terminates the relationship. Wolfe vs. Howes, 20 N. Y. 197; Tiffany on Agency, page 144.

Where a firm consisting of two or more members is appointed as agent, the death of one of the members of the firm terminates the agency. Martine vs. International Life 

Ins. Co., 53 N. Y. 339.

However, where a "power coupled with an interest" or a "power coupled with an obligation" exists, the agency is not necessarily terminated, but the agent's personal representative may exercise the power as far as may be necessary to protect the interests of the agent's estate. 2 Corpus Juris 551.

Insanity of Principal. In general, the insanity of a principal terminates the agency except as to third parties who have dealt with the agent in ignorance of the principal's insanity and have so acted in reliance on the apparent authority of the agent that they cannot be placed in statu quo. Merritt vs. Merritt, 43 A. D. 68; 59 N. Y. S. 357.

A formal adjudication of insanity by a court of competent jurisdiction is, however, knowledge to the entire world of that fact, and instantly terminates all agency relationships. 

31 Cyc. 1319; Hughes vs. Jones, 116 N. Y. 67.

However, if the agent has such a “power coupled with an interest" (as previously explained) as will permit the agent to execute the agency in his own name, the insanity of the principal does not operate as a revocation of the agency. Matthiesson vs. McMahon, 38 N. J. L. 536; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 481; 2 Corpus Juris, page 552.

As to the effect of insanity of principal as power of agent to confess judgment see Johnson vs. National Bank, 320 I11. 

389; 44 A. L. R. 1306.

Insanity of Agent. As an agent is ordinarily appointed with a view of his personal fitness to exercise the trust imposed upon him, his insanity will generally terminate or suspend the relationship. Story on Agency, section 487; 31 Cyc. 1319. 

Bankruptcy of Principal. A formal adjudication of bankruptcy or a general assignment for the benefit of creditors operates to terminate an agent's authority over matters affected by the bankruptcy or assignment. 31 Cyc. 1311;

Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 483; Story on Agency, section 482; Tiffany on Agency, page 149; Franzen vs. Zimmer, 90 Hun (N. Y.) 103.

However, a “power coupled with an interest" is not affected by bankruptcy or insolvency. Dickinson vs. Central 

Nat. Bank, 129 Mass. 279; Tiffany on Agency, page 158.

Bankruptcy of Agent. Bankruptcy of an agent terminates his authority as to all financial matters but has no effect on the relationship if his power is merely to perform a formal act, such as to sign a deed. McKey vs. Clark, 233 Fed. 928; 

Audenreid vs. Bateley, 8 Allen (Mass.) 302; Mechem on 

Agency, Vol. I, page 484.

Marriage of Principal. The subsequent marriage of a man revokes any existing agency to convey his real estate insofar as the same affects the rights of his wife in the property. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 485; 31 Cyc. 1320.

Marriage of Agent. 

Marriage of an agent has no effect upon a contract of agency. Edgecomb vs. Buckhout, 146 N. Y. 332. 

War. The outbreak of hostilities between the country of the principal and the country of the agent generally suspends all existing contracts of agency and in some instances revokes those which contemplate commercial communication between. the principal and agent. N. Y. Life Ins. Co. vs. Davis, 95 

U. S. 425; Watts, Watts & Co. vs. Unione Austriaca di Navigazione, 224 Fed. 188. See Schulz Jr. Co. vs. Raimes & Co., 100 Misc. 697; 166 N. Y. S. 567.

However, as was stated in the case cited above, the law permits the payment of debts to an agent of an alien enemy, where such agent resides in the same state as the debtor. 

This, however, is subject to the limitation that the payment must not be made with the view of transmitting the funds to the principal before the termination of hostilities. Robinson vs. Life Ass'n Co., 42 N. Y. 54; Sands vs. Life Ins. Co., 50 

N. Y. 626. See L. R. A. 1917 C, page 667. 

Hubbard vs. Matthews, 54 N. Y. 52, announced the rule that an agent appointed before a war might continue to represent his principal in transactions not contrary to the policy or interests of the government of the agent's residence, although the principal might be a resident under the hostile government.

Destruction of Subject Matter. "If the agency contem plated the continued existence of a particular state of things, and, without the fault of either party, this condition should cease to exist, the agency is terminated." Steward vs. Stone, 127 N. Y. 500; Dexter vs. Norton, 47 N. Y. 62.

CHAPTER VII. 

ESTABLISHMENT OF AUTHORITY. 

In General the person alleging the existence of an agency has the burden of establishing the fact, as the law indulges no presumption as to the existence of authority in one man to bind another. 21 R. C. L. 822; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 183. (a) Agent's Declarations. A person cannot confer authority to act for another upon himself by merely declaring that he has such authority. Evidence of the declarations or statements of an alleged agent made out of court are inadmissible to establish the fact of the agency or to establish the nature and extent of his authority. Jaeger vs. Kelly, 52 N. Y. 

274; Mullen vs. Quinlan & Co., 195 N. Y. 109; Berry vs. 

Broadway Co., 148 A. D. 159; Sanford vs. Fountain, 49 Misc. 

301, 99 N. Y. Supp. 234; Bank of N. Y., etc., vs. American, etc., Co., 143 N. Y. 559; Wells vs. Chaffee, 207 A. D. 467.

However, the declarations of an alleged agent might be admissible if such declarations are open and notorious and have come to the notice of the principal and the latter has failed to act under such circumstances as would have caused a reasonably prudent man to act. 21 R. C. L. 281; Hawkins vs. Lindhorst, 17 L. R. A. (N. S.) 219 and note.

Although the declarations of an alleged agent cannot establish the agency, yet the alleged agent is a competent witness to testify to the fact of his agency. 21 R. C. L. 821; 

Lefkowitz vs. Iba, 114 N. Y. S. 29; Stone vs. Cronin, 72

A. D. 565; Norden vs. Duke, 106 A. D. 514; Appel vs. Lip man, 125 N. Y. S. 400; Kelly vs. Utica Fire Insurance Company, 203 A. D. 335, 196 N. Y. 795.

The testimony of an agent is no more conclusive than that of any other witness and the jury may disregard it and find the fact to be opposed to his testimony. Mechem, Vol. 

I, page 212; 21 R. C. L. 821; See American Box Machine Co.

vs. Bolnick, 36 Misc. 765; 74 N. Y. S. 846; Childs vs. C. E. 

Riley Co., 174 N. Y. Supp. 840; Loeb vs. Star & Herald Co., 175 N. Y. Supp. 412. 

(b) Communications Between Principal and Agent. 

Communications in the nature of letters, telegrams, etc., between the principal and the alleged agent, in which the principal expressly or impliedly admits the authority of the agent to act, are competent evidence to prove the fact of the agency. 

Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 186; Ettlinger vs. Weil, 94 

A. D. 291 (reversed on other grounds, 184 N. Y. 179). (c) Oral and Implied Authority. In instances where no formal writing is required to create an agency, the fact of the existence of the agency may be proved by parol. Anyone who heard the principal confer authority on the agent may testify as to the words spoken. Declarations and admissions of the principal which tend to establish the agency may be testified to by third parties who heard them. Badger vs. 

Cook, 117 A. D. 328. Authority of an agent may be established by adducing proof of acts, conduct or circumstances with which the alleged principal can be connected, which are sufficient to give rise to an implication of authority. In re 

Zinke, 90 Hun 127.

The fact that the alleged principal adopted or recognized similar acts done on other occasions by the assumed agent is evidence from which an authority to act in the matter in controversy may be adduced. Sarcol vs. McDonald, 127 A. D. 648. 

However, as was pointed out in Lippincott vs. East River 

Mill & Lumber Co., 141 N. Y. Supp. 220, a single act done under express authority is usually insufficient to justify an inference that the alleged agent had apparent authority to act for his principal on other similar occasions. See also Duryea vs. Vosburgh, 121 N. Y. 57; Smith Premier, etc., Co. vs. National, etc., Co., 72 Misc. 405.

Authority of the alleged agent cannot be established by proof of prior exercise of power of which the principal had no actual knowledge, unless the act done was so notorious or so related to the alleged principal that he will be estopped from asserting his ignorance of it. Mechem on Agency, Vol. 

I, page 193; 21 R. C. L. 821.

Relationship of the parties alone is not sufficient to give rise to an implication of authority in one to act for another. 

Rowan vs. Kemp, 103 N. Y. S. 775.

The following cases will illustrate the principles announced above: One who knowingly permits another to make collections for him is bound by payments made to such other. 

Gross vs. Owens, 86 N. Y. Supp. 266. 

A father sending his son away to school left the boy to make arrangements as to tuition. The father was held liable on a contract for a year's tuition made by the son. Center vs. 

Rusk, 35 Misc. 294; 71 N. Y. Supp. 767.

The fact that one was in charge of a truck with defendant's name upon it and delivered goods from it with bills and receipts bearing defendant's name, is evidence from which a jury might infer agency. Thiry vs. Taylor Brewing Co., 37

A. D. 391. 

The payee of a promissory note signed in the name of another as agent may rely on a previous admission of the executrix in her answer in an action on similar notes, of authority in her agent to execute the same, as a holding out of similar authority to execute other notes for a like consideration. Dodds vs. McColgan, 222 A. D. 126; 225 N. Y. S. 609.

Province of Court and Jury. Where the facts are in dispute, the jury must determine, under proper instructions from the Court, whether or not the evidence produced tends to establish an agency. Franklin Bank Note Co. vs. Mackay, 83 

Hun 511; see also 158 N. Y. 140; Dicksenson vs. Salmon, 36 

Misc. 169; Williams vs. Brandt, 90 A. D. 607; Delafield vs. 

Armsby Co., 99 A. D. 622; Merkel vs. Lazard, 114 A. D. 25.

The court determines as a matter of law what constitutes agency, whether the evidence offered is admissible to prove agency, and whether the evidence adduced is sufficient to warrant submitting the case to the jury. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 213. 

Whether the facts are undisputed and only one reasonable conclusion can be drawn from them, the court decides the matter as a question of law. 

If the facts are undisputed but different conclusions may reasonably be drawn from them, the matter is submitted to the jury for determination.

Written Authority. Where the authority of the agent is required by law to be in writing, the writing must be produced to establish the agency or the absence of the writing accounted for. Langbeen vs. Tongue, 25 Misc. 757.

CHAPTER VIII. 

CONSTRUCTION OF AUTHORITY. 

In General. After the fact of agency has been proved, the next question to be disposed of is as to the extent of the agent's power under the grant of authority shown to have been conferred. The authority so granted may be expressly prescribed and limited by the principal, or may be implied from the nature of the agency. - 

The burden is upon a person dealing with an agent, in general, to determine the extent of the latter's authority. See 

Dudley vs. Perkins, 235 N. Y. 448.

The general rules for the construction and interpretation of contracts are applicable to contracts of agency. 

Every grant of authority, if possible, should be construed so as to give effect to the intention of the principal in creating the agency. This rule is subject to the limitations that the intention is to be determined, not from the possible actual intention of the principal, but from the legal effect of the language or conduct constituting the appointment, and also the rule is to be so construed as to protect third parties dealing with the agent, provided the latter acted within the real and apparent scope of his authority, although he acted contrary to the intentions and privately expressed desire of the principal. 

West Side Savings Bank vs. Newton, 76 N. Y. 616; Mullin vs. Sire, 34 Misc. 540.

By way of description, rather than definition, it may be said that acts within the scope of one's authority mean those proper for the accomplishment of the end in view, or such as are usual in matters of this kind. First Nat. Bank vs. Nelson, 38 Ga. 391; 95 A. M. Dec. 400. "The sum of the matter is that each investigation of authority is a tub that must stand on its own bottom. Previous decisions are more or less illuminating guides; they are rarely precedents of law." Burden et al. vs. Robertson, 7 Fed.

Rep. (2nd Series) page 266.

EXPRESS AUTHORITY. 

(a) Written Authority. If a power in order to be effective is required by law to be in writing, or if a third party dealing with an agent knows of the existence of a written authortiy under which the agent is acting, the authority of the agent is limited to the authority actually conferred, and to such additional authority as can be fairly implied from the instruction.

Formal powers of attorney conferring authority are strictly construed according to the natural import of the language employed. Ferreira vs. Depew, 17 How. Pr. 418; Delafield vs. Illinois, 26 Wend. 192; Thompson vs. Erie R. Co., 207 

N. Y. 171; see also Corklite vs. Rell Realty Co., 249 N. Y. 1; 

Craighead vs. Peterson, 72 N. Y. 279.

When special acts are authorized and the power granted is followed by general terms of authority, such general terms are restricted to what is necessary to perform the particular acts mentioned. Lahn vs. Sullivan, 116 A. D. 669, 101 N. Y. 

Supp. 290; Taylor vs. Burns, 203 U. S. 120. 

Thus in Rossiter vs. Rossiter, 8 Wend. (N. Y.) 494, an agent was appointed to accomplish the adjustment of his principal's affairs in the state of New York and “to do any and every act in his name which he could do in person." This broad general power, it was held, must be limited to the doing of those acts only which were contemplated by the specific object of the appointment.

Where the meaning of the terms employed in a formal power of attorney is obvious, such meaning cannot by implication be enlarged beyond the natural and ordinary meaning of the language used. Hutchinson vs. Root, 2 A. D. 584, 38 

N. Y. S. 16; Porges vs. U. S. Mortgage Co., 203 N. Y. 181.

Informal instruments conferring authority, such as letters of instruction, etc., are not subject to such strict rules of interpretation as formal powers of attorney. They are usually

interpreted most strongly against the writer, especially when the power conferred is susceptible of two interpretations, and the agent and third party have in good faith adopted one interpretation. Winne vs. Niagara Fire Ins. Co., 91 N. Y. 185; 

Porges vs. U. S. Mortgage Co., 203 N. Y. 181.

The courts in construing written evidence of authority seek to determine what authority the principal intended to create. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 547; 31 Cyc. 1405.

However, the intention is to be derived from the legal effect of the language employed, which may not necessarily be the actual intention of the principal. Also, the authority will be construed so as to protect third parties dealing with the agent, when the latter acted within the apparent scope of his authority, even though the agent acted contrary to the express intention of the principal. West Side Bank vs. Newton, 76 N. Y. 616; Mullen vs. Sire, 34 Misc. 540, 69 N. Y. Supp. 953. 

(b) Oral Authority. .The same general rules of con struction apply to an oral grant of power as apply to an informal written grant of power, although in the case of an oral grant of authority, lack of knowledge of express restrictions imposed on an agent may preserve the rights of third parties. 

IMPLIED AUTHORITY. 

In General. By far the greater part of the authority exercised in the modern business world is not expressly conferred, but arises from the relation of the parties or from the manner of the conduct of the enterprise in question. 

Implied authority is that authority that the principal intends his agent to possess, and which is proper, usual and necessary to the exercise of the authority actually granted. 2 

Corpus Juris, p. 576. Implied authority is limited to the purposes for which the agency was created and to the acts and duties ordinarily intrusted to such an agent. Matter of Bauer, 68 A. D. 212; Rubenstein vs. Frost, 116 N. Y. Supp. 681.

It is but just that where one does not expressly define and limit the extent of his agent's authority, that he be not heard to complain if more flexible rules of interpretation be applied in construing the authority granted than had the power been expressly limited. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 559. 

However, only such implication as to the extent of the authority will be indulged, as the nature of the implied agency clearly warrants. Matter of Bauer, 68 A. D. 212, 72 N. Y. 

Supp. 439. "While a party dealing with an agent must be able to trace the authority of the agent on which he relies back to some word or deed of the principal, and the declarations of the agent as to his authority not made in the presence of the principal are not binding on the principal, nevertheless a principal who countenances the exercise of unauthorized powers by the agent gives to such agent an apparent authority which protects third parties who rely on this apparent authority." American National Bank vs. Bartlett, 40 Fed. (2nd) 21. 

The court held in Peterson vs. City of New York, 194 

N. Y. 437, that "In construing the power conferred on an agent, there will be implied the power to follow the usual and customary conduct adopted in the business."

Where the expression of authority is clear and unambig. uous, authority to do something different cannot be implied from custom or prior dealings. Green vs. Wachs, 254 N. Y. 437. (a) Medium Powers. As a general rule every grant of power implies and carries with it as incident, authority to do what is reasonably necessary and proper to effectuate the purpose for which the agency was created. 2 Corpus Juris, page 

578; Cullinan vs. Bowker, 180 N. Y. 93; Craighead vs. Peterson, 72 N. Y. 279; Benninghoff vs. Agricultural Ins. Co., 93

N. Y. 495; Bickford vs. Menier, 107 N. Y. 490; Burke vs. 

Bonat, 255 N. Y. 226.

For example, an agent to sell and convey real estate, unless specially restricted, may convey with general warranties. 

Schulz vs. Griffin, 121 N. Y. 294. But an agent with authority merely to find a purchaser has no implied authority to make any representations as to the condition of the heating and plumbing system of the building. Friedman vs. N. Y. 

Telephone Co., 256 N. Y. 392.

Also, an agent authorized to sell personal property, if he has possession of the goods and authority to deliver them, has implied authority to receive payment. Higgins vs. Moore, 34 N. Y. 417.

A chauffeur whose employer's car has broken down has implied authority to contract for storage of the car, but has not implied authority to order permanent repairs. Gage vs. 

Callanan, 57 Misc. 479 (Reversed on other grounds 128 A. D. 752). (b) Power Implied From Usage. In the absence of directions to the contrary, the authority of an agent will be construed to be co-extensive with the authority of other agents engaged in the furtherance of a similar trade or calling. Although mere custom or usage will not operate to confer, of itself, power, yet in determining the scope of the authority to act in a particular instance, it is pertinent to determine what is usually or customarily done under similar circumstances. 

21 R. C. L. page 854; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 559; 

Lowenstein vs. Lombard, etc., Co., 164 N. Y. 324. 

Thus in Keith vs. Atkinson, 48 Colo. 139, Am. St. R. 284, it was held that a traveler who goes to a hotel has the right to assume, until notified to the contrary, that the clerk and bell boys have the authority usually exercised by such employees at similar hotels, and may rely on such usage in enforcing liability in the case at bar.

Similarly, where an agent who is customarily governed by established usages, is appointed to perform an act within the general scope of his agency in behalf of a principal, it is presumed that the principal intends such established usages to govern the agent in the transaction in question. Huffcut on Agency, page 128. 

Thus in Ahern vs. Goodspeed, 72 N. Y. 108, it was held that where it was the established usage of the New York 

Stock Exchange that a warranty of commercial character should accompany sales of promissory notes, an agent, a member of the Exchange, appointed to sell such paper had implied authority to give a warranty of the type mentioned. 

It was held in Walls vs. Bailey, 49 N. Y. 464, that to bind the principal, the usage must be reasonable, not contrary to positive law, well established and publicly known, or at least known to the principal.

Contrary Restrictions. A principal may expressly impose such restrictions upon an agent's authority, that no implication of authority to exercise medium powers or to perform such agency in accordance with established usages can arise. Such limitation will operate as between the parties themselves, and those having knowledge of the facts, but a third party, without knowledge of the restrictions, may ordinarily assume that the agent has such power as is usually possessed by agents of like character. But where there is an expression of authority, that is, where the authority is spoken or written, the expression of authority governs, and there can be no implied authority of a different nature. Where there has been a clear, unambiguous delegation of express authority, no resort can be had to custom or prior dealings to establish an implied authority of a different nature. 

Green vs. 

Wachs, 254 N. Y. 437.

Apparent Authority. As a logical corollary to the preceding paragraph it may be stated in general that where an agent is appointed and the principal fails to disclose unusual

restrictions on the authority conferred, third parties in dealing with the agent may assume that he possesses such power in a similar agency. Steele on Agency, page 135; Law vs. Stokes, 32 N. J. Law 249; 31 Cyc. 1332; Cox vs. Albany Brewing Co., 6 N. Y. Supp. 841; Hanover Nat. Bank vs. American Dock 

Co., 148 N. Y. 612; Bickford vs. Meiner, 107 N. Y. 490; Newberry Co. vs. G. & R. Ins. Co., 257 N. Y. 184. "Where a principal has by his voluntary act placed an agent in such a situation that a person of ordinary prudence, conversant with business usages and the nature of the particular business, is justified in presuming that such agent has authority to perform on behalf of his principal a particular act, such particular act having been performed, the principal is estopped, as against such innocent third person, from denying the agent's authority to perform it." Johnson vs. Milwaukee & Wyoming Inven. 

Co., 46 Neb. 480; 21 R. Č. L. 856; Hannon vs. Siegel- 

Cooper, 167 N. Y. 244. 

The court in Neuman vs. Lee, 87 A. D. 116, 84 N. Y.

Supp. 106, stated the rule with reference to apparent authority to be that "Where an agent is entrusted with the transaction of a particular kind of business, he becomes, as between the principal and parties dealing with him, the general agent for the transaction of that business, and his acts in that particular line will bind the principal although he violates private instructions.”

However, a principal is responsible for the acts of his agent within the apparent scope of his authority, only where the principal is responsible for such appearance of authority. 'Churchill Grain & Seed Co. vs. Bushman, 204 A. D. 30, 197 

N. Y. Supp. 552; Figuera vs. Lerner, 52 A. D. 216, 65 N. Y. 

Supp. 293.

General and Special Agents. There is much wider ground for inference as to the possession of incidental or customary

powers in the case of a general agent than in the case of an agent employed to perform a single act. Yet, even in the latter case there will be implied the power to do what is usual and necessary to execute the agency. "The rule that one must ascertain an agent's authority or deal with him at his peril, and that a special agent cannot bind his principal when acting outside the scope of his authority, is subject to the qualification that, where an agent is entrusted with a certain kind of business he becomes between the principal and parties dealing with him, the general agent for such business, and his acts bind the principal, though he violates a private instruction.” 

Globe & Rutgers Fire Ins. Co. vs. Warner Sugar Refin ing Co., 176 N. Y. S. 32.

The terms "general" and "special" as applied to agents are of little importance as between the principal and third parties dealing with the agents. It is not the designation given the agent, but the acts he is authorized to do that determine the extent of his authority. See Dudley vs. Perkins, 235 N. Y. 448.

The early cases in New York State laid stress on the distinction between a general and a special agent in applying the doctrine of apparent authority. The following from Munn vs. Commission Co., 15 Johns. (N. Y.) 44, illustrates this attempted distinction: "The distinction is well settled between a general and a special agent. As to the former, the principal is responsible for the acts of the agent, when acting within the general scope of his authority, and the public cannot be supposed connusant of any private instructions from the principal to the agent; but where the agent is a special and temporary one, there the principal is not bound if the agent exceeds his employment." See also Rossiter vs. 

Rossiter, 8 Wend. (N. Y.) 497.

85% This is no longer a correct statement of the law. The most that can be said is that third parties should know that an agent, not acting in the ordinary course of a trade, business or profession, and delegated to perform a single act or even a number of disconnected particular acts, can possess but a very narrow limit of incidental authority beyond the limits of the real or actual authority. Huffcut on Agency, page 133.

In neither case will any private instructions to an agent, intended to limit the ostensible authority, be effective as against one who deals with the agent in ignorance of such instructions. See Graves vs. Miami S. S. Co., 61 N. Y. Supp. 115. 

Parties dealing with agent may reply on power of attorney, unless limitations on agent's authority are brought to their attention. Bradford Co. vs. Dunn, 250 N. Y. 461.

Notice of Limitations. In cases where a third party knows or is chargeable with knowing express limitations on an agent's authority, he cannot hold the principal in the face of existing restrictions. The question as to notice of limitations may arise in the following ways: 

1. Where the authority of an agent is required to be in writing, a third party is bound by the nature and scope of the written instrument conferring the authority. 

2. Where the third party knows or has reason to believe that the agent's power has been reduced to a writing, he must, at his peril, ascertain the tenor of the instrument. 

3. Where the third party has actual or constructive knowledge of limitations of the agent's authority, he will be bound thereby. Steele on Agency, page 142. 

See Editorial New York Law Journal, July 23, 1929.

CHAPTER IX. 

SCOPE OF PARTICULAR AGENCIES. 

In General. As has been indicated in a previous chapter, an agent, in the absence of express restrictions to the contrary, has implied authority to do what is reasonably necessary to the accomplishment of his agency. If third parties dealing with the agent have no actual or constructive knowledge of the existence of express restrictions, as to them the agent is held to have apparent authority co-extensive with the authority which he would have had in the absence of such undisclosed limitations.

When by custom the duties of a certain agency are well defined and publicly known, the law assumes, in the absence of express restrictions, that in any particular case the authority of such an agent extends to the performance of those duties for which he is ordinarily employed. 

AGENTS TO SELL. 

(a) Personalty. Authority to sell does not arise ordinarily from mere possession of the property, but the principal must have so acted as to clothe the agent with apparent authority to dispose of the goods. The possession of the agent must be coupled with the apparent indicia of ownership. 

Edwards vs. Dooley, 120 N. Y. 540; Smith vs. Clews, 105 

N. Y. 283; Saunders vs. Payne, 12 N. Y. 735.

Soliciting agents ordinarily have no authority to enter into a binding contract unless they have possession of the property for which the orders are solicited. Thus traveling salesmen, unless expressly authorized or unless there is a custom or usage in their particular business, have authority merely to solicit orders and submit the same to their principals for approval. 2 Corpus Juris 593.

A selling agent has implied authority to fix a reasonable price and to agree on the usual terms of the sale, but ordinarily has no authority to sell on credit unless such is the

known usage of the trade. Stern vs. Hoffman House Co., 8

Misc. 246. 

As an illustration of what is considered usual terms of sale, see Beck vs. Freund, 117 N. Y. Supp. 193, also Rubin. vs. Askins, 123 Misc. 155, 204 N. Y. Supp. 827.

An agent to sell has no implied authority to pledge. Anderson vs. McAleenan, 8 N. Y. Supp. 483.

If the agent has possession of the goods, he has implied power to receive payment. Caster White Lead Co. vs. 

Pounds, 65 A. D. 476.

However, if the agent is merely one to solicit orders, he has no implied authority to receive payment. Higgins vs. 

Moore, 34 N. Y. 417; Wright vs. Cabot, 89 N. Y. 570; Maxfield vs. Carpenter, 84 Hun (N. Y.) 450; Goldstein vs. Tank, 149 A. D. 341.

A salesman has implied authority in effectuating a sale to do whatever is necessary according to the usual course of procedure prevailing in the business. Ahern vs. Goodspeed, 72 N. Y. 108; Wait vs. Borne, 123 N. Y. 592; First Nat'l Bank vs. Farson, 226 N. Y. 218. The question of what it is usually necessary in that line of business for a salesman to do in order to effect a sale is a question of fact for the jury unless it is so commonly known that the court may itself take judicial notice of it. Thus it has been held that it is not a matter of common knowledge that it is so usual or necessary for a traveling salesman of a permanent wave machine to give a demonstration to a prospective buyer that the court could rule as a matter of law that it was within his implied authority to do 

Burke vs. Bonat, 255 N. Y. 226. 

So.

If the sale is one usually accompanied by a warranty, the agent will have implied power to make such warranties as are usual under the circumstances. Ahearn vs. Goodspeed, 72 

N. Y. 108; Bernzweig vs. Fensterstock, 176 N. Y. Supp. 836.

But this rule is not applicable if the article is not usually sold

with warranty. Smith vs. Tracy, 36 N. Y. 79; Argersinger vs. MacNaughton, 114 N. Y. 535; See L. R. A. (N. S.) 1916

A buyer who relies on a warranty made by a seller's salesman has the burden of proving the express authority of the salesman to warrant the goods, or the custom from which the implied authority to do so is presumed. Eichler vs. Kahnweiler, 178 N. Y. Supp. 257. (b) Realty. Authority to "sell" must not be confused. with mere authority to find a purchaser, such as a real estate broker ordinarily possesses. A real estate broker has merely authority to find a purchaser ready, willing and able to buy at the terms proposed by the principal. Smith vs. Herrman, 130 Misc. 832, held that a brokerage agreement to procure lessee on "ordinary covenants and agreements" did not authorize the recovery of commission without proof of such terms and conditions. Where broker is given full and complete terms, he earns commissions when he procures a customer on those terms regardless of whether or not the agreement is executed. He has no implied authority to sign a binding contract of sale on behalf of the owner. McDermott vs. Drumm, 115 Atl. 476 (see editorial New York Law Journal, March 1, 

1922). Mills vs. Gromette, 218 N. Y. Supp. 276. Where a broker is engaged to sell land, he may be given either: (1) an exclusive agency, that is, the owner promises not to employ another broker for that purpose, or (2) an exclusive right to sell. If the broker is given an exclusive agency, the principal is precluded from employing another broker. Duros Co. 

Inc. vs. Jannelli, 240 N. Y. Supp. 619, but is not precluded from making a sale himself without the broker's aid and if, acting in good faith, he does make the sale himself without the broker's aid, he will not be liable to the broker for commissions. McClave vs. Paine, 49 N. Y. 561. This is for the reason that the right of the principal to do in person that which he empowered his agent to do is an implied condition of every agency. Dole vs. Sherwood, 43 N. W. 569. The

right of an owner to sell his own land is an implied condition of every contract of agency, and unless expressly negatived, will prevail. Ingold vs. Symonds, 255 Iowa 82. However, where an exclusive right of sale is given a broker, the principal cannot make a sale himself without becoming liable to the broker for commissions. Alattery vs. Cothran, 206 N. Y.

Supp. 576. 

Authority to convey land must be conferred by a written. instrument and authority to contract to convey, that is, to execute a contract to convey land in the future as distinguished from the actual conveyance, must likewise be in writing. Real 

Property Law, 259 amended 1934.

An agent to "sell" has no implied authority to make representations as to the condition of the property so as to bind his principal. Friedman vs. N. Y. Tel. Co., 256 N. Y. 392. 

Where one is employed as a broker to sell property, he has authority only to search out a purchaser and to act as an intermediary to bring the seller and the purchaser together; he has no implied authority to sign the contract of sale for the vendor. Stone vs. U. S. Title Guar. & Indem. Co., 159 

A. D. 679, aff'd w. o. 217 N. Y. 656; Atl vs. Doscher, 102 

A. D. 344, 92 N. Y. Supp. 439; Rowland vs. Hall, 121 A. D. 

459; 106 N. Y. Supp. 55. The phrase "to sell" used in such brokerage contracts has acquired a restricted and conventional meaning; namely, to find a purchaser ready, willing and able to buy on the terms stated by the owner to the broker and to introduce him to the owner. The words "to sell" in a brokerage contract standing alone are not sufficient to authorize the agent to enter into a contract of sale binding the principal. Halsey vs. Monteiro, 92 Va. 581, 583, 24 S. E. 258, 259.

A power to sell real estate is strictly construed. 31 Cyc. 

1363; Hubbard vs. Elmer, 7 Wend. (N. Y.) 446; Schultz vs. 

Griffin, 121 N. Y. 294. 

Authority of agent to execute a contract for the sale of land to which principal did not have title is not presumed. 

Atkinson vs. Sanders, 250 N. Y. 170.

It does not confer power to mortgage, rent or exchange the lands. Bloomer vs. Waldron, 3 Hill 361; Forman vs. 

Berry, 163 A. D. 594, 148 N. Y. Supp. 959.

Although authority to sell and convey lands will authorize the agent to receive that portion of the purchase price which was payable at the time of conveying, yet ordinarily the agent has no implied authority to collect subsequent pay ments. Morrell vs. Cone, 22 How. 75. 

Agents to Purchase. The implied authority of an agent to purchase is usually subject to a much stricter construction than that of an agent to sell and will arise only when such purchasing is customary or necessary to enable the agent to accomplish that for which he was employed by his principal. 

Ashley vs. Downing, 203 Mass. 311, 89 N. E. 434.

Authority to purchase does not imply power to buy on credit, unless the agent has not been supplied with funds. 

Saugerties and N. Y. Steamboat Co. vs. Miller, 76 A. D. 167, 78 N. Y. Supp. 451.

Nor has the agent implied authority to execute negotiable paper in payment for the goods. Bickford vs. Menier, 107 

N. Y. 490.

If the agent is invested with general authority to purchase he has implied authority to fix the usual terms of the sale. 

Thus, he may select the seller, determine on the particular goods to be supplied and agree upon the price and terms of payment, provided they are ordinary and reasonable. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 659; Schley vs. Fryer, 100 N. Y. 71.

But the agent has no implied authority to buy goods of a different kind or amount or to buy from persons other than those with whom he is directed to deal. Olyphant vs. McNair, 41 Barb. 446, aff'd 41 N. Y. 619.

But if the agency were of such character that it would tend to create an appearance of authority to exercise discretion with reference to the particular goods mentioned above, the

principal will be bound if the agent acts within the scope of such apparent authority. Hill vs. Miller, 76 N. Y. 32.

Agents to Receive Payment. Authority to collect implies authority to receive payment in legal currency only. Black vs. Dundon, 83 A. D. 539; Finlay vs. Hayward, 35 Misc. 266; 

Fellows vs. Northrup, 39 N. Y. 117; Mogul vs. Lavine, 247 

N. Y. 20.

Agent empowered to sell for cash may receive so much of the purchase price as is to be paid at the time of sale, but he has no implied authority to receive payments to be made thereafter. 2 Corpus Juris 619.

If the agent is authorized to receive a negotiable instrument as payment, he has no implied authority to indorse it. 

Adler vs. Broadway Bank, 62 N. Y. Supp. 402; Thompson vs. 

Bank, 82 N. Y. 1; Robinson vs. Bank, 86 N. Y. 404.

Authority to receive payment does not imply authority to extend the time of payment. Hutchings vs. Munger, 41 N. Y. 

155; Ritch vs. Smith, 82 N. Y. 627.

Nor is authority implied to compromise the claim. Sier vs. Bache, 27 N. Y. Supp. 255.

Authority to collect interest on a bond and mortgage does not authorize the agent to collect the principal. Brewster vs. 

Carnes, 103 N. Y. 556.

However, if the agent who negotiated the loan is permitted to retain possession of the bond and mortgage after maturity, and this fact is known to the debtor, the mortgagee will be estopped to deny the agent's authority to receive payment of the principal. Crane vs. Gruenewald, 120 N. Y. 274.

A power to receive payment of an obligation does not imply power to accept payment before the obligation is due. 

Smith vs. Kidd, 68 N. Y. 130; Fellows vs. Northrup, 39 N. Y. 117.

An agent having general instructions to collect a debt or claim has implied authority to sue upon such claim and cause execution to issue. Bush vs. Miller, 13 Barb. (N. Y.) 481; 21

R. C. L. page 876. 

Agent to Execute Commercial Paper. In Morris vs. 

Hofferberth, 81 A. D. 512, aff'd 180 N. Y. 545, the court held: "It is perfectly understood as a matter of ordinary business observation and experience that almost the last authority which a man confers upon his agent is the right to bind him by signing or indorsing his name upon negotiable papers. I think the courts have respected and followed the general course of conduct of business men in dealing with this subject for they have always been slow to infer a power to perform such acts unless it was clearly given or fairly to be implied." 

See also Nixon vs. Palmer, 8 N. Y. 398; Rossiter vs. Rossiter, 8 Wend. (N. Y.) 494.

Authority to execute negotiable instruments will be strictly construed. Turner vs. Keller, 66 N. Y. 66; Craighead vs. 

Peterson, 72 N. Y. 279. See Note 35 A. L. R. 462.

A power of attorney to indorse the principal's name on a draft and to collect the amount of the draft does not include the power to indorse the principal's name on a check given in payment of the draft. Jennings vs. President and Directors of Manhattan Co., 203 A. D. 802, 197 N. Y. Supp. 401, aff'd 

236 N. Y. 552.

An agent to collect is without authority to indorse checks payable to his principal and a bank paying the proceeds to an agent is liable to the principal for the amount thereof. Looymans vs. Chatham & Phoenix Nat'l Bank, 200 N. Y. Supp. 898, 121 Misc. 152.

Although such authority is conferred in general terms it will nevertheless be limited to making and indorsing of such paper in the business of the principal, and for the benefit of the principal. North River Bank vs. Aymer, 3 Hill (N. Y.) 262.

If the question as to whether or not a note is executed in furtherance of the principal's business depends on facts peculiarly within the agent's personal knowledge, a third party, dealing in good faith with the agent, may rely upon the agent's representation as to the existence of the facts in question. Marine Bank vs. Butler Colliery Co., 52 Hun. 612; aff'd 

125 N. Y. 695; Huil vs. Allen, 87 Hun. 516; aff'd 156 N. Y. 658; See 21 L. R. A. (N. S.) p. 1046. 

Agents to Manage Business. An agent appointed to man age a business has implied power, in the absence of express restrictions, to do whatever is reasonably necessary to carry on the business in its usual and customary manner, taking into consideration the customary conduct of similar businesses in the immediate locality. 31 Cyc. 1386; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 707. 

In Schultheis vs. Caughey, 146 A. D. 102, it was held that the general manager of a stock brokerage firm in charge of a branch office was authorized to receive a protest by a customer such as to prevent the account from becoming an account stated.

Express restrictions do not bind third parties who in good faith rely upon the agent's apparent authority. "Where an entire business is placed under the management of an agent, the authority of the agent may be presumed to be commensurate with the necessities of the situation." 

Lowenstein vs. Lombard, 164 N. Y. 324.

No matter how broad may be the grant of authority, it will not be extended to imply authority in the agent to sell the business in which he is engaged, or to mortgage his principal's property, or to engage in an entirely different business. Kurlan vs. Gutman, 152 N. Y. Supp. 897. See also Bickford vs. 

Menier, 107 N. Y. 490; N. Y. Telephone Co. vs. Barnes, 85 

N. Y. Supp. 327; Mullins vs. Sire, 34 Misc. 540; Schultheis vs. 

Caughey, 146 A. D. 102.

Bank Cashiers. The implied authority of a bank cashier is extensive. As a cashier is the ostensible executive officer of the bank, he is presumed to possess all the power that is necessary to properly carry out the business of banking. He may ordinarily collect debts due the bank, deal in bills of exchange, draw checks on funds of the bank deposited elsewhere, borrow money on behalf of the bank, and certify checks. 

Barnes vs. Ontario Bank, 19 N. Y. 152. See also State Bank vs. People's National Bank, 118 N. Y. Supp. 641.

Factors. The powers of a factor are outlined in detail in 

Section 43 of the Personal Property Law (also known as the 

Factor's Act). In general a factor has implied authority to sell in his own name, fix prices, extend credit, receive payment, and pursuant to established usages, to make warranties. 

Argersinger vs. McNaughton, 114 N. Y. 535.

He can also pledge goods for charges against them, but not for his own personal debts.

Auctioneers. An auctioneer is an agent who sells property of others publicly to the highest bidder. His authority is, of necessity, very limited. He cannot sell at a private sale, or sell on credit, or accept anything other than money, or give a warranty, or delegate his authority to another. Furthermore he cannot sell for less than the price fixed by his principal even though the third party attempting to buy is not bound. Bush vs. Cole, 28 N. Y. 261.

If he exceeds the authority actually conferred, and that implied from the nature of the agency his principal is not bound. Bush vs. Sole, supra.

But if he keeps within the authority conferred, his principal is liable for refusing to complete the contract. croft vs. Muller, 71 N. Y. 367.

Cock- "An auctioneer receiving a deposit from a bidder is in the position of a stakeholder." He should hold the deposit intact until the transaction is fully completed. Millfield Realty Co. vs. Catena, 256 N. Y. 435.

Attorney at Law. The implied power of an attorney at law extends to the doing of all acts in and out of court which are necessary or incidental to the management of the matters intrusted to him, and which effect the remedy only and not the cause of action. Thus he may make affidavits, serve and accept the service of papers, stipulate when the action is to be tried, make admissions of fact for the purpose of the trial, order printing of necessary briefs, dismiss an action or agree to a non-suit. However, he cannot confess judgment, release a cause of action, compromise a claim or prejudice the substantial rights of his client. Lewis vs. Duane, 141 N. Y. 302.

RIGHTS AND LIABILITIES BETWEEN PRINCIPAL AND THIRD PERSON. 

CHAPTER X. 

FORM OF EXECUTION NECESSARY TO BIND PRINCIPAL. 

Sealed Instruments. In order that a principal be bound by a deed executed by his agent, the deed must appear on its face to be made, signed and sealed in the name of the principal. Thurman vs. Camron, 24 Wend. 87; Townsend vs. 

Corning, 23 Wend. 435; Briggs vs. Partridge, 64 N. Y. 357; 

Crowley vs. Lewis, 239 N. Y. 264.

The recitals of the instrument, particularly of the testimonium clause, and the manner of signing, determine whether the instrument is the obligation of the principal or the agent. 

It frequently occurs that a sealed instrument is so executed that neither the principal nor agent is bound thereon, as for example where the covenants in the instrument are those of the principal, and the agent has signed his own name and appended his own seal. Whitford vs. Laidler, 94 N. Y. 145.

The general tenor of the entire instrument determines whether or not a principal is bound. In construing an instrument under seal, the following matters will be considered: the party named as the grantor, the wording of the granting clause, the party obligated by the covenants, the wording of the testimonium clause, and the signature and seal. Kiersted vs. O. & A. Ry. Co., 69 N. Y. 343. 

Negotiable Instruments. As negotiable paper circulates in the business world as the representative of money, it is important that the parties obligated upon such an instrument he clearly disclosed upon the face of the paper itself. Negotiable 

Instruments Law sections 38 and 39 provide in substance that an agent may be authorized to execute a negotiable instrument, but that the principal must be disclosed in the instru-

ment, and the instrument must purport to be that of the principal. See Casco Nat. Bank vs. Clark, 139 N. Y. 307. Where the rights of bona fide holders for value are concerned, parol evidence is inadmissible to show that the party signing was acting in a representative capacity, unless it is so clearly indicated in the instrument. Briggs vs. Partridge, 64 N. Y. 357; 

McGowan vs. Peterson, 173 N. Y. 1. But, as between the maker and payee, parol evidence may be received to show that the payee knew that the maker was merely acting as agent for another and did not intend to bind himself individually, provided, however, there is some indication in the instrument itself that the maker was acting in a representative capacity. Kerby vs. Ruegamer, 107 A. D. 491; J. & E. Homan 

Co. vs. Payne, 127 N. Y. Supp. 418; Crandall vs. Rollins, 83 

A. D. 618; First National Bank vs. Wallis, 150 N. Y. 455; 

Metcalf vs. Williams, 104 U. S. 93.

Section 72 of the Negotiable Instruments Law provides that where an instrument is drawn or endorsed to a person as cashier or other fiscal officer of a bank or corporation, it is deemed prima facia to be payable to the bank or corporation of which he is such officer.

Other Contracts. In determining the liability of the parties on other simple contracts, the primary consideration is the true intention of the parties as expressed in the contract. 

If an agent desires to bind his principal and escape personal liability himself, he must disclose the fact of the agency and the name of his principal at the time of making the contract, and execute the contract in his principal's name. Bassett vs. 

Perkins, 65 Misc. 103; Good vs. Rumsey, 50 A. D. 280.

If an agent fails to disclose the fact of his agency, and apparently acting in his own behalf, executes a simple nonnegotiable contract for his principal's benefit and within the scope of his authority, the principal may be charged thereon, even though the third party contracted in the belief that he was dealing with the agent personally. Briggs vs. Partridge,

64 N. Y. 357; Coleman vs. First National Bank, 53 N. Y. 393.

This rule is founded on equitable principles, aided and extended by the fiction of the identity of principal and agent. 

In Huffcut on Agency, (2d Ed.) it is said (sec. 120): "In the case of a contract made by an agent in his own name as principal, the third party obviously relies upon the character, credit and substance of the agent and intends to acquire rights against the agent and against no one else and to incur obligations to the agent and to no one else. So far at least as the third party is concerned it is a contract between him and the agent and the principal is never for a moment in his contemplation. The strict application of the common-law rule would lead to the conclusion, therefore, that the principal could neither sue nor be sued upon the contract. "Yet just the opposite conclusion prevails. The case escapes the common-law doctrine and establishes the sweeping rule that an undisclosed principal may both sue and be sued upon a contract made in his behalf or to his secret use by his agent. If an agent makes a contract in his own name the principal may sue and be sued upon it; for it is a general rule that whenever an express contract is made, an action is maintainable upon it, either in the name of the person with whom in point of law it was made or his principal." 

The agent, however, may still be held liable on the contract, and the third party, on learning of the existence of an undisclosed principal, must elect within a reasonable time to hold either the principal or the agent. What constitutes an election is a question of fact, but there can be no election without a knowledge of all the material facts. Rosenzwirg vs. Raubitschek, 166 A. D. 488; Ranger vs. Thalmann, 178 

N. Y. 574; Georgi vs. Texas Co., 225 N. Y. 410; Swarthout vs. 

McGowan, 126 N. Y. Supp. 887; Cherington vs. Burchell, 147 

A. D. 16; Scholl vs. Baynes, 125 Misc. 114.

In an action by a real estate broker for commissions alleged to have been earned in procuring a purchaser for prop-

erty owned by a corporation, plaintiff has right under Sec. 

213 C. P. A. to join both the corporation and the individual who represented himself to be the authorized agent of the corporation in the transaction but whose acts were repudiated by corporation. Geer vs. Blemton Realty Corp. 218 A. D. 102; 

218 N. Y. Supp. 162.

Under certain circumstances a plaintiff may join the agent and undisclosed principal as parties defendant, and is not required to make an election until the close of the case. Tew vs. Wolfsohn, 77 A. D. 454; aff'd 174 N. Y. 272; Montague 

M. M. Co. vs. All Packages G. S. Co., 182 A. D. 500; Schwartzreich & Goodman Co. vs. Quitman et al., 181 N. Y. Supp. 784; 

May Co. vs. Mott Av. Corp., 121 A. D. 398; Schechtman vs. 

Solaway, 204 A. D. 549; Jamison vs. Lamborn, 207 A. D. 375.

It is important to note that the doctrine of undisclosed principal does not apply to negotiable instruments or to contracts under seal. Crowley vs. Lewis, 239 N. Y. 264.

Settlement with Agent. If an agent is furnished by his principal with funds to make purchases, but without the knowledge or consent of his principal he secures the property on credit, without disclosing his principal, then the principal will not be bound to the seller. Laing vs. Butler, 34 Hun. 144.

Similarly when the purchases have been made by the agent on credit authorized by his principal, but the agent has failed to disclose the fact of the agency and has pledged his individual credit, a subsequent settlement made by the principal to the agent in good faith, before the agency is disclosed to the seller, exonerates the principal. Harter vs. Continental Print ing Co., 117 N. Y. Supp. 1001; Knapp vs. Simon, 96 N. Y. 284. 

Montague M. M. Co. vs. All Package Grocery Store Co., 182 

A. D. 500.

CHAPTED XI. 

ADMISSIONS—NOTICE—LIABILITY OF PRINCIPAL FOR TORTS OF AGENT. 

I. ADMISSIONS. 

In General. "Declarations of an agent are not competent to charge a principal, upon proof merely that the relation of principal and agent existed at the time when the declarations were made. Something more must be shown. It must further appear that the agent, at the time the declarations were made, was engaged in executing the authority conferred upon him, and that the declarations related to and were connected with the business then depending, so that they constituted a part of the res gestae." Goetze vs. M. R. Co., 54 A. D. 365; Corn vs. 

Bergmann, 145 A. D. 218; Levy vs. Wallach, 178 N. Y. Supp. 216. 

The admissions or declarations of an agent are not competent to establish the fact of the agency or the nature and extent of the authority. They are receivable only after the agency has been established, and then only provided they have reference to some matter within the scope of the agent's authority, and in addition constitute a part of the "res gestae" of the transaction in which the agent was acting for the principal, and also serve to characterize that transaction. Breese vs. Graves, 67 A. D. 323; Steeher Lithographic Co. vs. Inman, 

175 N. Y. 124; White vs. Miller, 71 N. Y. 118.

Authorized Statements. The first condition to be met before the admissions of an agent will be held to bind his principal, is that the statements must be of such a character as the agent in the performance of his duty is authorized to make. In Magnus vs. Platt, 62 Misc. 499; 115 N. Y. Supp. 

824, it was held that in an action against an express company for mis-delivery of a package, statements of the driver of the express company's wagon as to the sufficiency of the address on the package were admissible, as such advice was within. the driver's authority as the agent of the company. See also

Miller vs. L. I. R. R. Co., 122 A. D. 463.

Statements Part of the "Res Gestae." The second con dition of admissibility is that the statements must be so closely connected with the act performed, or the contract made, as to be part of the transaction itself, and also must tend to characterize such transaction. Waldele vs. N. Y. C. & 

H. R. R., 95 N. Y. 274.

A contemporaneous statement is clearly part of the transaction, while a subsequent narration is not. 

In contract cases, the completion of the contract marks the termination of the transaction, and generally all admissions made during the negotiations are held to be part of the transaction. Nelson vs. Cowing, 6 Hill (N. Y.) 336; Jeffrey vs. Bigelow, 13 Wend. 518; Wernes vs. Reisner, 150 N. Y. 

Supp. 646; Smith vs. Finkelstein, 162 A. D. 128.

In tort actions, as the declarations of an agent sought to be introduced in evidence are adverse to his master's interests, to form a part of the "res gestae" they must be made at the very time of the transaction, so that they practically accompany it. Luby vs. Hudson River Ry., 17 N. Y. 131; Marlatt vs. Erie Ry., 154 A. D. 388.

For example, a physician on coming out of a house, found his buggy upset and near it a wagon, the driver of which said he had smashed the buggy. It was held that this statement was not admissible as part of the res gestae. Burns vs. Bor den's Condensed Milk Co., 98 A. D. 566. 

In Vicksburg Ry. Co. vs. O'Brien, 119 U. S. 99, the United

States Supreme Court stood four to five against admitting the declarations of a locomotive engineer made ten to twenty minutes after the accident. The minority dissented upon the ground that the modern cases have relaxed the stringent rule requiring "perfect coincidence" of time. The New York courts however, apply the rule strictly. Butler vs. Manhattan Ry. Co., 143 N. Y. 417.

II. NOTICE. 

In General. One who acquires property which is burdened with a lien or charge in favor of another takes subject to such lien or charge, provided he has actual or constructive notice of it. Our present inquiry will be as to whether or not such notice to an agent will charge his principal. When notice is acquired during the transaction which it affects, the principal is bound as fully as if he acquired the notice in person even though the agent does not in fact inform the principal. 

Bible vs. Insurance Co., 256 N. Y. 458. The fact that the agent had no present recollection of the facts constituting such notice at the termination of the transaction is immaterial. Small vs. Housman, 208 N. Y. 115; Jefferson County Bank vs. 

Dewey, 197 N. Y. 14, Corrigan vs. Bobbs-Merrill Co., 238 

N. Y. 58.

Knowledge acquired by an agent prior to the commencement of his agency will be imputed to his principal provided such knowledge was present in the agent's mind at the time of the transaction which it affects, and provided further that the knowledge was not obtained confidentially so that to disclose it would be a breach of professional secrecy. Sergent vs. Liverpool, L & G Ins. Co., 73 N. Y. Supp. 120; Gaspard vs. 

14th St. Store, 128 N. Y. Supp. 53; Drilling vs. N. Y. Life Ins. 

Co., 234 N. Y. 234.

In considering the effect of knowledge acquired by an agent outside of the transaction which it affects, but within the general scope of his agency, a distinction must be made between an agent who acts for his principal in totally different transactions, separated by periods of time, such as an attorney, and an agent who performs a continuous series of transactions all incidental to the furtherance of a general business, such as cashier. As to the former, the rule is the same as in cases where notice is acquired prior to the commencement of the agency. Constant vs. Rochester University 111 N. Y. 604. As to the latter, the rule is similar to that applied where notice is

1 acquired during the transaction which it affects. Holden vs. 

N. Y. & Erie Bank, 72 N. Y. 286. See 4 A. L. R. 1592; Cragie vs. Handley, 99 N. Y. 131. 

Exhaustive treatise in Hurst Boillin Co. vs. Jones, 279 

S. S. 392 (43 A. L. R. 742) deals with notice to salesman as effecting principal. A salesman's knowledge that a buyer was unauthorized to purchase goods unless the purchase should be confirmed by the buyer's employer was held to be binding on the salesman's principal. Gaspard vs. 14th St. Store 143 A. D. 

402; 128 N. Y. S. 53. 

Notice to attorney as binding principal, see Vital vs. Jandorf, 212 N. Y. S. 548; Farnsworth vs. Hezelett 38 A. L. R. 814 and note to Editorial New York Law Journal, Jan. 6, 1926,

Exceptions. Where an agent is engaged in a scheme to defraud his principal, the presumption that the agent will communicate all material facts to his principal no longer prevails, and his principal is not bound by knowledge acquired by his agent while engaged in such fraudulent purpose. Crooks vs. People's Nat. Bk., 177 N. Y. 68; National Life Ins. Co. vs. 

Minch, 53 N. Y. 144; Prudential Insurance Co. vs. National 

Bank of Commerce, 227 N. Y. 510. Under Insurance Law, sec. 58, the knowledge of the insurance company's medical examiner is no longer treated as the knowledge of the insurance company where the application is attached to the policy when it is delivered to the insured. Minsker vs. John Hancock Life Ins. Co., 254 N. Y. 333, overruling in part, Sternaman vs. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 170 N. Y. 13.

III. PRINCIPAL'S LIABILITY FOR TORTS OF AGENTS. 

In General. Most instances of the liability of one for the torts of his representative arise in the relationship of master and servant rather than that of principal and agent. As an agent is usually employed to bring his principal into contractual relationship with third parties, few tortious acts would

come within the scope of such employment. However, if in the performance of an agency and the furtherance thereof a tort is committed, or if the principal expressly authorizes or ratifies a tort, he becomes liable. See Nowack vs. Met. Street 

Ry. Co., 166 N. Y. 433 (investigator bribing witnesses); Simmon vs. Bloomingdale, 81 N. Y. Supp. 499 (salesman causing arrest-act ratified by general manager). Condit vs. Baldwin, 21 N. Y. 219; St. Johns vs. Fowler, 229 N. Y. 270; Burke vs. 

Bonat, 255 N. Y. 226.

Fraud. The liability of a principal for the torts of his agent usually arises in cases where fraud is practiced in securing the action of a third party.

The third person may seek to hold the principal liable in contract in an action for damages for breach of warranty sustained as a result of the false representations of the agent, or may seek to hold the principal liable in tort. In the first instance, the principal will not be liable unless the third person sustains the burden of proving either that the principal expressly or impliedly authorized the agent to make the false representations or had given him apparent authority or had ratified his making them. Smith vs. Tracy, 36 N. Y. 79; Jeffrey vs. Bigelow, 13 Wend. (N. Y.) 518; Forster vs. Wilhusen, 14 misc. 520. But in the tort action the principal may be held liable without any proof of authority or ratification for the doctrine is applied that the principal had it within his power to employ honest agents whereas the third person had no power over the agent and therefore the principal and not the third person should be made to suffer for the false representations of the agent. Farmers' and Traders' Bank vs. Butchers' 

Bank, 16 N. Y. 125. But a principal is not liable in tort for the fraudulent representations of an agent made outside the scope of his employment. Friedman vs. N. Y. Telephone Co., 256 N. Y. 392.

When an agent abandons the object of his agency and acts for himself by committing fraud for his exclusive benefit,

he ceases to act within the scope of his employment, and to that extent ceases to act as agent. Credit Alliance Corp. vs. 

Sheridan Theatre Co., 241 N. Y. 216.

For a case illustrating what frauds of an agent bind the principal, see Deyo vs. Hudson, 225 N. Y. 602; 46 A. L. R. 1207. 

Fraudulent Acts of Agent for Own Benefit. In the American courts there are two well defined and directly conflicting lines of decisions as to the principal's responsibility to a third person where the agent under color of his principal's business perpetrates a fraud for his own benefit. Both views rest on 

English cases for their authority. One group follows the New 

York rule which accepts the decision in Lloyd vs. Grace Smith

Co. (1912) A. C. 716 and holds the principal liable regardless of the intentions and purpose of the agent. Bank of Batavia vs. R. R. Co., 106 N. Y. 195. The doctrine as stated in the 

Batavia case supra is as follows: "Where the principal has clothed his agent with power to do an act, resting on the existence of some extrinsic fact, necessarily and peculiarly within the knowledge of the agent, and of the existence of which the act of executing the power is itself a representation, a third person dealing with such agent in entire good faith pursuant to the apparent authority may rely on the representation, and the principal is estopped from denying its truth to his prejudice." The other group of state courts and the federal courts have followed the rule stated in British 

Mutual Banking Co. vs. Charmwood Forest Ry. 18 Q. B. D. 

714 (1887), where the action against the defendant principal was dismissed on the ground that the deceit was for the agent's own benefit. Friedlander vs. T. & P. R. R. Co., 130 

U. S. 416. Mussey vs. Beecher 57 Mass. 511. In the case of

Gleason vs. Seaboard Air Line Ry. 49 Supp. Ct. 161, U. S. 

1928, it would appear that the Supreme Court has repudiated its former rule.

Under the New York rule it is necessary to show that the agent was acting within the general scope of his authority.

For a distinction on this point see Fifth Avenue Bank vs.

Ferry Ry. 137 N. Y. 231; Manhattan Life Ins. Co. vs. Ferry 

Ry., 139 N. Y. 146.

For a complete discussion see monograph in Cornell Law 

Quarterly, April, 1929, Vol. XIV, Page 363. Reprint N. Y. 

Law Journal, August 31st, 1929, page 2196. *

CHAPTER XII. 

LIABILITY OF THIRD PERSON TO PRINCIPAL. 

In General. If a contract is executed in the name and for the benefit of a principal, by an agent acting within the scope of his authority, the principal is bound to the third person, and the third person is bound to the principal and to no one else. Cochran vs. Mac Rae, 49 Misc. 529; Rand vs. Moulton, 72 A. D. 236. 

Sealed and Negotiable Instruments. As a contract under seal may not be enforced against persons not parties thereto, Crowley vs. Lewis, 239 N. Y. 264, the converse is also true, that an undisclosed principal may not enforce against a third party a contract that the third party has entered into under seal with the agent of the undisclosed principal, where the agent and the third party appear as the only parties thereto. 

Elliott vs. Brady, 192 N. Y. 221; Schaefer vs. Henkel, 45 

N. Y. 378; Henricus vs. Englert, 137 N. Y. 488; Spencer vs. 

Huntington, 100 A. D. 463. As the rights and liabilities of parties to negotiable paper must appear on the face of the instrument, undisclosed principals are precluded from proceeding thereon against third parties-Negotiable Instruments

Law sections 38, 39. However, a distinction exists in instances of instruments payable to cashiers or other fiscal officers of banks or corporations. See Negotiable Instruments Law, section 72; Bank of N. Y. vs. Bank of Ohio, 29 N. Y. 619; First 

Nat. Bank vs. Hall, 44 N. Y. 395.

Other Contracts. Liability to Undisclosed Principal. All simple non-negotiable contracts, executed by an agent in the furtherance of his commission, even though made in his own name without disclosing his principal, may be enforced by the principal in his own right. Talcott vs. Wabash R. R. Co., 159

N. Y. 461. The principal may intervene to enforce the contract, even after suit has been instituted by the agent. Alsop vs. Craines, 10 Johns (N. Y.) 396.

Where the principal knows that the third person does not want to contract with him, he sometimes resorts to the undisclosed principal device in order to obtain the contract through an agent who does not disclose the fact that he is acting only as agent. If there is active misrepresentation in this respect on the part of the agent, the third person is permitted to avoid the contract and the principal can neither have specific performance nor damages from the third person if the latter refuses to perform upon discovering the true facts. But mere failure to disclose agency is so common that it cannot be classed as a representation that there is none and hence in the absence of active misrepresentation the principal can recover damages from the third person for breach of the contract. 

Kelly vs. Barber Asphalt Paving Co., 211 N. Y. 68. But if specific performance is sought by the principal, it would seem that concealment of the principal with knowledge that the third person would not deal with him is sharp practice of the very sort that the unclean hands doctrine is designed to prevent. See 44 Harvard Law Review 1271. In such a case English courts have allowed specific performance. Nash vs. Dix, 78 L. T. 445, but American courts have uniformly refused to do so. Gwin vs. Tusa, 162 La. 949, 111 So. 339; Siess vs. 

Anderson, 159 Mo. App. 656, 139 S. W. 1178; Miller vs. Fulmer, 25 Pa. Super. Ct. 106; Cole vs. Hunter Tract Improvement Co., 61 Wash. 365, 112 Pac. 368.

Principal Excluded. A party to a contract may, however, exclude the possibility of an undisclosed principal subsequently appearing to enforce the obligation by express stipu lation in the contract. Moore vs. Vulcanite Co., 121 A. D. 

667; Jablon vs. Traynor, 135 N. Y. Supp. 545. Also if exclusive credit be given, or if the performance of some personal act, not yet done, is a condition precedent to a performance on the part of the third party, no undisclosed principal can force such third party to accept his performance in lieu of that of the person originally obligated. Boston Ice Company vs. 

Potter, 123 Mass. 28; N. Y. Bank Note Co. vs. Hamilton

Co., 180 N. Y. 280; Navarre Hotel Co. vs. American, etc. Co., 156 A. D. 795. 

Defenses. A third party is justified, before actual or constructive knowledge of the existence of a principal, in dealing with an agent as the only party to the transaction. So when suit is instituted by the undisclosed principal, the third party may interpose every defense arising from the terms of the contract itself, which existed against the agent at the time the principal intervened. Eldridge vs. Finninger, 25 Okla. 28, 28 L. R. A. (N. S.) 227; Feinstein vs. Ritter, 88 Misc. 559; 

Burnham vs. Eyre, 123 A. D. 777, aff'd 196 N. Y. 560. See

Article in 44 Harvard Law Review, page 1271, entitled "Per sonal Prejudice and the Doctrine of the Undisclosed Principal." (June, 1931).

Also a party contracting with an agent in his own name. without notice of his agency or reason to believe it exists, may set off a debt due him by the agent personally, when suit is instituted by the principal, even though the agent has violated instructions in contracting in his own name. Kent vs. 

De Coppet, 149 A. D. 589; Burnham vs. Eyre, 123 A. D. 577, aff'd 196 N. Y. 560; F. T. Banking Corp. vs. Gerseta Corp., 237 N. Y: 265.

A factor who has possession of goods has implied authority to accept payment, and a payment to him before the intervention of his principal is binding on the principal. Feinstein vs. Ritter, 88 Misc. 559.

Where a principal has clearly put it within the power of his agent to deceive third persons as to his representative character, he will be estopped from asserting his rights. For example, where an agent has possession of goods for the purpose of delivery to fulfill a contract, a payment to such agent in ignorance of the principal's ownership would bind the principal. Bliss vs. Bliss, 7 Bosw. (N. Y.) 339.

Similarly a third party who has acted in good faith, may, under situations similar to those above described, set off, when sued by the undisclosed principal, a debt due him by the agent, provided the same arose before notice of the agency. Mull vs. Ingalls, 62 N. Y. Supp. 830; Pallachek vs. Scholl, 51 A. D. 

319; Feinstein vs. Ritter, 88 Misc. 559. However, in contracting, facts that would put an ordinarily prudent man on notice cannot be ignored. Thus, where an agent, without disclosing his representative character, makes a sale of goods which are not in his possession and of which he has no indicia of ownership, the principal would not be bound by payment to such agent, nor could the third party set off a debt due him from such agent. McLachlin vs. Britt, 105 N. Y. 391. 

Money Paid Through Mistake. A principal may recover from a third party money. paid or property transferred by his agent to such third party through mistake, or under such other circumstances as would have justified a recovery had the principal himself paid the money or transferred the property. 

U. S. vs. Bartlett, 2 Ware (U. S.) 17; Mechem on Agency,

Vol. 2, page 1661. 

Property Wrongfully Transferred. Where an agent without authority transfers property of his principal, or where an agent who has possession of his principal's property, but not for the purpose of sale, fraudulently conveys the same to a third party, ordinarily no title passes and the principal may follow the property and recover it. Saltus vs. Everett, 20 

Wend. (N. Y.) 267. However the conduct of the principal may be such as to estop him from asserting the lack of authority in his Agent. As was announced in Barnard vs. Campbell, 55 N. Y. 456, two things must concur to create an estoppel by which an owner may be deprived of his property without his consent: 1. 

The owner must clothe the person who assumes to act as his agent with apparent title to the property, or authority to dispose of it.

2. 

The third party alleging the estoppel must have acted on the faith of such apparent ownership or authority, and have parted with value, so that he will be the loser if the appearances on which he relied are not real. See also Hentz vs. Miller, 94 

N. Y. 426.

Ordinarily mere possession of property by an alleged agent is not sufficient evidence either of title in the agent or authority to dispose of it. McNeil vs. Tenth National Bank, 46 N. Y. 325. However, if the subject matter of the agency is negotiable paper payable to bearer or indorsed in blank, or if the principal intrusts his property to an agent whose business it is to sell similar property, either as owner or agent for others, the rule would not apply. Bank vs. Vanderhorst, 32 

N. Y. 553; First Nat. Bank vs. Shaw, 61 N. Y. 283; Smith vs. Clews, 105 N. Y. 283.

Where an agent is entrusted not only with possession of his principal's goods, but also with the documents which constitute the usual indicia of ownership, the principal will be estopped from asserting his title where the rights of a bona fide purchaser for value have intervened. Delivering to an agent shares of stock indorsed in blank confers upon him the indicia of ownership. McNeil vs. Tenth National Bank, 46 

N. Y. 325.

Factors Act. In New York State and in many other jurisdictions, the legislatures have passed laws for the protection of third parties who have dealt in good faith with factors or other agents, believing them to be the true owners of goods. 

The New York statute (Personal Property Law section 43) 

substantially codifies the principles announced in the previous paragraph, and gives a factor the additional power to pledge goods in his possession for money advanced by him. 

Following Trust Funds. Money or other property of a principal in the hands of his agent is deemed to be held by such agent in trust for his principal. If the agent wrongfully

converts the money or property into another species of property, the trust will still follow as long as the original property can be identified. Bank vs. Peters, 123 N. Y. 272; Van 

Alen vs. Bank, 52 N. Y. 1; Roca vs. Byrne, 145 N. Y. 182; 

Baker vs. N. Y. Nat. Ex. Bank, 100 N. Y. 31. Thus in Ranger vs. Thalman, 178 N. Y. 574, a firm through its agent, purchased certain property, but title was taken in the name of the agent. It was held that a trust resulted in favor of the firm, and it was the equitable owner of the property. But if an agent, wrongfully using his principal's funds, purchases property and then transfers the same to an innocent purchaser for value, the trust cannot be enforced. The principal's title is equitable, and it cannot defeat a legal title acquired for value and without notice. Justh vs. National Bank, 56 

N. Y. 478.

Fraud. A third party, who in his dealings with an agent, commits a fraud, is liable to the principal as though the fraud had been committed on the latter person. Similarly if the third party in collusion with the agent, works a fraud on the principal, the latter may elect to sue both agent and third party for the damages he has sustained, or may rescind the contract and receive such relief as equity will decree. Waterbury vs. Barry, 145 A. D. 773. 

Causing Loss of Service. "The great weight of authority in this country and in England is to the effect that if A has a legal contract with B, either for the rendition of service or any other purpose, and C, having knowledge of the existence thereof, intentionally and knowingly and without reasonable justification or excuse induces B to break the contract, by reason of which A sustains damage, an action will lie by A against C to recover the same." Campbell vs. Gates, 236 

N. Y. 457; Lamb vs. Cheney & Sons, 227 N. Y. 418; see 45 

L. R. A. 1481.

RIGHTS AND LIABILITIES BETWEEN AGENT AND THIRD PARTY. 

CHAPTER XIII. 

LIABILITY OF AGENT TO THIRD PERSON. 

In General. As long as an agent acts within the scope of his real or apparent authority, and contracts in the name and on behalf of his principal, no personal liability attaches to him. 

His principal alone is bound. So also where an unauthorized act is subsequently ratified by his principal, the latter is solely responsible. Ernst vs. Thom, 65 Misc. 206; Bonynge vs. Field, 81 N. Y. 159. The agent becomes personally liable only in the event he acts beyond his authority, or acts within his authority but contracts in his own name, or is guilty of a tort in conjunction with his principal.

Simple Contracts. If an agent enters into a simple contract on behalf of an undisclosed principal, but contracts in his own name, he may be held personally liable at the option of the third party. In Meyer vs. Redmond, 205 N. Y. at page 

483, the court cites with approval the doctrine upon this subject as laid down in Magee vs. Atkinson, 2 M. & W. page 

440, as follows: "At this day the law must be considered as settled that a vendor or purchaser dealing in his own name, without disclosing the names of his principal, is personally bound by his contract, and it makes no difference that he is known to the other party to be an auctioneer, or broker, who is usually employed in selling property as the agent for others. Even where he discloses the name of his principle, if he signs a written contract in his own name merely, which contract does not upon its face show that he was acting as the agent of another, or in an official capacity in behalf of the government, he will be personally bound thereby." See also Levy vs. Shour, 178 N. Y. 

Supp. 227; Erie R. R. vs. Rosenstein, Inc., 249 N. Y. 241.

Where a party to a written contract is known to the other to be in fact acting as agent for some known principal, he does not become personally liable whether he signs individually or as agent. Ell Dee Clothing Co. vs. Marsh, 247 N. Y. 392.

Parol evidence is not admissible for the purpose of exonerating an agent who has entered into a written contract in which he appears as principal, even though he purposes to show that he disclosed his agency and mentioned the name of his principal at the time the contract was executed. Nash vs. Towne, 5 Wall. 689; Argersinger vs. MacNaughton, 114 

N. Y. 535. See also Metcalf vs. Williams, 104 U. S. 93.

If the body of the contract recites that it is made by the person signing it "as" agent or other representative, or "for and on behalf of" a designated principal, the form of signature is immaterial and the principal and not the agent is bound. Stearns vs. Allen, 25 Hun. (N. Y.) 558. But the mere addition of the term "agent" to a signature or the mere description in the body of the writing of the signer as agent creates no presumption that the signer did not intend to contract personally. Walker vs. Bank, 9 N. Y. 582; Argersinger vs. MacNaughton, 114 N. Y. 535. The burden is upon the agent, if he would escape personal liability, to fully disclose the fact of the agency and the identity of his principal at the time he enters into the contract with the third party. Cobb vs. Knapp, 71 N. Y. 348. For an interesting discussion of the liability of an attorney for the cost of printing briefs, see editorial in New York Law Journal, Feb. 23, 1924.

Where an agent purports to make a contract for a principal disclosing the fact that he is acting as agent, but not naming his principal, the rule is that unless a contrary intention appears, he makes himself personally liable on the authorized contract. Bender vs. Campbell (1925) 2 K. B. 410. 14 

Br. Rul. Cas. 295.

Where the contract is made with an agent who does not disclose his agency, the contract is made with the agent indi-

vidually and he may be held by the third person even though he was acting within the scope of his authority. Biedelman vs. Kelly, 51 Misc. 51. If, however, the agent discloses the fact that he is acting for a principal, and names the principal for whom he is acting, the agent is not personally bound. In such a case, the presumption is that the third person is extending credit to the principal and not to the agent, and the person giving the credit must look to the principal. However, even if the third person knows the existence and identity of the principal, he may prefer to extend credit to the agent instead. If the third person has clearly manifested such an intention, he cannot thereafter hold the principal, even though the agent was acting within the scope of his employment and for the benefit of the principal. Melker vs. Cleghorn, 44 N. Y. 

349. Where the agent informs the third person that he is acting only as agent but declines to name his principal, the third person may hold the agent personally liable on the contract unless it clearly appears that the third person preferred to take his chance with the unknown principal rather than with the credit of the agent. Meyer vs. Redmond, 205 N. Y. 478. In such circumstances the third party will presumably intend to contract with the agent rather than with the unknown principal and for this reason may hold the agent personally liable on the contract. If the agent was not acting for any principal at all, the supposed agent has been permitted to hold the third person to the contract, on the theory that the third person presumably does not care who the principal is, where the agent states there is one but declines to name him and nevertheless contracts with the agent, and therefore that the misrepresentation is immaterial. Williston, Contracts, sec. 286. 

Sealed Instruments. Whether or not an agent is bound on a sealed instrument which he has attempted to execute on behalf of his principal, depends on a construction of the instrument. Sealed instruments are strictly and technically construed, and the mere fact that one of the parties to the instrument has the word "agent" appended to his name will not necessarily relieve him, for such term may be considered

merely descriptio personae. 

See Whitford vs. Laidler, 94 

N. Y. 145; Briggs vs. Partridge, 64 N. Y. 357; Kiersted vs. Ry. 

Co., 69 N. Y. 343. 

Negotiable Instruments. Under section 39 of the Negotiable Instruments Law, a person who signs an instrument containing or to which he adds words indicating that he signed for or on behalf of a principal or in his representative capacity, is liable on the instrument if he has signed without authority. It was held in New Georgia Nat. Bank of Albany, Ga. vs. J. & L. Lippman, 164 N. E. 108, 249 N. Y. 307, that a complaint on a promissory note signed "J. & L. Lippman, L. J. 

Lippman, Pres." and demanding judgment in the alternative against the corporation or against the president personally, if, in signing the note in behalf of his corporation, he acted without authority, stated a good cause of action against the individual defendant. See Casco Nat. Bank vs. Clark, 139 

N. Y. 307; First Nat. Bank vs. Wallis, 150 N. Y. 455.

Public Officers. A public officer is not personally liable on a contract made in behalf of the government, unless he specifically pledges his personal credit. Hall vs. Lauderdale, 46 N. Y. 70.

ACTING WITHOUT AUTHORITY. 

In General. A contract executed in the name and presumably on behalf of a principal by an agent who has no authority to so act, binds neither the principal nor the agent. 

White vs. Madison, 26 N. Y. 117. It is clear the agent is not bound by the contract if he is not a party to it, and the courts will not create a new contract either against or in favor of him. Dung vs. Parker, 52 N. Y. 494; Simmonds vs. More, *100 N. Y. 140; Baltzen vs. Nicolay, 53 N. Y. 467; Taylor vs. 

Nostrand, 134 N. Y. 108. However, independent of the contract the agent is liable to the third party on the theory either of deceit, or breach of warranty of authority. See Columbia 

Law Review, Vol. 26, page 224; Yale Law Review, Vol. 35, page 625; also Harriss vs. Tams, 258 N. Y. 229.

Deceit. One who fraudulently represents that he has authority to act for another, is liable in an action ex delicto to a third party who has acted to his damage on such false representation. Dung vs. Parker, 52 N. Y. 494. An express representation of authority is not necessary to constitute a deceit, for if one deals with the third party as possessing authority, and fails to disclose his lack of authority, he is nevertheless liable. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 1004; 

21 R. C. L. page 915. If the contract which the agent assumed to execute was void under the Statute of Frauds, no liability would attach to the agent, as the third party can suffer no damage due to the deceit. Dung vs. Parker, 52 N. Y. 494; 

Baltzen vs. Nicolay, 52 N. Y. 467.

Breach of Warranty of Authority. In cases where an agent, in good faith, acts under a mistaken belief that he possesses authority, he is not liable ex delicto for deceit. protect third parties, however, the courts have evolved the fiction that "when a person assumes to act for another, he warrants possession of the authority which he assumes to exercise." Simmonds vs. More, 100 N. Y. 140; White vs. 

Madison, 26 N. Y. 117; Russel vs. Koonce, 104 N. Y. 237; 

Moore vs. Maddock, 251 N. Y. 420. But if the agent in good faith discloses to the third person all the facts on which he bases his assumption of authority, no warranty of authority can be implied. Hall vs. Lauderdale, 46 N. Y. 72; Notes 34

L. R. A. (N. S.) 522. 

The liability of an agent to one dealing with him without knowledge of any limitations upon his authority is not upon the ground that the contract is that of the agent, but on the ground that he warrants his authority. The agent's liability is therefore not measured by the contract, but is for all injuries resulting from his want of power. Moore vs. Maddock, 224 A. D. 401; 231 N. Y. Supp. 291.

Damages. The usual measure of damages in contract cases for breach of warranty of authority is the amount that could have been recovered against the principal for breach of

contract, had the contract been authorized. Simmonds vs. 

More, 100 N. Y. 140; Bush vs. Cole, 28 N. Y. 261; Taylor vs. Nostrand, 134 N. Y. 108. It follows that if the contract could not have been enforced against the principal, no recovery can be had against the agent. Dung vs. Parker, 52 

N. Y. 494. 

Liability for Money Received. An agent who, in good faith, has received money, but under such circumstances as would entitle the third party to recover it had the principal in person received it, must, on demand, return the money to the third party or answer in damages for his refusal. But if the agent has paid over the money to his principal before notice of the claim of the third party, he is protected. La 

Farge vs. Kneeland, 7 Cow. (N. Y.) 456; Ledwith vs. Merritt, 77 N. Y. Supp. 341, aff'd 174 N. Y. 512. See Sagone vs. Mackey, 225 N. Y. 594. However, if the agent has not disclosed his representative character, and the third party has dealt with him as a principal, a payment to the real principal before notice of the third party's claim would not excuse the agent. Klotz vs. Gordon, 117 N. Y. Supp. 240; Holt vs. 

Roso, 54 N. Y. 473. In every instance where an agent acquires money wrongfully, he is personally liable even though he has delivered the funds in toto to his principal. Repley vs. Gelston, 9 Johns.. (N. Y.) 201. 

Money Received from Principal. If a principal gives money to his agent to be delivered to a third party, the principal may, at any time before the agent has actually paid over the money or has assumed the obligation to do so, countermand the order. Seaman vs. Whitney, 24 Wend. (N. Y.) 

260. The agent will not be liable to the third party unless in his relations with the third party he has so recognized or assented to the appropriation of the money that a privity of contract has arisen between them. In a situation of this type the principal's right to revoke the commission is lost, and the agent must perform. Wyman vs. Smith, 2 Sandf. (N. Y.) 

331; Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 1072.

Liability for Torts. The general rule is that both principal and agent are jointly liable to a third party for a tort committed by the agent in the furtherance of his duties, if the tort was occasioned by the malfeasance or misfeasance of the agent, and this is true even though the act is done at the express direction of the principal. Spraight vs. Hawley, 39 N. Y. 441. Thus an agent may be personally liable for a trespass committed under the direction of his principal. Page vs. Dempsey, 184 N. Y. 245. Similarly the agent who, for his principal, sells or otherwise disposes of the property of a third party is liable in an action for conversion, even though the agent acted in good faith, believing the property to be that of his principal. Spraight vs. Hawley, 39 N..Y. 441.

Where an agent by actual fraud obtains money, he may be made to restore it in a suit to rescind, though he is not a party to the contract, and though he has turned the money over to his principal. Peterson vs. McManus, 187 Iowa, 522, 172 N. W. 460. In Laska vs. Harris, 215 N. Y. 554, 556, it was said in regard to a counterclaim: "The fact that in making the false and fradulent representations the plaintiff may have been acting for others may have rendered his principals liable also, but gave him no immunity for the false and fradulent representations that he made. If the plaintiff made the false representations alleged, with a fradulent design and damage. resulted from them to the defendant, the plaintiff is liable, though he may not have profited from them, or had any interest in the deception and although the fraud may have induced the defendant to contract with others as well as the plaintiff."

The New York courts make a distinction, in cases involving the liability of an agent to a third party in torts, between cases of nonfeasance and cases of malfeasance and misfeasance, the rule being that an agent is not liable to a stranger for mere nonfeasance. Van Antwerp vs. Linton, 89 Hun 417, 35 N. Y. Supp. 318, aff'd in 157 N. Y. 716;

Potter vs. Gilbert, 130 A. D. 632, 115 N. Y. Supp. 425, aff'd 

196 N. Y. 576. The following is a quotation from Van Antwerp vs. Linton: "*** For whatever may be the rule in other jurisdictions, it is conceded that in this state an agent or servant is not liable to third persons for nonfeasance.

As between himself and his master, he is bound to serve him with fidelity; and for a breach of his duty he becomes liable to the master, who in turn, may be charged in damages for injuries to third persons occasioned by the nonfeasance of the servant. For misfeasance the agent is generally liable to third parties suffering thereby. 

The distinction between nonfeasance and misfeasance has been expressed by the courts of this state as follows: "If the duty omitted by the agent or servant devolved upon him purely from his agency or employment, his omission is only of a duty he owes his principal or master, and the master alone is liable; while, if the duty rests upon him in his individual character, and was one that the law imposed upon him independently of his agency or employment, then he is liable."

Liability of Agent Purporting to Act for Non-existent 

Principal. Where a promoter represents that a corporation is already in existence and that he has authority to contract for it, the law is clear that he is liable. His liability is based on the breach of an implied warranty of the existence of the corporation and authority to contract for it. Where a promoter assumes to make a contract for a corporation admittedly not yet in existence, there is no room for the application of the doctrine of implied warranty of authority. However, it is generally said that the promoter is liable on the contract itself, unless it is expressly agreed that the other party shall look for performance only to the corporation when formed. In the case of Weiss vs. Baum, 217 N. Y. Supp. 820, defendant, one Charles Baum, represented to the plaintiff that he was

organizing the "Ruth Realty Corp." and induced the plaintiff to enter into a written contract to sell land to such corporation. 

The contract was signed "Ruth Realty Corp. By Charles 

The corporation was in fact never organized and the plaintiff brought an action against the defendant for specific performance. From an order granting defendant's motion to dismiss the complaint on the ground that it did not set forth a cause of action the plaintiff appealed. It was held that an action for specific performance of the contract as though made with the defendant individually could not be maintained. It would seem as in the Baum case, supra, that where the contract is in writing and is made in the name of the intended corporation that there is an exception to the general rule above stated and that the promoter shall not be held liable on the contract. The fact that no one can be bound is not enough to make the promoter a party to the contract. 

LIABILITY OF THIRD PERSON TO AGENT. 

On Contract. A contract, properly executed in the name and on behalf of a principal, is the principal's contract, and his agent has ordinarily no authority to sue on it. Buckbee vs. Brown, 21 Wend. (N. Y.) 110.

In cases where an agent has executed a sealed or negotiable instrument in such a manner that the principal is not bound, but the agent is, then the agent may enforce such contract in his own name. Cleary vs. Heyward, 123 N. Y. Supp. 

334; Buge vs. Newman, 61 Misc. 84.

Also where an agent executes a simple non-negotiable contract in his own name, but in behalf of an undisclosed principal, the agent may enforce the obligation until the principal intervenes. Manet vs. Simpson, 61 Hun (N. Y.) 620; 

Ludwig vs. Gillespie, 105 N. Y. 653. But where the agent has a vested interest in the subject matter of his agency, such as a lien for services or a special property right, his right of action will be protected as against the right of the principal to intervene. Minturn vs. Main, 7 N. Y. 220. In an action

of this type the measure of the agent's recovery would be the same as though the principal has instituted the action, and the agent will be deemed to hold the proceeds in trust for his principal. U. S. Tel. Co. vs. Gildersleeve, 96 Am. Dec. 519.

Liability for Broker's Loss of Commissions. Where a broker is employed by an owner to sell real estate and a person whom the broker seeks to interest in the property submits a counter-offer to the broker which is acceptable to the owner, and the prospective purchaser knowing that the broker will receive a commission from the owner if the deal goes through, breaks off the deal, he is not liable to the broker for loss of commissions. Grossman vs. Herman, 240

A. D. 525, 270 N. Y. Supp. 669. (App. Div. First Dept.) 

overruling or disapproving of Pease & Elliman, Inc. vs. 

Gladwin Realty Co., 216 A. D. 421. In the Grossman case the court said: "Where a plaintiff is employed as a broker to sell, he must look for his compensation to the party with whom he made the contract and by whom he was employed. If a broker is to be permitted to resort to the practice here followed, anyone to whom he endeavors to sell a piece of property who makes a counter-offer would be liable for damages if the owner accepted and the sale was not thereafter consummated." 

Defenses. A third party may interpose as defenses to an action brought by an agent in his own name, all defenses good at law or in equity against either the agent or the principal for whose benefit the action is instituted. Ewell's Evans on Agency, page 387. 

Liability for Money Paid. If an agent pays out money of his principal to a third party under a mistake of fact or because of fraud on the part of the payee, the agent may sue in his own name to recover it. Kent vs. Bornstein, 12 Allen (Mass.) 342.

Tort Liability. An agent who has a special property or interest in the goods of his principal may recover in an action against a third party damages for a violation of this right of property or possession. Bank vs. Bank, 60 N. Y. 40; Fitzhugh vs. Wiman, 9 N. Y. 559. Thus a bailee of goods or a factor may maintain an action in his own name against the wrongdoer for damages to the property which is the subject of the agency. Pullman Car Co. vs. Gavin, 21 L. R. A. 298.

An agent may maintain an action against a third party who maliciously and without cause induced his principal to discharge him. Curran vs. Galen, 152 N. Y. 33.

RIGHTS AND LIABILITIES BETWEEN PRINCIPAL AND AGENT. 

CHAPTER XIV. 

OBLIGATION OF AGENT TO PRINCIPAL. 

In General. The mutual obligations and duties arising out of the relationship of principal and agent are contractual in nature. In general, an agent must obey instructions, exercise the care and diligence necesary to prudently discharge his agency, act in good faith, and account to his principal for money received.

Duty to Obey Instructions. An agent is under the absolute duty of following out express instructions given him by his principal, provided the instructions are lawful, and is liable for all losses sustained by his principal due to the deviation, although the course he pursued was reasonable and intended for his principal's benefit. Laverty vs. Snethen, 68 

N. Y. 523; Evans vs. Root, 7 N. Y. 186. Thus if an agent be intrusted to ship goods at a certain time and by a designated carrier, and he nevertheless ships at another time and by a different carrier, he is personally liable to his principal for any loss sustained. Johnson vs. N. Y. Cent. Ry., 33 N. 

Y. 610.

However, if the deviation of the agent affects merely the manner of the execution, and not the result, and causes the principal no loss, substantial damages could not be recovered against the agent. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 913. 

Although no express instructions are given, the nature of a particular agency will frequently give rise to certain implied instructions which have the same binding force as expressed instructions. Tiffany on Agency, page 397; Story on Agency, section 189. Thus an agent to collect is ordinarily limited to collecting cash. 

Justification for Disobedience. (1) An agent instructed to perform an act which is illegal, immoral or opposed to

public policy, is not liable to his principal for non-performance. Brown vs. Howard, 14 Johns. (N. Y.) 119. (2) In the face of a sudden emergency which has arisen without the agent's fault, and which threatens the safety of the principal's property, the agent may in good faith and in the exercise of discretion, diviate from the instructions given him, if a literal adherence to them would cause loss. Milbank vs. Dennistown, 21 N. Y. 86; Jervis vs. Hoyt, 2 Hun (N. Y.) 637. (3) If the instructions given an agent are so ambiguous that they are capable of two interpretations, and the agent in good faith adopts one, he will not be liable, even though the principal intended the other interpretation. Shelton vs. Merchants Dispatch, 59 N. Y. 258. (4) An agent, as, for example, a factor, who has advanced money or incurred obligations with respect to his principal's property, may disregard instructions which will imperil his security, unless he is indemnified by the principal. Marfield vs. Goodhue, 3 N. Y. 62.

Duty to Exercise Care and Diligence. An agent, by accepting an appointment impliedly undertakes to exercise in the furtherance of his agency that degree of skill, care and diligence which the nature of the undertaking and the circumstances of performance reasonably demand. Heinemann vs. Heard, 50 N. Y. 35; Whitney vs. Martine, 88 N. Y. 535; 

First Nat. Bank vs. Fourth Nat. Bank, 77 N. Y. 320. An agent is not ordinarily an insurer, nor is he required to exercise the highest possible degree of care. Good faith, reasonable diligence and an application of such care and skill as are ordinarily possessed by persons of ordinary capacity. engaged in a similar business, are sufficient. Whitney vs. Martine, 88 N. Y. 535; Seventeenth Ward Bank vs. Smith, 51 

A. D. 259; McCauley vs. Bank, 245 N. Y. 245.

Whenever the agency in its nature requires the possession and exercise of professional skill (lawyer, doctor, etc.),

the agent who assumes performance must bring to the undertaking such knowledge and skill as is possessed by competent men engaged in a like trade or calling. Harriman vs. Bair, 6 A. D. 518; Wille vs. Maier, 256 N. Y. 465. Where the agency is in its nature gratuitous, there is no obligation on the part of the agent to enter into performance. Thorne vs. Deas, 4 Johns. (N. Y.) 84. But having once entered into performance he must exercise that degree of skill and care that he expressly or impliedly undertook to use by accepting the appointment. Hun vs. Cary, 82 N. Y. 65; Barile vs. Wright, 256,

N. Y. 1. An agent's liability does not depend on the reward he is to receive, and the absence of compensation is merely evidential in establishing. the nature and extent of the care he impliedly undertakes to use. In cases where the agent holds himself out as possessing particular skill, the absence of compensation is of no significance whatsoever. Isham vs. 

Post, 141 N. Y. 100; Candon vs. Exton-Hall, etc., 80 Misc. 369. 

Duty to Act in Good Faith. "Loyalty to his trust is the first duty that the agent owes to his principal. Without it the perfect relation cannot exist." Mechem on Agency, Vol. I, page 867; Keller vs. Am. Chain Co., 255 N. Y. 94. An agent may not acquire an interest adverse to that of his principal; thus, without the full knowledge and consent of his principal, an agent to purchase may not sell his own property to his principal. Conkey vs. Bond, 36 N. Y. 427. Similarly an agent to sell will not be permitted to purchase his principal's property for himself. Bain vs. Brown, 56 N. Y. 285; Helfat vs. 

Whitehouse, 258 N. Y. 274. The principal may repudiate the transaction in either instance whether he has been damaged or not. An agent will not be permitted to acquire on his own account a lease of the principal's premises which he knows. the principal desires and intends to renew. Davis vs. Hamlin, 108 Ill. 39; Gower vs. Andrews, 59 Cal. 119. An agent may not, without permission, act for both parties to a transaction, for each is entitled to the disinterested efforts and judgment of his agent. Carman vs. Beach, 63 N. Y. 71. If, however, the agent's employment is merely to bring two parties

together to contract, and nothing is left to his discretion, the rule is different. Knaus vs. Brewing Co., 142 N. Y. 70. See 

Visigraph vs. Spiro, 122 Misc. 852, 204 N. Y. Supp. 813.

The mere fact that an employee creates an invention while in the service of his master does not thereby give to the employer the right to letters patent which are procured for the invention. The employee is entitled to the exclusive title in the patent. Hapgood vs. Hewitt, 119 U. S. 226.

Where one employs a skilled workman to make improvements on certain machinery, he is not entitled to a conveyance of the patents secured by the workman on inventions made by him during the course of his work, in the absence of a specific agreement to that effect. Barber vs. National Carbon Co., 129 

Fed. 370; Burr vs. DeLaVergne, 102 N. Y. 415; Goodyear 

Tire & Rubber Co. vs. Mills, 14 Fed. 2nd 776.

Information respecting trade secrets, lists of customers and the like, acquired by an agent under an express or implied contract not to disclose it or under such circumstances that make the information confidential is likewise protected and the principal may restrain the agent from utilizing the knowledge so acquired to the principal's detriment. Reichenbach vs. Eastman Kodak Co., 79 Hun (N. Y.) 183. Kaumagraph Co. vs. Stampagraph Co., 235 N. Y. 1.

However, this rule does not prevent the agent from utilizing the experience, skill or training that he acquires in the course of his agency. It was said in New Era Gas Co. vs. 

Shannon, 44 Ill. App. 477: "Every agent has a lawful right to carry with him into a new employment all the skill and knowledge acquired in his previous engagement and nothing short of an express contract on his part not to do so will debar him, and then only under the strict rules of law especially established to protect trade secrets." See also Proctor vs. Mahin, 93 Fed. 875.

"If the agent deals with the subject matter of his agency, and obtains better results than could have been obtained other-. wise, the principal can claim the advantage thus obtained, even though the agent may have contributed his own funds. or responsibility in producing the results." Dutton vs. Willner, 52 N. Y. 312. An agent may be enjoined from disclosing trade secrets learned from his principal. Little vs. Gallus, 4 A. D. 569; S. W. Scott & Co. vs. Scott, 174 N. Y. Supp. 583. 

Duty to Account. An agent is under an absolute duty to account to his principal for all the funds belonging to his principal, which come into his hands by virtue of the agency. 

Hartmann vs. Schrugg, 113 A. D. 254; aff'd 188 N. Y. 617.

In addition, an agent must keep his principal's money and property separate from his own. If he commingles the property, everything not clearly proved to be his own, will be deemed to belong to his principal. Hart vs. Ten Eyck, 2 

Johns, (N. Y.) 62. If an agent deposits money belonging to his principal in a bank in his own name, and the bank fails, the agent is liable to the principal for his loss. Williams vs. 

Williams, 55 Wis. 300; 42 Am. Rep. 708.

At what time an agent must pay over the funds in his hands belonging to his principal, depends on the original agreement, the principal's instructions, and the nature of the agency. Ordinarily an agent is bound to account on demand, or within a reasonable time. Mechem on Agency, Vol. I. page 972. In rendering his account, an agent may usually deduct from the claim of his principal, his commission and expenses, but he may not deduct a claim due from a transaction not arising out of the agency. Duff vs. Blair, 74 A. D. 

364; Picker vs. Weiss, 39 Misc. 22. See also National Surety 

Co. vs. Empire State Surety Co., 128 N. Y. Supp. 10. Usually a demand upon the agent for payment is a condition precedent to the maintenance of a cause of action by his principal. Colvin vs. Holbrook, 2 N. Y. 130; Baruch vs. Platt, 114 N. Y. Supp. 26. However, circumstances may exist which would indicate a duty on the part of the agent to pay over

without an express demand. The rule was announced in Carr vs. Thompson, 87 N. Y. 160, that the necessity of a demand depended on whether or not the agency was a continuous one. 

An agent is liable for interest where he retains money of his principal after demand is made, or wrongfully keeps it when no demand is necessary. People vs. Gasherie, 9 Johns. (N. 

Y.) 71.

Sub-agents. Whether the agent is liable personally to his principal for the default of a sub-agent, or whether the sub-agent alone is liable, depends on whether or not there is privity of contract between the principal and the sub-agent. 

Ayrault vs. Bank, 47 N. Y. 570. See also Wayerhauser vs. 

Dunn, 100 N. Y. 150; Dunn vs. City National Bank, 58 Fed. 174.

CHAPTER XV. 

OBLIGATION OF PRINCIPAL TO AGENT. 

In General. In the absence of circumstances that will relieve him from his obligation, a principal is under a duty to pay his agent for services rendered, to reimburse him for expenses properly incurred in the furtherance of his commission, and to indemnify him for personal loss occasioned in a proper execution of his duties. 

Compensation. The right to compensation may arise by express agreement between the parties or may be implied from the nature of the services rendered and the conditions under which the employment was entered upon. Simonson vs. Simonson, 53 Hun (N. Y.) 634. However, conditions may be such that no implication of an agreement to pay for services rendered may arise. In cases where near relatives render services for each other, there must be a clear and unequivocal evidence of an agreement to pay, for the law presumes that the acts were inspired by motives of affection. Collyer vs. 

Collyer, 113 N. Y. 442. Similarly no compensation can be had for services which were voluntarily offered, or were performed as a mere gratuity. Bartholomew vs. Jackson, 20 

Johns. (N. Y.) 28; Ehle vs. Judson, 24 Wend. (N. Y.) 97. 

If a principal ratified an unauthorized act done in his behalf by another, he is generally under a duty to compensate such other party. In the absence of a stipulation in the contract of agency as to the amount of compensation, an agent is entitled to the reasonable value of the services rendered. Bear vs. Koch, 2 Misc. 334.

When Compensation is Due. As a general rule an agent must fully complete his undertaking as a condition precedent to becoming entitled to compensation. Hale vs. Brooklyn L. 

Ins. Co., 120 N. Y. 294; Phelan vs. Mayor, 119 N. Y. 86. 

See also Aldrich vs. N. Y. Life Ins. Co., 121 A. D. 18; Hercules vs. Met. Life Assur. Co. vs. Brinker, 77 N. Y. 435; Hale

vs. Brooklyn Life Ins. Co., 120 N. Y. 435. Where an agent has fully accomplished all he has set out to do, an arbitrary refusal of his principal to accept his services does not deprive him of his right to compensation. Jacquin vs. Boutard, 157 

N.Y. 686. Thus a broker who secures a purchaser who is ready, willing and able to purchase the principal's property upon the terms announced by him to his agent, is entitled to his commission whether the sale is consummated or not. 

Mooney vs. Elder, 56 N. Y. 238; John Reis Co. vs. Zimmerli, 120 N. E. 692; O'Hara vs. Bronx Consumers Ice Co., 254 

N. Y. 210.

The following is quoted from Slattery vs. Cothran, 210 A. D. 581: 

"The general rule is that where an exclusive right of sale is given a broker, the principal cannot make a sale himself without becoming liable for the commissions. 

Moses vs. Bieiling, 31 N. Y. 462; Levy vs. Rothe, 17 Misc. 

Rep. 402; 9 C. J. 622. But where the contract is merely to make the broker the sole agent, the principal may make a sale himself without the broker's aid, if such sale is made in good faith and to some purchaser not procured by the broker. McClave vs. Paine, 49 N. Y. 561;

Parkhurst vs. Tryon, 134 A. D. 843; Davis vs. Van 

Tassel, 107 N. Y. Supp. 910.

For other cases involving right of broker to commissions, see Colvin vs. Post Mortgage and Land Co., 225 N. Y. 510; 

Taylor vs. Crucible Steel Co., 192 A. D. 445, aff'd 232 N. Y. 583.

In this state it is the law that an insurance agent is entitled to commissions on renewal premiums paid after the termination of his agency, unless his contract expressly provides to the contrary. Aldrich vs. N. Y. Life Insurance Co., 235 N. Y. 214.

Where a principal, by abandoning his business, prevents his agent from earning commissions on future sales, the agent

is entitled to sue for damages for breach of contract.

Green berg vs. American Technical Book Co., 33 Misc. 786. 

As to a broker's right to commission where the owner sells property to a customer of broker at less than stipulated price, see Note 43 A. L. R. 1103. The general proposition is "that if property is placed in the hands of a broker for sale and sale is brought about through broker as a procuring cause, he is entitled to commission on the sale even though the final negotiations are conducted through the owner, who in order to make the sale accepts a less price than stipulated to the broker." Martin vs. Silliman, 53 N. Y. 615; Martin vs. 

Fegan, 95 A. D. 154, 88 N. Y. Supp. 472; Travis vs. Bowron, 138 A. D. 554, 123 N. Y. Supp. 290

Where a broker sends his customer direct to the owner, who carries on the negotiations himself, resulting in a sale at a lower price, the result is the same. Martin vs. Fegan, supra; Levy vs. Coogan, 16 Daly 137, 9 N. Y. Supp. 534. 

For cases in line with Sibbald vs. Bethlehem Steel, see 

Levy vs. Coogan, supra; Schano vs. Storch, 56 Misc. 484, 107 

N. Y. Supp. 26; Gaillard Realty Co. vs. Rogers Wire Works, 213 N. Y. Supp. 616.

Rule that a broker must produce a financially responsible purchaser is not applicable where enforceable agreement for sale is made, as seller is deemed to have indicated his satisfaction with purchaser's financial ability by executing contract. Flicker vs. Ragan, 126 Misc. 185, 212 N. Y. Supp. 703.

A broker is entitled to commission nothwithstanding sale is not consummated, where failure to complete was due to fact that owner had misdescribed property. Kasofsky vs. 

Monahan, 138 Misc. 19.

Where the contract provides that the broker shall be entitled to his commission "on the closing of title," it is a condition precedent to the principal's liability for the commission that title be closed on the day named or on an adjourned day,

so that if the principal agrees with the purchaser to relieve him from the contract upon payment of a sum of money in settlement therefor, the broker has no cause of action for his commission. Amies vs. Wesnofske, 255 N. Y. 156. The vendor of property does not even impliedly promise his broker that he, the vendor, will take active steps or assure performance by the vendee of the contract of sale. A vendor is under no duty to his broker to seek specific performance by the vendee when the commissions are conditional upon performance. Hence the vendor may accept forfeiture by the vendee, retain the down payment made, without thereby becoming liable to the broker for the commission. "A cause of action for loss of commission which plaintiff would have earned from a third party with whom he brought defendant into contract, resulting in an agreement for the exchange of their properties, which defendant afterwards refused to complete, is properly dismissed, where, although defendant had agreed to pay plaintiff commission on the agreed value of his own property, it does not appear that he had agreed, expressly or impliedly, to pay the commission which the broker might receive from the other party if the deal went through or that the parties when they entered into the brokerage contract had in mind, and contracted with reference to such com missions." Alan Fox Co. vs. Wohl, 255 N. Y. 268. 

Revocation of Authority. As has been previously announced, an agent's authority, unless it be coupled with an interest or obligation, may be revoked at any time by his principal. When the agency is not created to last for any specified time and the principal discharges the agent without cause, the agent is entitled to receive the reasonable value of the services rendered. U. S. vs. Jarvis, 2 Ware (U. S.) 278. When a principal discharges his agent without cause and in express violation of the terms of the contract of hiring, the latter may elect to consider the contract rescinded and recover the reasonable value of the services he has rendered, or he may sue for

damages for breach of contract. Howard vs. Daly, 61 N. Y. 

362. If he elects the latter remedy, he may immediately institute suit and recover his probable losses, or he may wait till the end of the term stipulated in the contract and recover his actual losses. Weed vs. Burt, 78 N. Y. 192. If the action is brought and the trial takes place before the expiration of the term stipulated in the contract, the damages are prima facie a sum equal to the stipulated compensation from time of discharge to the expiration of the term. Howard vs. Daly, 61 N. Y. 362; Griffin vs. Brooklyn Ball Club, 68 A. D. 566.

However, the principal may reduce this amount by showing the possibility of the agent's having secured other employment of a similar nature before the expiration of the term. Merrill vs. Blanchard, 7 A. D. 167, aff'd 158 N. Y. 682. Although the agent must use reasonable diligence to secure other employment in order to mitigate damages, he is not required to accept services of a different kind than those he was performing, nor to seek employment in far distant localities. Fuchs vs. Koerner, 107 N. Y. 529; Costigan vs. Railroad Co., 2 Denio (N. Y.) 609. 

Misconduct of a substantial nature on the part of the agent entitles the principal to revoke the appointment. If the contract provides for periodic payments of salary and does not contemplate entire performance as a condition precedent to receiving compensation, the agent may recover any unpaid arrears of salary earned in a period prior to the period in which the breach of contract occurred, subject to recoupment by his principal for damages due to the breach. Walsh vs. 

N. Y. & Ky. Co., 88 A. D. 485; Tipton vs. Feitner, 20 N. Y. 

423. But in no event may the agent recover compensation for the period in which the misconduct occurred. Seaburn vs. Zackmann, 99 A. D. 218. If an agency is terminated by the death or subsequent insanity of the principal, the agent is entitled to recover the reasonable value of the services he rendered up to the time his employment was terminated, but not for the damages he may be occasioned due to the termination of his agency. Wolfe vs. Howes, 20 N. Y. 197; Lacy

vs. Getman, 119 N. Y. 109. But if the agency is terminated by the bankruptcy of the principal, the agent may recover damages for the refusal of the principal to employ him after the bankruptcy. In re Silverman, 101 Fed. 219. See editorial

New York Law Journal, Nov. 18, 1926-also Grosner vs. 

Gropfer Mills, 213 N. Y. Supp. 433. 

Renunication by Agent. An agent hired for an indefinite term or employed under a contract which provides that either party may terminate it in the event of dissatisfaction, may abandon his employment and recover the stipulated compensation for the services he has actually rendered. Spring vs. 

Ansonia Clock Co., 24 Hun 175. If the conduct of the principal is such as to justify an agent in abandoning his work, the agent may recover the reasonable value of his services. 

Patterson vs. Gage, 23 Vt. 558; 56 Am. Dec. 96. 

Where an agent wrongfully abandons his employment, the question as to whether or not he is entitled to recover for services actually rendered depends upon whether the contract of employment is entire or severable. Clark vs. West, 137 A. 

D. 23 aff'd 201 N. Y. 569. If the contract is entire, and complete performance is a condition precedent to receiving compensation, by renouncing his commission before its completion, an agent forfeits all rights to remuneration for work done. Cahan vs. Platt, 69 N. Y. 348. But, especially in contracts for work, labor and services, where there is an agreement to pay from time to time during the term, a recovery may be had for previous installments actually earned, even though the agent abandons the service without cause before the expiration of the term. This amount may be reduced on the part of the principal by counterclaim for the damages he sustained by reason of the breach of contract. Mernagh vs. 

Nichols, 132 A. D. 509; Walsh vs. N. Y. & Ky. Co., 88 A. D. 

477. But if the agent be an infant, the principal's counterclaim would not lie. Whitmarsh vs. Hall, 3 Denio (N. Y.) 

375. Illness which incapacitates an agent for a brief time from rendering services, does not entitle the principal to consider the contract as abandoned. Gaynor vs. Jonas, 104 A. D.

35. But if the illness is of so serious a nature as to interfere with the interests of the principal, the latter may rescind the contract. If he does not do so, and subsequently takes the agent back, no deductions may be made for time lost. Bast vs. Byrne, 51 Wis. 531; Mott vs. Baxter, 13 Colo. App. 63;

Reiter vs. Standard Scale Co., 141 Ill. App. 427. 

Obligation to Reimburse. The principal is under a duty to reimburse his agent for all advances, expenses and disbursements made for the benefit of the principal and in the furtherance of the agency, provided such sums were properly incurred and paid in good faith and were not necessitated by any act of negligence on the part of the agent. Blazo vs. 

Gill, 143 N. Y. 232; Monnet vs. Metz, 127 N. Y. 151. See also Schaefer vs. Sherwood, 113 N. Y. Supp. 1068.

Obligation to Indemnify. If, in the furtherance of his commission, an agent is called upon by his principal to do an act which in itself is not apparently illegal and which the agent does not know to be wrong, the principal is under an obligation to indemnify the agent for such losses as the agent may be personally subjected to in a suit by a third person because of his performance of the act. Castle vs. Noyes, 14 

N. Y. 329; Fowler vs. N. Y. Gold Ex. Bank, 67 N. Y. 138; 

Howe vs. Railway Co., 37 N. Y. 297. This obligation to indemnify is not confined to tort liabilities, but embraces contractual liabilities expressly authorized, as well as those arising by implication. Thus in Dozier vs. Davison, 138 Ga. 190, where a principal induced a factor to sell in his own name certain bales of cotton which the principal agreed to furnish to him, but subsequently did not, it was held the factor was entitled to be indemnified for the losses he sustained by virtue of his breach of contract with the third party to whom he had agreed to sell the cotton. An agent is under no obligation to perform a palpably illegal act for his principal, and if he does so he is particeps criminis with his principal and is entitled neither to indemnity nor contribution. Coventry vs. 

Barton, 17 Johns. (N. Y.) 142.

Lien of Agent. “An agent may have a lien on the property or funds of his principal for moneys advanced or liabilities incurred in his behalf; and if moneys have been advanced or liabilities incurred upon the faith of the solvency of the principal, and he becomes insolvent while the proceeds and fruits of such advances or liabilities are in the possession of the agent, or within his reach, and before they have come to the actual possession of the principal, within every principle of equity, the agent has a lien upon the same for his protection and indemnity." Muller vs. Pondir, 55 N. Y. 325.

Liens are of two kinds, general and special. A general lien is a right to retain goods of another to secure a general balance due, while a special lien is a right to retain specific property for charges incurred with reference to the property in question. Evans on Agency, page 363. At common law, factors, bankers and attorneys at law have a general lien. 

Bowling Green Bank vs. Todd, 52 N. Y. 489. In all other instances, except where expressly provided by a statute or by contract, an agent has merely a special lien. See Muller vs. 

Pondir, 55 N. Y. 325; Nagle vs. McFeeters, 97 N. Y. 196. At common law, a lien attaches only to those goods of a principal which have come lawfully into the possession of his agent in his capacity as an agent. Thacker vs. Hannahs, 4 Robert (N. Y.) 407. The possession must also be continuous, and a voluntary surrender of the property terminates the lien, which is not revived even though the identical property subsequently comes into the hands of the agent. McFarland vs. Wheeler, 26 Wend. (N. Y.) 467.

Various liens exist in New York State by statute as for example, provision is made for the lien of an attorney for services rendered (Judiciary Law, section 475); for the lien of a factor for money advanced (Lien Law, section 182); for the lien of an artisan who alters, repairs, or in any way enhances the value of personal property (Lien Law, section 180); and for the lien of a garage owner for storage, etc. (Lien Law, section 184). It is interesting to note that the latter lien is

not dependent on continuous possession. Sections 200-210 of the Lien Law prescribe the manner of enforcing a lien on personal property. 

Stoppage in Transit. If an agent purchases goods with his own funds or by pledging his individual credit, and consigns them to his principal, he may stop them in transit if the principal becomes insolvent. The law considers the relationship of the parties under these circumstances analogous to that of an unpaid vendor and purchaser, rather than that of principal and agent. Hollins vs. Hubbard, 165 N. Y. 534; 

Moors vs. Kidder, 106 N. Y. 32; Farmers Bank vs. Logan, 74 N. Y. 568.
