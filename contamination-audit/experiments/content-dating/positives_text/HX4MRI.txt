To Any Reader: 

In March 1878, five months after his sixteenth birthday, my brother Homer went to Washington for a fortnight. He had never been out of New England before. He was the guest of Congressman W. W. Rice of Worcester, whose son Charles, a schoolmate, went with him.

I was a little girl then and can just remember that our parents were pleased with a Journal he kept during his visit. Until it slipped out of a bundle of family papers this summer, I had never read it; and, as far as I know, no one had looked at it for sixty years. His letters even as a little boy were very careful with few slips in grammar or spelling-but this was written in gay haste for reference only; and for the entertainment of a few old friends it is here reproduced as he wrote it. 

It may be well to remind readers that the Congressional Library was then housed in the Capitol, for the present Library of Congress was not finished until 

1897. Also, the Dean-Field case, now generally forgotten, was the result of an 1876 election in the 3rd 

Congressional District of Massachusetts when W. A. 

Field, R., afterward Chief Justice of the Massachusetts

Supreme Court, ran against Benjamin Dean, D. The newspapers of that time are full of it, "one of the most celebrated election cases that ever commanded the attention of Congress"; and the accounts are in accord with this Journal. "Florence" was a much loved older cousin, Florence 

Tryon, a beautiful woman, who had married Dr. Jedidiah Baxter, Surgeon-General of the United States Army. 

Another old cousin used to say, "Homer was born grown-up"; and although he reflects some current opinions which he did not hold later, many of his comments seem thoughtfully mature. Yet on the whole the Journal is so full of life and so frank that I hope some readers may find in it the tang of eternal youth. 

-Mabel Carleton Gage DECEMBER, 1938

WEDNESDAY MAR 20 LA RAKY 

We arrived in Washington shortly after one o clock this P. M. Mr. and Mrs Rice were at the depot to meet us and Sens Dawes and Hamlin with Mrs. H. were at the depot to meet their sons. Sen D I had seen before. 

Mr. H appeared to be a very old gentleman and not very handsome. Was introduced to them. Sen. H. speaks with a peculiar accent rather disagreeable. He is a remarkable old man and very cunning-We went home and took some lunch and went back to the capitol. After mounting the centre steps and looking forth up Penn. Av. at Smith. Inst. 

Wash. mon, new state building, Treasury &c, we went into the library of Cong. Here were thousands & thousands of books, and several men were engaged in reading about the tables. 

The need of more room is obvious to all. 

The rotunda next engaged our attention. 

It is very large and contains numerous large paintings. One of the most striking objects is the painting which is going on upon the walls. It is almost certain to be called basrelief, it looks so natural. We saw then the old representative's hall now filled with

statuary. Saw the statue of Gov King of Me. which provoked such an ill-timed attack by the "stalwart" Blaine. Then we turned to the gallery of the House. Mr. Rice came up to see us, and told us the names of most of the prominent men who were then present. Gov. 

Hendee of Vt. was speaking on the bill establishing a gov. for the D. of C. Mr. 

Blackburn of Ky. spoke also. Gov. H. is fine looking old man with a very strong and clear voice. B. is a fair speaker but a coarse looking man which corresponds very well with his character as I consider it a fire eater of the South I saw F. Wood sitting in an upright position (hands in his arm-pits) pay quite good attention-C. H. Potter appeared to me a very fine looking gentleman, very particular (too much so in fact) in regard to dress. A. S. 

Hewitt, M. I. Townsend, Gov. Claflin, W. A.

Field, Eugene Hale, (who was speaker protem) Speaker Randall, & others were all present and pointed out to me. On our way home we met Sec. Schurz. Mr. Finley (dem) of Ohio is staying at our boarding place. He is quite agreeable but not scholarly-Mr. 

Allen (correspondent of Boston Adv.) is also here and has kindly offered to go with us to

the senate tomorrow. He informed us that 

Blaine would probably attack Hoar in some way & that Stanley Mathews was to speak so there will be an exciting debate probably. 

In eve. we went to an auction sale of Chinese decorated ware & then to bed 

THURSDAY MAR 22 [21] 

Our steps were then After breakfast we read the papers and prepared to go to Mrs. Hoar's. We found her just going out but she returned with us to her rooms where was a great deal of Chinese ware purchased at the auction, some very pretty. 

Sen. Hoar had just gone out. Chas and I then started for Dr. Baxters but we met 

Florence on the way. directed to the capitol- We went up in the dome-I didn't go to very top-and looked out upon the city. Then to a lunch in the house resteraunt- The senate was reported to [be] the scene of a contest-so thither we went for the afternoon-When we went in Teller of Col. was making a speech on the railroad bill. He had it all written out and read it very fast. As I was not "up" on the subject I could not form a good opinion of the matter he dealt with. The "Timber Bill" was then brought up by unanimous consent and Jones

of Fla. made a lengthy speech, attacking Sec. 

Schurz and defending his constituents from the charge of being "timber thieves." He is

Irish and speaks with a sort of brogue. He made a tolerably good speech but often repeats himself and seemed full as well pleased with his effort as anybody else did. Mr. Morgan of Ala. followed in the same strain. He is a fine looking man and good speaker but did not make a very strong argument. Then followed Stanley Mathews. He rallied to the defense of the Sec. and made by far the best speech of the day. He refuted the arguments of his opponents and made a successful defense of Schurz' action. A fine delivery, good clear strong voice combine with his straightforward and candid statement of his case to make a most interesting speaker. He was interrupted by Blaine once or twice but on nothing important. B. sat in his seat paying little or no attention but told Mr. Rice that he should speak tomorrow. Teller also interrupted M. He appears to be nothing more or less than a tool of Blaine, Conkling was in the chamber but a few minutes. Mr. Hoar also gave evidence of an intention of speaking. 

In the evening the whole family including a Miss Norcross (daughter Rep. Norcross of 10

Mass) went to hear Lotta. She is very bright and dashy and her character showed her to good advantage. It was very interesting and the scenery was particularly good. 

Thence to bed

FRIDAY MAR. 23 [22] 

This morning C. and I remained in doors till about ½ past ten-then we walked round the public buildings. We saw the White 

House, New State Dept. building and others which we did not know. We were then led to the capitol by the desire to hear Blaine, but were dissappointed as he said very little. We saw the House called to order but the confusion was very great indeed. In the Senate, as we entered, Mr. Macdonald of Ind was speaking on the railroad bill. He is not a very eloquent speaker but his remarks were very good as regards their subject matter. Sen

Hoar sent a page into the gallery to ask us to come down on the floor and we did. The "Timber Bill" was then taken up. Mr. Beck of Ky. defended his amendment and Morgan replied. It was then slightly changed and passed. Quite a discussion then rose on an amendment offered by Mr. Clumb of Kan. which was finally passed. 11

It is reported in the papers and elsewhere that Monday is to be the beginning of a deliberate attack on the Southern & Civil Service policy of Hayes. It seems to me that it will be a very foolish move and sure to result in the discomfiture of those who try it. The masses believe in Pres. and it will take more than the united efforts of such demagogues to shake their faith. After the House adjourned we met Mr R. and Mr Norcross who said he knew Dr Gage very well indeed and was very glad to see me. He is a very pleasant & social man with a great deal of ability. 

We four were to wait until the ladies came to go with us to the Hospital. In the mean time we went round a good deal in the capitol. Saw the engine room and boilers, the pillars designed by Jefferson with ears of corn on top, and the entrance to the crypt. Up stairs we went into the marble room and pres. room, two of the finest furnished rooms I was ever in. 

Then we met Mrs Rice, Miss Norcross and all (six) got into our carriage. We had a most elegant dinner out there and saw quite a fair entertainment— We were quite tired on our return and went right to bed 12

SATURDAY MAR 23 

Made plans to go to Arlington but defferred them till afternoon for the convenience of 

Mr Allen who was going to accompany us.

Charlie and I went to an antiquarian bookstore on F. st. where Charlie bought some books on Mathematics. I did not make any purchases then but probably shall next week.

We then went to a billiard hall opposite the Ebbett House quite a high-toned place and played one game, became disgusted (C. did.) and tried again at the Riggs House without better success. Charlie went home and I made a call on Florence. Then to lunch and we started for Arlington. After leaving the horsecars we were obliged to take a long walk across a toll-bridge (first I ever saw) over the Potomac, and over a most desolate looking country. I saw one thing which was very 

Southern-like; two old stores, (grocery-stores) one of which was closed and had a piaza around it, on which were seated 40 or 50 colored & white loafers. These were arranged just like the pictures of southern life. We entered the cemetery through first gate into the small bury-ground. Here were quantities of little slabs, some wood and some stone 13

indicating their names &c. One thing which we could not understand was the graves of so many ladies. It is a thing I must find out about. There very many bodies of colored troops buried where we entered. After a walk of about ½ a mile we came to the Lee mansion. It is a fair sized old house with lowstudded rooms but quite large ones. The most curious thing we saw were some attempts at painting over the back entry-hunting dogs indicating the revelries witnessed by that house years ago. There were more wasps in the attic than I ever saw before and we narrowly escaped being stung. There was a rostrum where I suppose speaking sometimes takes place. It was surrounded by an arbor supported by posts up each of which was twined a different vine nicely labeled. sauntered through the yard & grounds in the vicinity of the house and then went out by a different gate. We saw a negro sowing grass seed on the graves, & C. very properly remarked that it was a curious sight to see him caring for the graves of those that died for his liberty. We walked from there to Oak Hill one of the finest cemeteries I ever saw for natural beauty. There were some very fine monuments there too. We 14

SUNDAY MAR 24. 

We got ready to go to church when a violent shower sprang and prevented us. So 

I finished my letter to Papa and wrote a long one to Aunt Frances. Then I read the report of the R. R. committee of the [ ] on the Northern Pacific's request, so as to understand the subject. It seems to me that it should be granted. The papers and a little of the "Athenian Letters" occupied my attention until dinner.

I was occupied in various matters until five oclock when we (C. & I) went to walk with 

Mr. Rice. We went down to the old Burns mansion. From the occupant of which Washington bought the site of this beautiful city. 

It is said W. cheated poor Burns in this way. 

B. refused to take less than a certain price and 

W. thought it too much so he gave the old man an agreement which he signed. It gave him his own price but contained a provision saying that all the land taken for streets should be given free. Thus W. rather got an underhanded advantage. The old house in which he lived is still standing, a low, miserable shanty. 

Continuing our walk we passed by the houses. of many eminent men, viz. Sumner's mansion, Blaine's, Wood's, Evarts', Seward's where 15

his life was attempted and Stephen Decatur. 

About which there is quite a story. We saw the statute of Gen Rawlins. I learnt too the reason why the streets of W. were laid out in this manner. They were designed by a 

Frenchman during the French Rev. and he sought to avoid the possibility of a mob's obtaining possession by opening the streets to be swept by cannon. In the eve. we made a call on Mr. & Mrs Hoar and made arrangements with them to go to the State & War depts today [tomorrow] at ten oclock. 

MONDAY MAR 25 

In accordance with agreement Mr. and 

Mrs Hoar came round at ten oclock and Chas Mrs R & I joined them. We proceeded immediately to the State Dept. Mr. Evarts was not in his office then but a very gentlemanly and intelligent clerk showed us round. We saw first some Chinese and Japanese treaties they were gorgeous. Also trunks belonging to

Jefferson & Monroe & containing their private papers. Another room contained a treaty with the French Republic signed by Bonaparte then Consul, an English treaty signed by George IV, and an Austrian. Each of these had the great seal of the country 16

attached. We saw, too, the original draft of the Declaration of Independence with the signatures and also copy of it in the handwriting of Jefferson with corrections and additions by himself John Adams and Ben Franklin. After this we went into the diplomatic room, a perfectly gorgeous place and from there to see Sec. Evarts. C, Mr H & I then went in to see Gen. Sherman and had an interesting chat for a few minutes. Then to see Gen Devens and our party separated.

In Gen. D's room is a very interesting collection of paintings containing the portraits of every Attorney-Genl. Chas & I then went to the capitol and heard Judge Howe relieve his mind of a great burden evidently and if it pleased him, it didn't trouble anybody else.

It contained nothing new or important. He buried the Rep. party with a good deal of feeling, on his part & disgust on the part of his hearers. It was a weak speech. We staid at home during the evening and had a short call from Sen. Dawes and a long one from 

Miss Norcross.

TUESDAY 

In the morning we went to the Washington market. It was the largest place of the kind 17

I ever saw. It was very interesting. From there we went to the Smithsonian Institute.

We did not go into the library but all through the central building where the animals, birds, eggs, &c pertain to Natural History were and into a back room where there was a quantity of old China, Dutch tiles and the like. This was of course very interesting to a naturalist but-, Charles and I-, it was not. In the Agricultural Building where we went next were a quantity of very poorly stuffed hens & doves and other animals about which [I] could not judge so well. We didn't stay long. I came home and made a call on Dr. Baxter, then got some lunch and went to the capitol. Sen

Merriman was speaking in the senate on the railroad bill in a voice like Ben Abbotts. I heard that the Dean-Field case had been brought up so I went over to the house in time to hear two speeches for Dean on the dem. side and a most excellent speech by 

B. W. Harris for Field. It was delivered in a clear distinct voice and was an excellent argument but I did not think he dwelt on the fact of the 25 votes enough A Mr. Walsh of Md. (dem) spoke then for Field and though delivered in a very boisterous manner it contained a great deal of good common sense. 18

In the evening we went with Miss Norcross to an art lecture which was miserable, thence home & to bed.

WEDNESDAY MAR. 27 

I staid at home until ½ past eleven and wrote a postal to Hovey and a letter to 

Prescott. Then I went to the capitol to hear the debate on the Dean-Field case. After hearing part of an uninteresting debate on a new quarantine law, Chas who joined me at capitol, & Mr. R & I went to the resteraunt and got some lunch. When we returned to the 

House this uninteresting debate was still going on. "Sunset" Cox and Mr. Potter both made brief remarks. Then the election case came up. Mr. Cox of Ohio opened the debate with a very good earnest speech for Mr Field. He was followed by Gen Banks with a strong argument and then the indomitable rascal Ben Butler poured forth an earnest appeal to sustain the supervisors law which he was instrumental in passing. He made a very important point as he thought in the fact that 40 votes were thrown out of Dean's which had no congressional district marked on them and counted 25 for Field that had the wrong cong. district. He closed with an 19

earnest appeal for the ways of our fathers when "each man votes just once & no more and his vote is counted just once & no more. 

It was a weak argument throughout. What a contrast to the one that followed? Mr. Fryes speech was a masterly one. Refuting every argument, he clearly showed the fallacy of 

Dean's claim. Butler's grand argument was demolished first by an affadavit of Alderman Stebbins contradicting it & second by showing that he had read from the report in regard to an entirely different count, that of state senator. He was very eloquent and spoke in a strong & clear voice. Mr. Harris of Va. closed the debate. He has such a thick voice that he could be understood with difficulty, but he ridiculed & held up to scorn such democrats as were going to vote for Field and lashed the party whip. There was great excitement during the progress of the vote as it was seen how close it would be & when it was announced 120 yeas & 119 nays and the speaker cast his vote in the negative the excitement was intense. Mr. Conger of Mich. fearing that some had gone home moved to adjourn & carried it. Freeman of Pa. was telegraphed for and the dem are making desperate efforts to break their collegues who are honest. 20

In the eve. we went with Sen. Dawes son to the Minstrels-fair.

THURSDAY MAR 28 

In the morning I went over to cousin

Florence's to see if the rain would make any difference with our going with her, as we expected, to the Medical Museum. She said it would not and I agreed to be at her house at 

2 P.M with Chas. I then went to the House and met Chas. The final struggle was to come over the Mass. contested election case. All the Rep. were present or paired but a few 

Dem were absent so, to give them time to arrive, Mr. Springer (dem) of Ill. moved that the house "do now adjourn" and demanded the ayes & nays on that motion which was ordered. This consumed about an hour and then the final vote commenced. It was evident that it was very close and when the clerk finished the first call no one knew how it stood. After all pairs had been announced the speaker asked that his name be called and he voted aye. Just then Mr. Oliver of Ia. asked if he was recorded and being answered in the negative voted nay. This was a parliamentary trick. It was decided 124 to 123 that Mr. Dean was the lawful Represen. C & I then went 21

and met Florence who informed us that she had received an invitation from the Navy Yard to be present at a casting there. We gladly went and [saw] the largest propeller cast that was ever cast in the U.S. It weighed 9 tons. 

It was an elegant and almost startling sight to see the molten metal poured into the mould. 

After that was done we saw a ship being over hauled. It was most time to go then, and we returned Florence's house and staid to tea with a Dr. Highland. In the eve. after Dr. 

Baxter had shown us some tricks we played 

Go Bang and Sancho.

FRIDAY MAR 29. 

In the morning Mr. Rice took Chas and I over to the Treasury building and introduced us to somebody in "Bureau of Engraving and Printing" who gave us a guide. We went all over it and saw them making national currency and legal tenders. I was surprised [by] the number of different processes it had to go through before being complete. Almost every little additional piece of workmanship had to be done by a different machine. I saw them make a plate for a cigar stamp. No money was finished until it had been numbered and the bank notes had to be sent to the banks to 22

have the signatures of the Pres & Cashier.

We went into several large vaults containing millions of dollars. We saw the mutilated currency being counted and made ready for burning. The furnace was not open that day.

In the afternoon we went to the Corcoran Art gallery and saw the beautiful collection of paintings and statuary. The most beautiful of the former in my estimation were two landscapes, one a brook in the Catskills, the other a view on lake George. I then went on an autograph hunt but failed to get any valuable ones. In an old bookstore I found about 200 but none of them but were valued much too high. In the evening Chas and I went to inform Hamlin that we should go to Mt

Vernon Sat. and we staid at the Hamilton 

House all the eve. playing billiards.

SATURDAY MAR. 30 

We started today for Mt. Vernon. Chas.,

Hamlin and I went together. We had a very pleasant sail down the river touching at 

Annapolis, Fort Foote and Fort Washington

There were on board about 30 or 40 negroes going down the river to catch fish. They sang a good deal and made some money by it. I often thought of what a wretched race they 23

are, simply because of their ignorance and how much they might attain unto were they only educated. I think the care & education practical & moral is one of the questions which is going to engage the statesmen of the future. 

It is surely a worthy cause. 

Another very different colored man was on board. Fredk 

Douglas, a fair representative of what all of his race may become through the acquisition of knowledge. As we drew near we could see what a beautiful spot it was which Washington selected for his residence. We went by the new & old tombs to the house. The latter is a beautiful old mansion situated on the crest of a hill which slopes abruptly to the river. It commands a beautiful prospect and if a little more accessible would make an excellent summer residence. There are very many curious and interesting relics on the lower floor; among the most notable of which was the key of the old Bastile presented by 

Lafayette to Washington and by him hung in its case and never yet removed. Following the guide up stairs we looked into the different rooms; each in charge of some state and containing some of them the original furniture. 

Went into the rooms and saw the beds on which Washington & his wife died. After a 24

cool but pleasant ride, we arrived home much pleased with our journey. In the eve played 

Go Bang on Florence's board.

SUNDAY MAR 31 

Today, in marked contrast with our usual custom, we accomplished little or nothing. 

Chas. Mrs. Rice and I went to church this morning. The church All Souls, is very finely finished in Gothic style. After hearing a very good sermon, we met Cousin Florence and went to Mr. H. E. Willards to see his autographs. They were so numerous that I could not see half of them but he kindly invited me to come tomorrow and look at them. The afternoon and evening passed very quietly with the exception of a call from Mr. & Miss Norcross.

MONDAY APR. 1 

In the morning we paid a visit to Haydens museum. This is a collection of engravings, models of Indian villages, old crockery and so forth, which Mr. Hayden gathered in his geographical surveys in the west. These were very nice but what I was most interested [in] was a sketch-book by one of the young men in the office, some sketches taken while on his tour, 25

others about Washington. They were quite remarkable. After lunch I went over to Mr. 

Willards again and had a long talk with him about autographs during which he gave me a signature of Sam Adams and promised that if I would make out a list of what I wanted he would send them to me if he had them. This eve. I went to a sociable at All Souls church where I was introduced to Ex-Sen. Boutwell. 

He is not a very intellectual looking man and looks much older than I should think he could be.

TUESDAY APR. 2 

Today was our last in Washington and one of the pleasantest and most interesting trips I ever expect to take was brought to its close. 

In the morning I went with Mr. Rice to the White House and saw the East room and other smaller though no less beautiful rooms. 

I was also introduced to the Pres who has quite a pleasant countenance and is a very fair looking man. He was signing some papers while I was there. 26
