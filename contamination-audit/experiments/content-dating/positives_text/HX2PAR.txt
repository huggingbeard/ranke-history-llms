PREAMBLE. 

WHEREAS, the Industrial classes are the creators of all wealth, which has been heretofore wrested from its rightful owners by the speculative few, and then employed as a means to keep the creator in subservience to the creature, by the unholy means of peculative monopoly; whereby the usurpers of the soil secure to themselves great wealth by the aggregation of numbers, neces. sarily locating upon the land forestalled by capital for the purpose of speculation, adding great value thereto by said concentration, and thus the laborer becoming the pliant machine, whereby the designing few work their way to wealth and agrandisement at the expense of the toil and just rights of the many, making it necessary for the homeless to enter into combinations, to meet the combinations of others, to obtain a restitution of their common rights; and as there is strength in union, as well as power in knowledge, we trust we have the knowledge to unite for the purpose of securing those rights. Under these views it is designed for one thousand individuals to unite together for the purpose of securing soil not forestalled by capital, (for the purpose of speculation) sufficient for houses for said thousand-that by the concentration of numbers we may secure to ourselves the increased value of property that such concentrations will give, instead of casting from us the fruits of our toil and influence, as we must do under the present system of things, if we combine not to protect ourselves from the unjust power and influence of capital. 

Under these considerations, We, each and all of us, who subscribe to the Constitution of this Association, solemnly declare that we will do all in our power to carry its laws into effect.

The Library has long felt the need of a brief history of early Mount Vernon which would clarify some of the prevailing misapprehensions concerning the early settlement and add something to our knowledge of its development.

We are fortunate that Mr. Hufeland, a resident of 

Mount Vernon since early boyhood, was persuaded to write this account. A careful student of local history for more than a half-century, author of several works relating to the history of Westchester County, and discriminating collector of items bearing on its history, he offers here authoritative material in the concise form desired. 

We are particularly favored in the illustrations which were selected from Mr. Hufeland's collection, many of them photographs from his own camera. 

Some of these are familiar but several of them have not appeared elsewhere. Most of the buildings have long since disappeared.

Mr. Hufeland presented his manuscript to the Library and a small gift fund at the disposal of the Trustees makes its publication possible. 

Alice L. Jewett, Librarian,

PREFACE 

This is not a complete history of "Early Mount Vernon" but simply an outline written for people who want to know the main facts about the City's history. 

It was written to comply with a request from the Mount Vernon Public Library to supply these "main facts," chiefly for school children who could elaborate on them when they were asked to make an historical study. But it will do for older people as well. If it is required there is much more to write and this little book may serve as a guide for future searchers. 

A complete history of their home town is certainly desirable and if this study leads some one to prepare one, the writer will be gratified that his work has not been altogether in vain.

M OUNT VERNON has little ancient history and what little we are supposed to have is based on a misapprehension by the general public. 

It is generally believed that the settlement is a good place to start and this has led to the trouble. The township, of which Mount Vernon formerly was a part, was settled by a number of families that came from Fairfield in the State of Connecticut, on a piece of land that lies in the Bronx, somewhere near the existing 

Baychester Station of the New York, Westchester & 

Boston railroad.

Perhaps it is well to go outside of our history to explain this and dissipate some of the misunderstanding. 

The place where Eastchester town was first settled is on a small piece of land between Rattlesnake and Black Dog brooks facing the waters of Eastchester Bay and about half a mile south of the present Mount Vernon boundary. In the old titles it was described as the place "where the house stood." This "house" was the home of Anne Hutchinson who was driven from Rhode Island by the Puritans, and is the place where she and her family were massacred by the Indians. 

Reid's Mill and home were situated on a piece of this land through which the "Westchester Path" ran from

Westchester to the Boston Road. A piece of the lower end of this "Path" was still in existence a few years ago, but it has now disappeared with the mill and the home. The other end of the "Path" can still be followed as far as Rattlesnake Brook from the Post Road. With the exception of the Post Road it was the oldest road in Westchester County.

Unfortunately the original has not been preserved but the copy made by Richard Shute, the "Recorder" in 1668, is in the old town records. About twenty signatures, all before 1700, were added subsequently. 

Up to 1668 the settlement was attached to Westchester, but in that year Governor Lovelace decided that they were strong enough to go it alone, pay their own taxes, and mend their own roads. We have a "Land List of 1682" which shows 484 acres in possession of twenty individuals.

All of this was not in the territory covered by the

present Mount Vernon and perhaps should not have been included here, except to clear up the misapprehension previously noted.

In the course of a few years, due to additional grants and the settlement of lawsuits, the town was extended until it included the whole Town of Eastchester as we knew it and this is the place where the history of 

Mount Vernon should properly begin.

The Post Road to Boston, it will be understood, ran from "Kings Bridge" over the Harlem River to and through the County to Boston and was the only road at that time, and it was on that road that the Eastchester people located their little church just above where the Westchester Path came into it. Originally the Eastchester people had worshipped with those from Westchester, but in 1693 they passed the following resolution: "At a Meeting of the Inhabittants of Eastchester, held the 9th of May 1693, they have agreed by voat to Build a Metting hous according to the dimenshuns agreed upon." 

This "Metting hous" was one of the first houses in the present City of Mount Vernon and its "dimenshuns" were supposed to be "twenty-eight feet square and about eighteen feet to the eaves; the sides as well as the roof being shingled which latter met together in an apex." Around this small church were gathered the few houses that afterwards constituted the Village of Eastchester and this is the beginning of our history. 

The new stone church which followed this was erected on the "Green" opposite the old wooden church so that the roadway was between them. This we have from old Dr. Coffey, who told us that Philemon Fowler stepped into a hole where the rain had washed away the earth and exposed the foundation of the old church quite clearly. In fact, the old church was still standing during the Revolution because sol-

diers gathered some of the wood to make the new building more comfortable for the wounded who were put into the new building in 1776. This old building was probably east or northeast of the present stone church. Although the present building was begun in 

1764, it had no flooring in it during the Revolution and was not finished until 1787.

Around these two buildings were laid the remains of the fathers, mothers and children of the settlers and 

St. Paul's Church residents of the vicinity. They should not be disturbed. 

From the old field stone markers to the more elaborate monuments and vaults, they form the only connection this age has with the days of long ago. An old field stone, all that the time could afford, with the inscription "R. S. d. Dec. 14 1704" is still there and is the oldest inscription we can decipher. It is over the grave of Richard Shute who died December 1704, the old "Recorder" to whose loving care we are indebted for the record of all we know of the old settlers. It should be carefully protected.

The "Church Green" was the gathering place for the 'people of the vicinity. In 1733 a celebrated election took place there. A large number of people gathered there to elect a member of the Assembly. The decision was against the wishes of the governor and was at once overruled by him. This tyrannical proceeding was upset by a jury of the people in court. Any one desirous of reading the whole of this interesting trial may find it at length in Bolton's History of Westchester County, as it is too long to quote here. 

There was quite a little fighting during the Revolution on the ground covered by Mount Vernon now. 

On October 17th, 1776 Colonel Glover, who had been sent by General Nixon from Mile Square to watch the British and prevent them from cutting off Washington in his retreat from New York to White Plains, camped in a bend of the Boston Post Road where it crosses the Hutchinson River at Wolf's Lane, where the public playground now is. In the morning after his arrival he climbed the hill at the foot of the present McClellan Avenue and looking down the valley of the river, he saw a great number of vessels landing, down where the present Pelham Bridge crosses Eastchester Bay. He at once went back and ordered his men to march through Wolf's Lane and the Split Rock Road and post themselves behind the stone fences on the high side of the road and he himself marched with a small body of men to an eminence beyond and there he awaited the coming of the enemy. As soon as they came within range, his men fired and then retreated along the Split Rock Road to the next regiment posted behind the stone walls, who gave the British a similar reception and so down the whole line until they found the British occupying higher ground than the 

Americans, when the latter withdrew to their camp across the Hutchinson River, but not before they had

taken up the planks of the bridge which crossed that stream. This skirmish of about 750 men who opposed the British army of 4000 was completely successful and permitted Washington to retreat with his small army unmolested to White Plains. The flagstaff on the hill and the tablet just inside the playground entrance mark this important engagement. The American loss. was eight men killed, thirteen wounded, while the 

British loss was estimated at 140 to 150 killed and wounded. The British wounded were carried to the 

Eastchester church and the Americans withdrew towards Dobbs Ferry.

The bell in the church, which had been presented to it by the Rev. Thomas Standard, was taken down either before or after the battle and was buried together with the Bible and prayerbook. The general belief is that this was done to protect it from the British but the following resolution of the Provincial Congress shows that this probably was not the case. 

Fishkill Oct. 5, 1776. Resolved unanimously. That his

Excellency Gen. Washington be requested and authorized to cause all the Bells in the different churches and public edifices in the City of New York to be taken down . . . that the fortunes of war may throw the same out of the hands of the enemy ...

Besides, this was an Episcopal church none too friendly to the American cause. Another good reason was that it was buried in the Vincent place and the Vincents were thought to be tories. The Vincent House which stood just below the Mount Vernon boundary on the Post Road, was later purchased by Colonel Wm. 

S. Smith who was a son-in-law of President John 

Adams. During the prevalence of the yellow fever in 

Philadelphia he visited there and made it the "Nation's Capitol" for a few days.

In connection with the Vincent House it is necessary to relate an incident that occurred there in No-

vember 1779 which has caused some discussion. A body of American mounted troops under Colonel Armond passed through there on a secret expedition. It was found necessary to shoe a horse which Vincent, the local blacksmith, refused to do because it was Sunday and he was out of coal. The excuse was a poor one for in those days when horses could not be delayed on religious scruples or for any other cause, it was necessary to shoe a horse on Sunday as well as on week days. A dispute arose during which Vincent was shot. Vincent was a member of a suspected tory family and this brought the fight to a bitterness which resulted in his death. The original Vincent house was a small one, about 25 by 18 feet with a lean-to twelve feet deep, where he probably shod the horses, and one and one-half stories high, making the whole about 25 by 30 feet. Colonel Smith, when he purchased the property, built the portion that was two and one-half stories, that we knew as the Halsey house.

But to go back to the battle which was fought by Colonel Glover. A few days after that battle a small body of Americans stole across the Bronx and "started to carry off three tubs of shirts" from a house where washing was being done by the British. On being discovered they dropped the shirts and ran, but meeting a party that had been sent after them, they attacked the Hessian outpost, killing ten and taking two prisoners. This happened at the tavern of Robert 

Morell near where the Judge Mills house, now the Free 

Synagogue of Westchester, stands. 

On August 22nd, 1777 there was quite a skirmish at

Eastchester Church. General Putnam, hearing that the 

British guard at the hospital was small, sent General Varnum there to capture some hospital supplies, of which the Americans were so much in need. The ex-

pedition was successful in driving off the guard, and while they were busy gathering the supplies, Varnum sent a detachment out to investigate the neighborhood. 

While they were absent the British returned in force and drove off the invaders, killing a captain and a number of privates.

About the middle of July a body of Americans scouting along the Bronx at Mount Vernon were discovered by a larger body of British under Simcoe, and the latter tried to lure them into an ambuscade by sending a few cavalry down the hill opposite and placing an infantry column on both sides of the road that led to Hunt's Bridge hoping that the Americans would come down after the cavalry. But the officers of the ambuscade were seen by the Americans, who turned their guns on them and caused them to beat a hasty retreat.

A map prepared by Colonel Rufus Putnam, chief engineer of the American army, later in the year shows a heavy guard on a line extending from Valentine's Hill to Eastchester over the intervening Mount Vernon. 

There were Hessian grenadiers at Valentine's Hill, British infantry regiments on both sides of Bronx River, and Grenadier, Light Guards, and Light Dragoons be tween them on the road to Eastchester. 

As the war neared its end, the success of the raids on the British outposts became so common that the raiders became careless and even boastful. It was on one of these raids that the Westchester guide, Brom 

Dyckman, lost his life. On March 4th, 1782 Captain 

Honeywell, with a body of volunteers backed by a battalion of infantry under Major Woodbridge, made a raid on Delancey's camp at Morrisania. The latter were posted to cover the retreat while Honeywell, passing close to Fort Number 8, at daylight galloped into the British camp. Taken by surprise, the enemy fired

a few shots and ran, while Honeywell started back along the White Plains Road with twenty prisoners and as many horses. But the firing had aroused the garrisons of the fort and they followed the raiders as far as the present Scott's Bridge where Woodbridge's infantry were lying awaiting them. After firing a few shots the British retired to rest their tired infantry. While they were in this position Brom Dyckman and his cousin rode out from the American rear guard waving their swords as a challenge. A British rifleman who had crept up behind a stone fence fired a shot at long range which unfortunately terminated Brom's career. 

He was led from the field by his cousin and died a few days later. A monument to his memory was erected by the State at Crompond Church where he lies buried together with Colonel Greene and Major

Flagg who were killed later at Yorktown in the upper part of the County.

Mount Vernon was part of the "Neutral Ground" where there were almost daily fights between the cowboys and the skinners, both vagabonds and robbers, who robbed either side when they were able to beat the farmers.

In connection with the Revolution, perhaps Aaron Burr should be mentioned here. Court was held for a while in the Eastchester Church and Burr often had occasion to visit it on business. There was, however, another reason that brought him there. His wife came frequently to visit Frederick Prevost, her son by her first husband, who lived in the first house on the right hand side of the New Boston Post Road after you crossed the bridge (Lockwood's) into the town of Pelham.

The War of the Revolution was over and the country of which the territory now covered by the City of Mount Vernon was a part, resumed its peaceful aspect. 

Nothing worth recording happened over the quiet hills and plains except perhaps that the church was being finished after thirty years of delay, and was being used by the congregation. The taverns were the same. 

There was Billy Crawford's still standing opposite the church, Fowler's on the corner of the present Third

Avenue, Guion's on the Old Post Road, Morell's on the White Plains Road near the former home of Judge Mills, and Shute's opposite the point where the White 

Plains Road comes in.

On a map of the Town of Eastchester made in 1798, at the direction of the Supervisor, the following houses. appear: Israel Hunt's, Benjamin Morgan's, Drake's, Pinkney's, Aaron Ward's, Widow Fowler's, Pell's, Stephen Ward's, Valentine's, Shute's, Morell's, and a few others scattered over the large area. Milestones, giving the distance from the City Hall in Wall Street, New York, were placed on most of the old streets. 

Most of these have unfortunately disappeared. One that has survived may be found on the old White 

Plains Post Road, now Lincoln Avenue, at Claremont Avenue.

A Freemason's Lodge was established here in 1796 12

and held its meetings, first in Crawford's and then in 

Philemon Fowler's tavern, where a special lodge room was built for it. At a meeting of the lodge it was decided that "A Lodge be held every Monday night when the Moon shall be full." The members were residents of the nearby villages of the County and most of them came to the lodge meetings on horseback. It moved its meeting place to New Rochelle in 1814.

It was about 1840 that the unrest among the middle and working classes was growing and made them uneasy. They felt that while they were doing the work they were not sufficiently considered in the benefits. 

In Europe it was the cause of revolutions in France and 

Germany in 1848 and 1850. In New York and vicinity the theories of Fourier, Owens and others were strongly advocated by Horace Greeley, Brisbane and Godwin and organizations were formed to carry them out practically. A "Phalanstery" on purely socialistic principles was built in New Jersey and seemed to work out successfully. Greeley devoted one column every week of the "Semi Weekly Tribune," the most influential paper among the farmers of the country, to the support of these views and theories. 

This resulted in the gathering of members of the working class into associations to discuss how to escape the landlord and the payment of rent. By the purchase of suitable land by combined effort they could accumulate sufficient funds to obtain a "restitution of their common right" and become the owners of land themselves. In the years of foreign revolutions 

Morrisania and the surrounding villages were established and in the year 1850 the beginning was made that eventually resulted in the City of Mount Vernon. 

One result of this agitation was that John Stevens, a merchant tailor of New York, convinced that the arguments and theories were good, enthusiastically en- 13

deavored to improve the condition of the working class in New York. He believed that the first step was to create a property holding class out of the rent payers. 

His efforts to accomplish this was an undertaking that interfered with his business and his peace of mind, so that his family begged him to give it up. But he persisted and finally organized the "Industrial Home 

Association No. 1 of the City of New York." This was the organization to which we owe the purchase of the site of Mount Vernon. He became its president and they adopted the following:

PREAMBLE 

Whereas, the Industrial classes are the creators of all wealth, which has been heretofore wrested from its rightful owners by the speculative few, and then employed as a means to keep the creator in subservience to the creature, by the unholy means of speculative monopoly; whereby the usurpers of the soil secure to themselves great wealth by the aggregation of numbers, necessarily locating upon the land forestalled by capital for the purpose of speculation, adding great value thereto by said concentration, and thus the laborer becoming the pliant machine, whereby the designing few work their way to wealth and agrandisement at the expense of the toil and just rights of the many, making it necessary for the homeless to enter into combinations, to meet the combinations of others, to obtain a restitution of their common rights; and as there is strength in union, as well as power in knowledge, we trust we have the knowledge to unite for the purpose of securing those rights. Under these views it is designed for one thousand individuals to unite together for the purpose of securing soil not forestalled by capital, (for the purpose of speculation) sufficient for houses for said thousand-that by the concentration of numbers we may secure to ourselves the increased value of property that such concentrations will give, instead of casting from us the fruits of our toil and influence, as we must do under the present system of things, if we combine not to protect ourselves from the unjust power and influence of capital. 

Under these considerations, We, each and all of us who subscribe to the Constitution of this Association, solemnly 14

declare that we will do all in our power to carry its laws into effect.

CONSTITUTION Article II Qualification of Members 

Sec. 1-Any person of good moral character and industrious habits, who is in favor of our principles and objects may become a member of this Association by faithfully fulfilling the requirements of the Constitution and Laws.

Under the "Preamble" and Constitution one thousand and seventeen members paid their dues and the Association bought the land which afterward became the Village of Mount Vernon. It consisted of the farms of John M., Andrew Oscar, Andrew, and Sylvanus 

Purdy and of John R. Hayward, altogether five farms with a combined area of 369.089 acres, stretching substantially from the New York & New Haven Railroad to the Kingsbridge Road, at a total cost of $75,342.88 or about $205 per acre.

But Industrial Home Association No. 1 was not the only Home Association of the time. The dissatisfaction had spread far and wide for a "No. 2 Association" was formed about the same time for the Village of 

Westchester and a No. 3, at another place, all of them on a more or less cooperative basis. All sought relief from the oppressive rents and the grasping landlords. 

The laborer at that time had little protection and a small chance of bettering his condition, small as his demands were. He lacked the force of the organization of today and sought to improve it by combining in associations.

But our Association did not always think alike on some questions. An illustration of this is the naming 15

of our town when that question came up. Thousandville, Rising Sun, Fleetwood, Palestine, Columbia, Mon ticello, Stevensville, New Washington, New Amster dam, Olive Branch, Little New York, Jefferson, Washington, Linden, and Homeville, were some of the names suggested. On a vote the count stood Monticello 54, New Washington 24, Stevensville 12 and the rest scattering. With the name of Monticello having most votes the matter was supposed to be settled, but the Post Office authorities informed them that a Monticello was already in New York. New names were then voted for with the following result: Mount Vernon 

84, Fleetwood 54, and Fairchester 14, whereupon the name of Mount Vernon was unanimously adopted. It is curious to note that Mount Vernon had not appeared at all in the first vote. 

Copies of these proceedings have come down to us.

First, the original minutes of the meetings have been preserved. Then the "Constitution and By Laws" and the "Abstract of Title" of the lands purchased were printed as well as "Plans and Specifications for Grading of the Streets," circular broadsides showing the method of choosing and selecting the lots, as well as an extended newspaper account of the closing meeting. at which articles of silverware were presented to the officers of the organization. The original printed charter for the Village is also in existence so that we can fairly judge how they worked. This charter, however, was not adopted without a strong remonstrance to the court and a petition against its adoption signed by sixty-eight "residents, taxpayers, voters and owners of real estate in the Village.”

To show how they managed one of the most difficult problems, I will quote from a letter of Mr. George 

Stevens, son of the founder of Mount Vernon, who gives us the recollection of an eye witness present on the spot: 16

"Many of you know that the Association by which Mount Vernon was founded was composed of a thousand mem- ·bers, and that the drawing of the lots took place in the largest of a group of barns adjoining the Sylvanus Purdy house, now known as the Stevens Homestead. The order of the drawing having been previously determined by lot, the number of fifty each day for twenty consecutive days, journeyed from the City in order of their enumeration and arrived at the old barn, stimulated by the presence of substantial refreshment and a liberal cider barrel, made selection from the map of the lots of their preference, each member being allowed ten minutes wherein to make his selection.

Mr. Richard Wood was number one on the list, and the location of the depot having been previously determined, he selected lot 356 on the corner of Fourth Avenue and

First Street where he built the Franklin Hotel and Stables (destroyed at the time of the great fire in the Fall of 1873). 

The dimensions of that lot as indicated on the map were 

162 ft. on First Street and 72 feet on Fourth Avenue and the original cost to Mr. Wood was $91.50 . . ."

A similar proceeding was held when the lots in West Mount Vernon were chosen in a large tent erected somewhere on Mount Vernon Avenue, except that the refreshments were washed down with lager beer instead of cider, and the day was a picnic day for the Germans who participated in it. It was managed by Gustavus A. Sacchi who was the trustee of the Teutonia Homestead Association. 

Besides the Village of Mount Vernon there were a number of other locations laid out at about that time. 

West Mount Vernon in 1851, Central Mount Vernon in 1853, Fleetwood in 1853, South Mount Vernon in 

1851, East Mount Vernon in 1851, and a number of smaller ones that were all eventually absorbed by the City of Mount Vernon.

Mount Vernon had 39 newspapers up to 1912 and perhaps some others that were not listed. The first newspaper, if it can be called that, was issued on September 30th, 1854, and was edited by Henry S. Hill and E. A. S. Manning, one of the signers protesting against a charter for the Village. It was a weekly, of course, and contained local news and perhaps a few items from the neighborhood. It was called "The 

Mount Vernon Gazette" and lived until 1857. It was followed by the "Eastchester Democrat," "The Village 

News" and "The Weekly Sentinel," all weekly, but none of them were newspapers until Joseph S. Wood began "The Chronicle" in September 1869. Mr. Wood had views that he expressed in his paper in a manner that provided many a discussion and much feeling, but it was a newspaper everyone had to admit whether he agreed with him or not. He was the principal of the public school at the time and this flourished under him. The school's first commencement was 

Iduring his administration in 1867 and showed six graduates. In 1874 the school published a newspaper written by the students, which continued for a year at least. But Mr. Wood was not universally popular. 

In 1854 a development began on the west side which had its main office in West Mount Vernon though the land itself was on the other side of the 

Bronx River in Yonkers. George J. Mankin who was its owner proposed to convert the three hundred and sixty acres into a park that rivalled the present Bronx 

Park, to be known as Glen Park. He planned to develop the property into a garden with fine roads. 27

and a bridge that connected it with West Mount Vernon. A fine iron bridge across the Bronx with a lodge at the entrance to the property was built and remained in use for many years after the plan had failed. It was about three-quarters of a mile south of the Harlem railroad station and much money was spent in elaborate advertising. Mankin lived in a fine house on top of the hill in Yonkers and it remained until it was torn down to make room for the Hill View Reservoir of the New York City water supply. It was occupied for a long time by Major George W. McLean of the "Old Guard" of New York City.

Both associations had set aside lots for school use but that of the Teutonia Association was on a rock which covered the surface of almost the whole lot. The elected school authorities bought another lot in Union, now North High, Street, and put the old schoolhouse upon it, as described in another place. The Industrial 

Home Association had its first school in Journeau's Hall on the southeast corner of Fifth Avenue and Fourth

Street. This house was afterward moved across the avenue and became the nucleus for the residence of 

Mayor Brush. The West Mount Vernon school remained in use until 1868 when it was torn down to make room for the first brick building, now in place there and used by the Lighting Company. A wing of the present brick building was built in front of the old wooden one and used for some time but the number of pupils increased so fast that it was again found too small and the new building, built in 1876, was constructed a block farther south, where it now is. It was changed several times so that very little of it now remains. Some years ago it was named "Hamilton School" but to the older residents it remained "School No. 5."

Central Mount Vernon remained a waste of vacant lots for a long time. There was a large chestnut tree 28

in the center, about where North Seventh Avenue now is, that served as a guide for people who crossed the "hill" after dark. It is said that in the early years a circus once came there and that a country fair was once held there. Early in the sixties however, the "Una" Baseball Club made it the resort of the sporting people of the neighborhood and the old-fashioned game was played with great success. A few houses had been built there, notably the "Moll" house, afterward the Van Santvcord house, where people picnicked under the apple trees. It was torn down when 

Eighth Avenue was cut through. The other houses were those of the Cavanaugh family near Valentine Street in Seventh Avenue and one or two little cottages on the hill. It was not until later years that Central 

Mount Vernon began to build up. 

Chester Hill began in 1869 when Charles Crary tock possession. He laid out the building lots in a fashion that was the despair of the surveyors to find their boundaries. But notwithstanding it became a select community because he had the foresight to place restrictions on the use of the lots and buyers and it became a district of fine houses. 

The New York & New Haven railroad at that time ran on the surface for a large part of the way, and the sinking of the track was not undertaken until later. 

The social atmosphere of all Mount Vernon during its early years was genial and not exclusive. If you enjoyed Shakespeare and the poets, the drama, or preferred music, or, if you doted on balls and parties, you would find people to your taste. There was the 

Athenaeum, or the Mount Vernon Mannerchor to care for the more cultivated tastes. There was the firemen's ball with its colored fire dances. There were package parties, strawberry festivals, concerts by the Vesper

Quartette, lectures, the temperance meetings of the 29

Alliance Temple of Honor, dancing, masquerade parties, invitation hops by the "Una" Baseball Club, military companies, church fairs, Grand Instrumental and Vocal concerts to be held if it did not rain, in which case they were postponed to the next night. There was a brass band that gave concerts but most of this was done by local talent with little foreign help. If you were interested in improving local conditions you could join a Citizens' Association in 1869.

The political divisions during the war period were very bitter. The Eastern State Journal, the only paper published at that time that had any circulation in Mount Vernon for a long time, carried this statement at the head of its columns, long after Lincoln was elected and months after the Civil War had begun.

There was no other paper published in Mount Vernon at the time. There were Union meetings as well, in which many Democrats took part. Nearly every one in the County without distinction of party mourned when Lincoln was shot.

Toward the end of the war the lack of fractional currency was felt and groceryman Van Court issued "shinplasters," as they were called, to enable his customers. to make their purchases at his store in Fourth Avenue.

In the anti-slavery controversy before the war, Mount Vernon did its share and also was represented in a shining way by a powerful sermon preached in the Methodist Church by Rev. William H. Boole as an "Antidote to the Rev. H. J. VanDyke's Pro-Slavery Dis- 30

course" preached in the First Presbyterian Church of

Brooklyn on "The Character and Influence of Aboli tionism", in which Mr. Boole argued that the Bible did not approve slavery. It was a brilliant sermon and he proved the "faith that was in him" by resigning and joining the army as a chaplain. 

Shortly after the war, an event of political importance was the visit to Mount Vernon of Susan B. Anthony, the pioneer advocate of woman's rights. She came to Union Hall to urge the women property owners to insist on the right to vote on questions of appropriating money for public purposes. It was very shrewdly managed for most of the matters voted on did involve that question. She advised them to offer their votes and, if they were refused, to take the matter to the courts.

Another matter that played a role in the future elections was the temperance question. There probably were not very many political temperance men but a convention was called at Hunt's Bridge in 1854 to select delegates to the nominating convention to meet at 

Whitlockville in Bedford, on October 11th, 1854. Mount

Vernon had its "Alliance Temple of Honor," a temperance organization that built Willard Hall as early as 

1868 and perhaps earlier.

We now come to the churches. The first, of course, was St. Paul's Episcopal Church at Eastchester, and then the Methodist, close by. In Mount Vernon the first Episcopal service was held in 1852 in the old schoolhouse that stood near Scott's Bridge. It is presumed that all the church-going people of the City had some place where they met and worshipped, according to their beliefs and wishes and in accordance with the rites provided for them by their organization. We have now (1940) nearly thirty churches, with which many of our people are affiliated, but to determine when each of the churches was organized is a serious task. It would appear that the earlier churches were organized about in the following order: Methodist 

1852, Catholic 1852, Baptist 1853, Reformed 1853, St. 

Paul's Lutheran (German) 1855, Liberal 1855, and 

Trinity Episcopal 1856, all the other denominations following. The role that the churches played in the City was important and it would take a bigger book to do justice to their benefits alone. Their ministers were sincere and earnest men and women, and their influence extended far beyond the boundaries of their congregations. It is enough to say that the churches had a moral influence on our people that we could not do without.

Later in the sixties and early in the seventies Mount Vernon had an influx of politicians who were of doubtful benefit to this community. Tweed, Corson, Sweeney and others of the same company appeared in the Village and the County and started to make it over. 

They widened and straightened our streets and left us $300,000 in debt which took a long time to pay. Then came the catastrophe of 1870, when they had to leave their unfinished work. Whether it would have had any 34

lasting benefit in the future is doubtful. They did start a bank, "The Eastchester National Bank," with a nominal capital of $300,000, which did business here for a while, but when the rascality in the City of New York was uncovered, it dropped from sight. 

Burke Cochran, the Tammany orator and Congressman, began his career in this Village, serving as a clerk in Berry's dry goods store. He eventually became a school teacher in Tuckahoe. After a rather checkered career in Mount Vernon he was taken to

New York and became an adherent of Tammany Hall, first as a political orator and gradually as a prominent figure in politics in consequence. 

An important organization in the village affairs was the Fire Department. Fires were of rare occurrence in the early days and when they did occur every resident ran for his bucket and helped to extinguish it. As the 

Village grew, it was found necessary to provide for protection and the Fire Department was organized with hand-drawn engines. The companies were the Clinton 

Hook and Ladder Company, organized in 1856, the 

Washington Engine Company in 1860, and the Protection Engine Company in 1874, all manned by volunteers who served the Village with great efficiency and without pay. The great fire of 1873 put them to a severe test. It began on the westerly side of Fourth 

Avenue and burned all the buildings half-way up to Second Street. The only water they had was the little obtained from the wells and cisterns of the neighborhood. Their exertions saved the easterly side from destruction.

In June 1880 the Grand Army of the Republic organized Farnsworth Post here. It was an organization of 

Union Veterans of the Civil War with the noble purpose of helping comrades who needed help, and no veteran who was in distress appealed to them in vain. 35

All in all, Mount Vernon was a model community and it impressed the visitor when he first crossed the line from the Bronx. Its neat buildings, "not fine residences," its well-kept streets, and the general appearance of thrifty home-loving residents, who took good care of their village homes, made it a good place to live in. A common remark when one was going away, "When are you coming back?" was generally borne out by the return of the native to his own hearth and his old friends.
