INTRODUCTION 

In order to obtain information based upon Urban Renewal experience of cities with populations approximating the 250,000 to 500,000 class, twenty-six cities out of 29 within this classification were surveyed (see page 15). 

The material has been summarized and arranged in simple fashion within this report, and in it's preparation an attempt has been made at explanations and interpretations of the information gathered from the participating cities. 

Specifically, this report has been prepared in order to aid a Citizens Committee in Omaha, Nebraska in the preparation of a minimum housing standards ordinance for this city, and also, to convey to the committee some of the views of housing officials who are working with rehabilitation programs from day to day.

Appreciation for participation in the survey is expressed to those officials from cities participating. 

April, 1956 

Joseph F. Mangiamele

This indicates that the court action was brought about by the administrator and that few violators are appealing the orders of the administrator. 

This city also reports issuence of 7800 violation notices within the three and one-half year period of its operation. -7-

This information demonstrates the need for public understanding and acceptance of the program, in that voluntary compliance to minimum standards reduces costly surveilance and legal action. Rehabilitation officials stress the importances of public education and information programs, citizen groups made-up of informed leaders of the neighborhoods and a city wide advisory group. One administrator suggests that personnel be carefully selected and on the basis of ability to courteously confront citizens, analysis individual situations, and at all times be interested, sympathetic and helpful. This type of "investigator" is preferred in that he is more effective than the usual type of city inspector experienced in the building trades.

It is indicated by the rehabilitation administrators that the minimum standards ordinance is a public declaration of housing standards for the community, which are to be complied with as any other law. The administrator is given the duty of helping and informing the citizens of the individual and community benefits which are to be derived by meeting these standards. A programı involving minimum legal action and obtaining a high degree of voluntary compliance is considered a successful program. The program places upon the administrator the burden of aiding families to relocate themselves. One administrator advises in these cases the individuals must be given consideration of their specific problems and given sufficient time in finding adequate housing. -8-

It is further indicated that the community aid individuals in solving whatever financial problems may arise in complying with the standards. Here again the administrator's duty is to work toward solving the problems confronting those who need to take action in order to comply with the standards. Legal action should not be taken by the administrator until ample consideration has been given an individual in complying with community established standards. -9-

PROPERTY TAX AND REHABILITATION PROGRAMS 

Most of the cities report that the rehabilitation program has no effect upon taxes. Several cities report that if structural changes or additions are made, that a change of valuation can be expected. However, the assessor in the se cases is not directly informed of the improvements. 

In that the housing rehabilitation brought about by rahabilitation programs in these cities does not affect the tax base, it is evident that rehabilitation areas either carry and maintain preconceived low values by the assessores, or that the type of rehabilitation being carried out is such that although it might stabilize existing values it does not have an upgrading effect as far as tax values are concerned. 

Usually tax values are established on a long range basis, and temporary increases and decreases in market values are rarely reflected by property tax assessing methods.

Rehabilitation processes may be considered to be only temporary in nature and insufficient to be reflected in tax values. 

Of course, housing official are aware of the need to go beyond existing minimum housing standards to upgrade neighborhoods. 

In many cases some replanning of the areas and inclusion of modern neighborhood facilities are necessary to aid in the upgrading of these areas. -11-

The up-grading of neighborhoods must be such as to have long range effects before an increase in the market values and tax values can be expected. There are many factors within our society that must be taken into account in considering the values of neighborhoods.

In building new houses, owners are induced to build in new areas in order to prolong the values of their buildings; for homes in this country have a tendency to rapidly decline in value.

This may be due to such factors as the high family mobility rate, rapidly changing house styles, business promotion of new housing developments, the established process of building homes with similar values within certain areas and the tying of value of homes with family incomes as a basis of financing, the social significances given to certain areas, the income group separating effect of zoning laws, the short life expectancy of neighborhoods, type of construction materials and procedures and many other social and economic factors. 

The only definite report that may be made in relation to tax values at this time is that existing neighborhood and housing rehabilitation programs have not appreciably affected the tax values within the population class considered in this survey. -12-

GENERAL SUMMARY 

A majority of cities with population in the one-quarter to one-half million range have recognized the problem of Urban 

Rehabilitation. 

Definite action has been taken to establish rehabilitation programs. Most of these programs are relatively new when compared with the age of the cities in which the programs have been initiated. Many problems are not as impossible as may appear at first glance, while others are still to be solved. 

Traditional citizen participation is a significant democratic element of the program, with citizens taking part in the creation of the programs, and guiding their administration through advisory groups and appeal board actions.

Although techincal agencies are established to study the problems and to administer the programs, this is done within the general framework of citizen enlightenment activities, citizen participation, and citizen's guidance. It is also significant that community approved standards are generally established to guide the citizens and the program administrators in the bringing about of a practical and democratic community program. 

Many of the rehabilitation program leaders anticipate longrange improvements in the quality of housing and in the living condition for a great many of our citizens. Certainly health and safety conditions are improved through these programs. -13-

However, it is doubtful that the improvement of only the se conditions improve the economic values of the houses and the rehabilitation areas. The value factor is one of the most apparent of the economic aspects, i.e. the problem of creating long-run economic effects.

The fact that tax values have not increased in rehabilitation areas may be an indication that the raising of housing conditions to minimum health and safety standards is not sufficient improvement to effect property values. 

Evidently, rehabilitation programs do not renew neighborhood facilities to the extent that an increased market demand is created, at least not to the extent that the tax assessors have been significantly impressed.

Much is to be gained from the experience of the many cities which are operating rehabilitation programs. Many problems are being solved, and the housing officials are dedicating themselves to the many problems of the future within the framework of the rapidly developing cities of our times. -14-
