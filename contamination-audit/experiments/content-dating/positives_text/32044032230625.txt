Parole had its first beginnings in the early 18th Century in England. The transportation of criminals from England to the American Colonies, and later to Australia, was the first experiment in serving a part of the prison sentence outside the prison walls. These convicted English felons were released first by the King and later by the courts as "indentured slaves" and were transported to America by skipmasters as their property. Upon arrival of the "convicted felons" in the Colonies, their services were sold to the highest bidder. The felon thereupon was no longer referred to as a criminal but as an "indentured servant." Thus, the addage of "paying your debt to society" has a long, if not dubious, history. 

By 1865, the Crofton system had been widely publicized in America. This system enunciated the need for new types of prison programs to provide for the grading of criminals according to the degree of their reformation. It was also the beginning 

of the "mark" system or "good-time" concept for good prison behavior shortening the maximum sentence. This system is recognized primarily by the Elmira Reformatory in New York State. 

THE FEDERAL PAROLE SYSTEM: 

The Federal Parole System was created in 1910 with the passage of an Act authorizing the parole of prisoners sentenced to a term of one year or more. The prisoner was eligible for parole upon expiration of one-third of his sentence and was 1187849

eligible whether he was serving his Federal sentence in a state or Federal facility.

The releasing provisions of the Act were administered by institutional parole boards which consisted of the warden and physician of the facility and the Superintendent of Prisons from the Department of Justice. 

In 1930, Congress created a central Federal Board of Parole consisting of three full-time members appointed by the Attorney General. The Board was based in Washington, D.C. and was authorized, by statute, to make final decisions relative to parole and revocation.

Over the years a number of changes in the Board's structure followed and in 1950 the Federal Youth Corrections Act was enacted and companion legislation created the Youth Correction Division within the Board. The size of the Board was increased to eight full-time members, with three members serving on the Youth Correction Division while the other five members serve on the Adult Division of the Board. Thus, the United States 

Board of Parole now consists of eight full-time members who are appointed to staggered six year terms. The members, since 1950, have been appointed by the President subject to Senatorial confirmation. The Attorney General assigns the confirmed members to the Adult Board and the Youth Division and designates their respective Chairmen. 

The Youth Division is an integral part within the United States Board of Parole. Its specific responsibility is to act

PAROLE ELIGIBILITY: 

Life and General Law Sentences: 

Eligibility for parole is governed by the section of the United States Code under which sentenced. A prisoner sentenced to a Life Term is eligible for parole after serving fifteen years. Under the general law, the maximum term is set by statute. The individual sentenced pursuant thereto is eligible for parole after serving one-third of the statutory term. If he applies for parole, he appears before the Board for his parole hearing. 

Indeterminate Adult Sentences: The Federal Criminal Code's indeterminate adult sentences are codified under Section 4208 (a) (1) and (a)(2). Under Section 4208(a)(1), the sentencing court sets the minimum term but not to exceed one-third of the maximum term set by statute. After serving the minimum term stipulated by the Court, the individual appears for his initial hearing for the purpose of parole consideration. Under

Federal Juvenile Delinquency Act Commitments: The juvenile offender may be committed to a definite term but not to exceed his majority; or he may receive a minority commitment. The juvenile offender, whether committed for a term or for his minority, is eligible for parole on the date of his first appearance before the Board. As in the case of the youth offender, the juvenile appears after classification, usually sixty to ninety days after institutional admission.

The new rules and procedures of the 

Board of Parole provide for: 

a) Relying on well-qualified parole hearing examiners to conduct all initial parole hearings with inmates at the institution. Such hearings conducted by parole hearing examiners shall include a summary of the hearing, summary of facts, evaluation and conclusions, including a recommended decision. b) The Board Members remaining in Washington will review all summaries and recommendations by the parole hearing examiners and have ample time to "confer and deliberate" and study the entire file prior to entering a timely Board decision. c) Adopting scientific criteria for parole decision-making, such as heavier reliance upon the Nationwide Uniform 

Parole Statistical Reporting System and the Board's criteria for parole selection chapter in the new rules of the Board. 

We have recently received a $500,000 grant from the Law Enforcement Assistance Act administration to provide immediate on-line data from the computer at the University of California, at Davis. This will aid us in making more scientific parole decisions and reducing disparity in time served by Federal prisoners.

d) 

Establishing en banc appellate review procedures where, in certain cases, ninety days after a Division (Adult or Youth) decision of the Board has denied parole, an en banc appellate review by all eight members of the Board will be held to act as an appellate review body over prior decisions.

CONCLUSION: 

All Federal, state and local government agencies responsible for the administration of criminal justice must improve their methods, update their procedures and make use of modern scientific techniques if America is to meet the ever increasing challenge of crime in the Seventies.

Parole has a very important part to play in this total process. The United States Board of Parole is confident that our new rules and reorganized procedures especially the added scientific data in decision-making improve the quality of justice in the Federal system. will
