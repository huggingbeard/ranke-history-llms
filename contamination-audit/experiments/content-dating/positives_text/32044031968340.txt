PREFACE 

The principles of each topic within this subject are presented in the form of LEGAL GEMS. (1) If the principle is brief, clear and easily understood, it is left simply in the form of a statement. (2) If the principle is a bit complex or may not be easily understood, it is illustrated by a simple example immediately following the LEGAL GEM. (3) If the principle is very important or difficult to understand, it is illustrated by a CASE which follows the LEGAL GEMS 

on the topic. The CASE is also intended to present an ef fective method of constructing an opinion in the solution of a legal problem.

It is hoped that this method of presentation, (a) will give the student an over-all, bird's-eye view of the most important principles in this particular subject, (b) will enable him to see and understand more clearly those basic principles, and (c) will enhance his capacity in choosing the appropriate principles, in applying those principles to the facts, and in constructing effective opinions, in the solution of legal problems.

I. INTESTATE SUCCESSION LEGAL GEMS 

1. Intestate means that one dies without leaving a will. 

2. The property of an intestate descends according to law. 

3. At common law realty by the doctrine of primogeniture passed to the eldest son. (This never existed in the U. S.) 

4. At common law personalty passed to the administrator for the next of kin, which means nearest in blood relationship as explained below. 

5. One who took realty by descent was an heir. 

6. The word "descent" originally meant that property passed down the lineal line from father to son, but it now includes ascent as well, i.e. from son to father. 

7. Consanguinity means blood relationship and is either lineal or collateral. 

8. Lineal means that relationship between persons when one is descended directly from the other, e.g. grandfather, father, son. 

9. Persons are related collaterally when they have a common ancestor, e.g. brothers, A and 

B, have the same father or ancestor, F. 

10. In the U. S. the civil law method of computing degrees of consanguinity is used. `In the lineal line it is merely counting generations, excluding the first and including the last, ei-

The degree of blood relationship of collateral relatives is computed by counting from the claimant up to the common ancestor and down to the decedent or from the decedent up to the common ancestor and down to the claimant, excluding the first and including the last. 11. Taking per capita means taking by the head or as an individual, e.g. C dies leaving three children, X, Y and Z as his only heirs. They are related to C in the first degree so that each takes one third of C's property. 

12. Taking per stirpes means taking by the root or by right to represent a deceased ancestor, e.g. C dies leaving two children X and Y and grandchildren, M and N, the children of C's deceased child Z. In such case C's property is divided into three parts, one going to X, one to Y and the other third is taken by M and N per stirpes, i.e. through their father whom they represent. 

13. Adopted children acquired no rights of inheritance under the common law. Under modern statutes they usually inherit as do children born in lawful wedlock. 

14. An illegitimate child was at common law a child of no one and had no right to inherit. 

This has been much changed by statute. 

15. A possibility of reverter and a right of reentry for condition broken (called power of termination today) will descend from ancestor to heir even though it is not assignable at common law when not connected with reversion. 

16. There can be no heir of a living person. 

17. An heir apparent has a mere expectancy till his ancestor both dies and is intestate, and such expectancy is not transferable. 

18. Advancements apply only in cases of total intestacy because when a person dies testate there is a presumption of total testacy and that the testator has dealt with all of his property.

19. The doctrine of advancements is recognized for the purpose of equalizing the shares of children as heirs. 

20. Every advancement is a gift but not every gift is an advancement. Whether the gift is intended to be absolute or is an advancement to be deducted from the share of the child by inheritance is a question of intention of the intestate. 

21. No advancement can be taken from the donee but the recipient thereof must account for the amount received if he would participate in the distribution of the parent's estate.

Each of these five persons takes one thirty fifth of D's property. The reasons are these. CASE 2 

H and W are husband and wife. They have two children, A and B. B dies leaving two children, C and D. H and W adopt C and D. W dies testate giving her property to H. H then dies intestate and the statute provides that adopted children shall have the same rights as children born in lawful wedlock and that property of an intestate shall pass to his children and their descendants. How shall H's property descend? 

Ans. A should receive one fourth, C one fourth as a child of H, D one fourth as a child of H, and C and D shall take per stirpes the share of their deceased father, B, which would be an additional one eighth to each. The reason for such distribution is this: C and D are both children and grandchildren of 

H. As children they take per capita with A. As grandchildren they take per stirpes through their father, B, as the root or the stirpes. So A gets one fourth, C gets three eighths and 

D gets three eighths, which shares make up the total estate left by H. 

See 50 Ia. 532; 166 P.2d 257 (Utah 1946).

CASE 3 

H and W adopted S who was the natural child of A and B. 

W died devising all her property to H. H conveyed Blackacre to S as a birthday present. S saved his money and bought 

Whiteacre. S died intestate and unmarried. How should 

Blackacre and Whiteacre descend?

Ans. By the law of descent both Blackacre and Whiteacre would pass to A and B because the natural parents rather than the adoptive parents inherit from the adopted child. By statute today Blackacre is often inherited by H because it came to S from H.

CASE 4 

H died testate leaving all his property to his two children A and B. His will did not mention his child C who was born to H's wife eight months after H's death. A, B and C were 

H's only heirs as to the property involved. What are C's rights?

Ans. C has the same rights he would have had had H died intestate. A posthumous child is one born after the death of the testate or intestate. Such child is considered in being for purpose of inheritance and in this case would take one third of the estate. Only lineal, not collateral, relatives in the womb can inherit. C could also take as a pretermitted heir because he was not mentioned in H's will and is therefore considered forgotten or inadvertently omitted. 

See 57 Cal. 484.

CASE 5 

H murdered his wife, W. They were tenants by the entirety of Blackacre. Upon the death of W by the doctrine of survivorship, H became sole owner of the property. H was convicted of murdering W, sentenced to prison for life and W's heirs claim Blackacre as against H. W's life expectancy was greater than H's. What are the rights of the parties as to Blackacre?

15 Ans. H should continue to have his life estate but should hold the remainder of the fee in trust for W's heirs and be enjoined from conveying the fee. The reasons are that conviction for crime does not work forfeiture of estate so H's life estate should not be taken from him, and no one should be permitted to take by his own wrong. Hence, when W's life expectancy was greater than H's, any estate which H got must be because of his crime so he should hold such in constructive trust for W's heirs.

CASE 6 

H and W were married and had four children, one of whom was sentenced to life imprisonment in the state penitentiary. 

A statute provided, "A person sentenced to imprisonment in the state prison for life is thereafter deemed civilly dead.” (Ariz. 

Code 43-6116 is identical.) H died intestate and the court distributed his property to W and three children and gave the convict nothing. The Supreme Court of Cal. affirmed the decision holding that the right to inherit property is a civil right and is taken away by civil death. 

See 58 P. 61. In 1945 a N. Y. court held that a life prisoner could inherit property as an heir in spite of his civil death. 

56 N.Y.S.2d 568.

CASE 7 

H died testate owning Blackacre and his sole heir was his son, S. H by his will devised Blackacre to "my heirs". Does

S take Blackacre as heir by operation of law or as a purchaser through H's will?

Ans. S takes as an heir and not as a purchaser. This case is governed by the ancient doctrine of "worthier title." There are two ways by which one may get title to realty (a) by descent and (b) by purchase which means by the act of the party such as deed, will, or contract. To take by descent was considered worthier than to take by purchase so the rule became this one

cannot make his own heir his purchaser. It applied when the identical interest would pass by law as by the terms of the will. 

In this case Blackacre would have descended to S in fee had H died intestate. He is given the same by H's will. Hence, S takes by descent rather than by purchase for H cannot make his heir, S, his purchaser.

See The Doctrine of Worthier Title under Real Property herein.

CASE 8 

T owned Blackacre in fee simple and conveyed it to "Dee County so long as used for courthouse purposes". T then died intestate leaving his son, H, his sole heir. Dee County then ceased to use Blackacre for a county courthouse but still held possession thereof. H brings ejectment against Dee County. 

May H recover?

The answer is yes. T's conveyance to Dee County created in the County a determinable fee simple estate. There remained in T a possibility of reverter which is a descendible interest. Upon T's death intestate such possibility of reverter descended to his heir, H. When Dee County ceased to use the land for courthouse purposes it automatically reverted to H who is the owner in fee simple absolute with right to possess such. H may, therefore, eject Dee County from Blackacre. 

See 85 N.E. 267; Future Interests under Smith's Review of

Real Property. 

CASE 9 

T dies intestate leaving three sons, A, B and C, as his sole statutory heirs. T leaves no debts and his total estate consists of $1400 in cash. During his lifetime T gave to A, $300, to 

B, $100 and to C nothing. How should the administrator distribute such estate?

Ans. A should get $300, B should get $500 and C should get $600. When an intestate is a parent or occupies a position in loco parentis and during his lifetime makes a gift to the

INTESTATE SUCCESSION 

17 child, such gift is presumed to be an advancement which must be accounted for upon the distribution of the decedent's estate if the donee would participate in the distribution of the parent's estate. In this case there being nothing to the contrary the presumption applies and the gifts to A and B are advancements. 

The doctrine of advancements requires A and B to bring their gifts of $300 and $100 into hotchpot (or hotchpotch or hotchpodge) to be added to the $1400 of T's estate, thus making a total of $1800. Dividing such $1800 equally among A, B and 

C, each will then take $600. But, of course, A and B do not actually pay over to the administrator their advances of $300 and $100. It is merely calculated arithmetically. Thus, the $1400 left by T is distributed as follows. A is given $300 which, added to his $300 advancement, makes a total of $600. 

B is given $500 which, added to his $100 advancement, makes a total of $600. C, having received no advancement is given $600. Thus the total distributed is $1400. 

CASE 10 

T dies intestate leaving three sons, A, B and C, as his sole statutory heirs. T leaves no debts and his total estate consists of $200 in cash. During his lifetime he gave to A, $300, to 

B, $100, and to C nothing. How should the administrator distribute the estate?

Ans. B should get $50 and C should get $150. The gifts made to A and B are presumed to be advancements. But such presumption is rebuttable and may be a gift absolute. Every advancement is a gift which cannot be taken away from the donee. It is not to A's advantage to bring his advancement into hotchpot because if he did so he would lose $100. His adding the $300 to B's $100 and to the $200 left by T would make $600 of which A would take only a one third share or $200. 

Hence, A will not bring his $300 into the estate and will not share in the distribution. But it is to B's financial advantage to bring into hotchpot his $100 advancement and make the total of the estate $300 to be distributed equally to B and C or $150 each. So the administrator simply distributes to B $50 which, added to his $100 advancement, makes his total $150.

C having received no advancement receives the remaining $150 of his parent's $200 estate. It should be kept in mind that advancements apply only in case of total intestacy and should be compared with the doctrine of ademption in case of testacy.

II. WILLS (or TESTATE SUCCESSION) LEGAL GEMS 

1. One who dies leaving a will dies testate, and is called a testator. 

2. A person named in a will to administer the estate is an executor. 

3. A person appointed by the court to administer the estate of an intestate is an administrator. 

4. A person appointed by the court to administer the estate according to the provisions of a will is an administrator with the will annexed. 

This happens when an executor cannot serve or will not serve or when the will fails to appoint such. 

5. Disposition by will of land is called a devise, of money is called a legacy and of personalty generally is called a bequest. 

6. There is no natural or common law right to make a will-such right is wholly statutory. 

7. There is no will without compliance with the statutory requirements. 

8. A will is a legal declaration of one's intention to dispose of his property after death. 

9. There are THREE KINDS OF WILLS: (a) 

written or witnessed will which is one in writing the authenticity of which is vouched for by witnesses, (b) olographic or holographic which is one wholly written, dated and signed by the testator whose authenticity is vouched for by the testator's handwriting and (c) nuncupative which is oral. 

10. Every will is ambulatory which means that it can be changed at any moment before death by a competent testator. 

11. No will is valid without testamentary intent, that is, animus testandi, an intent to make a will.

12. No valid will can exist without the coexistence of three essential facts (a) a competent testator (b) physical compliance with the requirements of the statute and (c) animus testandi, the testator's intent that this shall be his will.

CASE 11 

The following instrument was presented to the court for probate as a will. "If case of death i sign everything that be longs to me over to Mr. and Mrs. Komadino Oatman Arizona 

JAMES MORRISON". The jury found that Morrison did not intend this instrument to be his will. The lower court was of opinion that it "was intended by him to be his last will and testament." The Supreme Court of Arizona said there was No 

Evidence to support the jury's finding and that the instrument showed "animus testandi" on its face. The court's words are, "The language of this instrument is certainly testamentary in character. It shows on its face that it was written in contemplation of death.

All the evidence is to the effect that he intended it as his will, that he spoke of it as his will, and that it was in fact and deed what it purported to be on its face. 

We think the court erred in not admitting the (olographic) will to probate." 

55 Ariz. 504, 103 P.2d 669.

CASE 12 

The following instrument was presented to the court for probate as an olographic will. "Dear Old Nance: I wish to give you my watch, two shawls, and also five thousand dollars. Your old friend, (signed) E. A. Gordon." The evidence disclosed that "Old Nance" was an intimate friend of Mrs. Gordon, that 

Mrs. Gordon executed the above instrument and gave it to "Old 

Nance" identified as Miss Ransom, and instructed her to ask the residuary legatee, Mrs. Gordon's brother, for the watch, shawls and $5000 and if he would not give them to her, then

she should "give the paper to Mr. Doyle and have it effected after her death." The instrument was admitted to probate. 

Held that animus testandi may appear either on the face of the instrument or outside the instrument by the surrounding circumstances. In this case it is the latter. 

See 50 Cal. 595.

LEGAL GEMS · 

1. One has testamentary capacity or sound mind if he has sufficient mind to understand (a) the nature and extent of his property and (b) the natural objects of his bounty and (c) the nature of the testamentary act. 

2. A testator is presumed to have testamentary capacity and the burden is on the contestant to show lack of such capacity at the time of the making of the will. 

3. An insane delusion is the belief in a state of facts when there is no evidence to support it and all evidence demonstrates the contrary, e.g. a man believes the bathtub in which he is bathing in clear water is full of green snakes.

For an insane delusion to invalidate a will, there must be the delusion and the will must be the product of such delusion. 

4. There can be no insane delusion unless the belief is subject to disproof by demonstrative evidence, e.g. a religious belief in the hereafter, the truth of which can neither be proved nor disproved, cannot be an insane delusion.

CASE 13 

T died testate leaving his nearest relative, a brother B, $1 and the residue of his estate to the Spiritualist Church. B attacked the will on the ground that the will was the product of two insane delusions, (a) the testator's belief in Spiritualism and (b) T's belief that B had caused the death of T's infant son. The evidence showed that T's infant child was being fed on milk from a cow belonging to B; that B sold the cow and T's child

had to be fed on milk from another cow; that the child then became sick and died. The will of T was held good. The belief in Spiritualism cannot be an insane delusion because it can neither be proved nor disproved by demonstrative evidence. 

The belief that B killed T's child cannot be an insane delusion because it is supported by some evidence, weak though it be, to wit: B did sell the cow and the child died after being fed on milk from another cow. 

See 81 N.E. 1044.

CASE 14 

H and W were husband and wife and joint owners of Blackacre. H met his death by the wrongful act of D. H's father was living at H's death and in his father's will H was the residuary beneficiary. H's will provided as follows, "I give to my friend, B, all of my interest in Blackacre and all of my interest in my father's estate and if I should meet my death by another's wrongful act I give such cause of action to B." What property would B get under such provisions of H's will? 

Ans. B takes nothing under H's will. A testator cannot by will dispose of property which he does not own at the time of his death. Applying such doctrine to the facts of this case we find that H's interest in his father's estate was a bare expectancy which never became vested in H because his father was still living and the father's will would not become effective in H's favor until the father died while H lived and with H still being a beneficiary under the father's will. In joint tenancy the doctrine of survivorship operates so that upon the instant of H's death, Blackacre became vested solely in W, the surviving joint tenant. By the time H's will became effective H no longer had any interest in Blackacre so his will was wholly ineffective as to the property. The cause of action against D for the wrongful death of H arose at H's death and was never owned by H. 

Hence, he could not by will dispose of same. Therefore, B got nothing by virtue of H's will. 

See Hunt v. Smith, 230 S.W. 936, 191 Ky. 443, 17 A.L.R. 

588, 597 note; 195 P. 1113.

Due execution of wills: nuncupative wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A nuncupative will is an oral will. 

2. A nuncupative will requires animus testandi. 

3. A typical statute on nuncupative wills requires (a) that such will be made during the testator's last sickness, (b) that it can dispose of personalty only, not realty, (c) the testator must indicate to the witnesses that he wishes them to witness his oral will, (d) that there be three (or other number) witnesses over the age of 14 years (or other age), (e) that probate of such will cannot be made after six months from the time the words are spoken unless they are reduced to writing within six (or other number) days after spoken. 

4. No special form of words is required either to make the will or to call the witnesses to attest.

CASE 15 

T on his deathbed instructed his lawyer to draw for him a written will which was done and the instrument sent to T. T was too sick to sign the paper so he told the three persons present after the will was read to him and he understood it, "I want that to be my will. See that my property goes that way." The statute on nuncupative wills was typical as indicated under LEGAL GEMS above and for written wills provided that such will should be "signed by the testator, or by some other person by his direction and in his presence." Upon T's death a day later, his property was claimed by his statutory heirs and by the beneficiaries named in the paper drawn by T's lawyer. Who should prevail?

Ans. The heirs. T died intestate. T did not execute a written will because he did not sign personally or by a third person in his presence and by his direction. Did he execute a nuncupative will? There was no intent by T that the words spoken should be a will. He intended to adopt by oral words

a writing as his will. This he cannot do under such statutes mentioned. T therefore, died intestate. 

See 106 P.2d 492. 

2. Due execution of wills: holographic or olographic wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Olographic wills are recognized in 19 states. 

2. Such wills are required to be wholly written, dated and signed in the handwriting of the testator. 

3. The authenticity of olographs is vouched for by the peculiarities of the handwriting of the testator. 

4. Olographs require animus testandi. 

5. If the statute requires that an olographic will be dated, then the date must be wholly in the handwriting of the testator.

CASE 16 

T took a piece of a newspaper about the size of a tablet page and on the margin where there was no printing wrote on his typewriter the words, "My Will." Below these words and at right angles to the printing on the paper he wrote in his own handwriting with a lead pencil the following: "I hereby give all of my worldly goods to my friend, Gunga Din. 9/15/46 T.”

The statute provided that an olographic will should be wholly written, dated and signed in the handwriting of the testator. 

Is this instrument a valid olographic will? 

Ans. No. (1) Writing a will with a lead pencil is a writing within the meaning that a will must be "wholly written." But writing with a typewriter is not "wholly written" by the testator for it dispenses with the peculiarities of his handwriting. (2) 

The date 9/15/46 is complete and unambiguous and satisfies the statute as meaning September 15, 1946. (3) This leaves but one question, to wit: do the words "My Will" in typewrit-

ing prevent the will's being wholly written. This is a fact question the answer to which is this, if T intended these two words to be part and parcel of his will, then the will is not wholly written by T and is not a valid olographic will. On the other hand if T used those words merely as indicative of the identity of the paper without intent that they should be part of the will, then they are no part of the will and the instrument is a good olographic will wholly written by T. It would seem immaterial that the writing was on a piece of newspaper when written across the printing in such way as to leave no doubt that the newsprint was not intended to form part of T's will. Many cases hold that a will is not wholly written if a single letter or figure is part of the will but has not been made by the hand of the testator. This typewritten will is not an olograph. 

Atk. 305; 94 C.J.S. Wills § 205. 

3. Due execution of wills: written or witnessed wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. There can be no will without compliance with the statutory requirements concerning age, writing, signing, publication, witnessing or attestation and presence. 

2. The policy of the law is to favor the heirs unless there is a will which conforms to the requirements of the statutes. 3. 4.

An attempt to make a will coupled with an intent to do so will not make a will unless the terms of the statute are fulfilled.

To constitute a writing there must be a readable inscription in any language on a substance which makes the recording thereof relatively permanent-obviously a writing on the sand of the seashore would not suffice but inscribing legible material on a slate ought to be good and on paper with lead pencil, pen and ink or typewriter is good. 

5. Any mark such as an x, a zero or check mark or name intended by a competent testator to

constitute his signature as authenticating his will is a valid signing. This is likewise true of the signature of a witness. 

6. A few jurisdictions require the testator to publish his will which means that he must declare to the witnesses that the instrument is his will but none requires the witnesses to know the contents of the will. 

7. Attestation means intent to witness plus the subscribing by the witnesses. 

8. The words "in the presence of the testator" or "in the presence of the witnesses" mean in the line of vision of the testator or witnesses. A blind person having no line of vision may make a will with witnesses subscribing in a place where if the testator did have vision he could see them. 

9. The signature of the testator may be affixed by himself personally or by some other person in the presence of the testator and at his direction and by the better view the same rule applies in case of a witness. 

10. The signature of the testator may appear any place on the will but if the statute requires it to be "subscribed" or "at the end thereof" then it must be preceded by the contents of the will. This principle applies also to witnesses. 

11. All signatures required must be on the paper which constitutes the will or on paper attached thereto by a pin, clip or otherwise.

CASE 17 

The statute provided that a will should be signed by the testator and attested by two witnesses who shall subscribe their names thereto. T wrote his will with a typewriter and asked X and Y to witness his will. All three sat around T's desk. T produced the paper and handed it to X and requested him and

Y to sign where he pointed under the word witnesses which followed the place left for T's signature. X and Y signed and thereafter T affixed his signature in the space above the signatures of X and Y. All of this was done within two minutes of time. T died and the will is offered for probate. Should it be admitted to probate? 

Ans. Yes. It is presumed that T is a competent testator in the absence of evidence to the contrary. The writing is by typewriter which makes a good writing and it appears that all the acts of the parties were done in the presence of each of the others. The only objection to the validity of the will would seem to be that the witnesses signed before the testator. Some cases hold that until the testator signs there is nothing to witness and hence no will. From the standpoint of pure logic this conclusion is unassailable. However, it will be noticed that both witnesses and testator were sitting at testator's desk and all knew what each was to do and each did exactly what he intended to do within the space of two minutes and that there was almost no possibility of fraud or imposition. Here was one entire transaction indivisible in its parts and on that ground the instrument should be declared T's will. The principle purpose of the statutory requirements is to permit a will to be made and to protect it against fraud and imposition. This purpose is accomplished by holding T's will good. The application of pure logic should not be permitted to thwart the legislative intent.

Atk. 287; 94 C.J.S. Wills § 176. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. To prevent a witness to a will from taking under the will the benefit must be direct and financial. 

2. A bequest to a town is good even though the will is witnessed by a taxpayer; to a corporation is good even though the will is witnessed by a stockholder; to a club is good even though the will is witnessed by a member. (See 137 A. 771.)

3. The competency of a witness to a will is determined as of the time of the execution of the will. 

4. A witness must sign with intent to validate the testator's act. 

5. An attestation clause is prima facie evidence that the statements made therein are true. 

CASE 18 

A statute provided that a will should be witnessed by two credible witnesses and that if any witness is named in the will as devisee or legatee and is also an heir of the testator, then said witness shall take no more than he would have taken had the testator died intestate. T executed his will before two subscribing witnesses, H and X. T died leaving as his sole statutory heirs his three sisters, W, A and B, each of whom would have taken by intestacy one third of T's estate. H and W were husband and wife. By the terms of the will T gave half of his property to W and half to X. Should the will be admitted to probate under the above statute? 

Ans. Yes. Under the English Statute of Frauds (1677) the word credible meant competent and one who was directly financially interested in the cause was not a competent witness. Under such view neither H, spouse of beneficiary W, nor X would be "credible" witnesses. But it is obvious that the above statute is intended not to disqualify the witnesses but merely to eliminate their financial interests in having the will established. 

Hence, X would be a good witness but his devise or legacy would be void and W, being the spouse of witness H, could take only one third of T's estate and not one half for she is financially interested in having the will established to the extent of the difference between one third which she would take by intestacy and the one half she would take under the will. In a jurisdiction where the common law disabilities of married women have been completely removed and a married woman may hold her property as if sole, W should be permitted to take the entire half given her in T's will because witness, H, her husband would have no interest in her gift under the will. The

balance of the estate should pass in equal shares to W, A and 

B, if no residuary clause. See Atkinson, 2d ed. p. 317; 94

C.J.S. Wills § 185. 

4. Joint and mutual wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A will written on a single piece of paper and executed by two testators as the wills of both is a joint will. When one dies it is probated as his will and when the other dies it is likewise probated as his will. 

2. If A executes his will on one piece of paper and B executes his will on another piece of paper and each therein gives his property to the other, there are mutual wills. If A dies first his will is probated and his property is distributed to B. Thereafter B's mutual will has no practical value for the beneficiary thereunder has predeceased the testator, B. 

3. Wills may be both joint and mutual with reciprocal provisions. 

4. Mutual wills standing alone without more do not constitute a contract between the testators not to revoke; either may revoke his will at any time. 

5. If A and B draw mutual wills in favor of each other and contract not to revoke such, each is bound by the contract and has no right to breach his contract; but each still has the power to revoke his will because by nature the will is ambulatory. If A revokes his will and gives his property to C and dies, then B's remedy, permitting A's later will to be probated and the property distributed to C, is in equity to compel C to hold in trust for B according to the terms of the contract, or B may make a claim against A's estate for damages for breach of the contract. 

Atk. 176, 164; 28 H.L.R. 241.

5.

Integration, incorporation by reference, non-testa mentary acts. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Integration means the discovery and determination of what papers constitute the testator's will. 

2. Incorporation by reference means that the testator by provision in his will has legally reached out and caused to be absorbed into the will extraneous material. 

3. A non-testamentary act of the testator, beneficiary or third person which ordinarily has independent significance may be so designated by the testator in his will that it determines property to pass or persons to take under the will.

CASE 19 

T executed his will which provided, "I give the property which I have described on the flyleaf of my Bible to the person whose name I have written on the flyleaf of my Bible." The will was executed January 2, 1947 and evidence showed that T had written on the flyleaf of his Bible on December 26, 1946 the following, "my player piano to my friend, Gunga Din.”

The residuary clause of T's will was in favor of Billy Doe. T died and the player piano is claimed by both Din and Doe. 

Which should prevail?

The answer is Din. This is a simple case of incorporation by reference. For the doctrine to apply three propositions must be established, (1) that the extraneous material was in existence at the time of the making of the will (2) that the will on its face refers to such material as being in existence at the time of the making of the will and shows an intention to incorporate it and (3) that the extraneous material offered as part of the will is the identical material referred to and described in the will. Applying these requisites to the facts it is clear that the writing was on the Bible flyleaf when the will was executed because it was written Dec. 26, 1946 and the will was not made

till Jan. 2, 1947; that T intended to incorporate the material because he described both property to pass and the person to take and without such incorporation the words used in the will, "I give the property etc.", would be meaningless; that the will on its face shows the extraneous writing to be in existence because T used the past tense by saying "which I have described" and "whose name I have written"; and that the extraneous material offered was that referred to in the will because the will provides, "on the flyleaf of my Bible" and the proof showed that T had written "on the flyleaf of his Bible." Hence, all the elements to satisfy the doctrine exist and the words on the Bible flyleaf "my player piano to my friend, Gunga Din” are read into the will as though it said, "I give my player piano to my friend, Gunga Din." The piano then passes to Din by express provision and cannot fall within the residuary clause of the will. 

Had the will provided, "I give the property which I will describe to the person I may name" the writing could not have been incorporated for these words read in futuro. In such case it would be immaterial that the words had in fact been written before the making of the will. 

See Probate Div., [1902] P. 238; Atk.2d p. 385; 94 C.J.S.

Wills § 163. 

CASE 20 

M. T. died leaving an unsealed envelope on the outside of which she had written, "This is my last and only Will. (signed) M. T." Inside the envelope was a paper on which she had written in her own handwriting what purported to be her will. But there was no signature on the paper. The envelope and enclosed paper were offered for probate as her last will and testament. Should they be admitted? 

The answer is no. This is a question of integration. Every olograph must be signed by the testator and the signature must be on the paper which is the will. Here the only signature is on the exterior of the envelope which is unsealed and which contains a paper which was without doubt intended to be M. 

T.'s will for she called it such on its face. The signature may be any place on the instrument unless the statute provides that

it must be at "the end" or "foot" thereof. But in this case it is not on the instrument at all. It is on an extraneous piece of paper and there is no connection in the writings on the two which would link one to the other. The signature is an essential part of the authentication of the will and without it there is no will. Further, to admit these two pieces of disconnected papers as the will of M. T. would open the door too far for fraud and imposition. 

See 153 P. 767 (Ariz.). 

Other examples: Suppose T dies and in a sealed envelope there are found four pieces of paper which appear to have been cut from the provisions of a completed will. On one piece is a paragraph stating an intention to make a will, on another designated "First" debts are directed to be paid, on another "Eighth" is a residuary clause and on another are T's signature, an attestation clause and signatures of witnesses. With no relationship between these pieces of paper and no internal coherence in sense they should not be integrated as T's will. It would open the door wide to fraud. "Any evil-minded person might, with comparative ease, take a will, and, by cutting from it sections or paragraphs, entirely defeat the object of the testator." 

See 108 A. 614.

Suppose T dies leaving four sheets of paper on which he has written what is offered as his will for probate. The top page is signed and if it is placed under the other three there is continuous literary sense from beginning to end and at the end of each of the first three pages there is no period or end of sentence but the sentence continues on in sense from the bottom of the preceding page. These four sheets being obviously connected as a single instrument should be admitted to probate as an integrated whole as T's will. 

See In re Maginn's Estate, 122 A. 264, 278 Pa. 89.

CASE 21 

T makes a first will giving Blackacre to A. He makes a second will giving Whiteacre to B. He makes a third will giving 

Brownacre to C. He makes a fourth will giving Greenacre and all of the residue of his property to D. T dies. All of the above wills are offered for probate. Which should be admitted? 

The answer is all four. Here are four wills wholly consistent one with the other and none containing a revocatory clause. 

This being true all four wills constitute the last will and testament of T. 

Atk.2d p. 380.

CASE 22 

T died testate providing in his will, "I give the rest, residue and remainder of my property to the persons who have been in my employ not less than ten years at the date of my death."

This will was made in the year 1915. T died in 1945. There were five employees who answered the description in the will and all of them had been employed by T since the making of his will. May the five employees take the residue?

The answer is yes. Here is a non-testamentary act of T, the testator, such act being the employing of these five employees. 

The act is not executed by signing or witnessing as required by the wills statute and is therefore non-testamentary. But the five beneficiaries are designated by an act which has significance ordinarily apart from designating them as persons to take under the will. Ordinarily an employer employs persons to work for the purpose of getting a job done or accomplishing a purpose. 

Hence, when T hired these five employees by a non-testamentary act having such independent significance, they are properly residuary beneficiaries under T's will.

6. Revocation of wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A will is always ambulatory which means that it is always subject to alteration or revocation by a competent testator. 

2. Revocation may be effected in three ways (a) by subsequent instrument (b) by physical acts of burning, tearing, canceling, obliterating or destroying (c) by certain changes in the testator's condition. 

3. Revocation by subsequent instrument may be either by (a) a later will (b) codicil or (c) revocatory instrument executed with like formalities as a will but which disposes of no property. 

4. No revocation is effective without animus revocandi-intent to revoke-expressed or implied. 

5. Dependent relative revocation means revocation on a condition or conditional revocation. 

6. If a will cannot be found at the testator's death and it was last known to be in his possession it is presumed that he revoked same but if it cannot be found and was last known to be in the possession of a third person then there is no such presumption. 

7. The contents of a lost and unrevoked will may be proved by parol evidence.

CASE 23 

T made a good will January 5, 1940 which gave all of his property to his friend, A. On June 1, 1947 he made a will which provided, "I give all of my property to my cousin, B, and 

I hereby revoke all former wills by me made." T died June 30, 1947. Both wills were offered for probate. Which, if either, should be admitted?

The answer is the will of June 1, 1947. Whether a subsequent will revokes a former will by words used is often a question of construction but when the later will contains a general clause of revocation as does the will of June 1, 1947 in this case, it is usually conclusive that it revokes the former will and should be so in this case. There is a second and quite as substantial a reason in this case for giving effect to the second will only. The first will gives ALL of T's property to A. The second will gives ALL of T's property to B. They are wholly inconsistent one with the other and the latter of the two governs. 

This is revocation by implication or implied revocation based on inconsistency. B should take all of T's property under the will of June 1, 1947. 

95 C.J.S. Wills § 274. 

CASE 24 

T died leaving a witnessed will in which one paragraph gave Blackacre to A. Across that paragraph on the face of the will from the lower left corner to the upper right corner of that paragraph but touching no other part of the dispositive parts of the will, written in T's handwriting and signed by him were the following words, "I hereby revoke this paragraph. 2/17/47. (signed) T." The residuary clause of the will was in favor of 

B. Should the will in toto or all but the paragraph mentioned be admitted to probate?

The answer is that the will without the paragraph giving Blackacre to A should be admitted. This problem raises several questions. First may a witnessed will be revoked by an olo-

graph? Second, may part of a will be revoked without revocation of the entire will? Third, what constitutes cancellation?

Statutes usually require that a revoking instrument be executed with "like formalities" as the will it is designed to revoke. In this respect an olographic instrument is considered as having been executed with like formalities as a witnessed will, the authenticity of the olograph being vouched for by the genuineness of the testator's handwriting and the authenticity of the witnessed will by the witnesses. An oral or nuncupative will is not of such "like formalities" and cannot revoke either an olo graph or witnessed will. The question states that the writing across the paragraph, "I hereby revoke this paragraph. 

2/17/47. (signed) T", was wholly written and signed by T.

The figures 2/17/47 read by people generally are understood to mean February 17, 1947. Hence, it is wholly written, signed and dated in the hand of the testator and is olographic. Had it disposed of Blackacre to another beneficiary it would have revoked the paragraph by implication as being inconsistent therewith. And it would seem that even though it be not a will in the sense that it disposes of property, it should be held to revoke the paragraph unambiguously referred to, for the intention is clear and there is little room for fraud or imposition. Cancellation of a will is accomplished by drawing over its contents criss-cross, vertical or horizontal lines or by writing across such contents, with intention to revoke. Both the act and the intent must co-exist. The writing across the paragraph in this instance would seem to fulfill the requirements of cancellation for the reason that not only is the writing across the entire paragraph but it touches no other part of the will and the intent to revoke appears on the face of the revoking material. Hence, partial revocation of a will being effective, the act done by T in this instance would cancel the paragraph in A's favor either by subsequent olographic instrument or by cancellation. The effect of the cancellation is to cause Blackacre to fall within the provisions of the residuary clause and to pass to B. 

95 C.J.S. Wills §§ 275, 279, 280, 296; Atk.2d 419; 131 

N.E. 330.

John jumped and grabbed the instrument off the fire but only after there had been a singeing of one corner of the paper but no part of the writing had been burned. Hence, the burning here was sufficient and the will should not have been admitted to probate. The act done and the effect in this case would not have constituted a destruction of the will for under such word some part of the writing should have been affected such as tearing it. CASE 26 

T made his will in favor of his brother, A. He then married wife, Mary. T died and A offered the will for probate and 

Mary contended the will had been revoked by the marriage. 

Who should prevail?

The answer is A. The rule was well established at common law that the will of a testator was revoked by both marriage and birth of issue but neither marriage nor birth of issue standing alone would revoke his will. At common law the widow was not an heir but was entitled to dower, will or no will. Hence, it was not material to her whether or not the will was revoked. 

On the other hand marriage alone revoked the will of a single woman for the reason that her marriage incapacitated her from making a will. Where modern statutes make a widow an heir the courts apply the same rule to the wife as to the husband and hold marriage alone does not revoke the wife's will. Under a statute which specifically provides that marriage revokes the will of a testator the courts hold that the word testator includes the word testatrix. 

See 82 P.2d 894; Atk.2d pp. 424, 426. (That divorce accompanied by a property settlement also revokes the testator's will by operation of law see 119 N.W. 219; 

Atk.2d 431.).

CASE 27 

T executed his will giving all his property to A. He left the original will with his brother, B, and a copy thereof with his lawyer, L. B's house burned and all its contents including T's will was destroyed. A few days later T died. A went to L's office and requested L to petition the probate court for admission to probate of T's will. L complied with the request alleging that T died testate and offered the copy in L's possession to prove the contents of T's will. B was the only heir of T and objected to the probate of the will and claimed T died intestate. 

Should the will be admitted?

The answer is yes. This question involves the probate of a lost will. When a person dies and it is shown that he made a will and that such will was last in his possession and it cannot be found upon his death there is a presumption that he destroyed it with intent to revoke it, animus revocandi. But when such will was last known to be in the hands of a third person, then such presumption cannot obtain. In this case it being shown that the will of T was last in the hands of B, of course that presumption cannot obtain. At common law a lost will, one which existed and has not been revoked but cannot be found, could be admitted to probate like any other will but its contents in substance had to be proved clearly and convincingly. 

In such case the burden is on the proponent. In this case the copy can be shown in proof of the contents of the original will. 

There are statutes which require that the lost will must have been in existence at the death of the testator or that it was fraudulently destroyed. 

See Atk.2d p. 506; Johson v. Kroc, 194 N.W. 633, 156 

Minn. 253, 34 A.L.R. 1300, 1304 note.

CASE 28 

T executed his will giving all of his property to A. Later he executed a second will giving all of his property to B and revoking all former wills. This second instrument was then destroyed by T with animus revocandi but with the expressed intention on T's part that the first will in A's favor be effective. 

T died and A offered said first will for probate which was contested by T's heirs and by B. Which should prevail?

The answer is A should prevail. This is purely an arbitrary answer for the reason that one can also justify a decision either for B or for T's heirs by both reason and authority. For A it may be said that the very nature of a will is that it is never effective till death. Then T's revoking will did not completely destroy his first will because the second will itself had not been made effective by the death of T. Such revoking will then merely made the first will dormant while both wills were extant and T lived. But when T destroyed the second will with ani·

mus revocandi, that will was rendered totally ineffective as though it had never been. The first will was then awakened by removal of the sleeping potion and became effective as the last will of T when T died. Some cases deny this result in the absence of an expressed intent on the part of the testator that the first will shall be effective but in this instance such expressed intent is given as a fact and parol evidence is admissible to establish such. B's case would rest upon the doctrine of dependent relative revocation-sometimes called conditional revocation. 

He would argue that the second will with its revocatory clause completely and effectively killed off will number one; that T revoked will number two only on condition that will number one were thereby made effective; that the condition having failed, will number two is still T's will and hence, B, the beneficiary thereunder should take. The heirs of T would argue that will number one had been effectively revoked beyond recall by the revocatory clause of will number two; that will number two was effectively revoked by destruction with animus revocandi and that will number one could not be brought back to potential existence as a will by a mere expression of intention that such be the case but that a complete re-execution thereof would be necessary to comply with the statutes. The result would be, say they, that T died intestate. 

Atk.2d p. 452; Onions v. Tyrer, Ch. 1717, 2 Vern. 742; 

House of Lords, [1924] A.C. 653; Atk. 386, 414. 

7. Republication and revival of wills.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Republication is used to describe (a) the validating of a former invalid will, (b) the reaffirming of an earlier will by a later will or codicil and making the earlier will speak as of the date of the reaffirming instrument. 

2. When will number one has been revoked by will number two and will number two is then revoked, it is said that will number one is thereby revived.

3. In the common law courts in England such was the rule but in the ecclesiastical courts will number one was not revived unless it was shown to be the testator's intention. 

4. In the United States each doctrine has its following. 

See Atk.2d 466; 95 C.J.S. Wills § 299.

CASE 29 

L owned Blackacre and executed a lease thereof to T for the term Jan. 1, 1940 to Dec. 31, 1945. On Nov. 1, 1945 the lease was renewed for the term Jan. 1, 1946 to Dec. 31, 1951. On

June 1, 1945 T executed a will in which he gave "my leasehold on Blackacre to my good friend, A.” On Mar. 1, 1947 T executed a codicil to his will which provided, "In all other respects 

I reaffirm the provisions of my will of June 1, 1945." There was a residuary clause in the will in favor of B. T died May 1, 1947 and the leasehold on Blackacre is claimed by both A and 

B. Who should prevail?

The answer is A should prevail. A general rule of construction is that a will is usually presumed to speak as of the date of the testator's death. But in this case when T made his will there was but one lease, the first one, existent as to Blackacre and he could not have known of the renewal lease. Hence, had there been no codicil there would have been a very effective argument in B's favor that the leasehold to which T referred in his will was non-existent and the renewal lease should fall within the residuary clause. However the codicil, which is a supplement to a will and must always be executed with the same formalities as a will, was executed after the renewal lease was made and it "reaffirmed" the provisions of the will. The legal effect of the codicil was to republish the will as of the date of the codicil at which date the expression "my leasehold" could have re ferred only to the renewal lease. Hence, it passes by the republished will to A and not to B. 

See 95 C.J.S. Wills §§ 298, 299; Atk.2d 468.

CASE 30 

The statute required two witnesses to a will. T executed his will on Mar. 1, 1947 with only one witness in favor of A as sole beneficiary. Later T executed an olographic codicil in which he "reaffirmed the provisions of my will of Mar. 1, 1947." Thereafter T died leaving B his sole statutory heir. 

Both A and B claimed T's property. Which should prevail? 

The answer is A. Here T's will of Mar. 1, 1947 is no will at all for lack of one witness. It is void. The statute must be complied with or there is no will. But when T executed a valid olographic codicil which referred to his earlier void will and reaffirmed the provisions of such will the will is considered as having been republished as of the date of the codicil and the last will and testament of T is the will plus the codicil. Hence, A takes under such last will of T. Strictly speaking there can be no republication when there has been no publication. The will being void was never published at all. This case should be solved by the doctrine of incorporation by reference and will come out to the same result. Here we have a codicil which (a) refers to the will as an extraneous document (b) shows on its face that the will is in existence and (c) specifically identifies it as the paper referred to. Hence, T's last will is the codicil which incorporates the will and makes A the beneficiary therein. But such case as this is solved by the courts under the doctrine of republication of wills. 

See Atk.2d 468; 95 C.J.S. Wills § 303.

CASE 31 

T executed a will giving all of his property to A. Later he executed a second will in which he gave all of his property to B and revoked all former wills. Still later he destroyed the second will with animus revocandi. T died and his property is claimed both by A and by T's heirs. 

Which should prevail?

The answer is A should prevail. By the common law when a second will which revokes a former will is itself revoked or

destroyed animus revocandi, then the first will becomes the last will of the testator. It is revived by the revocation of the second will. The theory is this-that no will becomes effective till death and the second will never having become effective by T's death, never did effectively revoke the first will. Under such theory the first will is effective at T's death and A being the beneficiary takes T's property. Strictly speaking there should be no such thing as a revival of a will because if it is effectively done away with as a will then it can never become a will except by a re-execution or republication in which case it is not revived at all. On the other hand if it was only made dormant by the revoking will which is later revoked, then it is not revived for it was never dead but only sleeping. The cases are not at all in accord on this subject and some statutes require re-execution of a revoked will to make it effective. The ecclesiastical courts required proof of testator's intention to revive the former will. 

See Atk.2d 474; 95 C.J.S. Wills § 301. 

8. Lapse of Legacies and Devises.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. A gift in a will is said to lapse when the beneficiary is living when the will is executed but predeceases the testator. 

2. A gift by will to a person already deceased at the time the will is made is void. 

3. Both void and lapsed gifts pass under a general residuary clause. 

4. Under the modern law there is no distinction between lapsed devises and lapsed legacies. 

5. When a residuary legacy or devise lapses it passes as intestate property.

CASE 32 

T died testate leaving a will which provided that Blackacre should go to A and $1000 should be paid to B and the residue of the property should go to C. Both A and B predeceased T.

T's statutory heirs and C claim Blackacre and the $1000 which was designated for B. Which should prevail?

The answer is C should prevail. When a person dies testate there is a presumption against intestacy either partial or whole. 

It is presumed the testator intended to dispose of all of his property. Furthermore when a testator puts in his will a general residuary clause, such clause is intended to carry all legacies and devises which for any reason have failed or lapsed. In this case the specific devise of Blackacre to A and the general legacy of $1000 to B have lapsed at the death of T for the reason that the intended beneficiaries are deceased and unable to take when the will becomes effective. Under both the rule of construction stated that total testacy is presumed and that the residuary clause in general terms is a "catch-all" intended to carry lapsed legacies and devises, all of T's property passes to C under such residuary clause. T's statutory heirs who would take only by intestacy take nothing. 

See Atk.2d 784; 96 C.J.S. Wills § 1226.

CASE 33 

T executes his will giving Blackacre to A and T's diamond ring to B. Before he dies T sells Blackacre and the diamond ring is lost. The money which T received for Blackacre is traced to a bank deposit. Upon T's death A claims the proceeds from the sale of Blackacre and B claims that the executor of T's estate should pay him the value of the diamond ring. 

Is the claim of either valid?

The answer is no. When T sold Blackacre and received money therefor the devise is said to be adeemed. It is removed from and extinguished as part of T's property and neither it nor the proceeds therefrom which obtain in wholly different form can be claimed under the devise. When T died the diamond ring was wholly nonexistent as part of his property and the legacy thereof to B is adeemed. Both of these cases represent ademption by extinction. The beneficiary of an adeemed legacy or devise can take nothing for the reason that the subject of the legacy or devise just does not exist. This result takes place irrespective of the testator's intention. The test is whether the thing devised or bequeathed still exists as testator's property at his death. 

See Atk.2d 741; 96 C.J.S. Wills § 1226.

CASE 34 

T executes his will giving Blackacre to his daughter, D, and $25,000 to his son, S. Thereafter and before T dies he conveys

Here there was the relationship of parent and child in which case there is a presumption that the parent intended to satisfy the general legacy of $25,000 to the extent of the gift inter vivos. Had there been no such relationship the intention that the gift was so intended by T would have to be proved independently of the gift itself. CASE 35 

T died testate. His will provided as follows: "I give my daughter, A, my piano. I give my daughter, B, $1000. I give my son, C, $1000 and direct that such gift to C be paid out of my account in the First National Bank of City X. All the rest and residue of my property I give to my three children, A, B and C, share and share alike." After making his will T sold his piano for $2300.00 and deposited such money in the State 

Bank of City Y. T drew out all the funds he had in the First 

National Bank of City X and spent it. T left no debts and the $2300.00 in the State Bank constituted his entire net estate after all expenses of administration were paid. How should it be distributed?

The answer is (a) A receives $100.00, (b) B receives $1100.00, and (c) C receives $1100.00. There are three classes of legacies, (1) specific, (2) demonstrative and (3) general. In this case A is given a specific legacy, the piano. 

B is given a general legacy of $1000.00 which is to be paid out of the general assets of the estate, but no particular or specific money is designated. C is given a demonstrative legacy which partakes of the nature of both a specific and a general legacy. It is like a general legacy in that C is given $1000.00 generally with no specific or particular money

designated, but it is like a specific legacy in that it is to be paid out of, but not only out of, a specifically designated account, the account in the First National Bank of City X. If there are insufficient funds in the estate to pay all creditors then these legacies are sacrificed in the following order to pay the decedent's debts, (a) general legacies, (b) demonstrative legacies in excess of the specific property designated to pay same and (c) specific legacies. To the extent that a demonstrative legacy is to be paid out of specific property or a specific fund, such legacy is like a specific legacy. In this case A's specific legacy of the piano was adeemed by T's sale for $2300.00. It ceased to exist as part of T's property by such sale and there is nothing in the estate for A in lieu of such for she cannot follow or claim the proceeds of the adeemed article. She has no right to any part of the $2300.00 by virtue of the specific bequest of the piano in the absence of a provision in the will to that effect and there is no such provision in T's will. B's legacy of $1000.00 is to be paid out of any assets or funds in T's estate. C's demonstrative legacy is to be paid first out of the account in the First National Bank to the extent of the amount in such account. But there is nothing left in such account. But T's will did not provide that C's legacy should be paid only out of the account in the First National Bank. Had it done so, it would have been a specific legacy and would have been adeemed by its withdrawal by T. Consequently C's legacy will be treated wholly as though it were a general legacy and be paid out of any assets available. 

There being only the $2300.00 in the State Bank available, it will be used to pay B's general legacy of $1000.00 and C's $1000.00 as a general legacy and the remaining $300.00, being the residue of the estate, will pass under the residuary clause, $100.00 to each of the children, thus giving A the sum of $100.00, B $1100.00 and C $1100.00. 

See Atk.2d 731.

III. ADMINISTRATION OF  1. The grant of administration. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. One who is authorized by the probate court to administer the estate of a deceased person is called a personal representative, whether appointed by will or by the court. 

2. If the personal representative is appointed by the decedent's will he is called an executor; if appointed by the court when the decedent died intestate he is called an administrator; if appointed by the court to administer the estate according to the terms of the will of the decedent he is called an administrator with the will annexed (in Latin, cum testamento annexo or c.t.a.); if appointed by the court to administer an estate which a prior executor or administrator has not completed he is called an administrator de bonis non or d.b.n. which means administrator of goods not administered. 

3. A special administrator is one appointed by the court to preserve the estate while regular proceedings get under way, e.g. to sell perishables in the grocery store of the deceased. (The proceeding is usually ex parte and his authority ceases automatically when a regular personal representative is appointed and qualifies.) 

4. Every personal representative must give bond to qualify to act unless the statute provides otherwise. 

5. It is the duty of the personal representative (a) to gather together and take possession of the assets of the decedent (b) to pay the decedent's debts and (c) to distribute the balance of the decedent's property to the persons

entitled thereto under the statutes or the terms of the decedent's will. 

6. Usually there is no time limit at which after decedent's death his will must be probated.

CASE 38 

T died intestate leaving Blackacre and a herd of sheep. H was T's sole statutory heir. C was a creditor of T for a large sum of money. H took possession of Blackacre and the sheep and sold them to X. C learned of T's death and petitioned the probate court for letters of administration. H appeared and objected to the granting of letters on the ground that there was no property in the estate left by T but if such objection were overruled H asked that letters be issued to him. What should the court do?

The court should overrule H's objection and appoint H administrator. All property of a decedent passes to the heirs or personal representatives subject to the claims of creditors. 

Hence, X holds such property subject to the claim of C. The court usually appoints one who has an interest in the property to administer the estate of the decedent if he is otherwise qualified and the statutes generally provide for such. Hence, H is preferred as administrator over the creditor, C. It is now H's duty to qualify as administrator by giving bond and to sue X if necessary to recover possession of the assets left by T for the purpose of using them to pay C. X may have a claim against 

H personally but none against him as administrator. One who intermeddles with the property of a decedent without qualifying as personal representative is an executor de son tort and is liable for any damage caused thereby to creditors or to those entitled to the property. H was an executor de son tort when he sold the property to X. 

See Atk.2d 570.

2. Courts having jurisdiction to grant administration.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Any state has jurisdiction to appoint a personal representative of a decedent if the deceased (a) was domiciled in the state or (b) left property within its borders. 

2. Usually the probate court has sole and exclusive jurisdiction to grant administration or appoint the personal representative. 

3. Within the state of his appointment the personal representative may control all of the property of the decedent which is located within its borders including (a) land (b) physical personal property (c) intangibles. 

4. For this purpose land and physical personalty give no trouble. Intangibles which are specialties such as promissory notes, bonds, certificates of corporate stocks and insurance policies made specialties by statute are treated as having their situs where they are physically located, like tangible personal property.

But among other intangibles the situs of a debt is where service can be had personally on the debtor and the situs of corporate stock may be where the corporation is domiciled or where it does business or where the certificate is found, usually the last. 

See Rest.Conf. § 477. 

5. No probate court has jurisdiction to administer the estate of a person unless and until that person is actually dead and any proceeding which attempts so to do is a nullity and absolutely void. 

CASE 39 

A statute in state X provided that one domiciled therein is deemed civilly dead if convicted of a felony and sentenced to a penitentiary in any state and is presumed to be dead if he absents himself and has not been heard from by relatives or friends

for a period of seven years. T had been absent from state X for more than seven years and had not been heard from and he had been sentenced to the penitentiary for life for murder in another state. D was granted letters of administration of 

T's estate in state X and completed proceedings in probate and distributed the property according to statute. Six months after his property was distributed T was pardoned and returned to his home in state X and sued D for trespass and conversion. 

May he recover?

The answer is yes. Neither civil death nor presumption of death nor a finding by the probate court that a person is actually dead will confer jurisdiction on a probate court to issue letters of administration if in fact the person is alive. Death alone confers such jurisdiction. In this case T was alive. Therefore, all probate proceedings involving T's property were absolutely void for want of jurisdiction in the court. Hence, every act which was done by D without lawful authority as to T's property made D a trespasser for which he is liable as a wrongdoer. 

Further, any statute which attempts to confer jurisdiction on a court to deal with property of a living person on the theory that he is dead when in fact he is alive and which gives no protection to the absentee, would take property without due process and is unconstitutional. However, such a statute drawn in the form of a statute of limitations would be valid. 

See leading case Scott v. McNeal, 154 U.S. 34, 14 S.Ct. 1108, 38 L.Ed. 896; 3 N.Y.S. 460; Beckwith v. Bates, 200 N. 

W. 151, 228 Mich. 400.

CASE 40 

T, a resident of state X, was killed by the wrongful act of D in State Y where T owned no property at the time of his death. The answer is no. An action for wrongful death did not exist at common law. It is wholly statutory. When a statute provides that the personal representative is the proper person to sue, it impliedly says also that a court within the jurisdiction has authority to appoint such person. Unless the statute so provides it is immaterial that the decedent was a resident or owned property within the state. Were this not the proper construction of the statute, then the result would be that a right would be created without procedure to enforce it. When such personal representative is appointed and qualified he does not act as an executor or administrator as such but as a statutory trustee and must proceed against the wrongdoer for the benefit of those named in the statute as beneficiaries. In the role of such trustee he may sue the wrongdoer any place he may find him whether he be within or outside of state Y, because the cause of action is a transitory one and belongs to him.

3. Necessity for probate of will and of administration. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. Probate of a will means the proving thereof in the probate court. 

2. Every will must be admitted to probate in the probate court before it is admissible in evidence for any purpose in any other court. 

3. To establish his title to personal property under a will the legatee must prove (a) the will (b) the appointment and qualification of a personal representative thereunder and (c) distribution of the personal property to the legatee. 

4. To establish his title to real property the devisee must prove the will in the probate court.

5. Note carefully that one may have title without probate of the will but one cannot prove his title without probate of the will. 

6. To protect unpaid creditors of the decedent and to prevent his debtors from paying twice there must be probate of the will in case of testacy and there must be regular administration proceedings in case of intestacy. 

7. Many estates of deceased persons are never probated but probate proceedings are absolutely necessary (a) to prove title to personal and real property (b) to protect unpaid creditors of the decedent and (c) to protect debtors of the decedent. 8.

Gifts inter vivos or mortis causa, living trusts, creation of joint estates in personalty or realty and other devises may avoid probate proceedings but these devises serve different purposes and quite different effects flow therefrom. 

See Atk.2d 503; Blood v. Kane, 29 N.E. 994, 130 N.Y. 514, 15 L.R.A. 490 note.

CASE 41 

T died testate bequeathing all of his personal property to X. 

Among the items of such personalty was a herd of sheep in the possession of D. Without probating the will X demanded the possession of the sheep from D which D refused whereupon X sued D in replevin and offered in evidence the unprobated will as evidence of his right to possession of the sheep. May X recover?

The answer is no. In the first place the unprobated will is not admissible in evidence for the reason that the probate court alone and exclusively has jurisdiction to determine will or no will. So the courts of general jurisdiction, law and equity, will not receive in evidence as proof of title or right to possession an unprobated will. It is not always necessary that a will be probated in order for title to vest in a beneficiary but there can be no legal proof of that title without the will be first admitted

to probate. So in this case in order for X to prove his title and right of possession it is absolutely necessary that he first prove the will in the probate court. Even if he did prove the will and that he was a legatee thereunder, still he would have neither title nor right of possession to the sheep for the reason that an executor named in the will or an administrator with the will annexed would be appointed and qualify and would have both title and right of possession of the sheep as of the date of T's death. The rule therefore is this: probate of a will is absolutely necessary to prove title to personal property in a legatee under that will. 

See Atk.2d 505; 51 A. 674. Likewise, for a devise of real property under a will to prove his title he must first probate the will. Id.

CASE 42 

T died intestate leaving two sons, X and Y, as his sole statutory heirs. D owed T on an open account the sum of $100 at

The demurrer should be sustained. The reason is plain. 

While facts well pleaded are taken to be true on demurrer, the facts here show that there is no administration in the probate court which has exclusive jurisdiction to appoint an administrator who is the only person in law entitled to own and collect a debt of a deceased person, and the only court having jurisdiction to determine heirship. The real reason that these heirs cannot collect is that administration in the probate court is the only way by which the unpaid creditors of the deceased

T died intestate leaving Blackacre and $1000 in cash. statute half of her property passed to her husband, H, and the other half to her children, A and B, both of whom were adults and sui juris. First, all property of the decedent passes to the statutory heirs subject to the payment of debts so in this case H, A and B took their shares of the $1000 subject to paying T's debts, if any, and the purchaser of Blackacre held that property subject to the payment of T's debts. If, however, (a) there are no debts of the decedent, (b) all the heirs are sui juris and (c) they do make an agreement relative to the division of the estate of the deceased ancestor, such agreement is binding on them. The legalistic reasoning as to the personal property is this, there being no debts an administrator would mere-

Such is the reason for the title to the money in this case being in H, A and B. Here, the family settlement dispensed with the necessity of administration. But it should be noted that while the purchaser of Blackacre, P, there being no debts of T, has good title to Blackacre, he has no record title thereof because on the record there is a break in the chain of title between T on the one hand and H, A and B on the other who conveyed to P.

4. Title of personal representative. 

LEGAL GEMS 

1. When a person dies testate or intestate the title to his personal property vests in the personal representative when appointed and qualified and relates back to the instant of death. 

2. When the personal representative distributes the personal property, after payment of decedent's debts, to the legatees in case of testacy and to the next of kin or statutory heirs in

case of intestacy, then title vests in the legatees and statutory heirs respectively. 

3. When a person dies testate the title to his real property vests in his devisees subject to the right and duty of the personal representative to take possession thereof and use for the purpose of paying debts. 

4. When a person dies intestate the title to his real property vests in his heirs subject to the right and duty of the personal representative to take possession thereof and use for the purpose of paying debts. 

5. In general at common law all contract actions and causes of action ex contractu except breach of promise to marry survive the death of the owner and pass to the personal representative. 

6. In general at common law all tort actions and causes of action ex delicto with the possible exception of trover, die with the person and do not survive in favor of the personal representative. Statutes enlarge the scope of actions which survive. 

See 55 Me. 142; Atk.2d 641, 683. 

7. A cause of action for wrongful death never belonged to the decedent so could not survive; it is created by statute and generally the personal representative is made statutory trustee for specific dependents of the decedent and the recovery is not part of the estate subject to the claims of creditors. 

See Mahoning Valley Ry. Co. v. Van Alstine, 83 N.E. 601, 77 Ohio St. 395, 14 L.R.A., N.S., 893 note; Atk.2d 642.

E cannot use any funds or property recovered in such suit for the benefit of the decedent's heirs or next of kin.

the A statute provided that "actions for damages for injuries to person shall not abate by reason of the death of the defendant". P sued D for (a) libel and slander and (b) for interfering with P's right of privacy, because D had published without his consent a picture of P in connection with a crime story. 

During the pendency of the action D died and P sought to substitute D's administrator in place of D as defendant in the action. May he do so under the above statute?

The answer is yes as to right of privacy and no as to libel and slander. It should first be noticed that the word used is "action", not cause of action. Action means that the machinery to enforce a right has been set in motion. The action had been brought so that part of the statute is satisfied. Is a suit for libel and slander an "injury to the person"? Is a suit for right of privacy an "injury to the person"? At common law both of these actions would have died with the death of either plaintiff

or defendant before judgment. So if either survives it must be on account of the quoted statute. While libel and slander may cause mental disturbance of the plaintiff, yet the essence of such action is injury to one's character and reputation as an asset. 

In that sense the count for libel and slander is not an "injury to the person" and would not survive under the statute. So as to that count the administrator could not be substituted for his decedent. On the other hand an interference with one's right of privacy is in essence an injury to one's sensibilities, his mental processes and feelings and one's mind is as much a part of his person as are his arms and legs. Hence, in that sense the count for the right of privacy is an "injury to the person' under the statute and survives the death of the defendant. It was so held in Reed v. Real Detective Publishing Co., 162 P.2d 133 (Ariz. 

1945). See too Atk.2d 641, 683.

CASE 46 

A statute provided that "any person, or the personal representative of any person, may maintain an action against the executor or administrator of any testator or intestate who in his lifetime has wasted, destroyed the property of any such person

The words "goods and chattels❞ had been used in the prior statute but the legislature substituted the word "property" in their stead. H had a wife, W, and minor children, A and B. S murdered H and then committed suicide. E was appointed the administrator of S's estate and 

W, A and B brought suit against E to recover not for wrongful death of H but for destruction of "property" by S under the Do these facts constitute a cause of action under the statute. statute?

Ans. Yes. It will be noticed that in this case the statute says a personal representative may maintain an action which means that the action can be commenced after the death of the wronged person. It also says that the action can be maintained against the personal representative of the wrongdoer which means that the cause of action survives the death of the wrongdoer. When the legislature changed the statute from "goods and chattels❞ to "property" it meant to enlarge the scope of causes of action

which survive. So the question is, does the relationship between husband and father on one side and wife and children on the other, together with the material advantages and support which flow therefrom, constitute "property". If it does, then 

H, A and B have stated a cause of action which survives against E. It was so held by the Supreme Court of California in Hunt v. Authier, 169 P.2d 913 (1946). 

5. Territorial limitations on powers of personal representative.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. An executor or administrator as such can exercise no powers beyond the boundaries of the state wherein he is appointed. 

2. If a personal representative is appointed in 

State X he can act as such in State Y only by virtue of a statute in State Y. 

3. If a decedent dies domiciled in State X and leaves property in States X and Y, usually administration is necessary in each state. 

4. The administration in X is called primary or domiciliary administration and in Y is called ancillary administration. 

5. Statutes usually permit the domiciliary personal representative to act as ancillary administrator but this is not true where the latter is required to be a resident. 

6. The principal purpose of ancillary administration is to assure the payment of the debts of the decedent to the local creditors. 

7. The ancillary administrator's duties are to (a) collect decedent's assets (b) pay the local creditors and (c) distribute the remaining assets to the domiciliary personal representative. 

8. If a will is admitted to probate at the decedent's last domicile the validity of the will is

conclusive in all jurisdictions as to personalty. 

9. The validity of a will concerning realty is determined by the state in which the land is located.

CASE 47 

T died domiciled in State X and E qualified as his executor in State X. At his death T owned an automobile in State Y. 

E conceived it to be his duty to gather together the assets of T wherever they were and demanded the car in State Y from D who had possession thereof for T. D refused and E brought action in replevin to get possession of the car in State Y. May he recover?

The answer is no. The cloak of authority conferred on E by 

State X through its probate court is coextensive with the borders of State X. The order appointing E had no extraterritorial effect. To make his case E would have to say, "I am suing as executor". Hence, the rule that title to the decedent's personal property passed to his personal representative is limited in its application to the state wherein the personal representative is appointed. The real reason why E cannot recover the car in 

State Y, however, is not one of title or lack of it but the desire of State Y to protect local creditors of T who reside in State Y. 

If E could recover the car and take it to State X, then local creditors of State Y, would have their claims subjected to the laws of State X and if it turned out that T's estate were insolvent, then T's creditors who lived in State Y would have their claims paid only after T's creditors residing in State X were paid in full. 

See Patchen v. Wilson, 4 Hill 57 (N.Y.1842); Rest.Conf. § 

507; Stumberg on Conflicts of Laws 410; Goodrich on 

Conflicts of Laws 2d ed. 478 et seq.

CASE 48 

T died domiciled in State X and E qualified as his executor. 

E discovered that D, a resident of State Y owed T $100 on a promissory note to T's order in E's possession and also $100 on an open account. E's lawyer advised him that he could sue 

D on the note in any state because he is the owner thereof but that he could not maintain an action on the open account because its situs is where he can get service of process on D, but that E cannot sue thereon beyond the borders of state X. D, however, came into State X wherein E served process on him both as to the note and as to the open account. Can E maintain the action?

The answer is yes. In such case it may be said that the domiciliary representative has the duty to collect the assets of T's estate within the state of his appointment. The open account being owned by T at his death and the note being an order instrument in the name of T, title to each must be claimed by E in his role as executor. For such purposes it may be said that the situs of both these debts is where service can be had on the debtor. Service being had on D within State X wherein E can claim to have title to such debts by virtue of his appointment as personal representative of T, the suit can be maintained. 

See Goodrich, Conflict of Laws 3d ed. 542, 543; Rest.Conf. §§ 52, 103, 467, 509.

CASE 49 

T, domiciled in State X, died and E qualified as his executor. 

Among T's assets was an automobile in State X. Sticky Fingers stole the auto after T's death and drove it into State Y where Fingers lived. E went into State Y and sued Fingers in replevin to recover possession of the auto. May he recover?

The answer is yes. When T died and E qualified as his executor the title and right of possession to the car passed to E as of the date of T's death. E owned the car even though he had to account for it. When Fingers stole the car he created a cause of action in State X which T never owned but which belonged

to E. When E sought to recover the car in State Y, he would proceed in the role of owner and not in his representative capacity as executor. He would sue in his role of trustee or legal title holder of the chose in action which came into being after T's death. E need not and should not allege in his complaint that "I am an executor" because in such role his cloak of authority would drop at the border of State X, the state which through its probate court made him executor, and only by statute in State Y could E exercise authority of an executor in State Y. But State Y will recognize E's right in the chose in action to recover the car because the car belonged to E when stolen. 

See Rest.Conf. §§ 483, 508; 188 S.W. 1105; Stumberg,

Conflicts of Laws 412. 

CASE 50 

T died domiciled in State X and E qualified as his executor. 

E took judgment against D in State X on an open account which D had owed to T before T's death. E could find no assets of 

D in State X to satisfy the judgment but knew that D had assets in State Y and that he could get personal service of process on D in State Y. E brought suit on the judgment in State Y and served D personally. May he maintain the suit?

The answer is yes. The original judgment in State X was taken in favor of E as executor in his representative capacity. 

The original debt became merged in the judgment which was then owned by E and by him only. It was never owned by T. 

Therefore when E sues in State Y he does not sue as representative of T but in his role as owner of the judgment. Such contract of record arose after T's death and no one can raise the question of E's authority as executor in the suit in State Y. 

See Goodrich on Conflicts of Laws 2d. ed. 487; Hare v. 

O'Brien, 82 A. 475, 233 Pa. 330; Stumberg, Conflict of 

Laws 412. 

Comment: Suppose in the foregoing case E had a promissory note payable to bearer which he found among T's assets. E could sue in State Y as owner thereof

without mentioning his being executor and what is said above on suit on the judgment would be true here.

CASE 51 

T died domiciled in State X and with property therein. E was appointed and qualified as T's executor in State X. Among 

T's assets were a Ford car in State X and a debt of $1000 owed to T by D who lived in State Y. Towed $500 to C who lived in State Y. K converted the Ford car in State X and drove it into State Y. On these facts C petitioned the probate court of 

State Y for letters of ancillary administration in State Y. E appeared and objected to C's petition on the ground that the probate court had no jurisdiction because there was no property left by T in State Y. Is the objection good?

The answer is no. There must be assets of the deceased in the state to invest the probate court with jurisdiction to appoint an ancillary administrator. Did T die with assets in State Y in the facts given? Surely the Ford car did not constitute assets of T in State Y for the reason that at T's death that car was in State X and became the property of E and he could replevy it from K in Y without suing in his representative capacity but as owner thereof. On the other hand the situs of the $1000 debt owed by D to T was in State Y. No collection of that debt could be made without service on D in State Y. On this ground the probate court in State Y would have jurisdiction to grant letters of ancillary administration to C. Upon C's being appointed and qualified he could collect the $1000 debt from D and give D a good discharge for the payment thereof. He could then deduct the expenses of administration and his own debt of $500 and distribute the balance to E in State X. 

See In re Cornell's Will, 196 N.E. 396, 267 N.Y. 456.

6. Revocation of administration and effect thereof.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The probate court has original and exclusive jurisdiction to determine whether a decedent died testate or intestate and to appoint a personal representative, whether executor, adininistrator or administrator with the will annexed. 

2. Once being properly appointed and qualified as such, the acts of a personal representative within the scope of his authority are valid and binding until his letters are revoked. 

3. If such personal representative sells property of the estate to a purchaser the purchaser is protected in his purchase. 

4. If one who owed a debt to the decedent pays the debt to such personal representative, such debtor is protected in such payment. 

5. If the letters testamentary or of administration of the personal representative are revoked and a new one is appointed and qualifies, such new one cannot undo the acts of his predecessor either as to sales or as to the collection of debts owed the decedent. 

6. But the newly qualified personal representative can compel return of property of the estate which the former personal representative has distributed to the next of kin or legatees who are, of course, mere volunteers, provided such next of kin or legatees have not changed their positions. 

See Atk.2d 502, 800.

CASE 52 

T died and A was appointed and qualified as his administrator. In performing his duties A collected a debt of $100 which K owed to T and sold to R a herd of sheep which was part of T's estate. A paid T's debts and distributed the remain-

ing property to T's statutory heir, J. Thereafter E found and proved a will which T had executed and within the allowed statutory period asked that A's letters of administration be revoked, and that letters testamentary be issued to E. This the court did. T's will gave all his property to H. E then demanded that K pay to E the $100 debt which he owed T and that R return the sheep to E as part of T's estate and demanded possession of all the assets which had been distributed to J. All refused E's demands. May E enforce his demands against any of the three?

The answer is, he may not enforce his demands against K or R but he may do so in equity against J provided J has not changed his position. First, on this set of facts the court had jurisdiction to determine will or no will. It decided there was no will and appointed A. A was then the legal personal representative of T and had power to act as such. He performed his duty when he collected from K the $100. It being his duty and he having authority to collect he could and did discharge K from further liability when he accepted payment from K of the debt he owed T. Hence, K need not pay a second time. 

Also A being the qualified administrator had title to and could pass title to the sheep and did so to R, assuming of course that R had no knowledge of the will. Hence, R may keep the sheep. But the case of J is different. He is a mere volunteer in the sense that he has given no value for the goods distributed to him. Hence, he must hold in trust for E such goods as were distributed to him and which he still possesses. If, however, he has sold the goods and spent the proceeds he would have a good defense to that extent on the ground that he has changed his position. Were these holdings not correct then there would be a constant cloud on the titles of property purchased or received through a decedent and the value of estates would be quite unprotected. Hence, the rule is that if the probate court has jurisdiction and determines that there is a will or no will and appoints a personal representative, such appointment is effective until revoked and the acts of the personal representative in the interim are valid and binding. 

See Atk.2d 627; Harrison v. Carter, 36 S.E.2d 700, 226 N.C. 36.

7. Contracts of executor or administrator.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The personal representative, like a trustee, is personally liable on his contracts. 

2. The personal representative is entitled to reimburse himself from the assets of the estate for reasonable expenditures made for the estate. 

3. Note carefully that debts of the estate are debts owed by the decedent while he lived whereas expenses of administration are obligations undertaken by the personal representative for the estate after the decedent died. 

4. The reasonableness of expenditures by the personal representative for the estate is determined by the discretion of the probate court. 

5. Reasonable amounts for decedent's funeral, for preservation of estate assets, for attorney's fees and other costs of administration may be deducted from the estate by the personal representative. 

6. Expenses of administration have priority of payment over debts of the decedent. 

7. The personal representative is bound to perform an impersonal contract of the decedent, e.g. a promissory note to pay money, but need not perform a purely personal contract, e.g. try a lawsuit. 

8. The personal representative has no authority to carry on the business of the decedent unless empowered expressly so to do by the will, by consent of the interested parties or by order of the court. 

9. The personal representative has title to the personal property of the estate and power to sell, pledge or mortgage same.

10. The personal representative under court order has power to sell the real property of the estate if necessary to pay the debts of the decedent.

CASE 53 

T died owning Blackacre but insolvent. E qualified as executor and was authorized to sell Blackacre. E listed the property for sale with broker, P. P sold the land and entered his claim for commissions with E who rejected the claim. P then brought suit against E to establish his claim and the court ordered it paid "in the due course of administration". From this order P appealed contending that his claim was not a debt to be paid "in the due course of administration" but an expense of administration having priority. Should P prevail?

The answer is yes. A debt of the estate is one which was incurred by the decedent while he lived. These are paid in the "due course of administration". An expense of administration is one with which the decedent had nothing to do and which has been incurred by the personal representative after the decedent's death. The distinction between the two is unimportant if the estate is solvent and can pay both. But when, as in this case, the estate is not solvent, then it is important to distinguish expenses of administration from debts because the former has priority of payment. Expenses of administration must be all paid before the creditors of the decedent get anything. In this case it is obvious that E employed P to sell 

Blackacre. E is personally liable for the commissions agreed to be paid. T was dead when this obligation was undertaken. 

Hence, this is an expense of administration and should be paid as such and prior to the payment of debts of the decedent which are paid "in due course of administration". 

See Garver v. Thoman, 135 P. 724 (Ariz.).

CASE 54 

T contracted to build a house for P but died when the building was only partially completed. A qualified as T's administrator and P demanded that A complete the house which T had agreed to build. A refused and P brought suit in equity for specific performance to which A answered that P had contracted with T because he relied on the personal skill of T as a builder to do a good job. P replied that the plans and specifications for the building were definite and that he was indifferent who built the house. On the trial P established these allegations of his reply. Should P prevail?

The answer is yes. If a decedent makes a contract which involves his personal skill or ability the parties presuppose that he will live and perform. But in such case his death terminates liability on the contract, and his personal representative has no obligation to complete it. On the other hand if the personal element has been squeezed out and there are mere things to be accomplished which another can as well do as the decedent and the parties did not intend that the decedent alone should perform, then death does not terminate the obligation and the personal representative must perform. Usually building contracts are of this latter class. In the case at hand when P proved that he was indifferent as to who built his house and that the plans and specifications were definite and certain, he made out his case for specific performance against A. When A undertakes performance of the building contract he will be personally liable for all labor and materials procured by him after T's death. To the extent that he uses good business judgment and yet sustains a loss on the performance, he may be reimbused for such out of the assets of the estate. 

See Atk. 2d 657.

8. Claims against the estate.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The property or estate left by a decedent is subject to the payment of (a) family allowances (b) funeral expenses (c) costs of administration and (d) decedent's debts and, if insolvent, usually in that order by statute. 

2. Debts are matured if they are due at the time of decedent's death and, perhaps, if due before the time expires for presenting them to the personal representative. 

3. Debts are unmatured if they are certain to become due but are not due by the time the creditor is required to present them to the personal representative. 

4. Debts are contingent if it cannot be ascertained at decedent's death whether they will ever become due. 

5. Statutes often provide that "all claims, whether due, not due or contingent" must be presented to the personal representative by a certain time or be "forever barred". 

6. Such statutes are called statutes of "nonclaim" and a claim which is not presented within the period provided is not only barred procedurally but it ceases to exist legally. 

7. Neither the personal representative nor the court has the power to allow such a claim after the nonclaim statutory period has elapsed. 

8. If the general statute of limitations has run on a debt of the decedent before his death the personal representative should not pay such claim and if he does he cannot charge the estate therewith. 

9. A secured creditor may collect the value of the security without presenting his claim to the personal representative but will lose the excess of the claim, if any, over the value of

the security if he does not do so within the period of nonclaim. 

10. An heir, legatee or devisee who receives mortgaged property is entitled to have the personal representative pay the debt, out of the general assets, if the deceased owed it and have the property free of the mortgage, unless the will or statute indicates otherwise. 

11. Overpayment made to creditors, legatees or distributees may be recovered by the personalrepresentative on the ground of mistake of fact. 

CASE 55 

T died July 1, 1947 owing two notes to P, one for $100 due 

June 1, 1947 and the other for $200 due Feb. 1, 1948. A was made administrator and published notice to creditors for the first time on Sept. 1, 1947. The statute of nonclaim provided that "all claims" against the estate of a decedent must be in writing and verified and presented to the personal representative within four months of the date of the first publication of notice to creditors. Shortly after A qualified as administrator 

P spoke to A about the notes which T owed to P and T said, "We'll take care of all of the claims in due time". On January 15, 1948 P presented written verified claims for the amounts on the two notes to A which claims A rejected as being too late. 

P brought suit immediately to establish the validity of his claims against the estate of T. May he recover?

The answer is no. The purpose of the nonclaim statute is to enable the personal representative to settle up the decedent's estate within a reasonable time. It requires "all claims" against the estate to be presented within the four months period after first publication of notice to creditors. "All claims" includes both matured and unmatured claims. Such statutes compel creditors to act in some instances when they would have no right to proceed were the decedent living. Furthermore, the effect of such statutes is not only to bar the claims not presented but actually to wipe them out as claims. Hence, the personal representative cannot waive the effect of failure to make proper

1 presentation of the claims under the statute. In this case the oral statement of A to P is immaterial even though it may have had the effect of lulling P to sleep. The statute required that the claims be presented in writing and verified. Under such statute an oral claim is ineffective. Furthermore, the time for presentation of claims expired four months from Sept. 1, 1947 which would make Jan. 1, 1948 the last day for presenting claims unless such day be a holiday in which case Jan. 2, 1948 would be the last day. The fact that one note was due before 

T's death and the other was not due until Feb. 1, 1948 is immaterial. Under the statute requiring "all claims" to be presented within four months both the claim which was due and the one which would become due must be presented within the period or both are lost forever to the creditor. P has failed to comply with a mandatory statute and he is without redress Such is the harshness of the nonclaim statute. Such is the law 

See Halloran-Judge Trust Co. v. Heath, 258 P. 342, 70 Utah 

124; Latham v. McClenny, 285 P. 684, 36 Ariz. 337 (1930).

CASE 56 

T died and A qualified as his administrator and found that T's total property at death in excess of costs of administration consisted of $1000.00 to his credit in B bank. A published notice to creditors and X presented a claim for $400 and Y presented a claim for $400. A thought the estate was solvent so paid the claims of X and Y in full. On the last day when claims might be presented Z presented a claim for $1200, to pay which A had only $200 left. After some figuring A learned that T had left $1000 with which to pay $2000 of debts and that A should have paid each creditor 50% of his claim. A demanded that X and Y each pay back to him $200 which each refused to do. A brought suit against each for $200. May he recover?

The answer is yes. A made a mistake of fact in concluding that T's estate was solvent. The excess over 50% which A paid to each of the creditors, X and Y, was paid under such

mistake. Under the circumstances A had no right to pay 100% to any creditor and the creditors had no right to receive such. 

Hence, A has the right to have X and Y make a refund of $200 each because of the mutual mistake of fact or for money had and received. Upon recovery of $200 from each A will have $400 to add to the $200 he had left after paying X and Y which will leave him $600 in all with which to pay 50% of Z's claim of $1200. Thus each creditor would receive 50% of his claim. 

See Wolf v. Beaird, 15 N.E. 161 (Ill.1888); Atk.2d 800.

CASE 57 

T purchased Blackacre from G for $10,000 and paid for same by giving $5000 in cash and executing to G his note and mortgage on same for $5000. He died testate devising Blackacre to X and the residue of his estate to Y. A qualified as administrator, paid all the debts except the $5000 note to G, and had $15,000 cash left in his hands as residue. Y insisted that the entire residue of $15,000 be paid to him. X insisted that $5000 of the residue be used with which to pay the note to G which would exonerate Blackacre from the mortgage. 

Should X or Y prevail in the argument?

The answer is X. It is the duty of the personal representative to pay the debts of the decedent. The note which T made to G was a debt and should be paid like any other debt unless T's will or a statute provides otherwise. The mortgage is only an incident to the debt which is the principal thing. Of course, the effect of paying the debt will be that Blackacre is exonerated and the devisee X will have $5000 more in value whereas Y, the residuary beneficiary, will have $5000 less in value. But the rule of the common law was that the heir, devisee or legatee of mortgaged property has the right to have such property exonerated from the burden of the mortgage debt. Such debt must be paid by the personal representative out of property in his hands in the following order (a) undisposed of property if a will (b) residuary property. The doctrine is not a favored one and leads some courts to hold that property available for

general legacies should not be used to exonerate mortgaged property. 

See Atk.2d 764; Hill v. Hill, 122 A. 818, 95 N.J.Eq. 233, 29 A.L.R. 1242, 1246 note. 

9. Final decree of distribution.

LEGAL GEMS 

1. The decree of final distribution is a judgment and is conclusive of the rights of the legatees, devisees and heirs, subject only to the right of appeal or relief for fraud. 

2. The decree of final distribution finds the persons entitled and their respective shares under the will and determines heirship and the respective shares under the statutes of distribution. 

4. The decree is res judicata both as to persons to take and the shares of each even if there is obvious error. 

5. The decree is not subject to collateral attack. 

CASE 58 

T died testate and in his will was this provision, "I give to my son, A, an undivided one third of my property, to my son, B, an undivided one third of my property, to my grandson, X, an undivided one sixth of my property and to my grandson, Y, an undivided one sixth of my property". There were no debts, no other legatees or devisees and no pretermitted heirs. M was appointed and qualified as administrator with the will annexed and gave proper notice of final settlement of account of the estate which provided for distribution of the assets which consisted of $8000 in cash as follows: "an undivided one fourth of such $8000 to each of following persons, to wit: A, B, X and Y". A hearing was had and an order settling the account was made in accordance with such provision. A decree of final distribution was made which provided that $2000 was dis-

tributed to each of the legatees, A, B, X and Y. The administrator made out his check to each of such persons for $2000 and each being sui juris endorsed and cashed his check. There was no fraud and the time for appeal for such decree was six months. One year after the date of the final decree of distribution A discovered that his check should have been for one third of $8000 or $2666.67 instead of $2000 and brought suit against M, X and Y seeking relief. The defendants set up res judicata as a defense. Is the defense good?

Ans. Yes. The final decree of distribution discloses manifest error.

Both A and B should have had $666.67 more than each received and both X and Y received $666.67 more than each should have received. But proper notice of the final settlement was given to A and B and they made no objection. 

Further, after the final decree of distribution was entered each had six months in which to appeal. Neither did so. There being no fraud, when the time for appeal expired the final decree of distribution became res judicata as to the rights of all of these parties even though there was manifest error therein. 

The reasons for such holding are clear. Litigation must be brought to an end and the estates of deceased persons must be finally settled sometime. Furthermore, when the court has jurisdiction to enter the decree and the parties are given their day in court, the decree is and must be binding on the parties. 

In this case there is obvious conflict between the provisions of the will and the provisions of the final decree of distribution. 

When the decree is entered the provisions of the will are merged in the provisions of the final decree of distribution and the will exists no more as an effective instrument and the decree becomes the source of all the rights of the parties. Thus the final decree of distribution is absolutely binding on the legatees, devisees and heirs of T and there can be no relief for A and B who had proper notice and had their day in the probate court which had jurisdiction to enter such decree. 

See Atk.2d 798; Shattuck v. Shattuck, 192 P.2d 229 (Ariz. 1948).
