HISTORY OF THE INSTITUTION 

The Federal Correctional Institution at Texarkana, Texas was built under the Public Works Administration program at a cost of approximately $1,750,000. The purchase of land for the site was made possible through contributions from local citizens of Texarkana. The institution is located approximately six miles southwest of the city, just south of U. S. Highway 59, and covers an area of approximately 693 acres. It was officially opened in 

August of 1940, and the first prisoners arrived that month. 

Texarkana is one of the series of classified institutions which comprise the Federal Prison System. It was built to meet the need for more housing for the ever-increasing thousands of persons convicted and sentenced each year for violations of Federal laws. Originally built to accommodate about 

400 men, the population has often exceeded 500. It is classed as a medium security institution and was originally intended to house men serving sentences of eighteen months or less. However, today, the average length of sentence is approximately 22 months.

Visitors and prisoners entering the institution are amazed at their first view. Beautiful landscaping surrounds the green colored buildings, and everywhere inside the fenced enclosure one sees flowers and shrubbery that add a touch of beauty.

THE PERSONNEL AT TEXARKANA 

All members of the personnel have been examined and are qualified under the high standards established by the U. S. Civil Service Commission for prison work. Most employees enter the service as custodial officers, and promotions are usually made from the ranks. New employees enter a basic training period of from six to eight weeks, and a training program functions at each institution periodically for all employees. Those with special skills and the necessary education for staff positions are given every opportunity to apply their experience and training in related positions in prison work. 

Probably the most important single group of employees is the custodial staff. They are in constant contact with the men in practically every phase of their activity. The relationship maintained between this group and the men determine to a great extent the over-all success and operation of the entire program. Much of the success of a well organized program depends upon the approach of the officers in their dealings with the men. The officers at Texarkana, like those throughout the Federal Prison Service, are understanding men and they realize that the inmates need counseling and guidance in solving their everyday problems. They reaalize that the objective of the program is to teach acceptable social behavior, and they are interested in helping each man develop attitudes and social values which will aid him to adjust properly and return to a free community as a respectable, law-abiding citizen. 84734 -1-

THE MEN IN TEXARKANA 

Approximately three-fourths of the men in Texarkana are over 21 years of age. Most of them are married; many are fathers, and some are grandfathers. They are Protestant, Catholic, Jewish-religious and irreligious. 

They are white, black, and brown. They come from all sections of the United 

States and Mexico, and from cities, farms, and villages. Some are illiterate; some have a grade school education; a few have completed high school; and a small number have had some college education. The majority of the men are in good physical condition. 

The interests of the men vary according to their previous experiences and work history. Some are contented to remain as they are, but many make a sincere effort to improve their education and occupational abilities. 

Many will return to the farm, and these men are given every opportunity to learn the latest in farming practices and methods. Some will enter industry on the outside and they desire to learn more about their choice of trade while here. 2 

SECURITY IS ESSENTIAL 

The primary responsibility of any prison is the security of the men and prevention of escapes. The last escape attempt at Texarkana was in the summer of 1954, when one man successfully scaled the fence surrounding the athletic field but was caught immediately. Very few escapes have been attempted at Texarkana since the opening of the institution. There are no walls around this institution and visitors are surprised to see four or five hundred men on a large athletic field watching a baseball game with only two or three officers supervising and only a fence around the area. Security is not essentially a matter of walls, guns, and clubs, but involves proper techniques in handling men.

While every employee of the institution is responsible for the safe keeping of the men, it is the custodial staff which is primarily charged with this function. The Supervisor of the Custodial Service has the responsibility of assigning the officers to the different details, and the men to work on these details. He has had years of experience in this field, and upon his ability to place the men where they can work best and fit into the over-all program of the institution depends to a large extent the morale and conduct of the population.

Each detail officer is responsible at all times for the custody of the men assigned to him. He is not just a "boss" on the job, but is trained to study his men and to observe their work habits, their ability to get along with others, their attitude toward the institution as a whole, and their efforts to improve their thinking and attitudes toward life on the outside. 

Counseling is a part of every officer's job. The men learn to know the officer; they have confidence in him; and many will discuss their personal problems with him.

The rules and regulations of the institution are few and only those necessary and practical for the proper maintenance of good order and conduct are imposed.

In all institutions there are a few men who fail to observe the rules and regulations of the institution or who have difficulty in adjusting to its close community life. At Texarkana these cases represent only a few, most of whom can be adjusted by counseling and guidance. Regardless of the difficulty, each case is adjusted on the basis of the recognized situation. No weapons are carried inside the institution, but all employees must qualify in physical training and in the use of firearms The living quarters in the institution are small units usually housing from 20 to 35 men. The units are kept in an excellent sanitary condition at all times, and the essential items for comfortable living are furnished. - 4 -

INTRODUCTION FOR THE NEW INMATE 

The first month of incarceration is recognized as the most difficult period for the men. They must learn to adjust to a new way of life which is considerably different from that of a free community, and during this time they are restricted from some of the activities of the institution. The orientation period has a very definite purpose. Each new inmate is given a complete physical examination during this time in order that any diseases or defects may be noted and treated. Each man has a chance to learn the intitution routine and to plan his program with the staff members before classification. He is given psychological and achievement tests, and during the interviews he expresses his choice of work assignment based upon his past work experience or his desire to learn a new trade which he believes will be beneficial to him upon his return to society. 

During the group discussions conducted by staff members, the new inmate receives adequate information about the institution in order that he may adjust satisfactorily to the over-all program. He learns that the institution is a community within itself and that the staff members and officers with whom he comes in contact desire to help him with all possible information. He has every opportunity to ask questions about procedures he does not understand. He wants to know what to do if he feels ill, when -5-

he can write home, who can write to him, how to subscribe to newspapers and magazines, whether he can have a picture of his family sent in, when he can "make commissary", and many other questions which come to each man as he gradually adjusts to his new way of life. Every effort is made to provide a constructive orientation period for the new inmate. He finds a library available for his group only and he is given a limited recreation period each day. He is allowed to attend the movies and other entertainments provided for the general population. Each man has a room of his own in this unit and radio reception is optional. Through his cooperation with the unit officer he soon learns to adapt himself in an acceptable manner to the community in which he will soon become an active member. 

In addition to the group discussions conducted by the staff members, each man is interviewed privately in order to gain a knowledge of his personal history and background; his family status; his educational, vocational, and intellectual attainments and abilities. It is on the basis of these findings, and his future plans, that each man's institutional program of custody, work, and training is determined. 

CLASSIFICATION 

At the end of a 30-day period in the Orientation and Admission Unit, the new inmate appears before the Classification Committee. He should, by this time, be thoroughly familiar with the institutional procedures and routine. 

He is familiar with the opportunities available for him and he has probably expressed a preference for some particular type of work and training program. The findings and observations of the staff members and the unit officer have been compiled into a Classification Study. Each staff member on the committee has made his recommendation for an institutional program for the man. Out of the variety of analyses of the man's personality, his abilities, and his plans for the future, an individualized treatment program is determined. He is informed at the time what his work assignment will be and what additional activities he will participate in, such as evening school, day school, vocational training, or on-the-job training. The program outlined is believed to be the best one to meet his specific needs and is based upon his preferences insofar as these are compatible with his abilities, his future plans, and the facilities available in the institution. 

It is recognized by the committee that some measure of satisfaction in his routine is essential to each man in maintaining his morale. Classification here is an individualized treatment program, and to be effective the program of every man must be reviewed periodically. Adjustments may have to be made for various reasons.

Following classification, the man is moved into another housing unit of the institution and instructed as to when and where to report to his detail officer to begin his work program. He is now a member of the institutional community and one who will be treated as an individual. 

In his new unit he has more freedom of movement and more privileges after working hours. He may go to the recreation yard after the evening meal and participate in the games or just visit and watch the activities on the yard. He may use the main library and check out books of his choice. 

He may attend school or go to the auditorium after school hours and watch television. And, most important of all perhaps, he may now have visits with his family on week ends. -6-

Ordinarily he will take part in several of the activities of the institution and contribute something worthwhile to his group while learning a trade, improving his education, learning to share with others and making his plans for the future.

No man's program is final at classification, however. Each man, throughout his stay in the institution, has the privilege of requesting interviews with staff members concerning any problem he may have. The individualized treatment program at Texarkana begins when the man enters the institution and does not end until he is released. EDUCATION AND TRAINING 

The majority of prison inmates are usually somewhat deficient in education. While the grade range at Texarkana is from illiteracy to three years of college, about one-fourth of the men here have less than a fifthgrade education. Very few have completed high school, and a very small number have had any college training. The average grade equivalent for the population at Texarkana in 1951 was 5.1, as determined by the Stand- -7-

ford Achievement Tests given to each man who enters the institution. While modern criminologists and penologists do not believe that lack of education is itself a significant factor in crime causation, it is believed to be a related potential in correlation with other factors involved in misconduct. It does represent a handicap in competition with others for jobs in our highly industrialized society of today. 

The Objective 

At Texarkana, every effort is made to aid the men to improve their educational status. We are particularly interested in stamping out illiteracy. The program has been geared to remove common school level deficiencies an to offer an opportunity to all men who wish to do so to complete the requirements for high school equivalency. Through the Texas Education Agency we administer the General Educational Development tests to qualified men who have prepared for such tests for one term or more and, if they successfully pass this battery of tests, they are given a certificate of high school equivalency by the Texas Agency.

Also during these months a .feature movie is shown to the men in the auditorium once each week. Leisure Time Activities 

The institution library consists of approximately 5,000 volumes, of which some 2,500 are fiction and are in continuous circulation. Thirty-six popular magazines and seven newspapers are subscribed for the reading room, and both the library and reading room are open during the entire day and on week ends when the weather prevents outside yard activities. 

Alcoholics Anonymous 

In November of 1951, six men organized an Alcoholics Anonymous program at Texarkana. This organization now consists of 93 regular card members, and meetings are held every Sunday evening in the auditorium. Outside -10-

members in groups from surrounding cities are allowed to attend these meetings with the men. The highlights of the activity are semi-annual and anniversary meetings, at which as many as 100 outsiders are sometimes present. The AA group publishes its own periodical known as the "Theme," composed of articles written by the men here on subjects relating to alcohol and narcotics.

While we cannot rehabilitate the men, we can furnish "tools" and opportunities for rehabilitation and retraining and trust that many will find new courage and new hope through these media for a new way of life in the future.

RELIGION 

Religion is recognized as an essential part of every person's life, and since much of the process of reformation must come from within, it is the chaplain's function to stimulate the men to embark upon a moral and upright life. A criminal record is generally an admission that the individual has rejected, temporarily at least, the basic principles of ethical conduct. 

Many prisoners have had little or no contact with a church, and many of them need spiritual guidance and counseling. 

Part-time Catholic and Protestant Chaplains conduct religious services in the auditorium and educational department every Saturday and Sunday. All men at Texarkana have opportunity for religious worship-here Protestant services are conducted for men of that faith -12-

In addition to regular services, special services are from time to time conducted by the chaplains and by visiting ministers from other communities. 

A member of the educational department staff teaches a Bible study class one night each week, and every Monday evening a Sunday School class meets in the visiting room with one or more members from one of the local churches in charge of the program. 

On different occasions during the year, several nights are reserved entirely for revival meetings in the auditorium with quite a number of outsiders participating. These meetings are well attended and most of the men are sincere in their efforts to gain spiritual knowledge. 

MEDICAL SERVICES 

The medical, dental, and hospital services of this institution, as throughout the Federal Prison System, are under the supervision and direction of the United States Public Health Service. This service provides a trained professional staff for each institution. At Texarkana the staff consists of a Chief Medical Officer, a Chief Dental Officer, and five Medical Technical Assistants. In addition to this staff, there are several local physicians who serve as consultants. $11 The Chief Medical Officer and Medical Technical Assistant making their hospital rounds -13

The hospital at Texarkana is completely modern and well equipped and has a bed capacity for 29 patients. This can be increased in emergencies. 

During the first 30 days in the institution, all men are given thorough physical examinations, blood tests, and the necessary innoculations and vaccinations. If any special medical treatment is indicated at that time, the new inmate is interviewed and a treatment program is planned to meet his needs.

During the course of a year, hundreds of physical examinations are given, a number of men hospitalized, and operations, both major and minor, are performed if indicated and desired.

The Chief Medical Officer is a member of the Classification Committee and plays a part in determining the over-all program of each man. He, or a member of his staff, also participates in the group discussions conducted for the men in the Prerelease Unit and makes weekly sanitary inspections of the institution.

FOOD AND MORALE 

Food is an important part of everyone's life, and in the routine of a prison it is recognized as the greatest factor influencing morale and contentment among the population. This is kept in mind and is of primary importance in planning the meals for the men at Texarkana. 

The culinary section serves from 1,500 to 1,600 meals daily, depending upon the population. These meals are served at six different settings, two for each meal, a necessity due to the smallness of the dining hall in proportion to the population. The culinary staff makes every possible effort to improve the food service within the limitation of a reasonable economy program. The meals are simple but attractive and well balanced to provide the men with all the needs of present-day diet requirements. Variety is said to be the "spice of life" and that is essentially true in the meal preparation at Texarkana. Special holiday meals are more elaborate than the regular meals and include the "extras" which would be found in any home or in the best cafeterias on such occasions. Menus are carefully planned ten days in advance to provide for variation and quality in food selections. 

Sanitation is a "must" in the culinary section, and the men assigned to work in this area take great pride in maintaining the highest possible standards of cleanliness. Regular inspections are made to insure proper sanitation and safety conditions at all times. 

The men in this department are trained and supervised by experienced personnel, and modern equipment is used. Men with a desire to learn such trades as cooking, baking, and butchering have opportunity to do so through the vocational training program offered by the culinary staff members. They give both individual and group training in these fields, and the men learn useful skills which may prepare them for employment when they are released. 

We can readily see how expensive the preparation of these meals would be if all of the food items had to be purchased outright. Without a farm to supplement the needed food supply the cost would be almost doubled.

THE FARM 

The farm at Texarkana comprises 506.30 acres of cultivated land, of which 30.4 acres are given to vegetable production and the balance to field crops and improved pastures. The purpose of the farm is twofold: to produce food for the institution, and to serve as a laboratory for training prisoners in scientific farming. 

The farm is made up of four enterprises: vegetable production, field crops, dairying, and swine production. In 1954 the farm produced 159,128 pounds of vegetables; 21,357 pounds of soybeans; 152,128 pounds of shelled corn; 343,660 pounds of grain and legume hay; and 170 tons of ensilage. The farm also produced 61,227 pounds of dressed pork; 8,505 pounds of dressed beef; and 511,190.20 pounds of milk-and all this in spite of very little rainfall throughout the entire year. -15

Vocational training is an integral part of all farm operations. In 

August of 1946, a full-time vocational training officer was employed and classes were organized in vocational training. Each farm staff member shares the responsibility of inmate training in all skills necessary to do good work on a modern farm. Classes are conducted in field and truck crops; slaughtering and processing meats; farm machinery operation and maintenance; dairying; and swineherd management. 

In 1954, an average of 55 inmates were assigned to the farm, garden, and dairy details and, with the exception of aliens who could not understand English, all were enrolled in one or more classes of vocational training. The purpose of the institution in providing farm training opportunities is to teach the men to do better work while they are here, and to enable them to make a better living on the farm when they return to their home communities. 

The institution dairy is an excellent one. Several of the cows approach, and some exceed, 90 pounds of milk production daily. For several years it has been the policy to exchange surplus bull and heifer calves for stock feed here in the farm states area. These calves have gone directly into many of the local dairy herds as breeders where they have greatly improved the productive capacity of the herds. Inmates learn how to operate modern farm machinery -16

The institutional farm sets an example of progressive and scientific farming in the community. It is a well established practice for county agricultural agents, public school vocational agricultural teachers, and G. I. agricultural instructors of the four states to bring their classes of students here for instructional purposes.

MAINTENANCE 

The person responsible for the proper operation of all facilities of the institution is the Construction and Mechanical Supervisor. Under his supervision the foremen in the different shops, garage, powerhouse, and laundry must keep all machinery in perfect operating condition. In addition, the functions of this section include painting of all buildings, plumbing installation and repair, carpentry work, electrical servicing, welding, lathe work, and operation of the garage and powerhouse.

Mechanical and construction work has a dual role in the life of the institution: the maintenance of all institutional facilities, and on-the-job training in useful employment of inmates. Actual experience in skilled trades and development of good work habits are provided for the inmates assigned to this section and it plays an important role in the treatment program. 

FREEDOM AGAIN 

Statistics show that about 95 percent of all prisoners are eventually released. With this fact in mind, it should be the concern of every prison administration to offer all possible re-educational techniques relative to the transition from the prison community to the free-enterprise economy of civil life. While the primary responsibility of the prison is custody, the program itself should be directed toward the eventual release of prisoners to society. 

Every inmate looks forward to the day of his release. He lives in hopeful anticipation of parole after his eligibility date, which is at the expiration of one-third of his sentence. Approxmately 25 percent of the eligible men at Texarkana are granted parole. A member of the United States Board of Parole visits the institution every three months to interview eligible applicants for parole. Through good conduct, inmates may reduce the time they must actually serve in the institution, and additional good time may be earned through employment in the prison industry or by performing outstanding service on the job indicating an effort in self-improvement in their over-all program. Regardless of how an inmate is released, if before 180 days of his full-time expiration, he will be under the supervision of a United States Probation Officer in the community to which he is released until his full sentence has expired.

Certain conditions are imposed upon all men so released, and a violation of any of these conditions subjects the violator to return to prison to complete his entire sentence.

For many of the men being released from Texarkana, the release problem is a crucial one. They have been removed from society for some time; quite a number of them have been incarcerated for several years. They have -18-

learned to adjust and accept the regimentation of routine prison life. As their release date approaches, these men realize the seriousness of the situation and they are anxious for the opportunity to bring their problems to the attention of the prison staff members and community representatives who can advise and help them solve their immediate problems. 

Prerelease Unit 

In September of 1954, a Prerelease Unit Program was organized at Texarkana for the purpose of offering practical guidance on problems of release. The primary objective of this phase of an over-all program is to aid the inmates soon to be released in their preparation for satisfactory social and employment adjustment. A committee was appointed by the Warden to function as a guiding staff in the operation of the program. The committee has made every effort to provide worthwhile information from qualified sources for the benefit of these men. The men are assigned to the unit only if they desire to participate in the program, and they become eligible during the last 60 to 90 days before their release dates. Every effort has been made to give this unit an atmosphere of freedom and friendliness. The men who Lounge room in the prerelease unit -19-

live there are allowed a few extra privileges, such as additional hours of visiting time and permission to leave their lights on until 11:30 p.m.; they may also eat all three meals on Sundays in the officers' dining room. 

Informal discussions are conducted each week by members of the staff, and frequently persons from other agencies and professional men from the local community are invited to discuss specific phases of the problems of these men. Probably the two most important problems with which they are concerned are employment and supervision. The institution has been fortunate in obtaining the cooperation of the local state employment representatives and probation officers in the area as participants in the prerelease program. 

The Federal Correctional Institution at Texarkana is one of the smaller institutions of the Federal Prison System. As in the other institutions of the system, the primary concern is the safe custody of the men committed. Yet the Texarkana program is geared to offer every opportunity within the limits of its facilities for the men to reconstruct their lives so that they may, if they desire, return to society with a better understanding of the social values in life and the ability to make a successful adjustment. 

The over-all program of the Bureau of Prisons is progressive and the trend has developed in the direction of individualization. Texarkana is doing its part in keeping up with the policies to which the United States penal system is dedicated. ¥20 jo
